## 10. A Hand That Lies Well

My father used to say that a type case is a city in miniature — every letter a citizen with its proper address, and woe to the compositor who lets a *q* wander into the *p* district after dark. He said it so often that Tam and I could mouth it along with him behind his back, which is a form of filial disrespect available only to children raised in print shops.

I had been thinking about cities a great deal lately, and about who got sorted into which districts, and I had come to the conclusion that my father's metaphor was more accurate than he intended. Because a census was coming through Vellamark the way a compositor runs a proof: pulling off an impression of every soul, examining it for flaws, discarding the defective sheets. And somewhere in the government's galley, our family's page had a flaw in it. Mother's name appeared in no parish book. Her ink had never dried on any register they recognized. On paper — and by 1899, paper was the only country anyone acknowledged — Ilse Vane did not exist.

So three nights a week, after Father banked the furnace and Tam went up to his cot, I sat at the proofing table by the one lamp I was allowed, and I taught myself to lie in lead.

* * *

The first transit papers I copied were real ones — or rather, they were the specimen Kettle had left behind like a snake leaving its skin, folded into the census packet "for household reference." The Bureau of Movement issued them on watermarked stock, dense as communion wafers, with a border of interlocking keys printed so fine it looked at arm's length like gray corduroy. At reading distance it looked like what it was: permission. Leave the city. Cross the bridge. Board the barge. Exist, officially, in motion.

I had stolen three sheets of the watermark stock from the foundry's own stores. This required no theft at all, technically; the paper had been ordered two years ago for a job that never materialized — a commission from the Harbor Trust that collapsed when the Trust's treasurer collapsed into the river, though not, everyone agreed, by accident. So the stock sat in the flat files, waiting, the way everything in Vellamark seemed to be waiting.

The problem was never the paper. Paper is honest; it just sits there. The problem is that a document is a sentence spoken by a machine, and machines have accents. The Bureau's presses put their letters down with a particular pressure, a particular bite into the fiber, and every character stood in its line with the posture of someone who had been told exactly where to stand. My first attempts looked right if you squinted and wrong if you breathed. The spacing was too even. That was the tell, absurdly — the Bureau's typesetters were rushed men working piece rates, and their lines sagged here, crowded there, drifted off-true by degrees no foreman bothered to correct. My forgery was *better* than the genuine article. It was too well-behaved. It read like a lie told by someone who had rehearsed.

A real lie, I was learning, has loose threads left deliberately visible.

That night I set the words TRANSIT PERMIT — BUREAU OF MOVEMENT in twelve-point Caslon, then pulled a proof, held it to the lamp, and hated it. Pulled another. Hated that slightly less. By the third hour I had stopped hating it and started studying it, which is the moment any craft gets its hooks into you — when criticism turns to curiosity. Where did the Bureau's line drift? A hair leftward after the second word. Their space between PERMIT and the serial number was a fat em where mine was a tidy en. Their *R* leaned, just barely, like a tired clerk.

I adjusted. I pulled. I adjusted again. Somewhere past midnight I caught myself smiling at a sheet of paper in a way I would have been embarrassed to be seen doing, and I decided that embarrassment was a luxury the century ahead probably wouldn't stock.

Tam found me at the fourth attempt.

He came down the stairs in his nightshirt the way Tam did everything — loudly in intention, silently in fact, a boy built like a rumor. One moment the loft creaked; the next he was sitting cross-legged on the type cabinet, watching me pull a proof with his chin on his fists.

"You're forging," he said. Not a question. Twelve-year-olds have no use for questions when statements will do.

"I'm practicing typography," I said.

"On government paper."

"Typography requires government paper."

He considered this. Tam considers things seriously, which is different from considering them long. Then he said, "If they catch you, they'll take your hands."

"They'd have to find out first."

"Will you tell Father?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the patrol went by on the quay, boots keeping their stupid perfect time. I thought about lying to him, and then I thought about what a strange household ours would become if we started spending lies on each other when we had a whole government to spend them on.

"No," I said. "Not yet."

"Then I didn't see anything." He slid off the cabinet, paused at the stair, and added, over his shoulder, with terrible gravity: "Your *R*s are good but your kerning's for garbage."

And he was gone before I could decide whether to be furious or proud. I settled, as one does, on both.

* * *

My father discovered me on the sixth night, and I want to record accurately how it happened, because how it happened is the whole chapter of my life that followed.

He wasn't supposed to be downstairs. He had gone up at ten with the cough he'd been nursing since the notices went up on the bridge — a dry, official-sounding cough, as though his lungs had begun filing reports. But fathers do not sleep properly once their households begin keeping secrets; some animal part of them counts bodies in the dark, and mine had counted one missing.

I heard the stair and had time only to sweep the proof under the blotter, which was idiotic, because the evidence remained everywhere: the Bureau stock open in the flat file, the composed line still locked in the chase, the lamp burning at an hour no honest compositor keeps. Aldous Vane came around the press and looked at the table the way he looked at a misprinted sheet — without surprise, with a kind of professional grief.

Then he lifted the blotter, and we both looked at my forgery together, father and daughter, in the small circle of lamplight, while the city slept around us believing itself counted and safe.

"How many," he said.

"Six proofs. That's the best."

He picked it up. He held it at arm's length, then close, then tilted it against the light to catch the impression — the way he handled every sheet that came out of this foundry for thirty years, including the ones with my name spelled wrong on my first apprentice card. I waited for anger. I had rehearsed defenses during the walk down from supper: it was practice, it was theory, it was for the family, it was for Mother. I had my chin set at the angle that made him call me stubborn, which in our house was a compliment wearing a disguise.

Instead he sat down across from me, took off his spectacles, cleaned them on his apron, put them back on, and said:

"Show me your spacing method."

"What?"

"Your spacing. How are you justifying these lines?"

"By eye," I said. "Evenly."

"There it is," he said, and there was something in his voice I couldn't name yet, something between sorrow and pride, like a chord played in a minor key. "Evenly. You've set it like scripture. Scripture wants evenness. Bureaus don't produce scripture, Hazel. They produce paperwork, by tired men, at speed. If your lie is neater than their truth, the truth will convict you."

I stared at him.

"You're not angry," I said.

He was quiet a moment. The lamp hissed. When he spoke, he spoke in the voice he used at the case, teaching voice, the low one, the one that meant the lesson mattered more than the mood.

"I spent thirty years teaching you that type is honest," he said. "Every character does exactly what it's told, stands exactly where it stands. I believed that made printing a moral trade. Set true words in true forms and the world might — " he waved a hand that took in the press, the cases, the whole dim shop — "might align. Like a forme locked tight."

"And now?"

"Now the world has started setting *us*," he said. "Sorting us into galleys. Deciding which characters get to stay in the line." He turned my forged permit over, studied the back as though the watermark confessed better from behind. "I find I've revised my theology. Type isn't honest, Hazel. Type is obedient. And obedience is a weapon, depending on who holds the stick."

He rose, went to the far wall, and took down a case I had never seen him open — the top case, the good one, the French Old Style he'd bought with his first independent earnings and treated ever since like a relic. He set it on the table between us with a care that bordered on ceremony.

"If you're going to do this," he said, "you'll do it properly. Sit."

I sat. And my father — Aldous Vane, printer, moralist, the man whose sermons about honest work had furnished my entire childhood — began to teach me how to lie so well that God himself would need a loupe.

* * *

What follows is the inheritance. Not the foundry — the foundry was already mortgaged twice over, and anyway the government had ideas about it. Not the name — names were precisely what the Bureau was in the business of revoking. This: the tricks. The craft inside the crime. I give it here as he gave it to me, in order, because order matters in composition and in confession.

**First, the drift.** No Bureau line runs true, because no Bureau compositor is paid to make it run true. Every line should wander a half-point off-square by the third word and correct itself by the end, overcorrecting slightly. "Their lines breathe badly," Father said. "They inhale toward the margin. Give your lies a bad chest."

**Second, the worn sorts.** Real offices have letters that have been dropped, chipped, battered by ten thousand impressions. An *e* with a nick in the bowl. A *t* whose crossbar has gone thin. A new document full of pristine characters is a document from a press that doesn't exist. So he took his graver — his actual graver, the tool his own master had pressed into his hands twenty-five years ago — and he nicked a lowercase *e*, deliberately, lovingly, the way a forger of paintings cracks his varnish. "There," he said, handing me the sort. "Now your Bureau employs a clumsy man named Pieters, who drops his *e*s on Thursdays. Print only on Thursdays."

**Third — and here he leaned close, and lowered his voice as though the press itself might inform — kerning.** The spaces between letters, the invisible architecture. Amateurs think forgery lives in the shapes of letters; professionals know it lives in the silences between them. The Bureau's men spaced by habit, not measure: too much room around capitals, starved gaps before commas, a signature quirk where the second word of any line sat a point too near the first. "Read their specimens," Father said. "Not the words. The weather between the words. Every office has a climate. Forge the climate, and the words can say anything at all."

We worked until three. He showed me how to fake the uneven inking of a roller past its prime — a ghosting at the left edge of each impression, because the Bureau's man always re-inked from the left, lazy, consistent, human. He showed me how to age paper in the steam above the kettle without cockling it. He showed me how to break a line in the same place their compositors broke it, mid-phrase, against all sense, because sense costs money and bureaucracy never spends it.

And somewhere past two o'clock, pulling a proof together — four hands on the bar, his over mine, guiding the pressure — I felt the strangest thing. Not guilt. I'd expected guilt; guilt had been queuing in me for days like everyone else queues in this city. What I felt was recognition. His hands teaching mine. His knowledge pouring down into me like lead into a mould, taking the shape of whatever vessel received it. He had meant to pass me an honest trade. The times had melted it down, and he was recasting it without complaint, and I understood that this — the quiet, competent, unprotesting adaptation of conscience to necessity — was also an inheritance. Maybe the deepest one. Vane men and women do not ask whether the work is clean. They ask whether it is done *well*.

"Father," I said, as the proof came off the stone, crisp and false and beautiful, "does it trouble you? Teaching me this?"

He looked at the sheet a long time. In it, a woman named Ilse Vane — who existed in every way except the ways the government counted — was granted leave to exist in motion, aboard a barge that hadn't yet been announced, bound for a destination neither of us could pronounce without our voices going strange.

"It troubles me," he said finally, "that my daughter needed to learn it before her father thought to teach her. Everything else—" he tapped the forged permit, once, gently, like knocking on a door he hoped someone would answer— "everything else is just kerning."

* * *

I burned the six failed attempts in the furnace before dawn, and watched the flames eat the Bureau's watermark, and felt nothing so simple as relief. Then I locked the seventh proof — the good one, the living one — into the chase beside my father's finest type, and went up to bed as the sky over Vellamark went the gray of unsized paper.

I was nearly asleep when I heard Tam's cot creak, and his whisper come down through the floorboards, muffled and delighted:

"Told you. Kerning."

But sleep did not come, because under the whisper, under the furnace ticking as it cooled, I heard the other sound — the one the whole city had learned to listen for now. Boots on the quay, earlier than usual. And beneath them, fainter, stranger: voices at our own door. Three of them. Measured. Official.

One of them, I would have sworn on my father's nicked lowercase *e*, belonged to Ambrose Kettle.
