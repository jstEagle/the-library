## 14. Doors Marked in Chalk

My sister thinks I don't notice things. Hazel is eleven years my senior and carries her eleven years like a satchel of ledgers she's decided the whole family must read. She notices everything and writes it all down in that face of hers, every flicker of it, so precise you'd think noticing was a trade like typesetting, with apprenticeships and guild secrets.

I notice things too. I just don't advertise.

For instance: I noticed, three mornings before the soldiers came, that the milk cart stopped at our door for four seconds longer than it stopped anywhere else on Cutter's Row. I noticed the milkman never actually looked at our house while he was looking at it, which takes a certain professional discipline. And I noticed, two days after that, a man across the street pretending to read a handbill he was holding upside down. He stood there for twenty minutes. If Vellamark had a guild of terrible spies, he would have been its master.

So when I came around the corner on Thursday morning with a sack of coal dust for the stove and saw three soldiers standing at our door with chalk in hand, I wasn't surprised so much as disappointed. I'd been hoping to be wrong about something for once.

* * *

Let me explain about chalk, because nobody explains things in this city anymore; they just do them to you and expect gratitude for the efficiency.

The seizure mark is a single stroke crossed by two shorter ones — a sort of lopsided star, or a bird seen from underneath. The clerks call it *the requisition glyph*, which is the most government sentence ever assembled from four syllables. To everyone else it means: this building belongs to the Crown now, and whatever lives inside it has until dusk to remove itself, if "itself" is on the list of selves permitted to exist elsewhere.

They'd marked six doors on our row already. The Habermanns'. The old cobbler who'd gone deaf and therefore hadn't heard his own door get chalked, which I maintain was a mercy. The widow Ostrow, whose door they marked twice because the rain smudged the first one and a clerk's work must be legible even when it's monstrous.

And now ours.

Here is what I did not do. I did not run. Running is what they want; running confirms you're the sort of person doors get chalked for. Instead I did what any twelve-year-old with a sack of coal dust and a plan older than the sack does.

I walked past them.

It's harder than it sounds. Three soldiers make a lot of noise — boots, buckles, one of them whistling something cheerful that I decided on the spot was my new least favorite song. I kept my eyes down and my shoulders round, the way boys look when they're carrying something heavy and thinking about nothing, which is a performance I have been rehearsing my entire life without knowing it. Coal dust helps. You smear a little on your cheek and suddenly you're furniture. I've been furniture on more street corners than I can count since the census started.

"Oi," said the whistler.

Furniture, Tam. Be furniture.

"You live there?" He nodded at our door, at the fresh white bird dying on the wood.

I looked where he pointed with the expression of a boy being asked an extremely boring question. "That's the type foundry."

"I know what it is. Do you live there?"

"My dad works there." This was true. It has been true for nineteen years. I have learned that the best lies are load-bearing walls built entirely out of truth.

The soldier looked me over. I watched him decide I was nothing — a scrawny thing in a man's cast-off coat with black hands and a blank face — and I felt the particular cold satisfaction of being underestimated, which I am beginning to suspect is the only power a person my size gets issued in this century.

He went back to whistling. I went inside.

* * *

Hazel was at the press. She spends most mornings at the press now, doing legitimate work with an illegitimate expression, and I have made a private study of the difference. When she sets type for a paying client, her mouth does one thing. When she sets type for the other thing — the thing she thinks Father doesn't know about, the thing she thinks *I* don't know about, though I've known since October when I caught her practicing Father's handwriting and swore silence in exchange for first refusal on all future secrets — her whole body changes. She leans closer. She breathes through her mouth. Forgers, I have concluded, are mouth-breathers.

"The door," I said.

She didn't stop setting. "What about it?"

"They chalked it."

Her hands stopped. That's the thing about Hazel: her face lies beautifully but her hands are honest as churchbells. They hung above the case for a full second before she turned around.

"When."

"Ten minutes ago. Three of them. One whistle-happy. The bird with the crossed wings." I drew it in coal dust on the edge of the composing stone, because I think in pictures and she thinks in letters and between us we nearly make a whole newspaper.

She stared at the little white star. Then she did something I didn't expect. She laughed. One short, ugly sound, like a hiccup with opinions.

"Of course," she said. "Of course it's today. Kettle said end of the week, and Kettle keeps his appointments the way plague keeps its promises." She was already moving, pulling the loose boards behind the lead cabinet aside, checking the hollow where she keeps — well. Where she keeps the other work. "Dusk, Tam. We have until dusk before someone comes to inventory us out of existence."

"So we scrub it."

She looked at me the way she looks at a line of type with a letter upside down. "Scrubbing it doesn't unmake the record. There's a clerk somewhere with a book saying our door bears the mark. Scrubbing makes us *liars*, and liars get—"

"Liars get berths," I said. "Liars get barges. Liars are the only people still living indoors come spring."

That landed. I watched it land. My sister, who can argue the sun into setting earlier, had nothing.

"It won't hold," she said finally. "A day, maybe. But a day is—" She stopped herself. A day is what we needed once. I knew which day she meant. Everyone in this family has started measuring time in days we needed, the way sailors measure in fathoms.

"A day is a day," I said, and went for the bucket.

* * *

You would be amazed how much thought goes into erasing a symbol. Or maybe you wouldn't; maybe you're the sensible sort who assumes a rag and some elbow does the trick, in which case you've clearly never been twelve years old in a police state with a reputation to protect.

Plain scrubbing leaves a ghost. Chalk is calcium and spite, and spite binds to oak. Hazel figured it out faster than I did — she always figures things faster, but I'm learning to catch up — that you need lye first, then pumice, then oil to feed the wood back its color, then a final wash so the patch doesn't sit darker than the door and announce itself to every passing eye. We worked from inside the doorway, reaching out, because working from the street is how people get arrested in broad daylight.

It was nearly noon when I got greedy.

Here's my confession, since this chapter seems determined to collect them: I wanted the door *perfect*. Not clean — perfect. Because the door is Father's door. He oiled it himself every season, he planed the swell out of the bottom board the winter I had fever, and the idea of a white bird sitting on his oak until strangers came to carry away his life was a splinter I could not leave under my skin. So when the last pass left the faintest shadow — a ghost of a wingtip, nothing, a smudge a magistrate would laugh at — I took the bucket and the brush and stepped off the threshold into the open street to finish it.

The street was empty.

That should have been my warning. Cutter's Row is never empty at noon. Empty streets mean eyes.

I got exactly three strokes in when the boots started. Two pairs, fast, from the direction of the bridge. No whistling this time. I did the arithmetic of distance — thirty yards, maybe less, and a bucket, and a brush, and legs that were still mostly promise — and understood with total clarity that I had been an idiot, that idiocy at my age is not charming, it is fatal, and that Hazel was going to kill me anyway so the soldiers would really just be saving her the trouble.

I dropped the brush. I didn't run — running confirms — but walking suddenly seemed like a thing other people did, in other cities.

"Boy."

I turned. You have to turn; not turning is worse. The tall one had his hand on his baton but hadn't drawn it, which I filed under *probably survivable*, and the short one was squinting at our door with the expression of a man comparing a picture to the original and finding the original uncooperative.

"That door had a mark on it," the short one said.

"Did it?"

"Don't." He came close enough that I could smell his breakfast. "We marked it this morning. Requisition order, signed, sealed. Now it hasn't got one. Explain that."

I had explanations ready — rain, children, the general wickedness of chalk — but they were explanations for a boy caught scrubbing, and what he saw when he looked at me was a boy holding a wet brush over a wet door, which is not explanation territory, it's confession territory. My mouth opened. Nothing good came out.

And that is when the stranger arrived.

* * *

He came out of the alley between our foundry and the Habermanns' place, and he moved like men move when they've done this before — unhurried, sideways, apologetic, the way you approach a pair of horses that might bite. He wore a printer's apron, ink-stained at the hip where a compositor wipes his hand, and he was carrying a tray of type under one arm like it was the most natural burden in the world.

"There you are," he said to me.

Every head turned, including mine, including mine twice, because I had no idea who he was.

He shook his head at me with warm, weary disappointment, the universal expression of masters toward apprentices everywhere. "I send you for lye and you come back with the whole afternoon gone. Mr. Brenner will have my hide, and yours after." Then, to the soldiers, lifting the type tray slightly in salute: "Morning, gentlemen. Trouble?"

"The door was marked," the short one said.

"It was indeed." The stranger set the tray down on our step — our step, casual as Sunday — and produced from his apron pocket a folded paper. "And then it was unmarked, by order." He unfolded it with the flourish of a man who handles paper professionally. "Brenner and Sons, jobbing printers, contracted by the Office of Census and Removal for the printing of requisition marks, transit forms, and related instruments. Your clerk's chalk was applied to the wrong address — easy done, the rows all look alike — and corrected per amendment." He handed it over. "You'll want to note the seal."

The tall soldier took it. He read it the way soldiers read, lips moving, trusting his mouth more than his eyes.

I read it differently. I read the *type*. I couldn't help it; I grew up in a foundry, reading type is how other families say grace. And the type on that amendment was set in a face I knew better than my own reflection — Garamond's heir, the cut Father calls *the honest widow* — except for one detail. The lowercase e. Our e has a slightly closed eye. Father says it's a flaw in the matrix. Hazel says flaws are just signatures waiting to be recognized.

This e was wide open. Someone else's type. Someone else's press. Someone else's forgery, done better and faster than anything I'd seen Hazel manage, on official paper with a real seal, carried by a stranger who happened to be standing in an alley at the exact moment a boy needed saving.

The soldiers handed the paper back, muttered something about clerks who couldn't read their own lists, and left. The whistling one, arriving late to his own scene, passed us going the other direction and whistled louder, as if volume could restore his dignity.

Then the alley was empty again, the street was ordinary, and the stranger picked up his type tray.

"You'll want to re-chalk nothing," he said quietly, to me, to the door, to whoever he actually reported to. "And you'll want your sister to know her e needs closing. Anyone who matters will see it." He was already moving. "Tell her the widow Ostrow sends her regards."

"Widow Ostrow is deaf," I said.

"She reads excellently," he said, and turned the corner by the coal chute, and was gone the way smoke is gone — technically present in the room, practically nowhere.

* * *

Hazel listened to the whole account standing very still, which for Hazel is the equivalent of another person screaming.

"He knew about the e," she said for the third time. "He's seen my work."

"He's seen lots of people's work, probably. He runs a whole underground print shop, apparently, with seals and amendments and—"

"Tam." She sat down on the composing stone. My sister never sits on the composing stone; Father's rules about the composing stone are the closest thing our family has to scripture. "There are maybe six presses in Vellamark that could turn out a document like that overnight. I've thought about which ones. I've *had* to think about which ones. And I never once considered—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "They've been watching us. Since when? Since Kettle? Since the ledger? Since when, Tam?"

"Since the milk cart," I said, and told her about the four seconds, and the upside-down handbill, and the discipline of the milkman's gaze.

She lowered her hands and looked at me with an expression I want to write down exactly, because I suspect I'll need it later: half terror, half the look she gets when she finds a font she's never catalogued. "You knew. You knew and you let me walk around thinking I was the careful one."

"You *are* the careful one. I just count seconds."

"*Widow Ostrow.*" She laughed that ugly laugh again, but it broke in the middle this time, into something younger. "The deaf woman who can't meet Mother's eye on the bridge. She's been running messages past the registration queue for — how long? Under their noses. While we sat here forging one page at a time like children practicing scales."

"We *are* children practicing scales."

"We're dead children practicing scales unless we get better." She stood, and there it was, the change I'd started recognizing — the lean, the mouth-breath, the whole body tilting toward the press like a compass finding north. But different this time. Bigger. "An amendment, Tam. A real one, on real paper, with a real seal, delivered in ten minutes. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere in this city there's a shop that doesn't wait for dusk. It means there are people who decided the Vanes were worth a forgery, and I intend to find out why before Kettle comes to collect on Friday."

"Maybe they just like doors."

"Nobody likes doors, Tam." She pulled the loose board aside and began lifting out the hidden work, sorting it with quick fingers. "People like what doors keep safe. Go count the coal. If we're staying, we're staying warm, and if we're leaving—" She didn't finish it. Nobody finishes that sentence in this house anymore.

I went to count the coal. On my way past the front door I stopped, just once, and put my palm flat against the oak where the bird had died. The wood was still damp. It smelled of lye and pumice and, faintly, of the ink the stranger's paper had left behind — a smell I have decided, at twelve years old, in the last winter of the worst century on record, is the smell of not being alone.

Friday was coming. Kettle was coming. And somewhere across the city, a deaf woman who reads excellently was waiting to hear whether we'd take the hint.

I counted the coal. There wasn't enough. There was never going to be enough again.

But upstairs, the press started moving, and it wasn't setting anyone's name this time.
