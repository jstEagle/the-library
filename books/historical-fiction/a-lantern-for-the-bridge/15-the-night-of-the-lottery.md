## 15. The Night of the Lottery

I had set type for funeral notices before. I knew the weight those words carried in the hand—the way *beloved* and *suddenly* and *survived by* settle into lead like stones into a riverbed. But I had never set type for a proclamation that was, in every sense that mattered, a death notice for a whole city.

The Vane foundry had been commissioned—through channels I did not ask about, because asking questions of the men who delivered the order felt like poking a sleeping dog with a stick made of questions—to print the lottery notices for the last barge of the century. Ten thousand berths, give or take. A city of sixty thousand souls.

Father read the figures aloud twice and then sat down on his stool without taking off his coat, which in thirty years of foundry work I had never once seen him do.

"They want us to print our own arithmetic," he said. "Sixty thousand. Ten thousand."

"Paper is cheaper than people," I said. "Everyone knows that. It's practically the municipal motto."

He didn't laugh. That was how I knew we had crossed some border neither of us could name, where the jokes stopped working like ballast and started feeling like something you'd say at a funeral to avoid crying at it.

Tam wanted to come to the bridge. He argued his case with the thoroughness he brought to everything since he'd started mapping the city's underbelly—he knew which staircases gave the best sightlines, which rooftops overhung the crowd, which coal chutes offered escape routes if things went wrong. He said *if*, not *when*. Twelve-year-olds still got to have their *ifs*.

"You'll stay home," Father said.

"I can help—"

"You'll stay home." And then, softer, in the voice he used when he was admitting something rather than commanding it: "There are things a boy your age should see once, Tam. Not twice."

So it was Mother who came with me, wrapped in her gray shawl, walking beside me through streets gone strange with anticipation. Shops had closed early—not boarded, not abandoned, just dark, as if the whole city were holding its breath behind its own windows. People moved toward the bridge the way water moves toward a drain: individually reasonable, collectively terrifying.

"Ilse," I said, using her name the way she'd taught me to when we walked in public, two women of no particular family, nothing worth counting, "stay near me."

She squeezed my arm. She had been squeezing my arm for weeks now, ever since Kettle's visits began, and each squeeze carried a message she couldn't speak aloud: *Whatever comes, I have already made my peace with it. Don't you make yours too early.*

The bridge at night was a country I didn't recognize.

Lanterns everywhere—hundreds of them, hung from the ironwork by families who wanted light to find their names by, so that the whole span glowed like a string of festival lights except nobody was celebrating and everybody was praying. The census officials had erected a platform at the center, draped in bunting the color of official optimism, and behind it, drums of paper slips sealed with wax. Soldiers lined the approaches in ranks loose enough to look casual and tight enough to remind you they weren't.

We found a place against the railing about forty feet from the platform. Close enough to see. Far enough, I hoped, to remain furniture rather than participants.

A magistrate whose name I will not dignify here—I had printed enough documents bearing his seal by then to feel I knew him intimately, the way you know a man whose signature you've forged—stepped up and read the rules. Every registered household head would draw one slip. Gold wax meant a berth. Gray wax meant another drawing next quarter, or the quarter after, or whenever the machinery of mercy ground out its next small ration. One slip per household. One chance per family.

"One slip per household," he repeated, and the crowd murmured the number back like a psalm: *one, one, one.*

Mother's hand found mine again. Hers was very cold.

The first drawing went smoothly. The second. The third. A fishwife named Marta Ostrander drew gold and screamed loud enough to crack a lantern, and her neighbors lifted her onto their shoulders and carried her toward the quay already celebrating, and for one bright moment the whole bridge believed in the mathematics of luck.

Then a cooper named Brenner drew gray, and looked at the slip, and did not put it down.

I saw it happen from forty feet away and understood it completely: the moment when a piece of paper stops being paper and becomes the whole of your future. Brenner held his gray slip up to a lantern and read it as though reading might change it. Then he looked at the drum, at the thousands of slips still inside it, at the arithmetic Father had sat down beneath, and something in his face simply closed, the way a shop closes, shutters down, done for the day, possibly forever.

"It's rigged," he said. Not loudly. Just clearly.

"It is not—" the magistrate began.

"It's *rigged*." Louder now, and the word went through the crowd the way a dropped torch goes through a hay barn. "Ten thousand slips and I've watched thirty drawn and twenty-nine gray. You counted us, didn't you? Counted us like cattle and now you shake us in a drum and call it fate—"

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do, so that afterward everyone remembers different first causes. A soldier stepped toward Brenner. Someone shoved the soldier. The soldier's lantern went down and rolled, and flame licked across spilled lamp oil, and the crowd—which had been standing shoulder to shoulder in good order—became suddenly aware that it was standing shoulder to shoulder, and that the railings were behind them, and that the river was below.

I have thought often about what a crowd becomes at the moment it stops being a crowd. It does not become a mob, whatever the newspapers said later. It becomes water. Water finding its level. And the level, that night, was the platform.

People did not storm the drums because they were lawless. They stormed the drums because ten thousand slips sat inside them and every single person pressing forward was doing arithmetic of their own—*my mother, my children, my wife who coughs blood, my son who is twelve and deserves an if*—and the arithmetic said: *the slips are right there.* Why trust a drum when you can trust your hands?

The soldiers fixed bayonets. That is not a figure of speech; I heard the metal click and snap of it, a sound like the foundry's clamps closing, and I understood that men were about to die for wanting paper.

"Hold the rail," Mother shouted into my ear, and her grip on my arm went from comfort to anchor.

The press came toward us like a tide. I saw Mrs. Halloran from the chandlery go down—Mrs. Halloran, who had sold Father lamp oil for twenty years, who had given Tam sweets when he was small—and the crowd walked over her the way you walk over cobblestones, without malice, without even noticing, and I screamed her name and my scream vanished into six thousand other screams.

A man next to us tore his own coat getting free of the crush and stood holding the torn half like a man holding his own skin. An old scholar—I recognized him from the reading room, Herr Vogel, who had once lent me a book on typography—was pushed against the railing hard enough that I saw the breath leave him, and the crowd moved on, and I don't know, even now, whether he got up.

They reached the platform. The drums burst. Paper rose into the air like a blizzard of white moths—gold wax and gray wax spinning together, indistinguishable now, worthless now, because a slip only means something when someone agrees to honor it, and the agreement had died somewhere between Brenner's shout and the first bayonet.

And in the middle of all that snowfall of paper, I saw the truth of it, cold and complete, the way you see a finished page:

*It was designed this way.*

Not the riot specifically. But the system. The lottery wasn't mercy—it was a machine for converting desperation into self-destruction. Give sixty thousand people ten thousand chances, make them draw against their own neighbors, and they will do your ugly work for you. No soldier needed to push Mrs. Halloran. The crowd did it gladly, desperately, each person convinced they were only trying to save their own, which is exactly what everyone else was convinced of too. The census had sorted us, the lottery had pitted us, and the men on the platform—safe behind their bayonets—would write reports describing an unruly populace, a regrettable incident, the inherent violence of the lower orders.

We were being taught to eat each other so no one would notice who had set the table.

Mother pulled me back from the railing. Her face was wet—not tears, I realized, but spray from the river, or maybe tears, there was no telling anymore—and she was shouting something at me, and it took three tries before I heard it:

"Not here. Not here, Hazel. Whatever you're going to do—do it where they can't see your hands."

I looked down at my hands. Ink in the cuticles, as always. Lead dust in the creases. Hands that had learned to lie beautifully.

Around me the riot burned itself out against the cold, the way riots do, leaving the bridge strewn with paper and shoes and one gray wool mitten nobody would ever claim. Soldiers moved among the wreckage making arrests mostly at random. Somewhere downstream, I supposed, the barge waited, patient as a coffin.

We walked home through streets full of people clutching slips—gold ones held like relics, gray ones held like verdicts—past doors where families were learning what one slip per household meant when the household had five names in it. At our own door, Tam flung it open before we knocked, and the relief on his face lasted exactly long enough for him to see ours.

"Marta Ostrander got gold," he said, grasping for good news the way a drowning man grasps driftwood. "The fishwife. Everyone's saying—"

"Mrs. Halloran went down in the crush," I said. "I don't know if she got up."

Tam looked at the floor. When he looked up again, something had rearranged itself behind his eyes—the same rearrangement I'd felt on the bridge, the horror settling and cooling into something harder and more useful. He was twelve years old, and he was finished being twelve, and I hated every adult in Vellamark who had done that to him, starting with myself.

"The maps," he said quietly. "Hazel. The sewers, the coal chutes, the old culverts under the customs house. If people knew—if it was written down where the gaps are, where the patrols don't go—" He stopped. Started again. "You can print things. Can't you?"

I thought of Mrs. Halloran under the feet of her neighbors. Of Herr Vogel against the rail. Of ten thousand slips fluttering down like snow that melted into nothing, while the men who shook the drum stood dry on their platform.

I thought of Kettle, ascending, and the price of pages, and the confession with the magistrate's seal folded in my apron pocket like a knife I hadn't yet decided to use.

"Yes," I said. "I can print things."

That night, after Tam slept and Mother kept her vigil at the window, I went down to the foundry alone and lit one lamp over the composing stone. I didn't set type for the broadsheet yet—that would take planning, care, Father's kerning tricks, all the discipline of the craft turned finally to its true purpose. Tonight I only stood there with my ink-stained hands flat on the stone, letting the horror finish crystallizing, watching it become not grief but geometry: the exact shape of what needed doing, and who needed it done to.

The lottery had been designed to make the desperate destroy themselves.

Fine. Then I would design something too.

Above my head, through the floorboards, I heard my mother moving toward the door, coat already on, going out into the frozen dark on whatever errand she believed I knew nothing about. I let her go. There are bargains mothers make that daughters must pretend not to count.

But I stood in the lamplight of the foundry, alone with the type cases of a trade that had raised me, and I understood that the next document I set would not be forged in anyone's hand but my own—and that it would not be a lie at all.
