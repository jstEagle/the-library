## 16. What Kettle Wants

There is a particular pleasure in being summoned rather than sent. For eleven years I have been the man who climbs stairs — to counting houses, to parish offices, to the damp upper rooms of widows who keep their papers in biscuit tins. I have climbed so many stairs that my knees have developed opinions. But tonight the summons comes to *me*: a note on Ministry card stock, delivered by a boy who will not meet my eye, asking Mr. Ambrose Kettle to attend Deputy Registrar Hollis at his convenience.

Hollis does not say *at your convenience*. Nobody in this city says that anymore except undertakers and liars. But the note says it, and I read it three times on the walk over, savoring the grammar of my own rising stock.

The Ministry building has grown lamps since autumn. They want it visible, you see — a lit building in a dimming city is its own kind of proclamation. *We are still here. We still count.* Inside, the clerks work behind glass now, little panes installed after the incident at the registry window in November, when a woman whose name had been struck from the rolls tried to reach through and strangle the boy who told her. She didn't manage it. But she left a crack in the glass shaped like nothing at all, like weather, like the future, and they never replaced the pane. You can see her crack there still, if you look, running through the word CIVIL in CIVIL REGISTRATION.

Hollis receives me in an office with an actual carpet. I make a point of noticing carpets. It tells you what a man thinks he deserves.

"Kettle." He doesn't rise. He's a heavy man with the complexion of a man who believes in butter, and he has the gift — rare in the Ministry — of making flattery sound like arithmetic. "Sit. Sit. Do you drink?"

"At this hour? Yes."

He pours two glasses of something amber and expensive, which tells me more than the carpet did. He slides one across. "You've done good work."

"Thank you, sir."

"The Vane household. Thorough file. Very thorough." He turns a page on his desk without looking at it, which is a performance, because he knows every line of that file; I wrote it, and I know what a man looks like when he's reciting his own notes. "Discrepancies handled properly. Evidence preserved. Chain of custody immaculate."

"I try to be careful, sir."

"Yes." He smiles. "Careful. That's the word for you, isn't it. Careful." He drinks. "There's a promotion coming. Berth assignment authority. The lottery's a farce, Kettle, we both know it — half those slips are printed by boys who can't spell *berth* — but someone respectable has to administer it. Someone with clean hands." He holds up his own hands, palms out, like a magician proving there's nothing up the sleeves. There is always something up the sleeves. "I've put your name forward."

I feel the warmth arrive before I decide whether to trust it. This is the danger of ambition: it bypasses judgment entirely and goes straight to the blood, like brandy. "That's — generous, sir."

"It's not generosity. It's investment. Which brings me to the Vane case." He sets down his glass and folds his hands, and now the butter melts off his face and something colder shows underneath. "Deliver it. The whole thing, formal charges, seizure order, the works — by month's end. Make the Vanes the example. A printer's family, falsified papers, right in the middle of the guild quarter — it will do more for compliance than fifty proclamations. People fear what happens to their neighbors' kind more than what happens to their own."

"And the family?"

"What about them?"

"They'll be removed, sir. On the barge or otherwise."

"On the barge, ideally. Publicly. Orderly. We're not barbarians." He refills his glass, generous with everything except futures. "Do that, and the berth office is yours. Think of it, Kettle. Every name on that manifest passes through your hands. Every family in this city will know who decides whether they float."

*Whether they float.* He says it the way another man might say *whether they dine*. I look into my glass and understand, with the mild shock of a man finding his own face already waiting in a mirror, that I am going to accept.

I had thought, walking over, that I might refuse. I had composed, somewhere in the attic of myself, a version of the evening where I set down my pen and said *no, sir, some pages should stay unturned*. That version of me is dead now, and what surprises me is how little funeral he requires.

"I'll need a week," I say. "To finalize the evidence."

"You have five days." He raises his glass to me. "To the new century, Kettle. May we all be on the right side of the water when it comes."

* * *

The foundry smells of hot metal and oil even at night, even cold. I stand across the lane from it for a full quarter hour before knocking, because I have learned that the first minute of watching a house tells you more than an hour inside it. Smoke from the chimney — someone's working late. A lamp in the upstairs window — someone's keeping watch. And a second lamp, low, in the workshop — someone's printing.

I knock.

It takes a long time for the door to open, and when it does, it opens only as far as the chain allows, and Hazel Vane's eye appears in the gap with the flat patience of a cat watching a trap it intends to spring itself.

"Mr. Kettle."

"Miss Vane. May I come in?"

"You may come in," she says, "and you may notice that I'm alone in this house with my twelve-year-old brother, which means you may keep the door open behind you, and you may sit where I can see your hands."

I admire this. I want to say I admire it out loud, but admiration is currency and I've learned not to spend it where it buys nothing. I enter. The door stays open. The December air comes in with me and stands in the corner like a chaperone.

The workshop has been stripped. That's the first thing I see. The type cases that were stacked to the ceiling in October are gone — crated, roped, labeled in a firm hand. Three traveling cases sit by the door. The great press wears a dust sheet like a widow. Only one composing stick remains on the stone, and beside it a single galley of type, covered, the way you'd cover a birdcage to keep it quiet.

"You've been busy," I say.

"We're leaving."

"One berth," I say. "One case. One name. That was the arrangement."

"That was your offer." She pours tea from a pot on the stove — she does not ask whether I want any, simply sets the cup near me, and the presumption is so precisely calibrated to unsettle that I almost applaud. "You'll forgive me if I've spent the interval doing arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I say, "is my trade."

"Then let's do sums." She sits across the stone from me, and up close I can see what these weeks have made of her: the softness burned off, the eyes gone from a girl's eyes to a ledger's. "You came here tonight to raise the price. I could tell from the way you knocked. Men who keep bargains knock once, quietly. Men who renegotiate knock like the rent is due."

I laugh. I can't help it. It escapes me like a sort of hiccup of respect. "Miss Vane—"

"Ambrose." She uses my Christian name the way you'd use a knife on a knot. "Let's not waste the courtesy. What do you want?"

I lay it out. There's no art to it anymore; we're past art. "Two berths. Your parents both travel. In exchange, the foundry. Deed, inventory, press, and type. Signed to the Ministry as forfeit compensation — the paperwork calls it voluntary restitution, and nobody will ever read past that phrase."

Silence. The stove ticks. Somewhere above us Tam Vane's floorboard creaks — awake, listening, doing his own arithmetic.

"The type," Hazel says at last, and her voice has gone very quiet, which is worse than loud. "You want my father's type."

"The press especially. A working press, in a city about to need proclamations. And the type with it." I spread my hands. "It's fair. Two lives for a roomful of lead."

"It's not lead." For a moment the ledger falls away and something older looks out of her — something that sits up nights cutting letters by lamplight while her father quizzes her on what fits in one case. "It's his whole life poured into sorts. Every letter he ever set. You're asking him to buy his wife's passage with his own alphabet."

"I'm asking *you*, Miss Vane. What he'd pay is between him and his conscience. Though I'll note the barge takes families under one identity, not one identity and a corpse of a trade."

She is silent for a long moment. Then she stands, walks to the covered galley, and lifts the cloth — not to show me type, I realize, but to give her hands something honest to hold while the rest of her works.

"No," she says.

"No?"

"No. Counter-offer." She turns around. "One berth. As agreed. My mother travels — you'll forge whatever internal paper makes that tidy; you're better at losing pages than I am, you've had more practice. In exchange, three things."

"Three things," I repeat. "You negotiate like my landlady."

"First: my silence. Permanent. About the pages you've erased, Mr. Kettle — and don't look surprised, I've seen the gaps in your file copies. A discrepancy here, a family there, each one paid you something. You've been running a private tollgate on the road to salvation, and I know where the receipts are buried." Her voice never rises. That's the frightening part. She speaks of my ruin the way she'd read aloud a shipping list. "Second: you leave the foundry alone. No seizure, no chalk on the door, no example made of us. The Vane file closes as *resolved — departed*. One household fewer, no spectacle, nothing for Hollis to wave at the guilds."

"And third?"

She reaches beneath the composing stone and brings out a single sheet of paper, folded twice, and lays it on the stone between us without opening it. Even folded, I know what it is. I've been dreading it since the night I named my price and watched her face go white and then calm, calm in the way ice goes calm.

"The magistrate's confession," I say. "With his seal."

"Your insurance against me," she agrees. "And mine against you. Because here's my third term, Ambrose: this page exists in duplicate. One copy you may hold — consider it your collateral, proof I'll keep our bargain. The other copy sits somewhere you will never find it, addressed and ready to post to the *Advocate*, to the Registrar-General, and to the magistrate himself, should anything happen to me, or to my brother, or to my mother's berth. You wanted leverage over a magistrate. Congratulations. So do I now. The difference is I'd rather never use mine."

I look at the folded page for a long time. The stove ticks. Upstairs, the floorboard creaks again, deliberate this time — the boy letting me know he heard everything, which means the girl arranged for him to hear everything, which means even this negotiation has witnesses she controls.

I think about Hollis's carpet. His butter-colored confidence. *Every family in this city will know who decides whether they float.* And I think about what Hazel Vane has just done in the space of ten minutes: taken a corrupt offer, converted it into immunity, armed herself with my own method, and made me complicit in her design while believing — genuinely, warmly believing — that I am the predator here.

"Do you know what the trouble is with you, Miss Vane?" I say.

"Enlighten me."

"You'd have been magnificent in the Ministry."

Something crosses her face — not a smile, exactly. Recognition. Two people meeting on a bridge at midnight, each realizing the other also cannot swim.

"So would you," she says, "in a foundry."

We shake on it. Her hand is small and ink-stained and does not tremble, and mine, I am mortified to discover, does — just slightly, just enough that she notices, and files it away in whatever cabinet she keeps behind those ledger eyes.

At the door, I pause. "Your father. When he learns what you've promised away—"

"My father," she says, "will learn that his daughter kept the family alive and the type intact, and he'll grieve the rest privately, the way he taught me to grieve misprints. Pull the bad sheet, burn it, lock up the forme. Good night, Mr. Kettle."

The door closes. Not quite slammed — closed with intention, the way you close a book whose ending you've written yourself.

I stand a moment in the lane, breath smoking, the folded confession in my breast pocket sitting against my heart like a coal wrapped in silk. Above me, the lamp in the workshop window goes out. Then the upstairs lamp goes out. Then — last, deliberately, a full minute later — the low lamp behind the press gutters and dies, so that I know she stood in the dark watching until I turned the corner.

Five days to report the Vane file resolved. A promotion waiting on the other side of it, wearing a carpet. And a girl of nineteen holding a dead man's seal over my head with the serenity of a woman watering roses.

I should feel robbed. I got one berth instead of a foundry; I surrendered my tollgate instead of expanding it.

What I feel instead — and this is the truth I will carry into the berth office like a stone in my boot — is the vertigo of a man who has just realized the game was never cards. It was typesetting. And she has been composing the page since long before I sat down at her stone.

Somewhere across the dark city, the bridge lantern burns over the water. In four days, the last barge of the century takes on its passengers. In five, I sign the papers that let the Vanes vanish — one name lighter than they arrived.

And I catch myself wondering, as I walk home past the shuttered shops, which of us Kettle and Vane will still be standing when the ice breaks in spring.

I suspect I already know.
