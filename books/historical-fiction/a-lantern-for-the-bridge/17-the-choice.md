## 17. The Choice

We held the vote on a Tuesday, in the back room of the foundry, with the shutters closed and the stove burning low because coal, like everything else in Vellamark that autumn, had become a currency of suspicion. Tam brought his slate. Ilse brought the good candle, which she had been saving for an occasion she refused to name and which I suspected she'd been saving for exactly this. Aldous Vane brought nothing but himself and the particular stubbornness he usually reserved for arguing about em-widths.

I stood at the head of the sorting table with Kettle's terms written out in my own careful hand, though I needn't have bothered. We all knew them by heart. One berth on the last barge. The foundry signed over as payment. The magistrate's confession delivered by Friday or the whole arrangement collapsed into chalk marks on doors and soldiers at the quay.

"One berth," I said, because someone had to say it aloud, and apparently I was the one who did things aloud now. "One berth, three of us who can't board, and one of us who can."

"Four," said Tam.

"You're twelve."

"I'm twelve and a half, and I know every sewer in this city better than the men who built them." He said it without bravado, which frightened me more than shouting would have. My brother had stopped performing courage sometime around the chalk on our door and started simply possessing it, the way you possess a scar. "Hazel stays to keep the press. Someone has to stay with her. Vote me staying."

"No one is voting anyone anywhere," my mother said. "That's not how families—"

"It's exactly how we're doing it," I said. "Because if we don't decide, the barge will decide for us, and the barge doesn't love any of us."

That silenced the room. Even the stove seemed to burn quieter.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about democracy: it works beautifully until everyone loves each other.

Aldous went first, as the eldest and the most impossible. He rose from his stool, straightened his apron — he wore his apron to the vote, as if we might forget what he was — and delivered his position like a man reading a verdict.

"The type goes or I go nowhere. That is my vote."

"Papa," I said, "the berth is for a person. It holds one person and one case of belongings, and even then they weigh your case at the gangplank."

"Then I'll wear the type."

"You can't *wear* two hundred pounds of lead."

"I have worn heavier things," he said, "for less reason." He looked around the room, daring us. "That type is forty years of my hands. Every letter in those cases I cut, cast, dressed, or mended myself. When they seized Herr Brummel's shop last spring, do you know what they burned first? Not the ledgers. Not even the seditious pamphlets. The type. They threw it into the canal because they understood something, my darlings, that we keep failing to understand: you can print another pamphlet. You cannot print another hand."

He sat down heavily, as though the speech had cost him blood. And God help me, I understood him. I had spent my whole life inside that clatter and smell, the sweet iron reek of hot metal, the music of sorts being set. The type wasn't his vanity. It was his handwriting, made permanent.

But understanding him didn't change arithmetic.

* * *

Ilse voted next, and her vote was four words long.

"Where Aldous goes, I go."

"That's not—" Tam began.

"That is my whole vote," she said, "and my whole argument, and if you require elaboration: thirty-one years ago I followed that man across a border with forged papers sewn into my coat lining, and I did not read them first to see whose name they bore. I went where he went. I will go where he goes. If he stays with his type, I stay with him, and the soldiers may chalk whatever they like on our door."

She said it calmly, folding her hands on the table, and I watched her do the thing she always did, which was make enormous sacrifice look like housekeeping. But there was something underneath it that night, something I only saw because I had spent weeks learning to read what people didn't print. She wasn't just refusing to leave Papa. She was refusing to be the one saved. She had already been to Kettle — I learned this later, and it rearranged my whole understanding of that winter — she had already knelt, in effect, before that dry little man and offered herself alone, and he had wanted more than she could give, and so she came home and folded her hands and voted for shared ruin rather than solitary rescue.

My mother would rather drown holding my father's arm than float alone wearing a lifebelt cut from his pride. I have never decided whether that is the noblest or maddest thing I have ever witnessed. Both, probably. Love keeps terrible books.

* * *

Tam's vote took longest, because he insisted on presenting it with maps.

He unrolled them across the sorting table — actual maps, drawn in his small fierce hand, the coal chutes marked in red, the sewer junctions numbered, the route from our cellar to the tannery district rendered in a dotted line with little annotations like *low beam — duck* and *dog, old, deaf, bribable with sausage*. He had been mapping Vellamark for escape since September, and looking at his work now I felt something crack behind my ribs. This was a childhood, laid out in ink. He had spent the season of being twelve building a labyrinth instead of a kite.

"We don't take the berth at all," he said. "All four of us stay. Here—" his finger stabbed the map, "—the old ice house under Foundry Row. Dry. Ventilated through the grate on Cutter's Lane. I've measured it. We fit."

"For how long?" I asked gently.

"Until the century turns. Things change at the turn of a century. Everyone says so."

"Everyone says so," Ilse repeated softly, "because no one wants to say *we are afraid*."

"They're marking doors," Tam said, and his voice did the thing it never did, which was waver. "They chalked the Kesslers' door Tuesday and Wednesday night the Kesslers were gone and their windows were open and their cat was sitting on the step like it was waiting for them to come back. Hazel. *Hazel.* If we all stay and they come, we all go where the Kesslers went. At least with the berth one of us—" He stopped. He looked down at his beautiful maps. "One of us lives. That's the whole point of the map, isn't it? Every map I ever drew, that was the point. Somebody gets out."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a patrol boot struck cobbles in the unhurried rhythm of men who owned the streets.

* * *

So there was the tally. Aldous: the type or nothing. Ilse: Aldous or nothing. Tam: hide, or failing that, give the berth to whoever the family judged most survivable, by which he meant me, because I could work the press and he could not bear the thought of the press going silent. And me.

I hadn't voted yet. I stood there with my hand flat on Kettle's terms and realized I had known my vote since before the meeting began, had perhaps known it since the night I first altered a census form with my father's alphabet watching over my shoulder. Some decisions aren't made. They surface, like drowned things finally letting go of the riverbed.

"Here is what's true," I said. "If we vote honestly, we deadlock. Papa won't leave the type; Mama won't leave Papa; Tam won't leave me; and I won't send a child into the ice house to wait for chalk. Four honest votes and we all stand here until the soldiers knock. So the vote fails, and I am ending it."

"Hazel—" Papa started.

"You taught me kerning," I said. "You taught me that a lie well-set reads truer than truth badly-set. You held my hands over the composing stick and told me the whole art of printing was making people believe a page. So believe this page."

I pulled the transit papers from my apron pocket. I had worked on them for six nights after the household slept, using the finest of Papa's type, the 8-point Baskerville he kept wrapped in flannel like a relic. Two names. Two descriptions, matched line for line to the registry requirements. One identity. Because the berth held one person, but the papers were mine to write, and a person is only what the paper says.

"Aldous and Ilse Vane," I read, "traveling as Aldous and Ilse *Vane*, married couple, one berth, husband listed as primary registrant and wife recorded as accompanying dependent, per the Household Consolidation Provision, Section Nine."

Tam frowned. "There's no Section Nine."

"There is now," I said. "I printed it Thursday. It's very official. It has a seal."

Papa took the papers the way he took proofs off the press — holding them to the light, tilting them, hunting flaws with eyes that had caught a hairline gap in a letterform at ten paces for forty years. The room waited. His thumb moved along the edge of the sheet, feeling the impression of the type, and I watched him find what I knew he would find and hoped he wouldn't: that the work was good. That it was better than good. That somewhere between the census form and the magistrate's confession and tonight, his daughter had become the finest forger in Vellamark, and that he had made her.

"The spacing here," he said at last, and his voice came out strange, sanded down, "between the surname and the registry number. A novice would have left it loose."

"You taught me tight."

"I taught you *tight*," he agreed. Then he set the papers down and covered his face with both hands, and my father, who had not wept when they chalked our door, who had not wept when the lottery turned into a riot and a man died on the bridge steps twenty feet from us, wept into his palms in the back room of his own foundry, quietly, the way he did everything, with craft.

Ilse put her arms around him. Tam looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — grief and awe sharing a chair.

"It's done, then," Mama said into Papa's hair. Not a question. Mothers get to state things.

"It's done," I said. "Friday I deliver the confession to Kettle. Sunday the barge sails. You board as the Vanes, both of you, under one name, and if a clerk blinks, the seal blinks back. Tam and I stay. We have an ice house, apparently, and a dog that can be bribed."

"And the lantern?" Papa asked, lifting his face. His eyes were red and absolutely clear. "Who keeps the lantern?"

It was the oldest thing in our family, older than the type. The lamp my grandfather had hung over the foundry door facing the bridge, lit every dusk, every night, through floods and funerals and one previous revolution that hadn't taken. *A city that can see its own bridge,* he used to say, *can't be told it isn't there.*

"I do," I said.

Papa nodded slowly. Then he stood, crossed to the type wall, and ran his finger down the cases — Baskerville, Caslon, the jobbing gothic, the little drawer of ornaments he loved beyond sense — touching each one like a man taking leave of children. When he reached the graver case he paused, and I thought: *this is where he breaks, this is where he refuses after all.*

Instead he lifted out the oldest graver, the one his own father had held, worn to a bright curve where forty years of thumbs had polished it, and set it on the table in front of me without a word.

"Papa—"

"Type is heavy," he said, in the tone he used for correcting proofs. "Gravers are light. I can carry the memory of the type. You'll need to carry the means of making more." He tapped the papers once, decisively, the final proof passed. "Set it well, Hazel. Whatever you print next — and you will print next, don't pretend you weren't going to — set it well enough that they can never prove it was anyone. Especially not you."

* * *

They went up to bed early, my parents, climbing the stairs together in the old unhurried way, two people traveling under one name ahead of schedule. Tam lingered, rolling his maps with unnecessary care.

"Ice house needs a new padlock," he said. "And I want to set one line in the first broadsheet. Just one. You choose the rest."

"What line?"

He thought about it. "'Somebody gets out.'" He shrugged, suddenly twelve again, suddenly not. "For the ones who don't."

Then it was just me and the stove and the type wall and the graver lying on the table like a small cold promise. I should have felt triumphant — I had beaten the arithmetic, threaded three impossibilities onto one forged page. Instead I felt precisely, terribly clear, the way you feel standing in triage, knowing you chose, knowing the choosing was the whole of your life from here forward, and that some part of you had died quietly at the sorting table to pay for it.

I picked up the graver. It fit my hand as if it had been waiting.

Tomorrow I would deliver a false confession to Ambrose Kettle and watch him climb another rung on his ladder of other people's bones. In two days my parents would walk down a gangplank as one legal person and vanish downriver into a life I was inventing for them with borrowed letters. And above the bridge, at dusk, a lantern needed lighting.

I banked the stove, took up the candle, and went to find the matches.
