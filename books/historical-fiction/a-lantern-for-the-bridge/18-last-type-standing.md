## 18. Last Type Standing

I have spent fifty-one years learning to hold a piece of lead so gently that it would not bruise, and on the morning of the last barge, I could not hold a teacup without spilling it.

Ilse noticed. She said nothing, which is her particular genius. She simply took the cup from my hands and drank my tea herself, looking out the window at the gray light coming up over the river, and I understood that she had already grieved for me in some private ceremony of hers and was now merely waiting for my body to catch up to what she knew.

The cases were packed by dawn. That is a lie. The cases were never packed; they were *arranged*, which is a different thing entirely, and I knew it even as I did it. Forty-one years of sorts—every size from six-point pearl to sixty-line wood, every ligature my father cut with his own hands before the war that killed him, matrices worn to the shape of ten thousand impressions, the whole vocabulary of our trade sorted into compartments like a hymnal—and I arranged them as if arrangement were preservation, as if neatness were a form of survival.

"Hazel will keep them," Ilse said.

"Hazel will melt them down for ammunition or sell them for bread," I said, "and she will be right to."

"That's not what you believe."

"No," I admitted. "What I believe is that a man who cannot carry his own alphabet deserves whatever happens to him."

* * *

The quay at Vellamark had been a place of commerce all my life. Grain barges, coal barges, the summer excursion boats with their brass bands and their vomit-scented cabins. I had set type announcing every one of them. Now it was a wound in the side of the city, and the city was bleeding people into it.

They had us queued by number. Not by name—by number, chalked on cards we were made to wear like the price tags on auctioned livestock. Mine said 214. Ilse's said 215. Somewhere behind us, Tam's said 216, and I could hear him narrating the whole spectacle under his breath to Hazel, cataloguing escape routes through the coal chutes as though the quay were just another map waiting to be drawn. A boy builds fortresses out of anything. Let him.

There was an order to the loading, of course. There is always an order; that is what these men believe in, order the way other men believe in God. An official with a clipboard moved along the line like a man counting spoons, and behind him stood Kettle—young Kettle, whose ambition sat on him now like a tailored coat, a little too fine, a little too new. He did not look at us. He looked at Hazel, once, briefly, the way a man checks a pocket he knows contains something dangerous, and then away again.

My hands wanted to shake. So I did what I have always done when my hands want to shake. I looked at type.

There was type on that quay. Discarded, scattered, trodden into the mud where someone had abandoned a case rather than carry its weight—a handful of sorts glinting between cobblestones like teeth knocked loose. And there, worse, near the customs shed: a printer's stone, one of ours, Vane & Son stamped in the corner, being used as a table by a soldier eating his breakfast.

Vane & Son. There has not been a son in the firm's name since 1871, but my father liked the sound of it, and I confess I kept the lie alive because a firm called Vane & Son sounds like it will outlive you, and a man arranges his own immortality however he can.

I found myself calculating, standing there in the queue, how many pounds of lead lay in that mud. How many letters were being walked on. The *e*'s especially—there are always more *e*'s than anything else, because the language is greedy for them—and there they were, face-down in the filth, silent, unsorted, uninked.

Aldous, I told myself, you are standing at the edge of your life counting letters. This is either madness or it is the truest thing you have ever done, and you do not have time to determine which.

* * *

They let us board at noon. Or rather—they let two of us board, and the arithmetic of that was done before we ever reached the rail, and everyone on that quay understood the sum except perhaps the officials, who had built the equation precisely so they would not have to read it.

One identity. Two names on one paper, in one hand, in ink that Hazel mixed herself—I know her inks the way other fathers know their daughters' voices. One berth. Two parents. Two children left standing on the stones.

I argued until the argument was a ruin. I said: the type goes with me, or no one goes. Ilse said: then no one goes. Tam said he would stay regardless, because he had maps, because he had tunnels, because twelve-year-olds are immortal. And Hazel—

Hazel stood in the middle of us with ink still under her fingernails and said nothing at all, which from my daughter is the loudest sentence ever spoken. She had already decided. I could see it settling over her like a coat. My daughter had become, somewhere in that winter of chalk marks and lotteries, a woman who decides things, and the decision was us, and the cost was herself, and she had signed it in her own hand the way she signs everything now—with a steadiness that frightens me more than any soldier.

So we boarded. Ilse first, then me, carrying one case. One case. Out of forty-one years, one case: a selection I made in the night like a man choosing which fingers to keep. Pearl, nonpareil, minion—the small sizes, the ones for fine work, for verse and scripture and the small print of contracts that decide lives. If I could only take one voice of the alphabet, I took the quietest one.

The barge groaned. The rail bit my palms. Behind us, Vellamark went on being Vellamark—the bridge with its lantern, the foundry with its dark windows, the census office with its tidy ledgers full of tidy erasures—and I discovered that a city does not look different when you are leaving it. It looks exactly the same. That is the cruelty of departure: the world declines to acknowledge it.

Ilse put her hand through my arm. "Look at me," she said, "not at the water," and I realized I had been staring at the wake as though the river might change its mind.

* * *

Then I saw Hazel's face above the crowd, and I saw what she was carrying.

Not the case I'd left for her—not the great cabinet, not the matrices, not the stone. She was carrying nothing but herself and her brother's hand in hers, and she was standing very straight, the way I stand at the case when the work is going well, and I understood in one cold flood that everything I had tried to give her was already given. Not the lead. The *hand*. Fifty years of craft compressed into a daughter who could forge a magistrate's soul onto a page, and here I was mourning furniture.

"Father!" she called, and the crowd swallowed most of it, but the word got through.

I turned back to the rail. And then I did the thing I had rehearsed a hundred times in the night and failed to do every time, because rehearsal is worthless against the actual moment.

I took off my apron first. Folded it. Set it on the deck like a man laying down arms. Then I drew out the graver—my father's graver, walnut handle worn to the shape of three generations of thumbs, the blade I had used to cut my first *H* at nine years old and my last ligature last Tuesday—and I held it out over the water between us.

She came forward through the crowd. They let her; desperation makes strange courtesies. She reached up and I pressed the graver into her palms and closed her fingers over it with both my hands, and her hands were steady, and mine were not, and that seemed right. That seemed exactly right. The tool should tremble in the old hand and rest quiet in the new one.

"It's not the type that matters," I said. My voice cracked like a forme dropped on stone. "It's the cutting. Do you understand? Anyone can cast. You have to be able to *cut*—to make the letter that doesn't exist yet, the letter they haven't thought to forbid." I leaned closer. The rail pressed a bar into my ribs and I welcomed it. "When they close every mouth in Vellamark, cut yourself a new alphabet, Hazel. Cut letters they can't confiscate because they've never seen them before."

"I know, Papa."

"You don't. You think you do, because you're twenty-two and certain. But listen—" and here the tears came, finally, disgracefully, running down a face I had always kept composed for the sake of the customers, and I let them, because there were no customers left, only my child, only the river widening between us. "Listen. I am leaving you my worst fear and my best hope in one object. Every time you pick it up, you will feel my hand over yours. That is not comfort. That is a debt. Pay it in truth."

The bell rang. The ropes came off. The barge swung its heavy shoulder out from the quay, and the gap opened—one foot, two, the width of a hand, the width of a life—and Hazel ran along the stones beside us, holding the graver against her chest, keeping pace the way you keep pace with anything that is leaving you forever, until the crowd thickened and the quay ended and she stopped.

The last I saw of her, she had raised the graver high overhead, the blade catching the winter sun, flashing once—like a sort held up to the light to check its face, like a letter saying yes, I am true, I will print clean.

* * *

Downriver, Vellamark shrank to a sketch of itself: bridge, lantern, smoke, gone. Ilse did not watch it go. She watched me, and when at last I sat down on our crate of small types among strangers' trunks and strangers' children, she sat beside me and let me weep into her shoulder the way I had not since I was a boy, and she said the only eulogy our life together required:

"He gave them everything, and it wasn't enough, and now she'll show them it was."

I slept that night on the deck of a barge full of the century's discarded, my head on the case of pearl and nonpareil, and I dreamed of letters the size of houses marching on the census office, each one cut by a hand I knew.

And far behind us—or so I told myself, or so I hoped, with the blind faith of a father who has finally surrendered the right to protect anyone—I could almost see the lantern over the bridge, lit, burning, kept by whoever had inherited the flame.

Keep it, Hazel. Keep it lit.
