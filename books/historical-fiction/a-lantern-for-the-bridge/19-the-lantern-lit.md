## 19. The Lantern Lit

The foundry smelled of cold metal and old smoke when we came back to it, and I stood in the doorway a long while before either of us touched anything, the way you stand at an open grave deciding whether the grief is yours or belongs to somebody else.

Tam went in first. Of course he did. He was through the door and up the stairs before my hand had left the frame, his boots hammering the treads, calling out to the emptiness the way children do—*anyone home, anyone home*—though we both knew the answer. The house above the shop had been stripped by the seizure officers two days after the barge left. They had taken the good chairs, mother's linens, father's framed engraving of the Vellamark bridge that he'd bought the year Tam was born. They had left, with a bureaucrat's precision, everything useless: the broken umbrella stand, a drawer of mismatched buttons, the smell of us soaked into the plaster.

"They took Papa's bed," Tam reported from upstairs, his voice carrying down the stairwell with a strange bright flatness, like he'd rehearsed it on the walk over.

"They took mine too," I called back. "We'll sleep in the workroom. It's warmer near the forge."

It wasn't warm. Nothing was warm that January. But the forge still held a ghost of heat in its iron, and I built up the fire anyway because building fires is what you do when you cannot build anything else, and the light of it moved along the walls where ten thousand sorts stood in their cases like a congregation waiting for a sermon no one had scheduled.

I had not cried at the quay. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, even if only in the privacy of my own skull where all my honest documents are filed. When the barge pulled away with our parents pressed together at the rail—father's face wet, mother's dry and fixed on me as if she were memorizing something for an examination she intended to pass—I had stood straight as a type stick and watched them shrink into the river fog without one tear. Tam had sobbed into my coat. I had held his shoulder and kept my spine a rod.

But standing in the empty foundry that first night, hearing Tam's boots overhead going from room to room touching what remained, I sat down on the floor beside the forge like a puppet whose strings had been cut, and I cried until my ribs ached, quietly, because there is a particular kind of crying that must not be witnessed even by a twelve-year-old who has already seen soldiers chalk your door for seizure. You cry alone so that the people depending on you can go on believing you are made of something sturdier than water.

Then I washed my face at the cistern, banked the fire, and went upstairs to tell him supper would be bread and the apples we'd carried back under our coats, and by the time I reached the top step my voice worked again. That is the whole secret of surviving, if anyone asks: not courage. Just the ability to wash your face and climb a staircase and lie well about how you feel.

* * *

Father had pressed his graver into my hands through the rail, and I will tell you what I did with it, because it matters.

For three days I could not open the case it lived in. It sat on the workbench where I'd set it, wrapped in its oilcloth, radiating guilt the way the forge radiated heat. Every time I passed it I thought: *he gave up forty years of lead for this, for this one tool, and what have I done with his life's inheritance except hide it under oilcloth like contraband?* Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was now. The government had seized the foundry's official stock; the press itself they'd left because it was too heavy to be worth the carting and because paper licenses had been revoked, making it, in their arithmetic, furniture. Furniture does not print. Furniture has never once printed anything.

On the fourth morning Tam found me sitting before the wrapped graver with my tea going cold.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"The staring. You stare at things like they owe you money."

"Everything in this city owes me money," I said, "starting with the government," and that got the small snort out of him I'd been fishing for, and while he was busy being amused at his own wit I unwrapped the graver.

It was smaller than I remembered it being in his hands. A plain wooden handle worn to the shape of his grip, a blade no longer than my finger, kept sharp enough to shave with—he'd been honing it the morning of departure, I realized now; that's what the whetstone sound was that I'd taken for nerves. He had sharpened his life's tool before surrendering it. There is a sermon in that if I were the sort to preach, which I am not, mostly.

I cut my first new sort with it that afternoon—a capital H, naturally, vanity being the last human instinct to die—and the metal took the cut clean, and something in my chest unclenched for the first time since the quay. Not healed. Unclenched. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole rest of this story.

* * *

The bridge lantern had gone dark eleven days.

Eleven days, and the city had noticed. You would not think a single lamp on a single tower could matter to a city of eighty thousand souls, but the lantern over the Vellamark bridge had burned every night since before the census, since before the evacuation barges, since before anybody alive could remember why it had been lit in the first place—some story about a ferryman, some story about a flood, some story nobody believed anymore but everybody needed. It was the kind of thing a city keeps without knowing why, the way a family keeps a chipped cup. And when the lamp room went dark, people crossing at night slowed their steps and looked up and felt, without having a word for it, abandoned.

The lamp room belonged nominally to the Bridge Commission, which belonged nominally to the magistrate whose confession I had printed, which meant it belonged, in any functional sense, to nobody. The key hung on a nail in the commission office, and the commission office had been closed since the riot, since Kettle's promotion, since everyone with a desk in this city stopped pretending to do their jobs and started hoarding coal and counting their own names against the lists.

So on the fourth night, Tam and I went and took the key off the nail.

I want to say it felt like theft. It didn't. It felt like the opposite of theft—like reclaiming collateral. The city had taken our parents, our trade, our name off the rolls; the least we owed ourselves was one lamp.

The lamp room was a stone cylinder at the bridge's midspan, reached by an iron stair that groaned like a man climbing out of bed. Inside: soot, pigeon droppings, a great brass-framed lens the size of a barrel, and the lamp itself, a monster of a thing with a wick wide as my palm, built to burn whale oil in quantities that made my purse weep just contemplating them. The reservoir was nearly empty. Of course it was. Everything in Vellamark in the winter of 1899 was nearly empty—larders, ledgers, patience.

"We'll need oil," Tam said, practical as a quartermaster. "A lot of it."

"Mm."

"People will ask why you're buying so much lamp oil."

"People will ask why a seventeen-year-old girl is buying lamp oil by the drum," I corrected, "and the answer is that her father was a printer and printers are eccentric, and eccentricity explains most purchases in this city if delivered with confidence." I turned the wick mechanism, watching the gears move. "Besides. We're about to become customers of several merchants who prefer not to be asked questions."

He looked at me then with an expression I'd started seeing on him lately—not the child's face but the apprentice's, the look of a boy taking measurements of the world and finding the world shorter than advertised. "What broadsheet?" he asked.

Ah. So he'd done the sums too. My brother, who mapped sewers for fun, had evidently mapped me as well, and concluded that a girl who buys lamp oil by the drum and sleeps in a seized foundry full of lead is printing something.

"You don't have to help," I said.

"Yes I do," he said. "You'd get the ink wrong. You always mix it too thin." Which was true, and which was, in the grammar of our family, a vow.

* * *

Here is what I knew about underground printing, which was nothing, and here is what I knew about my own motives, which was nearly as little, and between those two blanks I set my first form.

The broadsheet itself arrived in my mind almost fully grown, the way some things do—as though it had been waiting in the type case for years and only needed composing. One page. Big type, the sort readable by lamplight on a street corner. No argument, no essay; arguments take leisure and leisure was dead in Vellamark. Just facts, set hard:

*THE LOTTERY LISTS WERE FIXED. THE BERTHS WERE SOLD. THE NAMES OF THE BUYERS ARE KNOWN.*

And beneath it, in smaller type, three lines naming the officials who had traded berths—the ones I knew of, documented in pages I had copied before returning certain documents to certain places—and beneath *that*, at the very bottom of the sheet, where a signature goes:

Nothing.

I composed the blank space deliberately, choosing it the way another printer might choose an ornament. Everyone else in this affair signed things. Kettle signed his promotions. The magistrate signed confessions true and false. The lottery clerks signed receipts for bribes they recorded as fees. The whole century had been a contest of signatures, each one a claim staked on someone else's life. I would put my hand to work and keep my hand off the page. Let them hunt a ghost. Let them interrogate the entire literate population of Vellamark, one by one, while the sheets kept appearing, because a sheet with no author is a sheet with a thousand possible authors, and fear multiplies faster than evidence.

Though I will confess—for honesty's sake, here in my skull where the honest files live—that the anonymity began as cowardice and only later became strategy. I did not want to hang. I still don't. Revolution sounds very fine in retrospect and very cold at four in the morning when you are inking a form by a fire you daren't make large, listening for boots on the stairs. I am not brave. I am simply a woman with a press and a grievance, and it turns out that combination, given time and lamp oil, approximates bravery closely enough that no one can tell the difference. Including me.

* * *

We pulled the first proof at midnight.

Tam worked the tympan while I inked—I'd insisted on learning every motion myself before delegating, because a craft inherited secondhand is a craft half-learned—and when the sheet came off the press and lay there damp and black and true, we both leaned over it in the forge light like midwives over a newborn.

"It looks real," Tam said. "Like something official."

"That's the point. People believe what looks like it was printed by permission." I ran my thumb along the headline, ink smearing. "Permission is just fear wearing a uniform. This—" I tapped the page, "—is fear without the uniform."

"Say that in the next one," he said. "People will repeat it."

He was right, as it happens, though I didn't know yet how right. That is Tam all over: twelve years old, sewer-mapped, and able to see the current before the surface shows it.

We printed two hundred. Two hundred sounded enormous at midnight and absurdly few by dawn, when I imagined the city's eighty thousand mouths. But two hundred sheets, dropped in markets and chapel porches and the queue outside the relief office, pass through more than two hundred hands, and hands carry words home the way feet carry mud. By evening of the second day I heard a fishwife on the quay reciting our headline to a neighbor, garbled, embellished, improved—the lottery fixed, the berths sold, *everyone knows it now, it's printed*—and I stood there holding a basket of herring I had no intention of eating, feeling the strangest sensation of my life, which was my own sentences coming back to me in a stranger's voice, wearing different clothes, already more theirs than mine.

That is the moment I understood what father had actually given me at the rail. Not the graver. Not the trade. He had given me permission to make things that outlive their maker's safety. Every sort in that foundry was a small piece of the future cast in lead, waiting for a hand bold enough to arrange it.

* * *

The lantern went back up that same week.

Tam hauled the oil drum up the iron stair in relays, complaining with the thoroughness of a professional, and I trimmed the wick and filled the reservoir and struck the match with fingers steadier than they had any right to be. The flame caught, grew, climbed inside the great lens, and threw itself across the river in a long gold blade, and below us on the bridge I saw people stop. Just stop, mid-crossing, and look up. An old man removed his hat. I swear it on father's graver: he took his hat clean off his head, standing in the wind, facing a lamp.

"They think the commission lit it," Tam whispered. "They'll think things are getting better."

"No," I said. "They'll know the commission didn't. The commission can't wipe its own nose. They'll wonder who did." And let them wonder. Wonder is a door. Our broadsheets would walk through it soon enough.

We kept it lit after that, every night, one of us climbing the stair at dusk with oil and matches. It cost money we didn't have and sleep we couldn't spare and risk we preferred not to itemize. If the authorities traced the oil purchases, if a soldier grew curious about the lamp room, if Kettle—who was hunting the anonymous printer by then, I could feel it the way you feel weather changing—ever thought to look up instead of down—

Well. But a lantern is a promise, and promises are paid for. Father had kept the foundry's accounts balanced for forty years; I would keep the bridge's account balanced the same way, nightly, in fire.

* * *

Some nights, setting type for the next sheet, I talk to him.

Not aloud. Aloud would bring Tam running with questions. But in the silence behind my eyes, while my hands move through the case on roads they've known since childhood, I report. *Two hundred copies tonight, Papa. The fishwives have it by heart. Your graver cuts cleaner than any in this city because you never once let it dull.* It is a foolish habit and I recommend it to anyone who has lost someone within shouting distance of a trade: the dead stay nearer to the tools of their hands than to their graves, and graves were a luxury we couldn't afford to visit besides.

Mother I talk to differently, because mother would have understood the other thing—the lying awake, the arithmetic of fear, the way you hold a brother's whole future in one hand and a cause in the other and discover the hands weigh exactly the same. She had tried to give herself away for us at the end, offering herself to Kettle like a coin. I had refused the coin and spent myself instead. Some nights I think she would be proud. Other nights I think she would box my ears and demand to know when I last ate properly, and honestly the second thought keeps me better company.

The third broadsheet named the buyers of berths. The fourth reproduced, verbatim, a page Kettle believed he had erased—his own careful handwriting, lifted from the file I'd copied months ago, describing the "administrative fee" arrangement with the magistrate. I saved that one for a night with a north wind, so the sheets scattered wide before anyone could gather them, and by morning the city was humming, and by afternoon there were soldiers on street corners tearing papers off walls, which is the surest sign imaginable that the papers were working.

And by evening, Tam came home breathless with the news that men were reading our broadsheets aloud in the taverns and adding commentary of their own, and that someone had chalked on the seized foundry district wall—our wall, the one with the old chalk mark still faintly ghosting under the scrubbing—four words in a hand none of us knew:

*THE PRINTER SEES YOU.*

Which was meant, I think, as comfort. From whoever the stranger in the printer's apron had been, or whoever stood behind that stranger—an underground with roots deeper than I'd guessed, watching us back.

I read those four words by lantern light and felt the hair rise on my arms, because comfort and threat wear identical coats in a city like this. Somewhere out there, friends. Also somewhere out there, hunters. Kettle among them, promoted, furious, humiliated by his own handwriting in a hundred market squares, hunting an author who did not exist.

Let him hunt. The press was warm. The lantern was lit. And the century had three weeks left to run.

I set the next line of type and reached for the ink.
