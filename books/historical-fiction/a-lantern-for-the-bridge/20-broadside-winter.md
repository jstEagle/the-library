## 20. Broadside Winter

The first snow came on a Tuesday, and I remember it because it was the day Hazel let me pull the lever.

You have to understand what that meant. The press in the cellar of the Vane foundry was older than either of us—older than Papa, maybe, though he'd have boxed my ears for saying so—and it had opinions. It groaned in cold weather like an old man climbing stairs. It ate paper if you fed it wrong. And it did not, under any circumstances, forgive a nervous hand.

"Slow," Hazel said, standing behind me, her chin almost on my shoulder the way Papa used to when he taught us to set type. "The machine knows when you're afraid of it."

"That's ridiculous."

"So is everything else we're doing. Pull anyway."

I pulled. The platen came down with a sound like a door closing, and when she lifted the friskin, there it was: our first broadsheet, black and square and true, forty of them stacked and smelling of ink and lamp oil.

*THE CENSUS DOES NOT COUNT YOU. IT COUNTS AGAINST YOU.*

Hazel had written that line. I'd wanted something longer, more clever, but she'd shaken her head. "People can't shout clever," she said. "They can shout true."

We printed forty because forty was all the good paper we dared take from the shop stock above, where the shelves still held Papa's inventory like he'd only stepped out for tobacco and would be back by supper. Then we divided them between us, and Hazel wrapped hers in oilcloth against the wet, and we went out into the snow like two conspirators in a bad play, except nobody was watching, or so we thought, and the whole city lay white and quiet as a page waiting for type.

I scattered mine along Cooper's Row and the fish market. I tucked one inside a barrel of salt pork whose owner I knew to be loud and generous and likely to read aloud to anyone within earshot. That was my idea. Hazel's plan had been more careful—leave them in doorways, under stones, anywhere a person might find one without being seen taking it. But careful wasn't working anymore. Careful had gotten us a city full of chalked doors and empty berths.

So I made mine loud.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about printing: it is mostly waiting.

Waiting for the ink to stiffen enough not to smear. Waiting for Hazel to finish composing a page, which she did letter by letter in the old composing stick, her fingers moving through the type case the way other people's fingers move through prayer beads. Waiting for word to come back about whether the sheets had been read, torn up, or reported.

The waiting was the worst part, because in the waiting you could imagine everything. You could imagine Kettle kicking down the door. You could imagine soldiers in the alley below, their boots crunching the snow in that particular military rhythm that means *we are not here to buy bread.* You could imagine Mama and Papa downriver somewhere, warm, alive—or not. We hadn't had a letter. Hazel said letters took time in winter. She said it the way you say things you've decided to believe, which is a skill I was only beginning to learn at twelve years old.

What actually happened was smaller and stranger than anything I'd imagined.

A woman at the well started saying it. Not reading it—saying it, from memory, to another woman while they cranked the handle. *The census does not count you. It counts against you.* Just that, over and over, like a song with no tune. And then a man selling chestnuts said it to his customers, grinning, and they laughed, and laughing at a thing is halfway to believing it, and believing a thing out loud is halfway to doing something about it.

I told Hazel that night, breathless, standing in the doorway of the cellar with snow melting off my shoulders onto her clean floor.

She didn't smile. That frightened me more than soldiers would have. She just looked at the press for a long moment and said, "Then we're not writing for the walls anymore. We're writing for their mouths." And she sat back down at the case and began pulling sorts for the next one, faster than before, like a woman who has just realized the fire she lit is burning in a direction she didn't plan.

* * *

Ambrose Kettle—I know this now, though I didn't know it then—spent that December asking questions in rooms with better fireplaces than ours.

He was looking for a printer. Not a smuggler, not a forger, not any of the ordinary criminals a man of his station might chase. A printer. Because whoever was putting out those sheets wasn't just spreading sedition; they were spreading *typeset* sedition, set with real metal, in hands trained to the trade. He'd shown fragments around to every master printer in Vellamark, and every one of them had said some version of the same thing: fine work. Old-school work. Kerned by somebody who learned from the old school, which is a short list these days.

Which meant, though Kettle couldn't have known it, that the list had exactly three names on it, and one of them had left on a barge, and another was a girl of nineteen who ate supper across the table from him twice a week at the chophouse on Maren Street.

That part I learned later too—that he ate near us. That he watched Hazel order her stew and count her coins and complain about the price of coal like an honest tradeswoman, while in her pocket was the thing he'd have given his promotion to hold. There were evenings, I understand now, when he looked straight at her over the partition and saw nothing, and the nothing nearly drove him mad.

But in December, all he knew was that the broadsheets kept coming, each one sharper than the last. First the census line. Then:

*THE LOTTERY WAS NEVER A LOTTERY. ASK WHOSE NAMES WERE IN THE DRUM.*

Then, after New Year drew close:

*ONE BERTH FOR EVERY HUNDRED SOULS. THE OTHER NINETY-NINE ARE ALREADY SPENT.*

Each one signed the same way: with nothing. No mark, no seal, no signature. Just white space where a name should go, which turned out to be the most terrifying signature in Vellamark, because a man with no name cannot be arrested, and a rumor with no author cannot be silenced—it can only be repeated.

And Vellamark repeated it.

* * *

By midwinter, the sheets had become a kind of weather.

You'd wake and there'd be frost on the glass and a broadsheet folded under the door, or pasted to the customs house wall overnight, or—this was Tam's favorite trick, though I'll deny it if asked—slipped into the hymnals at St. Aldhelm's, so that on Sunday morning forty congregations opened to the psalm and found, tucked between the pages, a different kind of scripture.

The magistrates posted rewards. Ten crowns for information leading to the printer's capture. The reward notices lasted three days on the bridge before someone pasted our sheets directly over them, which I confess gave me more satisfaction than anything since the barge left.

Hazel read the reward notice once, carefully, and then laughed—not her usual laugh but a low, dangerous one I'd never heard before.

"Ten crowns," she said. "For me."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm pricing myself. It's good to know your market." She folded the notice into quarters and used it to light the stove, which felt like the correct use for it.

But she stopped going to the chophouse. She stopped walking the bridge. She went from the foundry to the well to the chandler's and back, always by different streets, always watching the windows above her. And at night, after we'd cleaned the press and damped the hearth, she would stand at the cellar door listening to the street until I fell asleep, and sometimes I woke past midnight and heard her still there, listening, a shadow against the darker shadow of the stair.

"You should sleep," I told her once.

"When it's done," she said.

"What's done?"

"The winter," she said. "The counting. All of it." And she went back to the case, and the little sounds of metal finding metal filled the cellar like rain.

* * *

Kettle, meanwhile, was drowning.

I know how that sounds—he was the one with the promotion and the coat and the office with two windows—but watch what the broadsheets did to him. Every sheet that surfaced was a question he could not answer without admitting the answer existed. His superiors demanded the printer. The printer produced nothing but paper and absence. And the worst of it, the thing that must have gnawed him like rust, was that the sheets knew things. About the lottery drum. About the erased pages. About a certain magistrate whose confession had appeared, sealed and damning, in a file that no honest clerk should ever have seen.

Someone was feeding the printer. That was the theory upstairs. A conspiracy, a ring, a cell. They wasted weeks hunting a network of dozens, because the alternative—the truth, that it was one tired girl and her brother in a dead man's cellar—was too small to see.

There was a night in late January when they came close. I'll tell it plainly because I've earned the telling.

Boots in the alley. Two of them, then four. Lantern light sliding along the shutters of the coal chute. I was on the stairs with our go-bag—the one we'd packed in November, holding papers, money, Hazel's forged blanks—and Hazel was at the press with her hand flat on the stone bed as if she could hold the whole machine down by will alone. Below us, in the cellar dark, forty fresh sheets lay drying on lines strung with twine, and every one of them smelled of our ink, our tallow, our life.

The knocking, when it came, was at the front door above. Civil. Almost polite. *Census verification,* said a voice, and Hazel closed her eyes the way you do when the blow lands somewhere you were braced for.

But here is the thing about a foundry: it is full of false doors. Papa had built half of them himself—panels that looked like shelving, cupboards that opened onto other cupboards—and the house had swallowed its own geometry generations ago. By the time the knockers had walked through the shop above, noting the dust, noting the empty type racks, noting the *closed for winter* sign in Papa's careful capitals, Hazel had the sheets bundled in oilcloth and behind the false wall, and I had the go-bag under the floorboard beneath the water cistern where even a determined man would think twice before reaching.

The voice above asked if anyone lived here. Hazel answered from the stairwell in her best shopkeeper's flatness: *My brother and I keep the place for the owner. He's downriver.* Which was true, every word, and lies made of truth are the strongest kind.

They wrote something in a book and left. Their boots faded up Maren Street toward the bridge, and Hazel sat down on the cellar steps and put her face in her hands, and I understood that she was crying, though she never made a sound, and I stood very still and pretended I hadn't, because some mercies are just the mercy of not seeing.

After a long time she lifted her head and said, "Tam. Fetch the type for tomorrow's sheet. We're behind schedule."

That was all. That was the whole speech. And I went and fetched it, because in our family that is what love sounds like: *we're behind schedule.*

* * *

February came in hard, with a wind off the river that made the lantern over the bridge moan like a struck bottle. But the city had changed underneath us, the way ice changes before it goes out.

It wasn't just the sheets anymore. It was the way people stood in queues—registration queues, bread queues, berth-inquiry queues—and talked. Openly. Loudly. The census line had become a proverb; children skipped rope to it. A carter refused to show his stamped card at the tollgate and eleven people backed him before the soldiers thought better of it. Somebody chalked *NINETY-NINE* on the customs house door, and the authorities scrubbed it off, and by morning it was there again in a different hand, and again, and again, until scrubbing it became the most expensive joke in Vellamark.

Hope, I learned that winter, is not a feeling. It's a contagion. It spreads through crowds the way fever does, mouth to mouth, and it needs feeding, and we were the ones feeding it—two children and a press in a cellar, printing the truth in runs of sixty now instead of forty, because Hazel had found a paper merchant in the Salt Quarter who didn't ask questions and charged double and was worth every crown.

One night in late February, Hazel set a new sheet, and I read it upside down as she composed it, letter by letter, and my chest went tight, because this one was different. This one wasn't a proverb or an accusation. It was an announcement.

*ON THE LAST NIGHT OF THE CENTURY, THE PEOPLE OF VELLAMARK WILL GATHER ON THE BRIDGE. NOT TO BEG. TO BE COUNTED—ALL OF US, TOGETHER, THE WAY THE CENSUS NEVER DARED.*

"They'll come," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd heard the city talking. I'd heard my own proverb come back to me in strangers' mouths.

"Some will," Hazel said. "Enough, maybe." She slid the last sort home and held the composed line up to the lamplight, checking it the way Papa used to, tilting it, squinting. "And when they do, everyone in this city will know exactly where to stand and when. Not because a general told them." She set the line down. "Because they read it on a Tuesday morning, tucked in their loaf of bread."

"And then what?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw that my sister—who had spent a year being careful, being flat-voiced, being a shopkeeper's ghost—was gone somewhere behind her eyes, replaced by somebody I recognized from stories: somebody from the old pamphlet wars, somebody from Papa's tales of printers who toppled kings.

"Then," she said, "we print the aftermath."

She banked the stove. I hung the aprons on their nails. Outside, the wind worried the bridge lantern, and the snow came down over Vellamark soft and endless, covering the chalk marks, the queue lines, the boot prints of the men who'd knocked—all of it, briefly, evenly, like a clean sheet waiting for type.

And that was when we heard it: footsteps in the alley below, unhurried, familiar somehow, stopping just outside the coal chute door—and a soft knock, three times, deliberate, like a man who knows exactly which door he's knocking on.
