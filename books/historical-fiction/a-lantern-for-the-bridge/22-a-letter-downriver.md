## 22. A Letter Downriver

The letter came on the fourth of April, which in Vellamark is the day the river remembers it's supposed to be beautiful and tries, half-heartedly, to make amends for winter.

I know this because Tam told me, twice, while I pretended not to watch the postman pick his way along the quay. The postman had developed a limp over the winter — I suspect from the weight of other people's bad news — and he stopped at the foot of the bridge tower longer than necessary, consulting his satchel with the gravity of a man defusing something. Then he held up an envelope, squinted at it against the light, and called my name.

Not *Vane*. My name. The one nobody had used out loud since October.

"Hazel," he said again, when I didn't move. "You've a letter. Downriver postmark."

Tam materialized beside me the way he does, all elbows and urgency, twelve going on thirteen and built entirely of questions he's too polite now to ask. "Who writes letters anymore?" he said. "Everything important gets printed." Which was his way of saying: is it from them, and is everyone alive.

I took the envelope. The paper was thin and river-damp at one corner, and the handwriting was my father's — that upright, fussy script he'd drilled into me at the composing stick before I could properly hold a pencil. *A hand should stand up straight even when its owner cannot*, he used to say, which was either typography advice or philosophy, depending on how much wine was in him.

It was addressed to *H. Vane, Keeper of the Bridge Light.* Someone downriver had invented that title for me. It wasn't official. There is no office of Keeper of the Bridge Light; there never has been. But the postal service of a collapsing province will honor any address it can find a body for, and so I had become, by bureaucratic accident, exactly what I'd been pretending to be since New Year's Eve.

"Open it," Tam said.

"I'm going to open it."

"You're just holding it."

"I'm appreciating it first. There's a difference."

There was, actually. I had spent five months learning the difference between things you grab and things you let arrive at their own pace. A knock on the foundry door at midnight: grab. A letter in my father's hand: appreciate. You don't rush toward news like this. You walk toward it the way you'd walk across ice, testing each step.

We went up into the lantern room, because that's where everything happens now. The lamp was trimmed and burning low against the morning — we keep it lit in daylight too these days, though no barges come and no authority requires it. It's become less a navigational aid than an argument. *This light burns whether or not you approve of what it illuminates.* The city below had learned to read it that way. So had the soldiers who occasionally marched past and decided, each time, not to climb the stairs. There were more interesting doors to chalk.

I sat on the cold stone sill with the river running gold beneath me, and I opened my father's letter.

*My dearest Hazel,*

*Your mother is making me write this sitting down, as apparently the last time I wrote her family standing at a ship's rail I produced handwriting she describes as "seasick." We are well. Read that sentence twice, as I have instructed your brother to do.*

*We are settled in Marrowfield, forty miles downriver, where the war has not yet thought to look. Your mother keeps a kitchen garden that would embarrass the botanic gardens, and I have taken work with a jobbing printer named Ossler who owns three cases of type and no opinions about kerning whatsoever, which is either restful or maddening, I have not yet decided. He lets me set his wedding invitations. Hazel, I set wedding invitations. Your mother says I have never been happier and suspects this says something damning about me.*

*I have enclosed nothing, because anything of value would be read by someone else's eyes between here and you. Instead I have written this letter twice — once for the censors, bland as porridge, and once in the hand you know, the small one, tucked inside the address fold where they never think to split the paper. If you are reading the true letter now, then you already know the trick, because I taught it to you at that same composing stick, back when the worst thing either of us forged was a school excuse for Tam.*

*Tam. Tell him the garden needs a boy's ruthlessness and that his mother talks about him to strangers, which frightens the strangers.*

*Now the part I have rewritten eleven times:*

*We know what you did. Not everything — your mother worked out most of it on the barge, in the dark, doing sums aloud the way she does, and I filled in the rest from a newspaper that reached Marrowfield in February with a broadsheet folded inside it, unsigned, describing certain irregularities in the berth assignments of the autumn lottery. The printer of that sheet knew things only a Vane could know. The printer of that sheet also kerned the word "berth" so beautifully that I wept onto my breakfast, which Ossler found very awkward.*

*Your mother wants me to tell you she forgives you, but she said I must write instead that there is nothing to forgive, which is harder to say and truer. You gave us the crossing. You kept the light. Whatever else you have made yourself into downriver — and the newspapers suggest you have made yourself into a great deal — you were our lantern first, before there was ever a bridge to hang one on.*

*Do not come to us. I know you have considered it. Do not. What you are building there cannot be carried; I learned that too late and lost my graver over the rail proving it, and if you are reading this you have the graver now, so use it. Cut your letters deep. Letters cut deep survive being handled by frightened people.*

*The bridge stands. I know this because your mother dreams about it, and because every rumor that comes upstream carries your light in it somewhere, the way river mud carries gold. Stay lit. That is the whole of my advice and the whole of my estate.*

*Your father, who stands up straight,*
*Aldous*

*P.S. — Ilse says: the lantern glass on the north face will be clouding by now. Vinegar and ash, rubbed warm. She also says she knows you haven't been eating properly, which is impossible, and correct.*

I read it four times. On the third reading Tam leaned in and read it too, silently, his lips moving, and when he got to the part about the garden his eyes went bright and furious in the way boys' eyes do when they're determined not to cry about vegetables. On the fourth reading I laughed out loud at the wedding invitations, which startled a gull off the railing and made Tam look at me like I'd gone strange.

Which, fair. I had gone strange. Five months of printing sedition out of a dead man's foundry will do that.

* * *

Here is what the city looked like that morning, from the lantern room, while the letter sat warm in my lap:

Vellamark in spring, cracked open like a seed. The census offices still stood on the square — Kettle's old empire, though Kettle himself had been reassigned to a records depot in the interior, which is what they call exile when they can't prove anything. His erased pages had surfaced in our December broadsheet, laid out line by line in type so clean it looked like scripture, and the crowd that gathered outside the census house that night had not needed us to tell them what to feel. They'd known. They'd always known. All we'd done was make their knowing legible.

That is the whole secret of what Tam and I did through the winter, if anyone ever asks, and people do ask now, in the market, in lowered voices, looking around first the way my mother used to look around before burning her letters. People ask *how do you decide what to print,* expecting some grand editorial philosophy, and I want to tell them the truth, which is: we print what they already know and have been told they're forbidden to know. A broadsheet isn't a lamp. It's a mirror with good lighting.

The uprising — the word makes it sound tidier than it is — had spread down the coal chutes and sewer runs Tam mapped when he was twelve and believed we could hide forever. He hadn't believed it by Christmas. Nobody had. But the maps turned out to be worth more for moving paper than for hiding people, and by February half the printers' apprentices in Vellamark could recite our routes, and by March the governor's proclamations lasted, on average, six hours before someone pasted a response directly beneath them, in better type.

Better type matters. I want that on the record. Half the reason the authorities couldn't catch us was that our work looked *official* — Aldous's kerning, Aldous's spacing, the tricks he taught me the night he found the forgery and chose inheritance over fury. Every proclamation we answered came back sounding more legitimate than the original. Citizens began trusting our sheets over the government's, on typographic evidence alone. Somewhere downriver my father is insufferable about this, and I hope he stays that way for thirty years.

The soldiers still marked doors in chalk. Fewer doors now, and more hesitantly. In March a lieutenant had climbed the bridge tower steps, boots ringing, and stood in the doorway of the lantern room while I sat at the case with ink to my elbows, and looked at me for a long moment, and said, "You're the keeper, then."

"I'm the keeper," I agreed.

He looked at the lamp, and the type, and the broadsheet drying on the stone — I didn't bother covering it; there seemed little point — and then he touched his cap, which was not regulation, and went back down. I have thought about that visit more than any battle. The revolution doesn't win when the soldiers are defeated. It wins when they need the light too, and know it, and are embarrassed.

* * *

That afternoon I went down to the foundry.

The Vane Type Foundry still says VANE above the door, because repainting it would cost money and because the name has acquired, through no effort of ours, a certain reputation. Tam calls the place "the office" now, in the flat ironic register of teenagers everywhere, but he sweeps the floor without being asked and he sets a clean galley faster than I did at sixteen, and last week he caught a widow's error in a memorial notice — *beloved husband* where it should have been *beloved wife* — before it went to press, and saved some poor woman from a grief with a typo in it. He'll deny caring. He cares enormously. He is his mother's son down to the bone and doesn't know it yet.

The cases stood where they always stood, minus the two my father left on the quay in December, minus the graver that rode downriver in my apron pocket and came back upriver in my fist. I had cut new punches over the winter, working by lamplight after the presses slept, and there are mornings when I run my thumb along the shoulders of those letters and feel my father's hands under mine like weather. *Cut deep,* the letter said. I had. The letters I cut now would still be legible when the metal wore thin and the century turned over again and somebody's grandchildren dug them out of some archive wondering who the anonymous printer was.

Let them wonder. Anonymity, I've found, is a kind of inheritance too. My father put his name on everything — every punch, every specimen book, *cut by A. Vane* in letters small enough to be humble and precise enough to be proud. I put my name on nothing. And yet everything I print is signed, in the only way that matters, because the hand is legible in the work. Anyone who knew Aldous Vane's type can read my initials in the spacing. He taught me that without meaning to. Your teacher lives in your letters long after the letters leave you.

I pulled down the form I'd been composing — the next broadsheet, due on the streets by Friday, the spring one, the one that would list, in plain figures, the names of the men who had sold berths in the autumn and the names of the people who had drowned waiting for the ones the berths were promised to. Two columns. The arithmetic side by side. No adjectives anywhere; we'd learned that numbers outrage better than epithets. Set it straight, set it clean, let the reader supply the rage — readers always do, given decent spacing.

I was locking up the chase when Tam came in with vinegar and ash in a bowl and an expression I recognized from the mirror.

"She says the north pane's clouding," he said. "In the letter."

"She says."

"So we're doing it tonight?"

"We're doing it tonight."

He set the bowl down and hovered, the way he does before asking for something real. "Did he really say stay lit? Those words?"

"He said it was the whole of his estate."

Tam considered this. "Estate means property. Land. Things you inherit."

"Yes."

"So we inherited a sentence."

"Best property in Vellamark," I said. "No taxes. Can't be seized. Soldiers march right past it."

He grinned, and for a second he was twelve again, mapping sewers, certain we could outrun the world. Then he was thirteen again, and picked up the ash cloth, because the lamp wanted cleaning and wanting is enough.

* * *

Evening came up the river the color of the letter paper, thin and gold at the edges. From the lantern room I watched the bridge fill — not with refugees now, not with lottery-mad crowds trampling slips of paper underfoot, but with people walking nowhere in particular, in the way people walk when they've begun to believe the ground under them might hold. An old woman sold roasted chestnuts at the span's midpoint where the registration queue used to form, and business was good, and nobody checked anyone's papers to buy chestnuts. Small mercies. Large ones, really. The whole catastrophe had taught me to price them correctly.

I cleaned the north pane with vinegar and ash until the glass cleared, and the light behind it steadied, and threw its long cone out across the water the way it had every night since December, unasked, unsanctioned, unextinguished.

Then I sat down on the sill with my father's letter and read it a fifth time, because some sentences improve with handling the way type does — worn smooth, cut deeper. *You were our lantern first, before there was ever a bridge to hang one on.* I thought about arguing with him, the way we argued over the type cases that last autumn, love expressed as dispute, each of us insisting the other was the thing worth saving. I could have written back: *no, you were mine first.* But he'd only send another eleven drafts, and my mother would edit them both, and somewhere in Marrowfield a jobbing printer would be very confused.

Instead I answered the only way I answer anything now.

I went down to the case and set a reply. Not a letter — letters travel through censors and get read by limping postmen and take a week. I set a broadsheet, one column, to run beneath Friday's arithmetic. At the top, where the anonymous sheets always carry their blank, I left the space empty as always. And beneath it, in type I had cut myself, in a hand that stands up straight even when its owner cannot:

*To the correspondent downriver: received, and read four times, and kept. The north pane is clean. The garden's ruthlessness is noted and will be reported to the appropriate party. The light is lit. It was never borrowed. It stays.*

I locked the form into the chase, hung the fresh copy of the old broadsheet on its hook by the door where the census notice used to be — full circle, though nobody down here believes in circles anymore, only in spirals that climb — and stood a moment in the doorway of the foundry with the evening coming on.

Below, the bridge stood. Above, the lantern burned. Between them, the whole long spring opening like a page.

I struck the lamp brighter, and went in to print.
