## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The lamps in Ward C burned down to their last inch of oil by four in the morning, and Orsen Thornbury had learned to read the dying light the way sailors read weather. When the flames went small and blue, the night was nearly over, and the men who were going to die before dawn had mostly done so.

She moved down the aisle of cots with her lamp held low, counting breaths. Thirty-one men. Two of them breathing shallow as paper. One — Corporal Finch, both legs gone at the knee since Ypres — sleeping so deeply she stopped twice to make certain of him. The stove ticked. Snow pressed against the tall windows like a hand trying to get in.

Marlow's Bend military hospital had been a wool warehouse once, before the war made everything into something else. Its beams still smelled faintly of lanolin and river damp, and in winter the whole building groaned like a ship at anchor. Orsen had been posted here eleven months, long enough that she no longer flinched when a man called out for his mother in his sleep, long enough that she could tell from across the ward which cough would end in the grave and which would only end in morning.

What she had not grown used to was the paperwork.

* * *

At half past four she sat at the duty desk beneath the one lamp Sister allowed kept burning, and opened the official register. Her pen moved through the entries: dressings changed, fevers charted, morphia administered, doses recorded in her neat, upright hand. She was careful with this book. Careful the way a woman is careful walking on ice she knows is thin — every step placed, nothing hurried, nothing that might crack.

The third page from the back held the night's business. Private Ansel Doyle, 2nd Battalion, died at ten minutes past two of wounds to the abdomen. She had held his hand through it. He had asked whether the river froze solid enough to walk on, and she had said it did, though she didn't know if it was true, and he had said *good*, because his home was on the far side and he meant to come back for harvest some year and cross it on foot just to prove he could.

Orsen wrote: *Private A. Doyle, condition improved, transferred to convalescent depot.*

Her pen hovered over the line. The order had come from Captain Rennie's office at six o'clock yesterday evening, typed, unsigned in any name that mattered. *All fatalities of a non-spectacular nature to be logged as transfers pending review.* Non-spectacular. As though a man bleeding out over four hours while calling for his mother were something too dull for the record.

Below Doyle there should have been a second entry. Gunner Pell, who had died at midnight without ever waking, his lungs drowned slowly by the gas they'd all pretended was only smoke. And below Pell, Stretcher-bearer Odo Marsh, who had lasted nine days and asked her on the eighth whether anyone would remember him, since he had no family left to do it.

Three names. Three lines in the register where truth should have sat, and did not.

Orsen set down the pen. She looked at her hands a moment — chapped, scrubbed raw at the knuckles, a plasterer's hands now rather than a nurse's — and then she glanced up the ward. Finch slept. The man in the far cot nearest the stove slept. The orderly on nights, young Hobb, had gone down to the dispensary and would not return for twenty minutes; he never did.

She rose, crossed to the corner of Ward C where the floorboards had been patched sometime before the war by someone in a hurry, and knelt. The loose board came up with the flat iron bar she kept hidden inside her boot shaft — a trick Marta Ostrova had taught her the first month, laughing, saying every woman in a river town learned young how floors lie.

Beneath the board, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay a school exercise book. Forty pages. Thirty-eight of them filled.

Orsen carried it back to the desk and opened it beside the official register, so that the two books lay together like a marriage gone wrong.

*Private Ansel Doyle,* she wrote. *Died 14th November, 02:10. Wounds to the abdomen. Held my hand. Asked after the ice on the river. Official record: transferred.*

*Gunner Tomas Pell. Died 14th November, 00:00 approx. Gas. Never woke. Mother in Ashford. Official record: transferred.*

*Stretcher-bearer Odo Marsh. Died 13th November, 19:40. Septic wound, right thigh. No family. Asked if anyone would remember him. I told him yes. Official record: transferred.*

She pressed hard enough on the last word that the nib nearly went through the cheap paper. Then she blotted each line, closed the book, and sat looking at the two registers side by side.

This was the thing she had never said aloud, not even to herself in plain words: she no longer knew which book was the forgery. On paper, the ledger was the false document — unauthorised, unofficial, written in secret by a nurse with no right to keep records at all. But her hand wanted to call it something else. The exercise book told what happened. The register told what was permitted. Somewhere between those two definitions, she suspected, lived the difference between a crime and a duty, and she had stopped being able to find which side of the line she stood on.

* * *

By six the grey came up through the snow, turning the windows from black to slate, and the day shift arrived smelling of woodsmoke and porridge. Orsen handed over the ward, gave her report in the usual flat phrases — *all quiet, two transfers pending review, supplies short again* — and heard herself speak the garrison's language fluently, the way one speaks any tongue long enough. That frightened her more than the ledger did. Lies wore smooth with use, like stones in a riverbed.

Outside, the cold hit her face like a door swung open onto winter proper. Marlow's Bend lay white to its chimneys, the river running dark between its banks, slow and heavy with slush-ice near the edges. Across the water she could see the far town lights going out one by one as the far side woke to its own morning. And above it all, spanning the river in a single iron reach, stood the Marlow Bridge — the only crossing for forty miles, the artery through which everything entered or left this place: coal, post, wounded men, orders, lies.

Tomorrow it closed.

Every year the same. When the ice began to run, the bridge company shut the span for repairs and fear of the floes, and Marlow's Bend became an island until spring thaw — six weeks, sometimes eight, cut off except for what crossed the frozen river on foot, which was to say, almost nothing. The last supply wagons would roll over tomorrow at noon. The last mail coach at dusk.

Orsen stood on the hospital steps and thought about what that meant, and found, to her own surprise, that part of her was glad.

History happened elsewhere. That was the sentence she had built her peace on, brick by brick, all these months. History happened in France, in Flanders, in the mud and the wire, in the dispatches she read in old newspapers a week late. Here there was only snow, and wards, and the slow work of keeping men alive, and the smaller, quieter work of keeping them remembered. The bridge closing was a wall between her and all of it. Whatever the war did next, it would have to do it somewhere else, beyond the river, and for six weeks Marlow's Bend could pretend the world was only weather.

It was a comfortable thought. It was also, she knew, a lie — the same species of lie as *transferred to convalescent depot*, dressed in kinder grammar. The war was not elsewhere. The war was in the ink of Captain Rennie's typed orders. It was in the space where Gunner Pell's mother's letter would go unanswered, or worse, answered with good news. It was under a floorboard in Ward C, wrapped in oilcloth, growing heavier by three names a week.

And tomorrow the last coach would rattle over the bridge, and whatever she failed to send with it would sit in her keeping until the thaw.

She turned that over as she walked to the nurses' quarters, boots crunching, breath smoking. Send it? The ledger? To whom? The war office address she'd copied off a circular was surely monitored; letters from a field hospital signed by a nurse and containing lists of dead men whose deaths were officially denied would land her in a cell before the ink dried, and serve the families nothing. Burn it? She had considered it, once, in October, standing over the stove with the oilcloth in her hand. She had held it there a full minute. Then she had thought of Odo Marsh asking if anyone would remember him, and put it back under the floor, and hated herself a little for having lifted it at all.

No. The ledger stayed. It stayed because it was true, and because true things, in her experience, eventually found their uses — the way floodwater finds the cellar, the way grief finds the kitchen at three in the morning. She need only keep it safe until spring, until the bridge reopened, until history deigned to pass this way again.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had the great virtue of requiring nothing of her but patience.

* * *

She slept badly through the short forenoon, the way she always did, waking at intervals to the sounds of the hospital going about its day — trolleys, voices, the scrape of a shovel on the path. In the middle of one such half-waking she heard Sister Halloran in the corridor outside, speaking to someone low and urgent, and caught only the tail of it:

"—came on the morning coach, poor woman. Asking at the gate. They've sent her to the commandant's office, but she says she won't leave without—"

Then footsteps, and silence, and Orsen lay staring at the ceiling with the words settling over her like snowfall.

A woman. At the gate. Refusing to leave.

In eleven months, mothers had come to Marlow's Bend exactly four times. She remembered each one. She remembered the way they stood in the receiving hall with their hats dripping, holding letters like talismans, asking after sons whose names Orsen either knew from her ward — or knew, far better, from the book beneath the floorboard.

Orsen got up and dressed quickly, fingers clumsy with cold and with something colder than cold. Through the frosted window of the quarters' little sitting room she could see the commandant's office across the yard, and a figure on the path before it: a woman in a dark coat, standing quite still in the falling snow, waiting with the terrible patience of someone who has already waited through worse than weather.

Orsen watched her, and felt the ledger's weight through the floorboards, through the joists, through the stone, as though thirty-eight pages had become thirty-eight stones sewn into her chest.

Tomorrow the bridge closed. Tonight, apparently, the war intended to stop being elsewhere.

She pulled on her coat and went out into the snow.
