## 2. Last Crossing

The bridge had a voice, Orsen Thornbury had learned, and on its last day of the season it sang.

She stood at the north rail of Marlow's Bend with her hands bare in the cold, listening. All autumn the iron span had murmured under cartwheels and boot-heels, a low complaint like an old man shifting in his sleep. But today, with the wind coming down the river valley hard from the northwest and the water beneath already wearing its first gray lace of ice, the bridge groaned in longer notes — the sound of something being asked to hold on just a little past its season. By morning the army would close it. By afternoon tomorrow, if the snows came as the ferry widow predicted, the whole town would be sealed inside itself like a letter no one would open until spring.

*History happens elsewhere*, she told herself, as she told herself every day. It happened in Flanders, in the mud of Passchendaele, in the chalk trenches she had left behind with her nursing apron still stiff with someone else's blood. It did not happen here, in a river town where the most consequential event of the winter would be whether the bakery ran out of flour before the road closed. She said it the way one says a prayer whose truth one has stopped examining too closely.

The supply wagons came first.

They crossed all morning in a slow iron procession — lumber, coal, crates stenciled with the marks of the Army Medical Corps, barrels of salt pork that would see the garrison through four months of isolation. Orsen watched them from the hospital steps between rounds, counting without meaning to count, because counting was what she did now. Twelve wagons by nine o'clock. Each one carrying things the war office had decreed this town could not survive without. And not one of them, she thought, carrying anything as heavy as the paper folded inside her uniform bodice, against her ribs, where it had lived for eleven days now while she decided.

The ledger was not large. That was the obscenity of it — how small a thing needed to be to hold thirty-one men. Thirty-one names in her own careful hand, each with a date, a wound, a hour of death, and a line noting what the official record said instead: *returned to duty*, *transferred to base hospital at Aldershot*, *discharged, condition satisfactory*. She had begun it in September with a single name she could not bear to watch dissolve — Corporal Ives, who had died at three in the morning holding her wrist and asking for his brother, and whose family would receive a letter saying he was convalescing. She had written his true death into the back pages of her pocket Bible first, out of superstition, as though Scripture might lend the fact some authority. Then she had bought a plain schoolboy's ledger from the stationer for sixpence, and the Bible had gone back to holding only holy words.

Now there were thirty-one. And the mail coach came at noon.

She saw it coming down the far approach before she heard it — the last coach of the season, its horses steaming, its roof piled with mail sacks the color of wet sand. The driver cracked his whip once, a sound that carried across the frozen fields like a small sharp announcement, and Orsen felt her heart do something complicated and unwelcome. Because here was the door. Here was safety, standing right there on the other side of the river in the form of a leather sack and a government stamp, and if she walked down to the coach now, this minute, and asked the driver to take one more parcel — a small one, wrapped in brown paper, addressed to a solicitor in London whom Wrenn had mentioned once and then begged her never to mention again — then whatever happened in Marlow's Bend this winter, the truth would already be across the water. Safe. Irrevocable.

And Commander Halsey's clerks opened every parcel that left this town. She knew that. Everyone knew that, or had begun to suspect it since the telegraph lines went quiet in November and the censor's office took over the corner room above the guardhouse. A parcel from a nurse, unexplained, addressed to a stranger? It would be opened by lunchtime and Halsey would know by supper, and there were still months of winter ahead, months during which she would have to walk past that man's window every day and hold her face still.

So she did not move. She stood on the steps with her breath smoking in front of her and let the mail coach come, and cross, and go — watched it shrink along the far road until it was a smudge, then a memory — and hated herself a little, and told herself it was prudence, and knew it was also fear, and understood that those two things would look identical to anyone watching, which was perhaps the only mercy in the arrangement.

* * *

The letter arrived at half past two.

It came up from the sorting room with the rest of the hospital post, a thin envelope among forty, and Sister Bramwell handed it to her with the particular expression — eyebrows slightly raised, mouth carefully neutral — that meant *this one is yours and I have not read it but I noticed*. The handwriting was round and unhurried, a hand formed by copybooks sixty years old, and the address read:

*Miss / Mrs. Nurse O. Thornbury, Military Hospital, Marlow's Bend Garrison.*

No rank. No regimental number. Whoever had written it had simply trusted the town to know her.

Orsen carried it to the linen closet, which was the only room in the hospital with a lock that worked, and stood among the sheets smelling of carbolic and cold, and opened it.

*Dear Nurse,* it began. *Forgive me writing to a stranger. The vicar at home says the nurses at Marlow's Bend are kind ones, and kindness is what I am asking after.*

*I am Ada Quill, of Fenmarsh, Lincolnshire. My son is Private Edmund Quill, 4th Battalion, and he has been at your garrison since the summer. His last letter came in October and it was a good letter, cheerful, he always could make a joke of anything. Since then I have written four times and had nothing back, and so I wrote to the War Office, and they tell me —*

Here the ink changed. Blotted, pressed hard, resumed.

*— they tell me he was returned to duty. But nurse, that is what they told me in June when he had the fever, and he wrote me himself the next week to say he had been ill as anything and near died. So you will understand why I do not altogether believe them. They use those words the way a shopkeeper uses brown paper, to wrap up whatever is inside.*

*I am not a foolish woman and I am not a hysterical one. I kept bees forty years; I know how to wait and how to watch. But I have a feeling about Edmund that I cannot shake, the way you know a hive has gone quiet before you open it. If my son is well, tell me so and I will trouble you no further. And if he is not —*

*If he is not, I would rather hear it from a kind nurse than a printed form. Whatever you know, however little, I will be grateful for it. A mother can carry a great deal if someone tells her truly what the weight is.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*Ada Quill*

Orsen read it twice. On the second reading her eyes stopped obeying her and went back, of their own accord, to the name.

*Quill.*

She knew that name. She had written it herself, in her own careful hand, on the fourth page of a schoolboy's ledger that was at this moment folded into oilcloth and hidden inside the false bottom of her instrument case, three floors above her head. Private Edmund Quill, 4th Battalion. Admitted October the nineteenth, gunshot wound to the abdomen, gangrene by the twenty-second despite everything Dr. Prewitt tried, which was everything, because Prewitt had liked the boy — everyone had liked the boy, he made jokes even with the ether mask going over his face, he had asked Orsen to tell his mother he'd been thinking about the bees, and then he had died at ten minutes past four in the morning on the twenty-third with her hand on his forehead.

And the official ledger in the adjutant's office said, in a clerk's indifferent copperplate: *returned to duty.*

He had been returned to duty. He was on duty now, somewhere, in the war office's version of the world — perhaps forever, perhaps still walking some parade ground of paper, a boy who could not die because a report said he hadn't.

Orsen leaned her forehead against the cold shelf of folded sheets and stayed that way for a long moment. Through the wall she could hear the ward sounds — the rattle of a bedpan, someone coughing, the eternal small music of the dying and the mending — and beneath it, fainter, from outside, the last groans of the bridge as the final wagon train went over.

There was still time, she thought. The coach was gone, yes. But the bridge held until morning. There were hours yet in which she might find another way — a farmer's cart, a favor called in, a parcel slipped into a hay bale bound for the far side. She could write to Ada Quill tonight and send the truth over the ice-road before it closed, and be done with the weight of it, and go back to being only a nurse in a town where history happened elsewhere.

But every route out of Marlow's Bend ended at the bridgehead guardhouse, and the guardhouse answered to Halsey, and Halsey had begun, in November, to ask questions with a new sharpness in them. Who writes letters besides their duty correspondence. What the nurses do with their evenings. Whether Chaplain Wrenn has been seen talking to anyone from the postal sorters. Small questions, dropped casually, the way a man drops pebbles down a well to sound its depth.

If she sent the truth tonight and it was caught, she lost everything — the ledger, the names, the thirty-one chances that someone someday might say these men existed. And Ada Quill would receive, instead of comfort, a visit from officers asking why she corresponded with a nurse who kept unauthorized records.

If she sent nothing — if the bridge closed with the answer still locked in her instrument case — then a woman forty miles away would spend the winter waiting on a lie, opening her door to every creak of the lane, and Edmund Quill would remain forever *on duty*, and Orsen would remain forever the one who knew and did nothing.

She put the letter back in its envelope, and her hand shook doing it, and she noted the shaking with a kind of clinical detachment, the way she'd note a pulse.

* * *

At dusk she walked out to the end of the north pier, past the lamp Tam Beckett was trimming for the night. The lamplighter nodded to her without question — he had a way of offering company without demanding conversation, which she had come to value more than she could say — and went on working his long brass pole against the wick until the flame stood up straight and yellow against the blue-black coming down off the hills.

Below her, the river moved sluggishly under its new skin of ice, and the bridge stretched away across it, empty at last, its iron bones creaking in the wind like a ship riding at anchor. Tomorrow soldiers would come with chains and boards and a painted sign — *CLOSED UNTIL SPRING BY ORDER* — and that would be that. Four months of snow. Four months of Halsey. Four months of being the only honest record in a town full of rewritten men.

"Last day," Tam said, coming to stand beside her at the rail. He said it the way he said everything, mild as milk, but his eyes were on her face and she had the sudden uncomfortable sense that he saw more than the tiredness there. "River'll be solid enough to walk by Twelfth Night, they say. Marta Ostrova claims she could take a sledge across by Christmas if the cold holds." He paused. "Course, walking on a frozen river and walking on a road aren't the same thing. One of them forgives you your mistakes."

Orsen looked at him. The lamplight caught the frost in his whiskers and the patience in his eyes, and she almost — almost — told him. The words got as far as her teeth.

But the guard lantern was moving along the bridgehead behind them, slow and regular, and a soldier had stopped to watch the two of them talk, and so she only said, "Good night, Tam," and turned back toward the hospital with Ada Quill's letter against her heart like a stone.

Behind her the lamp burned steady over the dark water, the last light on the last open road, and Orsen Thornbury climbed the hill knowing exactly what she had failed to do today — and beginning, in some cellar of herself where resolve is stored like winter apples, to plan what she would do tomorrow.

The bridge would close in the morning. The door was shutting.

But doors, she thought, had a way of making people remember they had legs.
