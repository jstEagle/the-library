## 3. The Woman Who Wouldn't Leave

The snow came down all night in the fat, unhurried flakes of a winter that meant to stay, and by morning Marlow's Bend had gone soft at its edges, as though the town were being gently erased. Orsen watched it from the hospital window while she pulled on her apron — the river a long grey seam between white banks, the bridge a dark stitch across it, and beyond, the far shore already losing its detail. Tomorrow the bridge would close. The word *closed* was painted on a board and nailed to the tollhouse door, but everyone knew that weather, not carpentry, made the decision. The last crossing would happen today or not at all.

She had slept four hours on a cot that smelled of carbolic and other women's hair oil, and she had dreamed of names again — not faces, never faces anymore, only names marching in columns like men going up to the line. She woke with her own handwriting behind her eyes.

In the ward, the stoves ticked and the wounded breathed in their several rhythms: the fast shallow pull of the fevered, the wet rattle of the lung cases, the long even tide of men simply worn down to their bones. Sister Prewitt had left instructions chalked on the board. Orsen read them, tied back her hair, and began.

It was mid-morning when the woman arrived.

Orsen saw her first through the ward's end windows, coming up the road from the ferry landing on foot, which no one did in this weather unless they had to. She walked the way people walk into wind they have decided not to acknowledge — head down, shoulders squared, one hand holding her hat to her head as if it were the last thing she owned. Behind her, the ferry widow's boat sat pulled up on the ice shelf at the landing, and Marta Ostrova stood beside it with her arms folded, watching the woman come, wearing the expression of someone who has tried to talk a person out of something and failed.

By the time the visitor reached the hospital steps, half the ward had noticed her. Women did not often come to Marlow's Bend. Wives came, sometimes, with parcels. Mothers almost never. The trains stopped short of the river now; the road beyond was a rumor. A mother who had gotten this far had spent money she could not spare and ridden in conveyances that smelled of fish and tallow, and every mile of it had been paid for by a fear she would not speak aloud.

She came into the admission room shaking snow from her coat, and Orsen was the nurse on duty, and so it fell to her.

"Madam," Orsen said. "You'll want to warm yourself first—"

"I'm not cold." The woman removed her hat anyway, revealing grey-streaked hair pinned with fierce practicality, and set it on her knee as she sat in the chair Orsen indicated. Her face was long and plain and stubbornly composed, the face of a woman who had wept everything she was going to weep somewhere between here and home and had packed the rest away for later. "My name is Quill. Ada Quill. I've come about my son."

Something turned over in Orsen's chest, slow and heavy, like ice shifting on the river.

"Your son is here?" she asked, though she knew — knew before the woman answered, knew from the moment she'd seen her crossing the yard — how this would go.

"Edmund. Private Edmund Quill, Fourth Battalion." Mrs. Quill produced a letter from inside her coat, an envelope gone soft at the corners from handling. "I had word he was wounded in the autumn. Then nothing. I wrote to the records office in October and received this." She smoothed the paper flat on her knee with both hands, the way one smooths a child's hair. It was an official form, stamped twice. "*Returned to duty,*" she read aloud. "That's what it says. Returned to duty."

"Yes, madam."

"He was shot through the leg," Mrs. Quill said. "His commanding officer wrote to me himself, in September. A very kind letter. He said Edmund was brave and would walk again." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "So he returned to duty. And then his letters stopped. Not gradually — stopped. Like a clock. I have written eleven times since September. Eleven. I have had two answers, both copies of this form." She held it out. "I want to see him. That's all. I don't want anything explained. I just want to stand in a room where my son is."

*Returned to duty.* Orsen took the paper because refusing it would have required a steadiness she did not possess. The stamp was the garrison's stamp. The signature was a clerk's clerk, some name that meant nothing, that signed hundreds of these a week, that signed men into existence and out of it with the same nib.

Because she knew the name. She knew it the way she knew all of them — in ink, in her own hand, in a ledger sewn into oilcloth beneath a floorboard three rooms from where she sat.

*Quill, E., Pte., 4th Bn. Died 14 Oct., retreat from the Kessel salient. Left leg. No record filed.*

Fourteen October. He had lasted nine days past the kind letter. There had been no bed for him by the time they carried him in — there was never a bed — and he had died in the corridor on a stretcher with another man's coat thrown over him because the blankets were all spoken for. Orsen had held the pen over the admission book and written *died* and had watched the adjutant strike it out an hour later and write *transferred*, and she had begun, that same night, keeping her own record, because someone in the world ought to know that Edmund Quill had existed, had liked — what? She didn't know what he'd liked. That was the worst of it. She kept their deaths and not their lives, and she could not even tell this woman what her son's laugh had sounded like.

"Madam," Orsen said carefully, "records are kept at the garrison office. As a nurse I have access only to—"

"You're the night nurse." Mrs. Quill said it without accusation, as a fact. "The ferry woman told me. She said you're the one who knows what goes on here. She said—" and for the first time the composure cracked, just slightly, a seam letting in cold air, "she said if anyone could tell me whether my boy is alive, it would be you."

Outside, the snow went on falling. Somewhere in the ward a man called out for his mother in a voice that hadn't broken yet, and another man told him quietly to hush, there, lad, there.

Orsen heard herself say, "Give me until evening. Let me make inquiries."

Mrs. Quill studied her for a long moment — a look Orsen recognized, because mothers made of themselves instruments for reading faces, and this one read hers thoroughly, page by page. Whatever she found there, she nodded once, put her hat back on, and said, "I'll be at the ferry house. Marta says I can have the corner by the stove." At the door she paused. "Nurse. If he's dead, you may tell me. I'd rather carry it than keep looking for it. Looking is heavier."

Then she was gone into the snow, and Orsen stood alone in the admission room holding a stamped lie in both hands.

* * *

She got through the afternoon the way one gets through deep water — stroke by stroke, without looking down. Dressings changed. Fevers charted. The young caller-for-his-mother settled with laudanum and a hand held briefly, uselessly. All the while the ledger sat beneath the floorboard like a coal banked in ash, and Orsen felt its heat through the boards, through her boots, through the whole building.

She could tell the woman. That was the simple arithmetic of it. Walk to the ferry house, sit by the stove, open her mouth. *He died on the fourteenth of October, quickly, and they wrote it out of the books because the retreat was a disgrace and disgrace is bad for recruitment.* Eleven words of truth and a mother could stop looking.

And then what? The truth would have no paper behind it. Mrs. Quill would go to the garrison office and be shown the form — *returned to duty* — and her word against the stamp would be worth nothing, less than nothing, would mark her as a hysterical woman and Orsen as a nurse who talked. Worse: the adjutant would ask how she knew. And beneath that question lay the floorboard, and beneath the floorboard lay thirty-one names, and Commander Halsey did not burn letters in the stove behind his desk for warmth.

She had told herself, all winter, that history happened elsewhere — in the salients, at headquarters, in ministries full of men whose signatures actually moved things. Marlow's Bend was a hinge, not a wheel. She kept her little book the way one keeps prayers: private, unprovable, a candle lit in a room no one enters. But a candle does no good in a locked room, and Ada Quill had come all this way and was sitting by a stranger's stove waiting for Orsen to decide whether her son had ever lived.

Tam Beckett found her at dusk, out on the hospital steps, where she had gone to stand in the cold because indoors had become unbearable.

He was lighting the lamps along the parade road, working his way toward her with his pole and his ladder and his breath smoking, and he did not hurry. Tam never hurried. He was a long, easy man with a lamplighter's patience and a face that had been kind so consistently that kindness had worn grooves in it. When he reached her lamp — the third one, the one that always needed a second try in damp weather — he said, "Evening, Nurse Thornbury," and lit it, and then did not leave.

"You've got your thinking face on," he observed, capping the flame.

"Do I?"

"You get a line here." He touched his own brow, above the nose. "Like the river's froze under your skin. My gran used to get it when the rent was due." He leaned on his pole. "I won't ask. But I'll say this much: whatever it is, it's the sort of thing that gets heavier the longer one person carries it. That's not wisdom. That's just physics."

Orsen looked out at the river. The ice along the banks had thickened to a dirty white crust; the channel still ran black and open in the middle, steaming faintly, like something alive and annoyed.

"There's a woman," she said, before she could decide not to. "At the ferry house. Come about her son."

"Ah." Tam said it the way men said *ah* when they understood more than the syllable contained. Every town had its mothers who came; most were sent home with forms. "And the answer's not the one on the paper."

"No."

"And you can't give her the real one without showing where you keep it."

Orsen turned to stare at him. Tam examined his lamp glass with great interest, turning it to catch the light.

"I light every lamp in this town," he said mildly. "I know which windows burn late and which go dark. I don't know what you do in yours, Nurse, and I haven't looked. But I know a keeper when I carry a lamp past one." He straightened up, shouldered his pole. "What I'm saying is: if you ever need a man who walks the whole town every night, sees every door, and is owed small favors by every watchman on the bridgehead — well. You'd know where to find me. Third lamp's a bad'un in the cold. Give it a knock after me and she'll catch."

He touched his cap and moved off down the row, and the lamps came alight behind him one by one, a string of small suns laid across the gathering dark.

Orsen stayed on the steps until her hands ached. Across the river, the far shore was invisible now; only the bridge lamps marked where land might be, a chain of orange beads leading nowhere. Tomorrow the bridge closed. Tonight a woman sat by a stove rehearsing how she would bear either answer.

*Returned to duty.* Orsen thought of Edmund Quill dying on a stretcher in the corridor, of the adjutant's nib scratching out *died*, of the ledger under the floorboard with its thirty-one names growing heavy as a stone in her chest. She thought: *a record no one can read is just grief with good handwriting.*

Resolve did not arrive like courage in a story — no trumpet, no lifted chin. It arrived like the cold, seeping in everywhere at once, until she noticed she had already changed. She went inside, past the ward, down the passage, to her room, and knelt, and drew up the floorboard, and took the ledger out, and held it in both hands under the single permitted bulb.

Thirty-one names. One of them belonged to the woman at the ferry house.

Tomorrow the bridge would close, and post with it, and Halsey's audits would begin in earnest — she had seen the new order chalked at the telegraph office that morning, *ALL OUTGOING CORRESPONDENCE SUBJECT TO INSPECTION BY ORDER OF THE GARRISON COMMANDER*. Whatever she was going to do, she would have to do it before the last mail coach crossed, and the coach left at noon.

There was a knock at her door — two soft taps, the hospital's signal for a messenger. When she opened it, a duty orderly stood in the corridor with a telegram slip on a clipboard, snow melting on his shoulders.

"Nurse Thornbury? This came for you. Marked urgent." He hesitated. "It's from the garrison office. Commander Halsey requests your presence tomorrow morning. Eight o'clock. He says—" the orderly glanced at the slip, uncomfortable, "he says it concerns outgoing correspondence."
