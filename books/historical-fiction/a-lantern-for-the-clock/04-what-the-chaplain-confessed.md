## 4. What the Chaplain Confessed

The chapel at Marlow's Bend had been a granary once, before the army requisitioned it, and it still smelled faintly of rye and mouse. Elias Wrenn liked that about it. A church that remembered being useful for something honest was, he thought, more church than most churches.

He was stacking hymnals when she came in — the nurse, Thornbury, snow in the seams of her coat, standing just inside the door as though the door might change its mind about admitting her.

"I'm not here for a service," she said.

"No one ever is." He set down the last hymnal and dusted his palms on his cassock. "Sit anyway. The stove works better if someone's pretending to enjoy it."

She did not sit. She stood with her hands folded like a woman at a graveside, and Wrenn thought: *she has come to ask me something she already knows, or to tell me something I already know.* In eleven months at this garrison he had learned that all conversations of consequence were conducted in that narrow country between what people knew and what they could admit.

"You were at Vellum Ridge," Orsen said. Not a question.

"Three years ago, yes."

"They removed you from the chaplaincy."

"*Reassigned*," he corrected gently. "The word matters to them. They are very fond of words, the men who write reports. *Returned to duty. Lightly wounded. Administrative transfer.* Words like little doors, closing one room and opening another, until you can no longer tell where the man went." He pulled out a chair for her, and this time she took it, slowly, the way one sits on ice.

"The officers say you falsified casualty figures," she said.

"The officers say a great many things." He fed the stove a split of pine and watched the flame take. "I said the dead outnumbered the reported dead. That was my crime. I did not invent numbers. I only declined to repeat theirs."

* * *

Outside, the wind worried at the frozen eaves. Inside, the chapel filled with resin-smoke and the particular silence of two people circling a thing too heavy to lift alone.

Wrenn studied her while she pretended to study the altar. She had the look he'd seen on nurses who worked night shifts too long — a face like paper held over a candle, translucent with light from behind, one careless breath from burning through. He had watched her at the hospital gate these past weeks, always pausing, always glancing back at the building as though it contained something she was afraid to leave unattended.

"You've been asking after Edmund Quill," she said at last. "Marta Ostrova mentioned it."

"I've been asking after everyone," Wrenn admitted. "It's an old habit. When they took my collar's authority, they couldn't take my curiosity." He hesitated, then decided — as he had decided once before, at the cost of everything, and would decide again — that the truth deserved a faster road than caution allowed. "His mother wrote to me. Ada Quill, from Ashford Hollow. She received a telegram saying her son was returned to duty. She wanted to know what duty looked like, whether he had a warm coat, whether he'd been confirmed. Mothers ask such small questions. It is the smallness of them that breaks you."

Orsen's hands tightened in her lap. He saw it. He had spent three years learning to read hands; soldiers' hands told you what their mouths had been ordered not to.

"And what do you know," she asked quietly, "about Private Quill?"

What do I know. Wrenn rose and crossed to the narrow window, where the river showed itself between the granary-chapel and the commissary — a white seam stitched between black banks, holding up its crust of ice like a secret held too long. Beyond it, the bridge, closed now for the season, its timbers furred with frost, its far end vanishing into weather.

"I know that on the fourteenth of October," he said, "a company withdrew from the forward posts at Kessel Ford under orders nobody will put in writing. I know the withdrawal became a rout somewhere past the second culvert, because the ground there funnels retreat into a single track and the artillery found them. I know that forty-one men were listed as killed, and that by morning the count had been amended to nineteen, with the remainder marked *straggled* or *returned to duty* — phrases chosen, I believe, because they generate no paperwork requiring follow-up. And I know that Private Edmund Quill was among those whose names were carried off the list before the ink was dry, because I saw the original ledger."

He heard her breath stop behind him.

"You saw it," she repeated.

"I was summoned to record last rites for the wounded brought back that night. The regimental clerk was still correcting the count at the adjutant's table. He was weeping while he worked, if that means anything to you. It meant something to me." Wrenn turned back. "I questioned the amendment. Formally. In writing. Commander Halsey responded by having me reassigned here — a garrison so quiet it might as well be underwater — and by instituting, shortly thereafter, certain reforms in how casualty reports are compiled. Reforms which consist chiefly of compiling fewer of them."

Orsen stood. She stood the way people stand when the floor has shifted beneath them and they need to test whether it will hold. "You're telling me his mother came all this way—"

"She came because the telegram lied, and some part of her knew it. Yes."

"Then someone has to tell her."

"Yes," Wrenn said. "And there is the difficulty."

* * *

They sat across from each other at the little table beneath the east window, where he prepared sermons no one required him to give, and he laid it out for her plainly, because he judged — correctly, he believed — that she was a woman who could carry plain things.

"Halsey audits every outgoing post," he said. "Every letter, every parcel, since the first of November. The telegraph office keeps a carbon copy of every wire, and the bridgehead guardhouse opens anything heavier than a Christmas card. There is a list — I have seen it — of words and names that cause a letter to be detained. Casualty numbers. Regimental designations. Names of the missing." He paused. "Names of mothers asking questions."

"He's sealing the town," Orsen said. "Because the bridge is closed, nothing leaves except what he allows."

"Precisely. Winter is convenient for a man like Halsey. The river does his censorship for him. By spring, whatever happened at Kessel Ford will have softened into rumor, and rumor into nothing, and the mothers will still be waiting for sons who were struck from the roll in an afternoon." He laced his fingers together. "I have been composing letters to the war office since October. I have written nine. All nine sit in my desk drawer, because I am a coward in installments, Mrs. Thornbury. Each month I write one, read it, and understand that sending it accomplishes nothing but my own arrest, and that my arrest accomplishes nothing for anyone else. A martyrdom without witnesses is only a death."

"And you think I have a way around that."

He looked at her steadily. "I think you are keeping something. I don't know what. But you pause at gates like a woman carrying contraband, and you look at the mail coach the way other people look at departing trains when they've missed them. And yesterday, when Ada Quill asked you directly whether you'd tended her son, you answered *I may have*, which is the answer of a person guarding a larger truth than the question requires."

The silence stretched. Through the wall came the muffled sound of the garrison band rehearsing something martial and flat.

"I keep a ledger," Orsen said finally, and her voice was very quiet, as though the walls had ears, which Wrenn suspected they did. "Of the men the reports lose. Their names, their wards, the dates, what actually killed them when the official version says otherwise. Thirty-one entries. Edmund Quill is the twelfth."

Wrenn felt something loosen in his chest — a knot he had carried since October, since the clerk's tears and his own nine unsent letters. "You've seen it too," he said. "The gap. Between what happens and what gets written."

"Every night," she said. "For months. And I told myself history happens elsewhere. That I was keeping a private record and someday—" She stopped. Her jaw set. "Ada Quill is staying at Marta Ostrova's ferry house. She has nowhere else to go until the bridge opens. She deserves to know that her son died at Kessel Ford on the fourteenth of October, and she deserves to know it from someone who isn't afraid of the word *dead*."

"Then tell her," Wrenn said. "But understand what it costs. If Halsey learns there's a written account outside his ledgers — an honest one — he won't content himself with reassignment this time. A clerk was caught burning pages at the adjutancy last week. Caught *burning* them, Mrs. Thornbury, on someone's order. Halsey is not tidying records. He is destroying evidence, and he knows others exist. He can smell it, the way wolves smell winter coming. If your ledger surfaces while we're sealed inside this town, it doesn't reach the war office. It reaches his stove."

"So we wait until the bridge opens."

"We wait, and hope he doesn't find it first. Or—" Wrenn stopped himself, but she was already looking at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the expression of a keeper of secrets who has begun to tire of the keeping.

"Or," she said, "we find another way out."

"There is no other way out. There is the bridge, and there is the telegraph, and both belong to him."

"Then we make a way that belongs to us."

* * *

He walked her to the chapel door afterward, into the blue cold of late afternoon, and they stood a moment in the lee of the doorway while the wind combed the snow off the commissary roof.

"Why did you really come today?" Wrenn asked. "To ask about Quill — or to find out if I could be trusted?"

Orsen considered him. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it away. "Both," she said. "I needed to know if the man they broke for telling the truth was finished telling it."

"And?"

"And now I know he isn't." Almost — almost — she smiled. "Come to the ferry house tomorrow evening. Marta cooks for whoever's willing to eat. Bring your nine letters."

Wrenn watched her go down the lane toward the hospital, small against the drifts, unhurried, a woman who had decided something. Then he went back inside, knelt at the drawer where his unsent confessions lay stacked like kindling, and began, for the first time in three years, to feel less like a man hiding a fire and more like a man waiting for a match.

He did not yet know, as he locked the drawer that night, that the match was already lit — that in the nurses' quarters above the ward, under a loose floorboard, thirty-one names lay sleeping beside a letter from Ashford Hollow — nor that by week's end, Commander Halsey would announce an inspection of every quarter in the garrison, and the search would begin at the hospital.
