## 5. Three Checkpoints

Orsen drew the map on the back of a laundry list, because no one looked twice at a laundry list.

She sat in the dispensary after the morning rounds had been signed off and the ward had gone quiet under the particular hush that followed morphine rounds — the men either sleeping or pretending to sleep — and she ruled three lines with the edge of a scalpel handle: one across the bottom of the page for the river, one vertical stroke for the hospital gate, another for the telegraph office, a third for the bridgehead guardhouse. Three marks between her ledger and the war office. She stared at them until they seemed less like lines and more like teeth.

*The gate first,* she thought. *Always the gate.*

Private Hollis manned it now, a boy of nineteen with a rifle he'd never fired and a conscience so transparent Orsen could read his mood from thirty paces by the set of his shoulders. He searched outgoing parcels — that was new since the autumn, since Halsey's memorandum about "unauthorized correspondence" — but he searched the way boys search: lifting a corner of a bandage bundle, patting a coat pocket, blushing at anything that might be a woman's underthings. He would not think to look at a nurse's apron bib. He would not think to look at a hymnal. But Halsey's adjutant, Lieutenant Corr, took the evening shift at the gate twice a week, and Corr searched the way a customs man searches, with flat hands and flat eyes, and he had once made Sister Amsel unpack an entire crate of surgical wool in the snow.

Then the telegraph office. That was Wrenn's territory now, or had been until yesterday; Orsen pressed her thumbnail into the wood of the desk as she thought of him sitting in the guard cell, still wearing his stole-less collar like a man who'd forgotten to take off a costume. The telegraph office was the choke point. Every wire leaving Marlow's Bend passed through Operator Fenn's hands, and Fenn read everything — everyone knew it, everyone wrote around it, filling their cables with weather and mother-love and nothing true. And now, per Halsey's latest order posted on the canteen door, all outgoing post — letters, parcels, wires — was subject to inspection at the telegraph office before it ever reached the mail coach. Which meant the mail coach itself, the last one out before the bridge closed, was no longer a door but a sieve.

And third, the bridgehead guardhouse. Even if a paper got past the gate and past Fenn's reading glasses, it would have to cross the bridge, and the bridge ended at a stone hut where Sergeant Braithwaite kept a stove, a dog, and a list of everything Halsey wanted found. The dog worried her more than the sergeant. Paper carried in a coat smells of the body; paper sewn into a hem smells of nothing at all; but a dog did not care about smell so much as about nervousness, and Orsen had been carrying nervousness in her coat for eleven months.

She looked down at her map. Three teeth. And behind the third tooth, winter — the bridge closing tomorrow at dusk, the river locking, the town becoming an island with one honest road and none of them permitted to walk it.

"You've the face of a woman doing arithmetic that won't come out even."

Marta Ostrova stood in the dispensary doorway with a basket of onions she had no official reason to be delivering, having delivered the real onions an hour ago. She came in without waiting for permission, which was Marta's way with doors, and set the basket down and looked at the laundry list upside down.

"That's not washing," she said.

"No."

"It's the town." Marta turned the list around with one blunt finger. "Gate. Telegraph. Guardhouse." Her finger stopped on the river line. "And this is what you're actually looking at."

Orsen said nothing. Eleven months of keeping a ledger had taught her that silence was a kind of currency, and she had never learned to spend it well with Marta Ostrova, who treated silence as a door left open and walked through it every time.

"The bridge closes tomorrow," Marta said. "You knew that when you drew your little teeth. So whatever you're carrying, you're not carrying it over the bridge."

"Marta—"

"I ferried this river for twenty-two years before the ferry company went to the war and never came back." Marta pulled a stool over and sat, forearms on knees, close enough that Orsen could see the rope scars crosshatching her palms like old writing. "My husband took grain across at night in '09 when the excise men were watching the bridge. I took people across in '14 when the quarantine was on and the garrison wouldn't let the sick leave. There is a way over the ice that is not a way over the ice, if you understand me."

"I don't."

"There's a channel," Marta said, "where the current runs fast under the freeze, coming off the mill race above Kessel's bend. The ice over it is thick enough to hold snow and thin enough to hold nothing else. Smugglers called it the Seam. You can't see it unless you know it — there's a willow stump on the north bank shaped like a crooked finger, and you keep the stump on your left shoulder and walk due south, and you pass within six feet of the Seam without ever stepping on it. My husband could do it blind drunk. I can do it sober. Nobody else in this town has done it in eight years."

Orsen looked at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because Tam Beckett asked me to." Marta said it plainly, the way she said everything, as though plainness were a kindness, which perhaps it was. "He came to me two nights ago and didn't tell me why, only that a woman he works with might need a way across the river that leaves no signature. He didn't ask me what you're carrying. I didn't ask him. But I watched you at the market last week when the casualty list went up, and I watched you count the names against some list inside yourself, and your lips were moving, and there weren't enough names on the board for your lips to finish." She stood. "I'm a widow, Nurse. I have spent five years being lied to by officers who wrote my husband down as lost with all hands when I later learned the barge was scuttled for insurance. I know what it does to a person, being the only one who remembers."

The dispensary clock ticked. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed, the deep wet cough of the ones who would not go home.

"If I take the Seam," Orsen said slowly, "I take whatever I carry onto ice that may not hold."

"It held in '14 with two grown men and a trunk of letters on it." Marta shrugged. "It may not hold now. The cold came late this year. I won't lie to you — I'll test it ahead of time, alone, with poles and rope. If it holds, we go. If it doesn't—" she met Orsen's eyes — "then you'd better find another way, because there isn't one, and the river will be shut until thaw regardless."

"And the patrols?"

"Halsey sweeps the shore road at night since the clerk was caught burning pages." Marta's mouth thinned. "That was a bad week. They say the clerk was just a boy. They say Halsey had him transferred to a punishment company rather than court-martial him — kinder, on the surface, and crueler underneath, because everyone understands what a punishment company is." She picked up her basket. "Lanterns sweep the bank every hour, roughly. Roughly is the word that matters. Drunk sentries are regular sentries with gaps in them."

When she reached the door she paused, not turning around. "Bring your book to the ferry house tonight. After ten. Come the back way, past the net racks. And come hungry — I cook for people who are working."

Then she was gone, and Orsen stood over her laundry-list map with her heart going hard, and understood that the thing had changed shape. It had been a secret, and then it had been a burden, and now it was a plan, and a plan required other people, and other people required telling, and telling was the one thing she had sworn off since the day she'd started writing names in a book instead of saying them aloud.

* * *

She told Tam first, that evening, in the lamp room above the chapel steps, where he trimmed wicks with the concentration of a surgeon and listened with the stillness of furniture.

"A copy goes with me," she said. "The original stays here, under the floorboards. If I'm taken, the original is still evidence. If the original is found, the copy still crosses."

Tam set down his trimming knife. In the lantern light his face was all planes and patience. "And if both are found?"

"Then at least Halsey burns two books instead of one, and somewhere in his burning he learns how many names there are, and a number is harder to burn than a page."

"That's not comfort, Nurse Thornbury."

"It's arithmetic." She almost smiled. "It's the only comfort I have left."

He nodded slowly, and then did something she did not expect: he took down a second lantern, filled it, trimmed its wick, and lit it, so that two flames stood side by side on the workbench, throwing doubled shadows up the wall.

"One for walking," he said. "One for reading by. If you're copying names onto ration cards in the dark, you'll want a light that doesn't smoke. I've a beeswax lot that burns clean — a lamplighter's trick. Sentries look for smoke glow on the horizon. Clean flame reads as nothing."

"You'd lend me your tricks."

"I'd lend you my hands, if you'd let me." He said it without flourish, which somehow made it heavier. "Copying a whole ledger alone takes weeks you don't have. Two of us, nights, in the lamp room — four days, maybe five. Ada Quill can cut the cards to size; she's got a steady hand, she did fine needlework before—" he stopped, hearing himself, and Orsen finished gently:

"Before her son was written back to duty."

Tam bowed his head. When he looked up his jaw was set. "Four days," he said again, as though pricing it. "And then Marta tests the ice, and then we walk."

"We?" 

"I light lamps on that shore road every night, Mrs. Thornbury. I know where every sentry stands by the shadows they throw. If you cross the ice, someone has to stand at the waterline and make sure the lanterns behind you go dark at the right minute." He touched the nearer flame, cupping it, and the shadow on the wall shivered and steadied. "Besides. A book of dead men deserves more than one pair of feet carrying it. It deserves witnesses. I've watched this town's lights go out name by name for a year. I'd like to carry a few of them back."

Orsen looked at the two flames for a long moment. Then she took the ledger's hiding place key from around her neck and held it out to him on its string, and watched his face as he understood what she was handing him — not a key to a floorboard, but a key to the whole weight of it, the trust entire.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "After ten. Bring the clean-burning lanterns. And Tam—" she paused at the stair, the cold rising up from the chapel below, "—when we're done copying, I want you to read the names aloud. All of them. Once. I've only ever written them. Someone ought to say them."

* * *

The ferry house smelled of tar, onion, and river, and by the time Orsen arrived past the net racks the back room already held Marta at the stove, Tam shaking snow from his coat, and Ada Quill seated by the lamp with a stack of ration cards squared before her like a hand of terrible playing cards. Wrenn's absence sat among them like a fifth chair. They had gotten word to him through the chaplain's boy — a message hidden in a psalter, passed at the exercise yard — and the answer had come back in the margin of the same psalter, four words in a cramped clerk's hand: *Fenn watches wires. Ice.*

So it would be the ice, then, or nothing. Marta spread her husband's old chart on the table, weighted at the corners with spools of thread, and traced the river's black spine with a finger gone white at the nail. The Seam lay there, invisible, a ghost in the ink. "We go Thursday," she said. "Moonless. I test the crossing Wednesday night with poles and rope, and if the ice sings — if it makes that long low note, like a bow on a bottle — we don't go, and we wait, and we hope Halsey's audits stay slow." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn, the way a captain looks at a crew. "Anyone who walks that ice walks knowing the river keeps what it takes. Say so now, or don't come."

Ada spoke first. Her voice was small and level and did not waver once. "The river already took my Edmund. It can't take anything from me that matters."

Tam said, "I'm in."

Orsen opened her mouth to give the same answer, and found that the words had rearranged themselves while she wasn't looking. A week ago she had been one woman with a book under a floorboard, keeping witness alone because alone was safe. Now she was one woman among four, with a route, a schedule, clean-burning lanterns, and a copy half-planned, and it should have felt more dangerous, and instead it felt like the first solid ground she'd stood on since spring.

"Thursday, then," she said. "But we start tonight. There are ninety-one names, and four days, and—"

The knock came then. Three soft raps, spaced like a code, and Marta blew out the lamp in the same motion, and in the sudden dark Orsen heard boots on the frozen mud outside the ferry house — many boots, unhurried, in the rhythm of a patrol — and a voice calling through the plank wall, courteous as a knife laid on a pillow:

"Ferry widow Ostrova. Commander Halsey's compliments. Open the door — there's been a theft at the telegraph office, and we're searching every house on the river road tonight."
