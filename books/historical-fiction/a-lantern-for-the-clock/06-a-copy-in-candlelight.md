## 6. A Copy in Candlelight

The lamp in Tam Beckett's loft had a crack in its glass chimney that threw a hairline of light across the ceiling, and by that hairline he had learned to measure the hours of his own courage.

He sat cross-legged on the floorboards with the ledger open on his knees and a fresh notebook beside him, a stub of pencil behind his ear and another in his fist. Outside, Marlow's Bend lay under snow so deep and silent it seemed the town had been folded away in linen, put aside until spring. The river ran black beneath its ice, patient as a held breath. Tomorrow night, if Marta Ostrova's calculations held, four people would walk out onto that ice carrying the only honest account of forty-one men.

Forty-one names. Tam had counted them twice before he began, the way he counted the lamps along Bridge Street each dusk — not because he doubted himself, but because counting was how he kept fear in its place. A lamplighter's whole trade was this: you walked into the dark on purpose, and you made a small fire at intervals, and you trusted that the dark was longer than you were brave but shorter than you were stubborn.

He dipped his pen — Orsen had procured ink from the dispensary, thin and brownish — and began again at the top of the page he'd ruined an hour ago when his hand shook.

*Quill, Edmund. Private. Died 14 October, retreat from Kessel Ford. Not "returned to duty." Not wounded. Dead.*

Writing it plainly felt like setting a match to paper. He could almost hear Halsey's voice in the strokes: *unauthorized correspondence, sedition of morale.* But beneath that voice there was another one, older and quieter, belonging to Tam's father, who had lit lamps through two wars without ever asking what the light was for. *A lamp doesn't choose what it shows,* his father used to say. *It only refuses to be dark.*

Tam wrote on.

The transcription was slow work because it had to be legible to strangers — clerks at the war office who had never seen any of these men, who would need clean letters and dates and places. Orsen's ledger itself was a wilder thing: her hand slanting across the pages, margins crowded with details no official form allowed. *He sang while they changed his dressing. He asked me to write to his sister in Welsh; I couldn't, so I drew her a picture of a lark instead. He was afraid of the dark and never once complained about the cold.* Those marginalia were the part that would break hearts open, and Tam understood now why she had kept them. A list proved deaths occurred. The margins proved lives had.

He heard the ladder creak and went still, pencil lifted, listening with the whole animal of him. Then Orsen's head appeared through the trapdoor, followed by Ada Quill's, and last came Marta, who climbed ladders like a woman boarding a boat she owned.

"Any trouble?" Tam asked.

"The gate guard wanted to know where I was walking," Orsen said, shrugging off her coat still powdered with street snow. "I told him nowhere worth remembering."

"He believed you?"

"Nurse Thornbury is a very forgettable person." She said it without bitterness, but Tam caught the flinch under it — the way she had begun to speak of herself as a thing that could vanish. He resolved, silently, to remember everything about her out of spite.

Ada settled herself on the crate by the stove without being invited. She had stopped apologizing for taking up space three days ago, and Tam liked her better for it. Grief had come into her like winter into a house, and instead of shuttering every room she had gone through opening windows. Tonight she carried a basket of bread ends from the garrison kitchen and a hymnal with a broken spine.

"I brought this," she said, holding up the hymnal. "You said flyleaves. This one has six blank ones, and nobody at the chapel will miss it — half the bindings are held together with hope as it is."

Orsen took it and turned the pages with something close to reverence. "Six leaves. At two names per leaf, written small—"

"Twelve men remembered in a book of songs," Ada said. "That seems right, somehow. They'd have sung these, some of them. Edmund did. Badly." Her voice caught on *badly*, and she let it catch, didn't smooth it over. Tam had noticed that about her: she refused to make grief tidy. She carried it the way other women carried water, openly, both hands, letting anyone see.

Marta dropped onto the floor beside Tam and looked over his shoulder at the notebook. "Your handwriting is better than mine," she said. "Which is fortunate, because my contribution requires none. Ice report." She spread her hands flat on the boards. "I walked the near third tonight, up past the reed bank. It held me. It'll hold us, if we go light and go spaced apart and go quiet. The far third worries me — there's a spring-fed channel under the ice by the old ferry stakes, and it sings when it's angry."

"Sings?" Ada asked.

"You'll hear it. Like a kettle deciding whether to scream." Marta said it matter-of-factly, and somehow that was worse than any warning delivered with drama. She turned to Orsen. "And the checkpoints? You've mapped them?"

"In here." Orsen touched her temple. "Hospital gate first — I can get us out of that one, I'm staff. Telegraph office second, though after Wrenn's arrest we should assume the operator reports everything now, including shadows on the glass. And the bridgehead guardhouse last, which is the teeth of it. Two sentries, a brazier, and orders to stop anyone on the ice road. Halsey posted them himself."

"And between the telegraph office and the bridgehead," Marta said, "there's the alley behind the chandlery where a certain widow keeps a door that opens onto nothing but her own reputation."

"That alley is watched since the clerk was taken," Orsen said.

"It's watched badly. Soldiers watch doors. They don't watch *over* doors." Marta tapped the sloped underside of the loft roof meaningfully, and Tam felt his stomach perform a slow roll. Over roofs. Of course. Lamplighters knew rooftops the way priests knew psalms.

They worked then, all four of them, and the loft filled with the particular silence of shared labor. Tam transcribed. Orsen sat opposite with the ration cards fanned out before her like a card sharp's hand, writing each name in pencil fine enough to pass for printing: *R. Calloway, extra bread allocation, ward three.* A name on a ration card looked like bureaucracy. It could pass through a search the way water passes through cloth. Ada inked hymnal flyleaves in her careful schoolroom hand, pausing sometimes with the pen hovering, and Tam realized she wasn't hesitating — she was praying, silently, between the names.

Once, Ada read aloud without meaning to. "Calloway, Reginald." She looked up. "He was in Edmund's company. Edmund wrote home about him — said he could whistle two notes at once and drive the whole barracks mad with it."

"He died the same day," Orsen said quietly. "Same hour, likely. They were found together."

Ada nodded slowly, and Tam watched her take that fact and do something with it — fold it into purpose the way a sailmaker folds canvas. "Then they go on the same page," she said, and wrote.

It was near midnight when the bell began.

Not the chapel bell — the garrison bell, harsh and arrhythmic, the alarm cadence. All four of them froze. Through the frost-feathered window they saw lanterns blooming along the parade ground, officers' voices cracking across the snow, and then a single amplified voice, brass and clipped, unmistakably Commander Halsey's:

*"By order of the garrison commander: effective immediately, a full inspection of all quarters, military and civilian, within the town limits. All papers, correspondence, and personal effects subject to examination. Noncompliance will be treated as aiding the enemy. Inspection begins at dawn."*

Dawn. Four hours.

Marta swore in three languages, fluently. Ada set down the pen and pressed her hand flat against the hymnal as if steadying a bird. Orsen stood at the window with her back very straight, and Tam saw her face in the lamplight doing arithmetic he couldn't follow — until she turned around.

"He knows," she said simply. "He can't prove anything yet, but he knows someone is keeping records. The inspection isn't a fishing expedition — it's a net closing. Which means the original ledger cannot stay under my floorboard tonight. If they find it, everything dies with it — the copy, the plan, Wrenn's sacrifice, all of it."

"So we move tonight," Marta said. "Across the ice, ahead of schedule, in the dark, with patrols already awake."

"With the ledger sewn into Ada's coat, as planned, and the copies distributed among all four of us so no one carries more than they can afford to lose." Orsen's voice was level, but Tam saw her knuckles white around the window frame. "And we finish the copying first. Every name. If we're crossing tonight, we cross complete."

Ada rose, took up her pen again, and said, "Then give me another hymnal. I'll work faster."

Tam pulled his father's spare lantern from the shelf — the good one, brass, the one he'd never lent to anyone — filled it, lit it, and set it in the center of the floor where all their hands could reach the light.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the town's dogs began to bark, one street at a time, as the patrols went out into the snow.
