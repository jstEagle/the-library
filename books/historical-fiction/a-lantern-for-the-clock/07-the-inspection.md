## 7. The Inspection

The knock came at half past six in the morning, when the light through the frosted windows was the color of skimmed milk and the hospital corridor smelled of carbolic and yesterday's porridge.

Orsen was braiding her hair in front of the small mirror above the washstand when she heard it — not a knock, precisely, but the flat of a fist against wood, three times, unhurried. She had heard that rhythm before. It belonged to men who did not need to ask twice.

"Nurses' quarters," said a voice she knew too well. "Open."

Her hands went still on the third crossing of the braid. In the glass, her own face looked back at her, pale as the river ice, and behind it the room: two iron cots, hers and Sister Calloway's; the washstand; the wardrobe with its warped door; the floorboards, plain pine, scuffed grey by decades of boots.

The ledger lay beneath those boards. Six inches of old timber between Commander Halsey and forty-one names.

*Forty-four now,* some cold clerk in her mind corrected. *You added the three from Tuesday.*

She finished the braid because finishing it was the only thing her hands could be trusted to do, tied it off with the ribbon she kept for Sundays, and opened the door.

Halsey stood in the corridor with the morning behind him, a tall man built like something meant to bear weight — a bridge, a gallows, an argument. His coat was buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the stoves. Two military policemen flanked him with the studied blankness of furniture, and behind them, half in shadow near the stairwell, stood Corporal Voss, the garrison clerk, holding a leather satchel against his chest like a man carrying someone else's child.

"Miss Thornbury." Halsey did not wait to be invited. He never had. "An inspection of quarters. Routine."

"Of course, Commander." Her voice came out level. She marveled at it, distantly, the way one marvels at a clock still ticking inside a dropped case. "Shall I wake Sister Calloway? She's on nights."

"No need." He stepped past her into the room, and the MPs followed, and then it was done — they were inside, all four of them, filling the small space the way water fills a lung.

* * *

Orsen had rehearsed this moment without knowing it. For weeks — since Wrenn's warning, since Marta's map drawn in coal dust on the ferry-house table — she had walked through these rooms in her mind's eye, asking herself what a searching man would see. The mattress, lifted and pressed. The wardrobe, swept with a hand along its top shelf where dust gathered undisturbed. The loose brick by the stove where she'd once imagined hiding things and rejected for exactly that reason — everyone imagines the loose brick.

She had settled, months ago, on the floorboard beneath her cot, the one whose nail head had worked proud of the wood and caught her stocking every second week until she'd pried it up herself, learned its weight, learned to seat it back so perfectly that even she sometimes forgot which board it was. Third from the wall. Under the cot's iron leg, where no man would kneel if he could avoid it.

But knowing the hiding place and surviving the search were different creatures, and she understood that now, standing with her back to the cold window while Halsey moved through the room like a slow tide.

He began with Sister Calloway's cot. He did not tear anything — that was the worst of it, Orsen thought. A tearing man is a frightened man, and a frightened man can be managed. Halsey lifted the mattress with two fingers, set it down square, ran his palm along the slats. He opened the wardrobe and went through Calloway's folded underthings with the detached thoroughness of a customs officer, refolding each garment before he set it down, so that nothing would look disturbed. He wanted her to know he'd been there and to have no complaint to file about how.

Then he turned to hers.

Orsen watched him cross the four feet of floor and felt the boards speak under his boots — she could not help hearing it, that particular note, the third plank from the wall, the one that gave up its small wooden voice whenever weight crossed it. His boot found it. The board spoke.

He paused.

It was nothing. It was a man pausing because a man pauses. It was everything.

"Mind the cold, Commander," she heard herself say. "The window frame's rotten. Draft comes straight through."

"I imagine you'd know," he said pleasantly, without turning. "Nights are long here."

He knelt.

Orsen had knelt beside dying men in dressing stations that smelled of iodine and bowels and rain, and she had learned that terror has a physiology — that it lives in the hands first, then the breath, then somewhere behind the sternum where the heart seems to forget its trade. She felt all of it now, arranged in its familiar order, while Commander Halsey, kneeling on clean floorboards six inches above forty-four dead men whose own army had unmade them, reached under her cot and took hold of the frame.

The cot shifted. The iron leg ground a half-inch across the wood.

"Something under here?" one of the MPs asked.

"Dust," Halsey said. He drew the cot out a handspan, bent, and looked into the shadow beneath it. Orsen watched his face in profile: the jaw working slightly, the eyes moving left to right with the patience of a man reading a page.

He straightened. He pushed the cot back. Its leg came down a finger's width from where it had been — not on the board, *beside* it, and Orsen kept her breathing even by an act of will that felt like carrying water in cupped hands across a crowded room.

He searched the washstand next. The mirror. The seam of the mattress. He even took down her hair ribbon from its nail, shook it out, and hung it back, and there was something almost courteous in it, almost gentle, that made her skin crawl worse than violence would have.

At last he stood in the middle of the room and looked around, and Orsen realized he was not looking for anything anymore. He was deciding something.

"You'll have heard," he said, "about the clerk."

"Sir?"

"Corporal Ames. Records section." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man mentions weather to a neighbor whose roof he intends to buy. "Caught last Thursday burning documents after hours. Pages from the casualty register." He let the word sit in the room. *Casualty.* "He claimed he was destroying spoiled copies. Misprints." Halsey smiled without any warmth reaching anywhere near his eyes. "We counted the ashes. There were more pages than misprints would account for."

Behind him, Corporal Voss shifted his weight, and the satchel creaked.

"It occurs to me," Halsey went on, walking slowly toward the door, his boots finding each board with deliberate weight — the first, the second, the third, *the third*, and Orsen's heart stopped on it like a bird against glass — "that fires are like fevers, Miss Thornbury. Where there's one, there's usually others coming. An institution learns things about itself in winter." At the doorway he turned, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely and she saw what lived underneath it, which was not cruelty exactly but something colder and more efficient: a man protecting the shape of a story. "If anyone in this garrison keeps papers I haven't authorized — sentimental records, private memorials, anything of that nature — they should consider what happens to a fire when it finds dry tinder."

"I keep no papers beyond my duty roster, Commander."

"No," he agreed softly. "I'm sure you don't."

He left. The MPs left. Voss lingered a half-second longer than the others, and in that half-second his eyes flicked — quickly, terribly — to the floor beside her cot, and then away, and he followed his commander down the stairs.

* * *

Orsen stood in the empty room for a full minute before she trusted her legs.

Then she knelt, slid her fingernails under the third board, and lifted it, and there it was: the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of lamp oil and the tallow candles Tam used for the copying. She did not take it out. She only laid her palm flat against the wrapping and felt the corners of the pages through the cloth, all those names stacked like sediment, and waited for her hands to stop shaking.

They did not stop shaking. She let them shake and thought anyway.

*Voss looked.* That was the fact she could not put down. Not at the cot, not at the window — at the *floor*. And he had said nothing. Had he seen the board's edge seated imperfectly? Had he marked it for Halsey and been ignored? Or — and here her mind turned over like a stone showing what lived beneath — had he marked it and chosen silence?

She thought of Ames, the burning clerk. She had passed him a dozen times in the records corridor, a narrow young man with ink-stained cuffs who held doors for nurses and never met anyone's eyes. All those weeks she had believed herself alone in the dark, the single keeper of the town's honest dead, and all those weeks Ames had been feeding pages into a stove one at a time, in secret, in fear, doing in his small way what she did in hers. They had been two candles burning in separate rooms of the same house, each believing the house dark.

And Ames had been caught. Which meant Halsey was not searching at random. He was hunting a *pattern* — a leak, a resistance, a thread of people who refused to let his version of the war stand unchallenged. He had stood six inches above her ledger this morning and told her, in his soft way, exactly how much he already knew.

The board went back down. The nail seated true. Orsen rose, checked her reflection, unpinned the calm expression she'd worn for the inspection, and pinned it back again, and went down to the ward to begin her shift.

Tam was waiting by the dispensary door, ostensibly restocking lamp oil, actually watching the stairs. One look at her face and the question left him.

"After dark," she murmured, passing close enough that only he could hear. "The ferry house. All of them. Bring the lantern."

"Why?" he murmured back. "What happened?"

She kept walking, hands steady now, voice pitched for no ears but his.

"Halsey's counting ashes. And the clerk who burned the pages — he wasn't working alone either. Somebody in that office is still ours, Tam. And I think I know who."
