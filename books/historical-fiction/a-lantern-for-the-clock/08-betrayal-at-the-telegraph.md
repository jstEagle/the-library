## 8. Betrayal at the Telegraph

Elias Wrenn had preached his last sermon in a church with a copper spire and four hundred souls, and he had thought, in those days, that courage was a loud thing — a voice raised against injustice, a congregation stirred to its feet. He knew better now. Courage, as it turned out, was a quiet thing: a man standing at a frosted window at half past two in the morning, watching a telegraph office across the square, waiting to learn whether the people he had come to love were about to be destroyed by his own confession.

The snow had stopped an hour before midnight, and the town lay under it like something buried alive. Marlow's Bend was beautiful in this state, Wrenn thought bitterly — all white roofs and blue shadows, smoke rising straight from the chimneys into a sky full of indifferent stars. A town that did not know what it was harboring. A town that believed the war happened elsewhere, on the far side of the bridge, in the country of official dispatches where boys came home wounded but honest.

He pulled his collar higher and counted the lit windows of the telegraph office for perhaps the hundredth time that night. One. The operator's room above it, dark. The side door where young Piet Halloran entered each evening at six and left, if the schedule held true, at dawn.

Piet Halloran was twenty-two years old, freckled, devout in the unshowy way of farm boys, and the sole reason their plan had any shape at all. It had been Piet who first approached Wrenn outside the chapel — Wrenn still technically held services there, attended by six people including two dogs — and said, in the artless way of the very young: *Sir, I key messages all day for the Commander, and some of them don't match what the men tell me before they go up the line. Am I mad, or is there something wrong?*

Not mad, Wrenn had told him. Only awake. And being awake in Marlow's Bend in the winter of 1917 was a dangerous condition.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been assembled like a patchwork quilt from whatever scraps they had. Marta Ostrova contributed the geography of the river; Tam Beckett contributed the patience of a man who climbed ladders in all weathers; Orsen contributed the ledger itself — that small, damning book of names, each one a boy the officers had written out of existence with a stroke of the pen. *Returned to duty.* Wrenn had read those words over Edmund Quill's file and felt something inside him crack along an old seam.

But the ledger could not cross three checkpoints on paper alone. Halsey audited everything now. Every outgoing parcel weighed, every letter steamed open over a kettle, every traveler's pockets turned out at the bridgehead guardhouse while a clerk copied down names. So they would not send paper. They would send light.

Piet would key the names westward in a commercial code — the cotton quotations, the grain prices, the dull liturgy of commerce that no censor troubled to read closely. Each name hidden in a figure. Edmund Quill became eleven bales at four shillings. The others became tonnage, freight rates, weather reports from towns none of them would ever see. At the far end, in the capital, waited a man Wrenn had known in seminary — Aldous Crane, now third desk in the war office records division, a bureaucrat with a conscience and, Wrenn prayed nightly, a memory good enough to reconstruct what the numbers meant.

One transmission. That was all they needed. One night of Piet's fingers on the brass key, and the truth would exist in two places instead of one, and no fire in Halsey's stove could reach it.

Wrenn had told himself, arranging it, that the risk was small. He had told himself a great many things. He was, he reflected, still a preacher after all — a man professionally acquainted with comforting fictions.

* * *

At ten minutes past three, the telegraph office door opened.

Wrenn's breath stopped. Not Piet's shift-end. Too early. Two figures emerged into the lamplight at the corner of the square, and even at distance he knew the cut of their coats — garrison military police, greatcoats buttoned to the chin, carbines slung. They stood a moment in the snow, talking, one of them smoking, the ember of his cigarette making a small red accusation against all that whiteness.

Then a third figure came out between them, hands visible, coatless.

Wrenn's knees nearly went. He gripped the window frame. It was Piet — he could see the shock of pale hair even at this range, see the boy's head turning back toward the office as they walked him toward the guardhouse, as though the building might yet speak for him. The door of the office opened again behind them and a fourth man stepped out: Sergeant Voss, Halsey's own, carrying a canvas satchel Wrenn recognized with sick certainty. The message log. Perhaps the draft itself — Piet kept his working papers locked in the operator's desk, and either the lock had failed or the boy had failed, or both.

They took him around the corner toward the guardhouse and the square was empty again, and the snow went on falling in its gentle, complicit way, covering footprints, covering everything.

Wrenn stayed at the window a long time, doing arithmetic with other people's lives. If Piet talked — and he was twenty-two, and frightened, and alone in a room with men trained in questioning — then the chain ran backward from the code to the seminary friend to the chaplain to the lamplighter to the ferry widow to the nurse, and ended at a floorboard in the nurses' quarters where a book of names lay sleeping under old floorboards, waiting to be burned.

He should run to them now. Wake them. Scatter.

Instead, Elias Wrenn sank into the chair by the window, put his face in his hands, and prayed the only prayer he had left: *Let me be worth something tonight.*

* * *

He found Orsen at the hospital before first light, in the corridor outside the ward where she'd been checking on a fever case. She saw his face and understood everything at once — he watched comprehension move through her like cold water through cloth.

"Piet," she said.

"Taken at three. Voss has his logs." Wrenn kept his voice low, level; a chaplain learns to preach through grief without letting it shake. "If they break him—"

"He won't talk."

"You can't know that."

"I know Piet." But her hand had gone to her apron pocket, where Wrenn knew she carried a stub of pencil and a slip of paper with figures written on it, the ghost of the plan, and her fingers were trembling. "Elias. What do we do?"

What do we do. As though it were shared equally, as though he had not engineered the whole fragile architecture. He looked at her — this woman who had spent months keeping a secret book because no one else would keep the truth, who had promised a grieving mother answers she could barely bear to give — and he made his decision the way one steps off a riverbank in winter: all at once, before the body can argue.

"They'll want the sender," Wrenn said. "Voss knows the traffic patterns. When the coded figures went out last week — my test transmission, the grain prices — someone keyed them. They'll want a name. So we give them one, before they find it themselves." He met her eyes. "Mine."

"No."

"Orsen—"

"You are sixty-one years old, you have a bad heart, and you are proposing to walk into that guardhouse and confess to espionage."

"I am proposing to walk in and confess to sending commodity prices," Wrenn said, and almost smiled. "Grain and cotton, nothing more. A disgraced chaplain with a grudge against authority — plausible, even flattering to them. While they spend today and tomorrow deciding what to do with me, they won't be searching your quarters, or Marta's boatshed, or Tam's lamp-oil store. You get a night. Maybe two. Use them."

"And when they discover the numbers mean more than grain?"

"Then I will have bought you time with the only coin I have left," Wrenn said quietly. "I questioned casualty reports once, Orsen, and it cost me my church and my name. I have regretted the manner of it ever since — too proud, too loud, all sermon and no service. Let me do it properly this time. Quietly. For the names."

She stared at him. Behind her, through the ward door, came the sound of a man moaning in his sleep, calling for someone called Tom in a voice worn down to thread.

"If they take you," she said at last, "and I cross the ice without you—"

"Then you testify for all of us," Wrenn said. "That is the whole point of a ledger, my dear. The dead cannot appear at the inquiry. Someone must stand where they stood." He straightened his coat. "Now. Before I lose my nerve, which is thinner than the river ice — is Tam watching the north road?"

Orsen closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, but her voice was steady, and Wrenn thought: *there it is. That is what courage looks like after all. Not loud at all.*

"The reed bank, past Marta's stakes," she said. "Midnight tomorrow. If the ice holds."

"Then hold to it," Wrenn said. "Whatever you hear about me — hold to it."

* * *

He presented himself at the guardhouse at nine o'clock in the morning, soberly dressed, collar white as the snow, and asked for Sergeant Voss by name. He said he had information regarding unauthorized transmissions from the telegraph office, and he watched the sergeant's face perform the small theater of surprise that professional men reserve for confessions they were already expecting.

In the interview room — a stove, a table, two chairs, a barred window full of gray light — Wrenn told his story with the fluency of a lifetime's sermons: bitterness at his disgrace, resentment of the garrison, yes, he had sent the figures, a private joke, a little smuggling of market news to a friend in the capital, nothing more. He recited cotton prices until his mouth tasted of brass. He gave them Aldous Crane's name as "an old schoolfellow fond of speculation," and prayed that Crane's conscience was quicker than Halsey's suspicion.

It nearly worked. He could feel it nearly working — Voss taking notes, the younger officer by the wall beginning to relax, the whole machinery of the morning bending toward the comfortable conclusion that this was one embittered clergyman and no more.

Then the door opened, and a clerk leaned in, and said words that dropped through Wrenn like stones through thin ice:

"Sergeant — the Halloran boy. He's asking for his mother. And he's asking whether the chaplain sent the grain prices too."

* * *

They did not beat him, in the end. They simply did not let him leave, and that was worse — hours in the chair while footsteps passed and paused outside the door, while the stove ticked and died and was fed again, while he composed and discarded letters in his head: to Crane, to his dead wife, to God, whom he addressed with increasing informality as the afternoon wore on. Toward evening they moved him to a cell in the guardhouse cellar, and through the stone he could hear, faintly, the town going about its frozen business above him — sleigh bells, a dog, the groan of the river ice shifting in the current like something turning over in sleep.

A soldier brought him bread and tea and, setting down the tray, said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

Wrenn ate, and prayed, and counted the hours. Midnight tomorrow. The reed bank past Marta's stakes. Somewhere above him, Orsen was deciding right now — he could feel her deciding, the way one feels weather changing — whether a night bought with his freedom was enough, whether the mission was worth the cost of the people she loved, whether a book of names justified leaving a man in a cell beneath the guardhouse of Commander Halsey.

He hoped she would go. He told God he hoped she would go.

And then, because he was honest down to the marrow on the rare occasions honesty was safe, he admitted to the dark that he hoped she would come back for him.

Near midnight, boots on the cellar stairs. Keys. The door swung open on lantern light and the silhouette of a woman in a nurse's cloak, snow melting on her shoulders, and behind her the broad shape of Tam Beckett holding a dark lantern, and Wrenn felt his heart turn over like the ice on the river.

"We couldn't leave you," Orsen whispered, crouching to his eye level, pressing something into his hands — a heavy coat, sewn thick along the seams, oddly stiff. He touched the lining and understood, and his breath caught. "The copy went into your coat, Elias. Ada's wearing the original. We cross tonight — all of us. Can you walk?"

Wrenn laughed, once, silently, a laugh like a prayer answered wrong and perfectly.

"I have been walking toward this river my whole life," he said. "Lead on."
