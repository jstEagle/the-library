## 9. Over the Ice

The moon did not rise that night, and Orsen Thornbury counted it among the small mercies of a winter that had offered her so few.

They gathered in Marta Ostrova's boathouse at half past eleven, where the smell of tar and frozen hemp hung thick enough to chew. Marta had lit no lamp. She worked by touch and by the grey seep of starlight through the slatted walls, her broad hands moving over Ada Quill's coat with the patience of a woman who had dressed the dead and the living both. The ledger lay open across her knees — forty-one names in Orsen's careful hand, each one a man the garrison had written into smoke — and she sewed it flat against the coat's lining with waxed thread, stitch by stitch, until the pages disappeared beneath the wool as though they had never been.

"Hold still," Marta murmured to Ada. "A wrinkle reads as a book. Flat reads as a heart."

Ada stood with her arms held out from her sides like a scarecrow, and Orsen watched her and thought: *she is carrying her son home.* Edmund Quill would never cross this river again, but his name would, sewn against the breast of the mother who had come three hundred miles on the strength of a lie. *Returned to duty.* Orsen had written those words herself once, under orders, before she began keeping her own account. She could still feel the pen's nib biting the paper.

Tam Beckett crouched by the water door with his lantern shuttered, running his thumb along the ice shelf where river met land. "It'll hold," he said, in the tone of a man reporting weather rather than wagering four lives. "Marta's walked it every winter since her husband drowned. There's a channel out past the second bend that never freezes clean, but she knows where it sleeps."

"I know where it *slept*," Marta corrected without looking up. "Rivers change their minds. So will I, if it asks me to."

Outside, the town was awake in the wrong way. All evening the patrols had come through — Halsey's men, six strong, boots ringing on the frozen ruts, lanterns swinging at knee height so the light climbed the walls of the houses like something climbing after prey. They had searched the ferry widow's woodshed at dusk. They had knocked at Tam's lamp room while he stood downstairs with his heart going like a telegraph key. Wrenn's arrest had bought them a night, but Halsey was not a man who wasted what he bought; he was spending it all at once.

Orsen pressed her palm flat against the cold of the wall and made herself breathe slowly. Fear lived in her chest now the way a splinter lives under skin — small, constant, and felt with every movement. She thought of Elias Wrenn sitting in some cell below the guardhouse, his collar gone, probably praying for them with the same stubborn gentleness he'd used to pray for dying men. She thought: *if we are taken, let it be worth what he paid.*

"Ready," Marta said, and bit the thread.

* * *

The river at midnight was not white but blue-black, a vast animal lying still, its back dusted with frost crystals that caught the starlight and threw it back in fragments. When Orsen stepped off the bank and onto it, her boot made a sound she would remember all her life — not a crack but a *creak*, long and low, like a floorboard in an empty house, like the earth itself clearing its throat to speak.

She stopped. Tam's hand came gently between her shoulder blades. "That's just it talking," he said softly. "Ice talks all night. It's when it goes quiet you worry."

So they went, four figures strung out along Marta's remembered line: Marta first with her iron-shod staff, testing each stride; then Ada; then Orsen; then Tam last, walking backward half the time, watching the town. The wind came down the valley and found every gap in their clothing. Orsen's breath froze in the wool of her scarf until the fabric near her mouth was stiff as bark.

Below her feet, two feet perhaps, maybe less, the river moved in the dark. She tried not to think of it. She thought of it constantly — the black water sliding under the black ice, patient, unhurried, holding in its memory the ferryman Ostrova and every other soul the crossing had ever claimed. Her nurse's mind supplied images unasked: the way a body looks after cold water has finished with it, the color of the lips, the particular stillness. She had seen drowning men pulled from this river in summer, during the bridge's brief working life. She had held their hands while they coughed the river back out or didn't.

"Here." Marta's voice, low. "Mind my tracks exactly."

Ahead, the surface changed — a faint paleness, a texture like breathed-on glass. The channel. Marta struck it twice with her staff and listened, head cocked, an old woman interrogating a river in a language only they spoke. Then she stepped wide around it in a long arc, and they followed her prints single file, each boot falling precisely where another boot had promised the ground would hold.

Halfway across, the ice answered.

It came without warning — a sound like a rifle shot, enormous, cracking up through Orsen's boots and into her teeth, and beneath it a groan that traveled away northward faster than any horse. Ada made a small sound. Marta froze mid-stride, arms out, and said, very calmly, "Nobody moves. It's settling. It's only the cold settling its bones."

But Orsen, looking down, saw the seam. A dark hairline had opened between her feet, thin as a pen stroke, running off toward the channel. Water wept up along it in a gleam no wider than a thread. She stood perfectly still and understood with total clarity that she was going to die here if the ice decided it, and that there was nothing in her hands, nothing in the world, that could argue with it.

Then she thought of the ledger, and of the forty-one names, and of Ada standing ten feet ahead carrying her son against her breastbone, and something in her steadied — not courage exactly, but the thing courage is built from. *Not yet,* she told the river silently. *Not these.*

The seam stopped growing. The groan faded. After a long minute Marta said, "Walk. Softly. Same tracks."

They walked.

* * *

They were perhaps two hundred yards from the far shore when Tam whispered, "Lantern."

Orsen turned. On the southern bank — their bank, the town's bank — a light had appeared at the top of the slope above the old landing. It swung in slow, deliberate arcs, the way soldiers sweep a yard they intend to search rather than merely pass through. Even at this distance she knew the rhythm. She had watched Halsey's men work the hospital wards the same way: thorough, patient, certain that whatever they wanted would be found because everything was always found.

"They've guessed the river," Marta breathed. "Old fool that I am — the boathouse. They'll have found the boat tracks."

"Can they follow?" Ada asked. Her voice was steady. Orsen marveled at it. Three days ago this woman had wept in the hospital corridor until her knees gave; tonight she asked tactical questions like a sergeant.

"Not on foot behind us — they'd need the same luck with the channel," Marta said. "But if they ride the bank to the rail depot road, they can be waiting when we land." She was already turning, scanning upstream. "Reed bank. There — see the pale break in the shore? We go to ground and wait for the patrol to commit itself. If it comes onto the ice, we move north along the shallows. If it stays on land, we wait for dawn."

"And if it waits too?"

"Then we learn to be reeds."

They reached the shore and folded themselves into the reed bank — a dense stand of last summer's growth, tall as men, frozen into a woven wall, snow drifted against its base in a lip three feet deep. They burrowed into it the way animals do. Tam killed his lantern entirely; the dark closed over them like water. Marta pulled Ada down beside her and put her own great coat around them both. Orsen found herself pressed shoulder to shoulder with Tam, his breathing shallow and controlled beside her ear, and she took the rhythm of it the way she had once taken the pulse of frightened boys — borrowing calm from whoever had it to lend.

The lantern crossed the ice.

It came out from the southern landing in a slow diagonal, three men by the shadows it threw, their boots making sounds the river carried straight to the reeds — crunch, crunch, crunch, each step a small report. The light swept toward them, nearer, nearer, and Orsen sank lower into the snow and willed herself into the landscape: *I am a reed, I am drift, I am nothing the war wants.* The beam passed across the face of the reed wall eight feet above their heads, and the frozen stalks lit up gold for one terrible instant, every seed head sharp as etching, and Orsen did not breathe, and beside her Ada did not breathe, and somewhere under the wool of her coat a dead man's name lay against a living woman's heart.

The lantern swept past. The voices came — muffled, bored, cold. One of them struck the ice with a rifle butt, twice, experimentally. The sound rolled away down the valley.

"Channel's back there somewhere," a voice said. "Ostrova knows it, but she's not stupid enough to—"

"Commander says search the bank to the depot road. Move."

The light withdrew. The boots crunched away southward, along the shore, toward the depot — toward the place they were meant to reach by morning. Orsen lay in the snow and listened to them go and felt the fear turn over inside her and show its other face, which was fury. Halsey would stand at that depot with his men and his lists and his confident handwriting, and he would find nothing, because four people were lying in frozen weeds refusing to exist, and the ledger would arrive at the war office anyway, and every erased name would walk back into the record over his signature.

Dawn was a long time coming. They waited through it shivering, taking turns at the small movements that kept blood in fingers and toes, sharing the bread and the brick of tea Tam had brewed and bottled at dusk, now cold as pondwater and the finest thing Orsen had ever tasted. Once, in the deepest hour, Ada spoke into the dark, so quietly it might have been prayer.

"He was afraid of water. Edmund. Never learned to swim." A pause. "I keep thinking the river should know that. I keep thinking I should tell it."

"The river doesn't hold grudges," Marta said, in the dark, in her gravel voice. "Only debts. And it's been paid enough on this crossing."

Orsen closed her eyes and memorized them: the lamplighter, the widow, the mother, herself, four shapes in a reed bed, bound together by paper and thread and the decision, made again each hour, to refuse the comfortable lie. Whatever waited at the depot — Halsey, the patrol, the whole machinery of the garrison waking to hunt them — they would walk into it in daylight, on their own feet, with the truth sewn between them and the world.

When the sky finally turned the grey of old linen, Marta rose out of the reeds like something the winter itself had grown, shaded her eyes, and looked down the shore toward the depot road.

Then she said, quietly: "There's soldiers at the platform. More than soldiers." She lowered her hand. "And someone's set up a table."

Orsen rose beside her and looked, and her stomach dropped: at the little rail depot, in the early light, Commander Halsey sat behind a folding table with a ledger of his own open before him, checking papers, checking names, checking faces — a man prepared to read every soul that boarded the morning train.

Beside her, Ada Quill touched the front of her coat, where the pages lay, and said, "Then we'll have to give him something honest to read."
