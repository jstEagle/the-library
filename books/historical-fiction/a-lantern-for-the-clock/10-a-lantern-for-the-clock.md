## 10. A Lantern for the Clock

The far bank of the river came up gray and slow out of the dawn mist, and Orsen Thornbury walked the last hundred yards as though the ground itself might change its mind.

Her boots had gone through the crust twice on the crossing — once near the middle where the current ran deep and the ice sang its thin, glassy note underfoot, once in the reed shallows where they had crouched through the whole of the night with a patrol lantern sweeping the shore like a slow yellow eye. Her knees ached. Her hands were cracked at every knuckle from cold and lamp-black. The ledger was sewn into the lining of Ada Quill's coat, pressed flat against her back between wool and cotton, forty-one names stitched into place with Marta Ostrova's sailmaker's thread, and Orsen could not stop glancing at it — at Ada's shoulders, at the small stubborn set of them — as if the book might be visible through the fabric, glowing like a coal.

But no one looked. That was the strange mercy of it. On the far shore there were only milk carts and a boy leading a limping horse, and the rail depot squatting at the edge of town with its chimney breathing smoke into a pinkening sky, and none of these things knew what they carried.

Tam Beckett walked beside her, his lamplighter's pole still over his shoulder because he would not put it down, not even here. "You're staring," he said quietly.

"I'm counting," Orsen said. "I've been counting since the reeds. Four of us went onto the ice. I keep waiting to be sure of four."

"You can stop now." He touched her elbow, briefly, the way he'd touch a lamp that needed steadying. "Four."

Behind them, the river lay white and enormous, and beyond it, half-drowned in fog, Marlow's Bend: the hospital roofs, the garrison walls, the bridge — the bridge! — where already, faintly, she could hear hammers. They were pulling the winter boards off the planking. The season had turned while they hid among the frozen reeds; the ice would hold perhaps another week, and then the water would come back and take the secret road away as if it had never existed.

"Look," Ada said.

They all turned. At the bridgehead, a workman had climbed the timber frame and was unhooking the great iron chain that sealed the span each winter. It came down link by link, ringing against the posts, and the sound carried across the water clean as a bell. The door that had closed on them in November was opening again — but they had not waited for it. That was the thing Orsen held onto, the thing she thought she would hold for the rest of her life. They had not waited.

* * *

The depot was nearly empty at that hour. A stationmaster with a red nose and an air of permanent grievance sold Ada four tickets without looking up from his ledger — his own ledger, honest about nothing more than fares — and then Orsen did the thing she had rehearsed against the reed bank wall in her head a hundred times.

She walked to the post counter and asked for paper, pen, and ink.

The clerk slid them across. For a moment she stood with the pen uncapped and her hand hovering, and she understood that this was the true last checkpoint — not the telegraph office, not the guardhouse, not Halsey's patrols, but this: a plain woman's hand on plain paper, writing her own name where anyone might read it.

*Orsen Thornbury,* she wrote. *Matron's assistant, military hospital, Marlow's Bend.* Not a cipher. Not ration cards with names inked small between printed weights and measures. Not hymnal flyleaves. Her name, spelled out entire, followed by the words she had been composing since the night Ada Quill first knocked at the hospital gate:

*I enclose a record of forty-one soldiers who died at Marlow's Bend between October 1916 and February 1917 and whose deaths do not appear in any official return. I kept this record myself. I witnessed most of these deaths. I am prepared to testify to each one, under my own name, before any board the War Office appoints.*

She wrapped the ledger in brown paper, tied it with the string Tam had carried coiled in his pocket since the ice, and addressed it to the office of the Inspector-General of Medical Services. Then, after a moment, she took the pen again and added a second line beneath the address:

*To be opened by the addressee personally. The sender is not hiding.*

Her hand shook only a little.

When she handed it over the counter, the clerk weighed it, stamped it, dropped it into the canvas sack behind him with all the other morning letters — the love letters, the begging letters, the bills — and that was all. Forty-one men went into a mail sack between a tailor's invoice and someone's birthday card. Orsen stood very still until the sack was cinched and carried out to the waiting train, and then Tam's hand found her shoulder and stayed there, warm through her coat, and she let herself breathe.

"It's done," Ada said. She said it simply, the way you'd say *the kettle's on*. "It's done, Miss Thornbury."

"Orsen," she said. "Please. We slept in a reed bank together. I think we've earned Christian names."

Ada smiled — the first full smile Orsen had seen from her, and it remade her face entirely, showed her the girl she must have been before Edmund, before the war, before grief taught her to stand so straight. "Then I'm Ada. And we are going to miss that train if we stand here being sentimental, Orsen."

They were not going to catch it. None of them were. The tickets had been bought in case the plan failed — in case the ledger had to travel alone and they had to scatter — and now the plan had not failed, and the tickets sat in Ada's glove like coins for a journey nobody needed to take. Orsen bought a second envelope instead, and wrote to the depot master of Marlow's Bend's sister station asking after Chaplain Elias Wrenn, held on Halsey's order since the telegraph arrest, and by evening a reply came back that made her read it three times: *Prisoner transferred to divisional custody pending inquiry. Inquiry convened at request of Inspector-General. No charges sustained.*

Wrenn was free. And the word *inquiry* appeared twice in five lines, which meant the ledger had arrived, which meant someone with rank enough to frighten Halsey had opened it and read it and not thrown it in the stove.

"They'll come for him properly now," Tam said, reading over her shoulder. "Your chaplain. They'll take his statement."

"He gave it already," Orsen said. "In the crypt, in December, when he told me what he'd seen in the command ledgers. He just didn't know yet that anyone would write it down." She folded the letter away. "He'll know tonight."

* * *

They took rooms above the ferry inn on the far side — Marta's cousin ran it, another widow of the river, another woman who asked no questions and charged fair prices and put extra bread on the table without being asked — and it was there, three nights later, that Wrenn found them.

He came in the blue hour before dusk, thinner than Orsen remembered, his collarless coat hanging off him, a week of custody written into the hollows of his face. But when he saw them crowded into the inn's low parlor — Orsen rising first, then Tam, then Ada, then Marta coming through from the kitchen wiping her hands — he stopped on the threshold as though the sight of them were a thing he needed to verify before trusting.

"I heard," he said, "that four people crossed the river on foot the night the patrols were doubled. I didn't dare believe—"

"Come in, Elias," Orsen said. "Sit down. You look like a man who's been fed on prison bread and suspicion."

"I have been." He sat, and Marta pushed a bowl of barley soup in front of him, and for a while he ate like a man remembering how, and nobody spoke, and the fire cracked, and outside the window the river went dark piece by piece.

At last he set down his spoon. "Halsey is suspended. Under investigation — falsification of returns, destruction of records, and the clerk, the boy who was caught burning pages — he talked. He'd been copying names too, Orsen. Before you. He thought he was the only one." Wrenn's voice caught. "We were never the only ones. That's what none of us ever knew. There were keepers all down the line, little fires lit in separate rooms, each thinking itself the last one burning."

Orsen looked at the fire and thought of her floorboard, and the long months of believing herself alone with the dead, and said nothing, because some truths are too large to answer and too kind to interrupt.

"And the inquiry?" Ada asked.

"They have my statement. They will have yours." He turned to Orsen. "A board sits in Marlow's Bend in the spring, once the roads open. They'll want you to testify in person. Under your own name. In front of officers, in front of the town." He paused. "It will not be gentle."

"No," Orsen agreed. "It won't." She thought of Halsey's boots passing overhead in the night, of the telegraph operator's wife weeping at the wire fence, of forty-one names that deserved better than gentleness anyway. "But I spent two years invisible, Elias. Two years watching men die and watching their deaths get tidied away like spilled tea. I'm finished with it. If they want my name, they may have it — spoken aloud, in a full room, where it can't be burned or buried or filed under *returned to duty*."

Tam laughed softly beside her. "There she is," he said, to no one in particular, to all of them. "That's the one who crossed the ice."

* * *

Spring came late to the river that year, and when it came it came all at once, as if embarrassed.

The ice rotted in a single week of rain. The ferry — Marta's old ferry, patched and repainted by Tam and Wrenn and anyone else who could hold a brush — went back into the water on a bright loud morning, and Marta stood at the tiller with her chin up like a woman greeting an old adversary she had finally beaten.

And along the north bank, where the path ran above the waterline, lanterns began to appear.

Ada made them herself. She bought the glass and the tin cheap from the far-town chandler, and she worked at the inn table every evening, fitting and soldering, and each lantern bore a name scratched into its base with a nail — Private Edmund Quill first, and then the others, one by one, all forty-one, in Ada's careful schoolroom hand. When each was finished she carried it out at dusk and hung it from the crooked post at the river's edge, or set it on a flat stone, or wired it to the willow boughs, until the bank below the inn glimmered with small steady lights reflected double in the moving water.

"What are they *for*, exactly?" Tam asked her one evening, gently, because he was a man who liked to understand a mechanism before he admired it.

Ada trimmed a wick with her fingernail. "So the river knows," she said. "And so anyone walking home late sees them and knows somebody was here, and was counted, and was loved. Officers keep their books in offices. This—" she nodded at the trembling row of flames, "—this is a book the water has to read."

Orsen stood beside her in the blue dark, and watched the lights, and felt the ache in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite peace but some third thing with elements of both.

* * *

On the last night of April, the whole of them gathered at the rail depot — the same depot, the same counter, the same red-nosed stationmaster, though now he tipped his cap to Orsen, having read the newspapers like everyone else. Wrenn was returning to his parish, reinstated, his disgrace converted retroactively into courage. Marta was taking the first spring ferry run at dawn. Tam had accepted the lamplighter's contract for the far town — *they say a hundred lamps and half of them broken, you'll be busy* — and had asked, in a voice he tried to make casual, whether Orsen intended to stay in Marlow's Bend for the inquiry and the summer and possibly indefinitely, and Orsen had said yes, and had let the silence afterward mean everything it meant.

One train stood waiting. One envelope remained to be posted: Orsen's formal statement for the spring board, signed, dated, witnessed by a chaplain and a lamplighter and a ferry widow, the most respectable company her name had ever kept.

She handed it to the clerk. He stamped it. The mail sack swung out to the carriage.

"There," Wrenn said quietly. "That's the last of the hiding done."

"Not the last," Orsen said. She looked out through the depot glass at the river, running high and silver with meltwater, at the bank beyond it where forty-one small lanterns were being lit one by one for the evening, Ada moving down the line with a taper like a priestess of some new and necessary faith. "The testimony isn't hiding. The board isn't hiding. There's still everything after that — the questions, the officers, the years it will take. But those aren't hiding either." She smiled, tired and certain. "I think the hiding is finished. Now comes the harder part. Being seen."

The whistle blew. The train pulled out. And on the far bank, one by one, the lanterns took up their watch over the water, and the clock above the depot door — stopped all winter, like everything else — ticked, shuddered, and began, at last, to move.
