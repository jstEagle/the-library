## 2. Orders Come in Winter

The circular came folded inside a grocer's invoice, which was how the government liked to speak to people like us—disguised as arithmetic, tucked between a figure for lamp oil and another for salt pork. I read it standing in the scullery with my coat still on, snow melting down my collar, and by the time I reached the third paragraph my hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

*By order of the Provincial Directorate,* it said, in the flat bureaucratic language that always sounded like a man reading a recipe for something terrible, *households identified with seditious publication, unlawful assembly, or correspondence with proscribed persons will be subject to search without prior notice. Servants and residents are advised that possession of prohibited material constitutes complicity regardless of ownership.*

*Regardless of ownership.* That was the sentence they'd built the whole machine around. It didn't matter whose pamphlets sat under your floorboards; if they sat under your floor, you were theirs.

I read it twice more, memorized the reference number at the bottom—the Directorate stamped everything, even its threats—and then held the corner of it over the spirit stove until the paper curled and went brown and then black, and the ink lifted off in a smell like burnt hair. The invoice portion I set aside to give to Marta, because we did genuinely need lamp oil, and there was no sense wasting good cover.

Three years I had been opening the Marchmont post before anyone else saw it. Three years of steaming seals over the kettle in my room, reading with two fingers the way a surgeon reads a wound, deciding what lived and what died before it ever reached a hand that might tremble. People think the dangerous part of a house is what happens in it. It isn't. It's what arrives at the door. A house is only ever as safe as the person who sorts its mail.

I hung up my coat, smoothed my hair, and went looking for Marta.

She was in the kitchen as she was always in the kitchen, a mountain in an apron, reducing bones to something nourishing. Lidiya sat at the scrubbed table doing sums she had already done twice, because Lidiya did everything twice—it was her way of daring the world to catch her in an error. Sef was in the corner with his sleeves rolled, cleaning the rollers of the little hand press he wasn't supposed to have dismantled indoors, ink to his elbows like a man who'd been arm-wrestling the night itself.

"Grocer's come," I said, and laid the invoice on the table where Marta could see it, and looked at her a half-second longer than the sentence required.

She caught it. She always caught it. "I'll check the stores," she said loudly, meaning *we need to talk*, and sent Lidiya upstairs to fetch the good linens nobody had used since summer, meaning *clear the room*, and Lidiya went, though not before giving me a look that said she knew exactly how much linen-fetching this household actually required.

That girl counted everything. One day I would have to decide whether that made her safe or doomed.

* * *

We stood in the cold pantry with the door shut and the hanging hams between us and the world, and I recited the circular from memory, because I never kept such things longer than it took to destroy them. Marta listened the way stone listens, arms folded, jaw working slightly as though chewing on each clause.

"Search without notice," she said when I finished. "Which means dawn."

"Which means dawn," I agreed.

"They've done it to the Kovalenko house. And the printer on Vodny Lane." She named them the way you name the dead. "Took the man's typeset and left his wife sitting in the wreckage of the flour bin."

"Then we do what we've always done. Only better."

Marta snorted. It was not a sound of contempt for me but for the whole arrangement of the universe, in which women who could run a kitchen that fed fourteen people and hid a printing press were still expected to stand aside and be searched like trunks at a border. "And the floor?"

"The floor stays. They won't pull up boards on a first visit—they're fishing, not digging. But the letters go. Tonight. All of them."

"Even his?"

"Especially his."

Her brother's letters. Fourteen of them, written from a prison cell over two years, in a hand that started bold and ended small, the last one barely a page. She kept them wrapped in oilcloth beneath the third board from the window, under the same floor that held the pamphlets and the pistol and every other piece of our hidden architecture. I had read them once, years ago, at her request, because someone had to know what was in that house besides her, and she had chosen me. I had never told her what it cost me to read them. Some weights you carry silently or not at all.

"He'd understand," I said quietly.

"I know he'd understand." Her voice roughened. "That's not the same as wanting to."

"No. It isn't."

We stood there among the hams, two women planning a funeral for paper, and after a while Marta wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist—a brisk, angry motion, the grief of a practical woman—and said, "The stories, then. We drill them tonight?"

"We drill them tonight."

* * *

We gathered them after supper, when the lamps were low and the windows showed nothing back but our own reflections. Patrik Marchmont sat at the head of the long table looking, as he always did on these evenings, like a man attending a lecture slightly above his station—he had married into radicalism rather than being born to it, and though he had proven braver than most who were born to anything, he still wore his convictions like a borrowed coat. He listened. He paid for things. Between those two acts he had built a life.

I laid it out plainly, because fear respects plainness.

"There's a circular. Raids on radical households, no notice, likely at dawn. Nothing has been found here in three years because nothing here exists to find—but only if everyone holds their story tomorrow the way they held it yesterday. So we say it all again. Every name, every date, every lie we've agreed to live inside."

"Ulyana." Patrik raised a hand, gentle, pained. "Must you call them lies? We've discussed this."

"They're lies that keep us out of prison, sir. I find it helps to respect them."

He had the grace not to press it. Around the table: Marta, arms crossed, already halfway into her part. Sef, seventeen and vibrating, ink still ghosting the creases of his knuckles. Lidiya, who was supposed to have gone to bed an hour ago and who had instead installed herself in the shadows by the dresser with a face of perfect innocence, which fooled no one and deterred no one either, because we had all decided long ago that pretending not to see her was safer than sending her away to imagine worse.

"Begin with the press," I said.

Sef straightened. "There is no press."

"There is no press. Say it again."

"There is no press."

"Again. Slower. As if it bores you."

"There is... no press." He got the boredom into it on the third try, and I nodded, and something in his chest deflated with disappointment, because Sef wanted so badly to be asked about the press—to be caught, to fight, to make the raid mean something. That boy carried fury the way other boys carried knives. I would deal with him later, privately.

"Marta. The pamphlets."

"What pamphlets?" Marta said, and her voice was so magnificently, insultingly empty that Patrik laughed despite himself, a short startled bark.

"That. Exactly that. Do it exactly like that at six in the morning with a man's boot on your doorstep."

"Lidiya." I turned toward the dresser. "You're asleep."

"I'm asleep," Lidiya said from the shadows.

"You were asleep all night. You heard nothing, you saw nothing, you woke to knocking like any child. What do children do when strangers come?"

Lidiya stepped forward into the lamplight. She was fifteen and thin as a winter branch, with her mother's dark eyes and nobody's caution. "Cry?"

"Cry. Cling to whoever's nearest. Be fifteen and terrified and boring. Can you do boring?"

"I can do anything," Lidiya said, "if I understand why it works."

"Why it works," I repeated. "All right. Here is why. A frightened child confirms nothing and contradicts nothing. An officer can bully a statement out of an adult, but a crying girl gives him mud, and mud sticks to his boots, and he wants to leave. Your tears are a locked door. So cry."

Something moved across her face—pride, and under the pride something older and sadder, the look of a child realizing the grown-ups' game has real pieces. She nodded once. "I'll cry."

"Patrik." Now the hard part. I hated this part every time. "You are a gentleman of leisure with reformist sympathies and a weak heart. You fund nothing. You sign nothing. You met most of these people through your late wife's charitable work and you keep them on out of sentiment."

"And if they ask about the money transfers?"

"There are no money transfers. There is household expenditure and your wife's legacy disbursements, both documented—" I tapped the ledger I'd spent two nights making dull, every entry true and every entry hollowed out, a book that said nothing by saying everything correctly "—and you'll refer all questions to me. You don't handle accounts. I handle accounts. You're a man who reads in the afternoon."

"A man who reads in the afternoon," Patrik murmured, and I saw the flicker in him, the pride that chafed at being made small in his own house. He swallowed it. He always swallowed it. But each time, I noticed, it took a little longer going down.

We ran it twice more end to end. By the second pass it had the smoothness of ritual, the worn places showing where the words had traveled before. That was the thing about our invisibility that no one outside would ever understand: it was rehearsed. It was a play with a cast of five and an audience of armed men, performed at irregular intervals for years, and we knew our marks, and we knew each other's lines better than our own, and somewhere along the way the performance had eaten so far into the truth that some mornings Marta greeted me with her empty voice before catching herself, and some evenings Sef glanced at the wall where the press wasn't and flinched.

A family is a story you agree to live inside. Ours just happened to be a story written for the police.

"All right," Patrik said at last, rubbing his eyes. "It's late. God willing, a boring night and a boring morning."

We dispersed. Marta banked the fire. Sef carried his imaginary press off to bed. Lidiya paused at the foot of the stairs, looked back at me with those counting eyes, and said, very quietly, "You burned something today. I smelled it on your coat."

I held her gaze for a moment. "You should be asleep."

"I'm asleep," Lidiya said, and went up the stairs, and I stood in the dark hall listening to her door close and thinking: *that child is becoming me, and I have never once wished myself on anyone.*

* * *

Inspector Rell worked late because his home was quiet in the way that made him want to drink, and his office was quiet in the way that let him think.

The Marchmont file lay open under the desk lamp, growing fat the way files grew when ambition fed them. He turned a photograph with one finger: the house on Karsavin Street, brick and ivy, utterly respectable. Inside it, according to four informants of varying reliability and one informant of no reliability whatsoever, a household that sheltered agitators, moved money for the movement, and printed leaflets that turned up in factory towns two hundred versts away.

Rell wrote facts on index cards because facts behaved on index cards. *Marchmont, P. — funds. Source: unconfirmed.* *Servant, female, driver/reader — name uncertain. Informant calls her "the grey one." Sees everything, says nothing.* He'd tried twice to place an ear in that house. Both times the candidate had lasted under a week before reporting, with genuine puzzlement, that the Marchmonts were dull. Dull households did not print sedition. Dull households also did not survive three sweeps of the district untouched while louder houses burned around them.

Someone in that house was very good.

He set down the card and rubbed his eyes. Above him, through the ceiling, he could hear Director Voss's boots crossing the corridor at eleven o'clock at night, which meant Voss was nervous, which meant pressure from above, which meant the lists had already been drawn and the Marchmont house was on one of them whether the file justified it or not. Rell had seen the circular go out that afternoon—*search without prior notice*, the phrase that ended careers when it produced nothing and ended careers when it produced too much. There was no version of the coming month in which he emerged clean. There were only versions in which he chose whom to disappoint.

He looked at the file again. The evidence against Marchmont was accumulating nicely—too nicely. Every informant corroborated every other informant the way schoolboys corroborate each other before an exam, identical errors included. In twenty-two years Rell had learned to smell manufactured cases the way horses smelled storms, and this one smelled of fresh paint.

But the list was drawn, and Voss's boots were heavy, and a man had a pension to consider.

Rell squared the cards, tied the file, and wrote across the tab in his small careful hand: *Marchmont household — surveillance ongoing.*

Underneath, where no one would look for it, he added a single word in pencil: *Soon.*
