## 3. What Marta Keeps Under the Floor

The kitchen at Marchmont House was Marta's country, and she governed it the way small nations are governed—through habit, vigilance, and an unforgiving memory for borders. She knew which floorboards sang and which kept silent. She knew that the third flagstone from the hearth, the one the scullery girl had never been taught to scrub properly because Marta did it herself, lifted on a hinge of waxed twine. And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the whole history of her disobedience: four hundred pamphlets in three languages, a pistol her brother had carried through two failed revolutions, and forty-one letters written in a hand she would know blindfolded, by touch alone.

She had not opened the stone in eleven months. There was a discipline to this. A hiding place visited too often became a confession booth; a hiding place forgotten too long became a grave. She kept to a rhythm of seasons, like planting.

Tonight was not a season. Tonight was a Tuesday, and Ulyana Iversen was sitting at her table drinking brandy out of a teacup, and that was why.

* * *

It had begun with the coughing. Marta had managed it for weeks behind the roar of the kettle and the thump of dough, but kitchens have ears even when houses don't, and Ulyana had heard. She said nothing all day—that was her way, saying nothing until the silence itself became a question—but at nine o'clock, when the household had gone up and the lamps were turned low, she came back down with a bottle under her shawl and set it on the scrubbed pine without a word.

"Brandy," Marta said, eyeing it.

"For medicinal purposes."

"I'm a cook, not a hypochondriac."

"You're a cook who's been holding her side when she thinks no one's looking." Ulyana poured into two of Marta's good teacups, the ones with the chip hidden by turning the handle just so. "I'm always looking. It's my one talent."

Marta should have sent her to bed. That was the old rule: affection, like contraband, was safest distributed nowhere. But the brandy was decent—better than decent, which meant stolen from Patrik Marchmont's cellar, which meant Ulyana had decided the rules of the house applied to everyone except herself, and something about that settled warm in Marta's chest before the drink ever touched it.

"To Tuesdays," Marta said, and they drank.

* * *

They sat across from each other the way women do when there is nothing left to perform. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them Sef dropped a boot, apologized to no one, and went quiet. Lidiya's light burned another twenty minutes—the child read past curfew the way other children stole sugar—and then the house was theirs alone, two servants in a great dark house, keeping watch over people who believed themselves the keepers.

"Your cough," Ulyana said again.

"Is a cough."

"Marta."

"It is *winter*," Marta said. "Winter makes everyone cough. The housemaid coughs. The chimneys cough."

"The housemaid is nineteen."

Marta looked into her cup. The brandy had gone amber in the lamplight, and for a moment she saw her own face reflected there, older than she kept accounts of. "My mother died of her lungs," she said finally, because the truth, once started, was hard to stop. "And her mother before her. We are a family of bad bellows. It skips no one."

"How long?"

"A while."

"*How long?*"

"Long enough that I've started salting things extra," Marta said, and hated how it sounded—like a joke, like a surrender, like both. "So whoever comes after me won't ruin the soup."

Ulyana set down her cup with a click that was very quiet and very final, the way she did everything. For a long moment she said nothing, and Marta braced herself for pity, which she had armed herself against her whole life. It didn't come. What came instead was worse and better: Ulyana simply reached over and put her hand flat on the table between them, palm up. An offer, not an embrace. Marta took it because refusing would have required a speech, and she hadn't the breath to spare.

"You will tell me," Ulyana said, "when it gets worse."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll find out anyway. I read everything in this house, Marta. Including faces."

* * *

Perhaps it was the brandy. Perhaps it was the hand. But when Ulyana rose to go and paused at the third flagstone from the hearth—only pausing, only shifting her weight, a woman with tired feet—and Marta heard herself say, "Sit down. I want to show you something," she understood that some decisions are made years in advance by people we haven't yet become.

She knelt. Her knees complained. She worked the twine hinge, felt the familiar resistance, the give, the cool breath of the earth rising up through the gap like a held thing finally exhaled. She lifted the stone aside and reached down into the dark.

"What—" Ulyana began, and stopped.

Marta brought up the first bundle: oilcloth, tied with string, heavy as a sleeping cat. She set it on the table and unwrapped it, and there they were, fanned out under the lamp like a hand of terrible cards. *The Woman Worker.* *Votes and Bullets.* A Russian pamphlet with a woodcut of a burning factory. A French one Marta couldn't read anymore but remembered memorizing, word by word, at seventeen, by candlelight, while her brother whispered the meaning.

"Pamphlets," Ulyana said. Flat. Reading the room before reading the page.

"Pamphlets," Marta agreed.

"And?"

Marta went down again. The second bundle was longer, narrower. She laid it on the table and peeled back the cloth, and the lamplight ran along the barrel of the pistol the way water runs along a pipe, finding every groove.

Neither of them spoke. In the walls somewhere a pipe knocked twice and went still, as if the house itself were clearing its throat.

"It was Andrei's," Marta said at last. "My brother's."

"I know you had a brother."

"You know what I've told you. Everyone in this house knows what I've told them. That he was a printer who died of fever in Odessa." She laughed, short and airless. "He was a printer, that part's true. He printed things that got men hanged, including eventually himself. He didn't die of fever. He died standing up, on a platform, with a hood over his head, and the last letter he wrote me took four months to arrive and said—" Her voice snagged. She drank instead. "It said *don't let them make you small, Martochka. Smallness is their favorite weapon.*"

Ulyana was looking at the letters now, or rather at the bundle beneath where the letters lay, the oilcloth corner visible, the string worn soft as ribbon from handling. Forty-one letters. Marta watched her not ask, which was its own kind of eloquence, and then watched her ask anyway, because Ulyana's silences were load-bearing but not infinite.

"May I?"

"No one reads those," Marta said. "Not even me. Not anymore. I used to, every year on his birthday. Then I started reading them every month. Then I noticed I was reading them to hear him answer, and he wasn't answering, and it was—" She pressed her lips together. "No one reads those."

Ulyana nodded slowly. Then, in the voice she used for delivering difficult telegrams: "You showed me this so someone else would know it's here."

Marta stared at her. The brandy sat in her chest like a lit coal.

"If you're taken," Ulyana went on, gently, terribly, "if there's a raid, if the floor gets pulled up—if anything happens to you, Marta, I need to know what I'm carrying. That's how I work. You know that. I can't protect what I don't know exists."

"I'm not asking you to protect it. I'm telling you it's there so that if the worst comes, you burn it. All of it. Him too." She touched the letters, one finger, brief as a kiss. "Especially him. Let the state hang him twice? Never."

"They won't find it. I'd see to it."

"They found Vasily's press in '07. They found everything, always. They have noses for other people's secrets." Marta drew the oilcloth back over the pistol with hands that were steadier than she expected. "You burn it, Ulyana. Promise me. Not save it. *Burn it.*"

For a moment Ulyana said nothing, and Marta knew her well enough now to know that the pause was the negotiation happening inside her—the driver's instinct to carry everything, versus the reader's knowledge that some cargo must be jettisoned.

"I promise," Ulyana said. "If it comes to that."

"*When* it comes to that. This household has been living on borrowed quiet for ten years. Quiet is a debt, and debts get called."

* * *

They repacked the cache together, which neither of them acknowledged as the ceremony it was. Marta lowered the bundles; Ulyana held the lamp low so the light wouldn't show through the gap to the garden. The stone went back into its bed with a sound like a book closing. Marta swept a handful of ash across the seam out of pure superstition, though the fit was perfect and always had been.

Then they sat again, and the brandy bottle stood between them with its neck open, and something had changed in the room's weather.

"My husband beat me for eight years," Marta said, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "Before Andrei got me out. Before I learned that a woman can be a country with borders." She turned her cup. "I've never told anyone in this house that."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I read it in your hands, years ago. The way you flinch when a door slams."

Marta almost laughed. "You're a horror, do you know that? A lovely, terrible horror."

"My father sold me to a caravan when I was six," Ulyana said, in the same level voice, and now it was Marta's turn to go still. "I don't remember his face. I remember the price. I've spent my life being the person who carries things because the last time someone carried me, they sold me." She refilled both cups, though Marta had not asked and did not need it. "So. Now we've traded. Now we're even."

"We're not even," Marta said softly. "Trading implies you got something."

"I got the floor," Ulyana said. "Knowing what's under it. In a house full of secrets, that's wealth."

They drank. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and the ice on the river groaned in its sleep—a long, deep complaint, the sound the river made every February, the sound of a season deciding whether to end. Marta had lived beside that sound for a decade and never tired of it. Tonight it seemed less like winter breaking and more like something else loosening, something further off.

"Lidiya asked me today where babies come from," Marta said, to change the weather. "Fifteen years old. I told her where babies come from and where revolutions come from, since apparently no one else would."

"What did you say revolutions come from?"

"Kitchens," Marta said. "Every one of them. History books talk about parliaments and barricades, but it's always some woman at a table, feeding people who are about to do something stupid and brave." She looked around her kingdom—the copper pots, the blackened range, the innocent-looking floor. "This room has fed more sedition than any printing house in the city. Don't tell the Inspector."

"I won't tell the Inspector," Ulyana agreed, and then, after a moment, in a different tone—one Marta had never heard from her, smaller, younger: "Marta. If they come for us. If it goes badly. You'd take Lidiya north, yes? Whatever happens to the rest of us?"

The question landed strangely, heavily, a stone dropped down a well whose bottom Marta could not hear. North. The word had been floating around the household for years like a moth—Patrik talking vaguely of a cousin's farm, of clean air, of starting over—but tonight, spoken in Ulyana's careful voice, it sounded less like a daydream and more like a timetable.

"Why north?" Marta asked. "Why tonight?"

Ulyana looked at the fire for a long moment. When she turned back, whatever had crossed her face had been folded away, neat as linen.

"No reason. Habit. A driver always knows the roads out." She stood, collected the bottle, and tucked it back under her shawl. At the door she paused. "Get a doctor. A real one, not the chemist on the corner who owes Patrik favors. Swallow your pride and pay him yourself."

"Yes, General."

"And Marta—" Ulyana hesitated, and for the second time that night Marta saw something unguarded move across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Thank you. For showing me. I know what it cost."

"It cost nothing," Marta lied. "Go to bed."

When the kitchen door had clicked shut, Marta sat alone with the lamp and the dying fire and the weight in her chest that was half brandy, half lung, half something else that didn't have a name yet. Three halves; she was drunker than she'd thought.

She thought of Andrei's last letter. *Don't let them make you small.* She had spent thirty years obeying him in secret—keeping the pamphlets, keeping the pistol, keeping the name alive under a floorboard like a seed under snow. And tonight, for the first time, she had shown someone the field.

Her chest tightened, and she coughed—deep, wet, honest. She pressed her handkerchief to her mouth out of long habit and, lowering it, looked at it longer than she wanted to. Just a shadow in the lamplight. Just the firelight playing tricks. She folded it small and put it in her apron pocket, and told herself she would see the doctor when the roads cleared, when there was time, when the household wasn't—

Above her, faint through the floorboards, she heard Ulyana's door close. And beneath the cough, beneath the brandy, beneath the warm ache of having finally been seen, a colder thought surfaced and would not sink again:

Someone had to survive this family. Someone had to carry them north when the ice broke. And Marta, pressing her hand flat against the place beneath her ribs where the trouble lived, understood suddenly that she was already doing arithmetic she had sworn never to do—counting herself out of a sum that hadn't been announced.

The river groaned again, far off in the dark. Eleven weeks, maybe fewer, until the ice broke.

She banked the fire, hung up her apron, and went to bed with the handkerchief still folded in her pocket, like a letter she hadn't decided whether to send.
