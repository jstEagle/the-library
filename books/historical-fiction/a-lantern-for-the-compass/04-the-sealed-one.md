## 4. The Sealed One

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the second post, riding on top of a grocer's bill and an invitation to a temperance lecture that Marta would mock and then attend anyway. Ulyana saw it before the postman's hand had fully left the tray—saw it the way she saw everything, in the half-second before the world arranged itself to be seen.

It was the wax.

Not red, which was fashionable; not brown, which was cheap; not the pale rose of the Marchmont cousins who sealed everything because sealing was what people of quality did with their idle hours. This wax was black, unadorned, pressed with a stamp so shallow it was almost not there—a circle within a circle, like a coin worn blind by handling. Official wax. Government wax. The kind that did not announce itself because it did not need to.

She stood in the front hall with the tray balanced on her forearm and let the other letters pass beneath her eyes without reading them. Grocer's bill: numbers, nothing. Temperance lecture: Mrs. Havel's handwriting, safe. And this one—this one addressed not to Patrik Marchmont by name but to *The Occupier*, which meant whoever held the house when it arrived, which meant her, because Ulyana Iversen opened everything first and had done for six years, and somewhere in some ministry someone had written *occupant* with the flat indifference of a man who had never wondered who actually occupied anything.

*The Occupier.* Not even a courtesy. A key turned in a lock they hadn't bothered to name.

She carried the tray into the morning room and set the others down where they always went, and then she stood at the window with the black-sealed letter in both hands, turning it over, feeling its weight—or rather its lack of it. Paper was so light. That was the obscenity of paper. A man had been hanged once, in Ulyana's girlhood, on eleven sheets of it, and the whole bundle would have weighed less than the bread she'd eaten that morning.

Outside, the gravel drive lay silvered with frost, and Lidiya was out there in her ridiculous borrowed coat, drawing something in the frost with a stick—Ulyana could see her breath, see the quick birdlike movements of her head—and Sef was wheeling the delivery crates toward the side door, and the house was doing what the house did every morning: pretending, beautifully, competently, that it was only a house.

Six years she had read their letters. Six years of steaming envelopes over the kitchen kettle while Marta kept lookout with a wooden spoon in her hand like a sentry armed against soup, of drying pages flat under the iron, of memorizing handwriting the way other women memorized hymns. She knew the Marchmont correspondence the way a riverbed knows the river. She knew which cousin wrote drunk and which wrote desperate. She knew the tone of every voice that entered this house on paper, and she had built, sheet by sheet, a wall around these people made entirely of her own attention.

No one had ever sealed a letter to her.

The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, and she put it down the way you put down something hot. But it sat there anyway, glowing. *The Occupier.* Even the state, which saw everything, did not see her. She was the hand that opened, not the name that received. She had made herself into a hinge, and hinges are noticed only when they fail.

The kettle was still warm from breakfast.

She told herself she would wait until evening, until the household scattered to its errands, until there was time and privacy and a defensible reason. She told herself this standing in the scullery with the letter already face-down on the bread board, the kettle coming back to the boil, steam gathering under the flap of the envelope like breath on glass.

Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook.

The wax resisted first—it was good wax, dense, the kind that meant the sender wanted the breaking witnessed—and then gave all at once, and the flap lifted soft and damp, and Ulyana slid out two pages of close, ruled text in a hand so neutral it might have been typed. No salutation. No signature. A reference number at the top like a scar.

She read it once fast, the way she read everything, taking the whole shape of it before the details. Then she read it again slowly, and the kitchen went very quiet around her, quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when a floorboard decides whether or not to creak.

*...in the matter of the explosion at the Nikolaevsky goods yard on the night of the 14th ultimo, resulting in the deaths of four persons...*

Four persons. She read past it. The words kept sliding; she pinned them down one at a time.

*...financial instruments traced through the account of P. Marchmont...*

*...correspondence recovered suggesting prior knowledge of the consignment...*

*...it is the determination of this office that sufficient grounds exist for formal inquiry...*

*...the bearer of this notice is requested to prepare the household for inspection within fourteen days...*

She set the pages down on the bread board, next to the flour dust and the knife marks, and looked at them for a long time.

Patrik Marchmont. Financier. Four dead men in a goods yard, four names she didn't know and would now know forever, because that was how guilt worked—it attached itself to the innocent with more tenacity than to the guilty, who at least had lies to hold onto. And the evidence—she read the third page again, the one listing what had been *traced*, what had been *recovered*, what had been *determined*—the evidence was tailored. She knew tailored clothing when she saw it. Every seam fit too well. Real evidence was like real correspondence: smudged, contradictory, arriving in hands that slanted when the writer was tired. This was ironed. This had been cut to measure by someone with a pattern and patience, and the phrase *prior knowledge of the consignment* was doing work that a dozen honest sentences could not have done, because it implied everything and proved nothing, which was exactly how such machines were built.

Someone had sewn this. Someone had sat in a warm room with a ruler and a grudge and stitched Patrik Marchmont into a bombing the way you stitch a name into a schoolboy's coat—so that whatever happened to it, everyone would know whose it was.

And they were giving her fourteen days.

Ulyana became aware, distantly, that she had picked up the carving knife. Not to use. Just holding it, the way a man holds a railing. She set it down and washed her hands, though they were clean, and dried them, and stood looking out the scullery window at the yard where the frost had burned off and Lidiya's drawing was already gone, dissolved to wet stone, whatever word the child had written erased by nothing more than sunlight.

That was the trick of it, she thought. That was the whole trick. You could write anything in frost.

* * *

She resealed the letter with the iron, pressing the flap flat and warming the wax stub over the flame until it could be persuaded back into something resembling wholeness. It wasn't perfect. Under a practiced eye the seal would show a hairline fault, a seam of reheated blackness—but a practiced eye was exactly what she had, and she judged it would pass anyone short of herself. She delivered it to Patrik's study at four o'clock, with the post, among the other letters, and watched him break it open with his thumb the way he broke open everything, casually, a man who had spent fifty years being handed things.

He read it twice. She was arranging papers on the shelf behind him and saw, in the window's reflection, his face do something small and terrible—the way ice does something small and terrible before it takes the whole weight of a cart. Then his face closed, and he said, "Thank you, Ulyana," in the voice he used for thanking servants, and she said, "Shall I bring tea?" in the voice she used for offering it, and the two of them performed the oldest duet in the world, master and servant, neither acknowledging that the stage between them was on fire.

But as she reached the door he said, quietly, without turning: "You opened this."

It was not a question. It was worse than a question. It was an accounting.

"Yes, sir," she said.

He was silent long enough that she heard the clock in the hall complete its quarter-hour, each note falling into the room like a coin into a well. "Did you—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "What do you think?"

No one had asked Ulyana Iversen what she thought in six years of reading other people's thoughts. She found that her answer was already waiting, fully formed, the way answers were when they had been living in you longer than the question.

"I think," she said, "that whoever wrote this has never seen a real letter in his life. Everything fits too neatly, sir. Real things don't."

Patrik turned then. In the last of the light his face looked older than it had at breakfast, and something in it—not fear, something adjacent to fear, fear's respectable cousin—was looking at her with new arithmetic, recalculating what stood in his study. Not a servant. Not a hinge. Something that read.

"Come back at nine," he said. "When the house is asleep. Bring nothing. Tell no one—not Marta, not the boy, not—" His jaw worked. "Not the child."

"No, sir," Ulyana said, and meant: *I have already decided this, I decided it in the scullery with a knife in my hand, I have been deciding it since the wax.* She went down to the kitchen, where Marta was kneading bread with the violence of a woman who had opinions about dough, and Lidiya was doing sums at the corner table with her tongue between her teeth, and Sef was cleaning type ink off his fingers with kerosene, and the family went on being a family around her, warm and loud and doomed and dear, and none of them knew yet that the frost had melted off a word written in it, and that the word was theirs.

Fourteen days. She began counting them the way she counted everything—silently, invisibly, carrying the number for all of them, as she carried everything, as she had always carried everything.

Nine o'clock was five hours away. For the first time in six years, Ulyana Iversen was afraid of what she would have to say when she opened her mouth.
