## 6. Lidiya Learns to Listen

The house had a grammar, and Lidiya Marchmont—Iversen now, though the paper trail was thin as communion wine—had begun to read it fluently.

It was in the way Marta salted the soup twice on bad days, once for flavor and once because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. It was in Sef's habit of whistling only when he wanted to be overheard being cheerful. It was in the particular quality of silence that came down through the floorboards from Patrik's study—not an empty silence but a loaded one, like a gun set down carefully on felt.

And it was in Ulyana Iversen's footsteps, which were not footsteps at all.

Lidiya had tested this theory for three weeks before she trusted it. She would stand at the top of the back stairs with her eyes closed and wait for the driver to pass below, and there was nothing—no creak of the third stair, no whisper of the loose board by the pantry door, nothing except the faint displacement of air and a smell like cold iron and lavender soap, and then Ulyana was simply *there*, already past, already carrying whatever she carried.

Nobody else in the house moved like that. Nobody else had learned to be a rumor of themselves.

Lidiya wanted to know how it was done.

* * *

She started with the dumbwaiter.

It was a relic from the previous owners, a wooden box on ropes that ran from the kitchen up through the walls to the old breakfast room nobody used anymore since Marta declared its stove possessed. The shaft was dark and smelled of forty years of butter and dust. If you climbed onto the kitchen counter, which you were never supposed to do, and pressed your ear to the little hatch, you could hear everything said at the servants' table, transmitted up the shaft with a clarity that felt almost holy.

On the fourth evening after the government circular arrived—the one Ulyana burned in the yard while telling everyone it was about drainage assessments, as if anyone believed that—Lidiya heard her name.

Not her name exactly. *The girl.*

"The girl should learn nothing," Marta was saying. "That's the whole point of her age. She gets to be innocent for another year or two."

"She stopped being innocent when we took her in," said Sef. He was fifteen himself, sixteen in spring, and he spoke about innocence the way he spoke about typefaces—with the authority of someone who handled them daily. "You can't raise a child on pamphlets and pretend she hasn't read them."

"We don't give her pamphlets."

"She reads them anyway. She reads everything. She's reading the labels on your preserves right now, I'd bet my apprenticeship."

Lidiya, cross-legged on the counter with her ear to the hatch, felt caught even though no one could see her. Then she felt something else, warmer: they knew. They talked about her the way they talked about things that mattered.

Then Ulyana spoke, and the warmth went complicated.

"Let her listen," Ulyana said.

There was a scrape—a chair pushed back—and then quiet, the specific quiet of two women deciding whether to fight.

"Ulyana." Marta's voice had dropped into the register she used for dough that wouldn't rise and men who needed scolding. "She is fifteen."

"I was nine," Ulyana said. "When I started listening. Under a dock in Riga, through a crack in the boards. Everyone who loves a child eventually has to decide whether the world will teach her gently or teach her anyway. We don't get to choose the second option going away. We only get to choose who does the teaching." A pause. "I'd rather she learned it from this table than from an inspector's notebook."

"And if listening gets her taken?" Marta again. "If one day they come with their lists and their questions and they find a child who knows too much—"

"Then knowing might be the thing that keeps her alive. Ignorance doesn't protect anyone, Marta. It just means they suffer without understanding why."

Silence again, longer. When Marta finally answered, her voice had gone soft in a way Lidiya had never heard from the kitchen. "You talk like someone who was left behind once."

"I talk like someone who was carried too long," said Ulyana. "It's different."

Lidiya sat very still on the counter until her legs went numb, memorizing it all, and then climbed down and walked into the kitchen as if she'd come for bread, and Marta looked at her with wet eyes and gave her a heel of rye with butter already melting into it, and nobody asked where she'd been, and that was the strangest kindness of all: they had decided, in her hearing, to let her hear.

* * *

The council of war convened properly on Sunday, when the house was fullest and therefore safest—Patrik's theory, delivered with the confidence of a man who had never been arrested, was that police raided houses that seemed empty and avoided houses that seemed lived-in. So while the front rooms filled with suffragists drinking tea and discussing petitions, the real meeting happened in the old breakfast room, and Lidiya discovered that the dumbwaiter had a rival.

The breakfast room shared a wall with the linen closet. Behind the shelves of sheeting, the plaster had gone soft near the baseboard—damp from some ancient leak—and sound came through it like through a curtain. You had to pull out the bottom shelf, wedge yourself between the linens and the wall, and sit on a folded quilt with your knees against your chest. It was uncomfortable and smelled of starch and camphor, and Lidiya loved it more than any room she'd ever been given.

She brought a pencil and a school copybook, the kind with a marbled cover, and began to write down what she heard.

Not everything. That was the first lesson she taught herself, within twenty minutes, because writing everything was impossible and also dangerous—if the book were found, every word would hang them. So she invented a shorthand. E for evidence. P for police. M for the man whose name appeared so often now, always in a lowered voice, always followed by a pause, as if the name itself had edges: the *financier*. The word *bomb* she wrote as a small drawing of a lantern, tipped over. She didn't know why she chose a lantern. It just seemed right—something meant to give light, knocked sideways, spilling fire.

Inside the wall, the adults were arguing about money, which surprised her. She had thought revolutions were about ideas. Apparently they were about bank drafts.

"Patrik can't move funds," said a voice she recognized as Mrs. Aldous from the Tuesday meetings, dry as chalk. "Any transfer in his name is a confession. Any transfer through the society's accounts brings the whole organization down with him."

"Through mine, then," said Sef. "I've got wages saved. Two hundred crowns, maybe two-fifty."

"That's not enough to buy a train ticket out of the country, boy, let alone—"

"It's enough to buy paper and ink. Enough to print his defense ourselves. Put it in every hand in this city. Let them read what's actually—" His voice cracked and steadied. "Let them *read*."

Lidiya wrote: *S — wants to print. 200 cr. FURIOUS.* And underlined *furious* three times, because she could hear the fury through the plaster, vibrating the shelf against her shoulder blade.

Then Ulyana's voice, low, and Lidiya leaned closer until her ear ached against the cold wall.

"If you print, you prove the raid justified. You hand them the press as evidence and the pamphlet as a confession and Sef as the criminal who signed both." A pause. "They are not looking for truth. They are looking for a shape that fits the charge. Don't build it for them."

"You say that like you know what they're looking for."

Another pause. Longer. Lidiya held her breath inside the linen smell.

"I've opened eleven hundred letters in four years," Ulyana said. "You learn to recognize a letter written to be intercepted. The ink too careful. The phrasing just slightly wrong for the person supposedly writing it. The circular that came last week—it was written by someone who knows this house. Not the layout. The *habits*. Someone who knows Marta shops on Thursdays and that Patrik drinks his coffee standing up and that I read the post before anyone sees it." Her voice did something Lidiya had no word for yet; later she would call it *flattening*, the way a road flattens before a drop. "Someone built our shapes and handed them to the police. The question isn't whether we're guilty. It's which of us they used to make us look guilty."

In the linen closet, Lidiya's pencil stopped.

She thought about that sentence for a long time afterward, lying in her narrow bed with the copybook under her pillow. Which of us they used. She turned it over the way you turn over a stone to see what lives underneath. The family had always presented itself to her as a fortress—all of them facing outward together. But Ulyana had just described a house with a door open somewhere, a door Lidiya had never seen, and the idea that the danger might wear a face she loved—that was the first truly adult thought she ever had, and she hated it, and she kept it.

She wrote it down in code: *One of us is a window.*

Then, beneath it, after a moment: *Find the window.*

* * *

The trouble with learning to listen, nobody warns you, is that you cannot stop.

Within two weeks Lidiya heard the house differently altogether. She heard Marta coughing in the pantry and pretending it was the flour sack. She heard Patrik rehearsing sentences alone in his study—actual rehearsal, saying the same denial six ways, *I was not there, I have never seen those accounts, you must believe me*—and each version slightly smaller than the last, a man sanding himself down. She heard Sef crying once, briefly, furiously, in the print room, the way boys cry when they think crying itself is another betrayal.

And she heard Ulyana, late one night, in the stable with the horses, speaking softly to them in a language Lidiya didn't know—Riga Russian or Latvian or just the private tongue of loneliness—and the tone of it undid her. There was no performance in it. No competence. Just a woman talking to animals because animals were the only listeners who couldn't repeat anything.

Lidiya stood in the dark outside the stable door for a long time, holding her copybook against her chest, and understood something she couldn't have explained to anyone: that being sharp was a kind of wound. That Ulyana's invisibility wasn't a skill. It was a scar shaped like a talent.

She went back to bed and added a rule to the front page of the notebook, in code, underlined:

*What you hear is not yours to use. Only to keep.*

She was not entirely sure she believed it. But rules were like fences—you put them up before you found out whether you were the sort of person who needed them.

* * *

Ulyana found the notebook on a Thursday, because of course Ulyana found everything.

It was an accident of laundry. Lidiya had hidden the copybook inside a pillowcase awaiting wash, reasoning that Marta never washed the pillowcases until they could stand up on their own, and had forgotten that the household's other great invisible force collected linens on Thursdays. Lidiya came upstairs from the schoolroom and found Ulyana in the corridor, standing very still, the marbled cover open in her hands.

The corridor got very long and very bright.

"I can explain," Lidiya said, which was the stupidest possible opening, and she heard it be stupid even as she said it.

"Can you," said Ulyana. Not unkindly. Her thumb rested on the page with the overturned lantern drawn on it. "This is good work."

Lidiya blinked. Of all the sentences in the language, that one had not been on any list.

"The substitution cipher is simple," Ulyana went on, "but the discipline isn't. You didn't write everything. You wrote what mattered and marked the difference." She turned a page. "*Furious,* three times underlined. That's not transcription, Lidiya. That's analysis." She closed the book and held it out, and Lidiya saw that her hands were not entirely steady. "Where did you learn that letters can be read for what they hide?"

"From watching you."

Something crossed Ulyana's face then—fast, weather-front quick, gone before it finished arriving. She looked out the corridor window at the bare lindens, and when she spoke her voice had the flattened quality again, the road-before-the-drop.

"When I was your age, I kept a book like that. Everything I overheard in the harborside houses where I scrubbed floors. Codes I made up. Names I wasn't supposed to know." She was quiet a moment. "A gendarme found it. I was twelve. He read it aloud to me, slowly, making me watch his finger move under each line, and then he asked me questions about every person in it, and I learned that the people in it suffered for my neat handwriting. Two families lost their places. One man lost more." She handed the copybook back. "So here is what I'll tell you, and it is not a punishment and it is not permission. Keep the book. Hide it better—under the third floorboard in your closet, the loose one, not the second; the second is where anyone trained would look. But understand what you're holding. Every page you write is a debt someone may have to pay. Write as if the person who pays might be Marta. Because someday it might be."

"Yes," Lidiya said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it.

Ulyana turned to go, then stopped. Without looking back she said, quietly, almost to the window: "I'm sorry. That we made you into this. We took a child in to save her, and we're teaching her to survive us instead." Then she was moving down the corridor, silent as ever, a rumor of herself, and Lidiya stood holding the notebook and feeling two things at once with terrible clarity: pride, hot and certain, that Ulyana Iversen thought her work was good—

and grief, cold underneath, like water rising in a hold, because she had finally heard an adult say out loud that becoming strong was something done *to* you, and that the doing could not be undone.

* * *

That night she hid the book under the third floorboard, as instructed, and lay awake listening to the house settle.

Around midnight she heard what she was not supposed to hear: voices in the old breakfast room again, though no meeting had been called. Sef's voice first, urgent and low, and Ulyana answering, and the words came through the floor in fragments—*press*, *tonight*, *before he does something we can't undo*—and then Patrik's voice rising, wounded and grand, and Marta's cutting across it, and the whole house seemed to lean toward the breakfast room like a ship listing.

Lidiya slid out of bed, lifted the floorboard, and took out her copybook.

She sharpened her pencil with her thumbnail, opened to a fresh page, and drew at the top, in careful strokes, a lantern on its side.

Then she pressed her ear to the boards, and began to write.
