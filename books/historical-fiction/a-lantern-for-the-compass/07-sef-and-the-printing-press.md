## 7. Sef and the Printing Press

The press lived in the cellar the way a heart lives in a chest: hidden, essential, and taken entirely for granted until it stopped.

Sef had found it three years ago, abandoned behind the print shop where he'd apprenticed, when old Herr Brandt died and his sons sold everything they could carry and left the rest to rot. It was a Chandler & Price, cast iron, older than Sef's father would have been if Sef's father had stayed anywhere longer than a season. He'd bought it for the price of its weight in scrap, then spent four months bribing, begging, and—once, memorably—flirting with a carter named Doss to move it eleven blocks in the dead of night, wrapped in sailcloth, disguised as a boiler.

He was seventeen when he'd done it. He was twenty now, and he still thought of that night as the truest thing he had ever done. Everything since—the apprenticeship, the pamphlets, the small run of handbills for the Marchmont circle, the careful work of setting type by lamplight while Marta pretended not to hear the clatter through the floorboards—all of it had grown from that one act of theft disguised as salvage. You looked at something the world had thrown away and you said: *this is not finished.* That was the whole of it. That was printing. That was, as far as Sef could tell, the whole of living.

And now they were going to let Patrik Marchmont hang for something he hadn't done, because folding laundry quietly felt safer than telling the truth loudly.

He heard them upstairs—the low murmur of the council of war that had been running, on and off, for two days now. Ulyana's voice he couldn't catch; she spoke like snow falling, evenly and without emphasis. Marta's he caught in fragments, always practical: *the ice won't break for weeks yet, there's time, there's time.* And Patrik's voice came through the floorboards like weather, sonorous and helpless, a man reading his own eulogy and objecting only to the punctuation.

They were talking about the berth. Sef knew about the berth—Ulyana had told him, because Ulyana believed that everyone who might be affected by a decision deserved to know a decision existed, even if she never asked them what it should be. One berth. One name. The last steamer north before the river closed its throat for winter, whenever that would be. Eleven days, Ulyana had said. Maybe fewer.

One berth, and five people who mattered, and Sef lying awake at night doing arithmetic that always ended the same way: with him being the remainder. Not because anyone disliked him—he didn't think anyone disliked him—but because he was the newest. Because Lidiya was fifteen and Marta was dying and Patrik was the whole reason any of this was happening and Ulyana was Ulyana, and what did that leave? It left Sef, twenty years old, strong back, no surname worth the paper it wasn't written on. A remainder. He knew how remainders were treated. They were rounded down.

Well. If they wouldn't fight for all of them, they could fight for one of them, and Sef knew which fight he was built for.

* * *

He waited until mid-afternoon, when the house emptied itself the way it did every day—Patrik walking his doomed constitutional around the square like a man visiting his own grave, Marta napping in the chair by the stove with her knitting unfinished in her lap, Lidiya at her lessons, Ulyana out with the motorcar doing whatever invisible errands invisible people did. Then he went down to the cellar and woke the press.

Not to print. Not yet. Just to touch it. He oiled the rails he'd already oiled that morning. He squared the type case, though it was already square. He read over the forme he'd been composing in secret for two days, locked in the chase, covered with a sheet, sitting in the corner like an unexploded shell.

It was good work. That was the terrible part. It was the best work he'd ever done.

*THE CASE OF PATRIK MARCHMONT: WHAT THE NEWSPAPERS WILL NOT PRINT*, the headline ran, and beneath it, in eight-point body type set so tight it sang, the whole shape of the lie: the timeline that didn't hold, the witness whose testimony changed twice, the sum of money that appeared in the accusation from nowhere, traceable to nothing. Sef had gotten most of it from listening at doors—which was Lidiya's crime, not his, but the household's crimes were apparently contagious—and the rest from Ulyana, who had given him three facts in her flat snowfall voice and no opinion whatsoever, which he had correctly understood as permission.

Two thousand copies. He'd calculated the paper stock twice. The press could do it in a night and a half if he pushed it, and he intended to push it. Two thousand copies of the truth, dropped at the courthouse steps, the newspaper offices, the market square, slipped into every pocket and under every door in the quarter, so that when they came for Patrik Marchmont they would have to come for him in front of the whole city, in daylight, with the lie already torn open and fluttering on every lamppost like a caught bird.

That was the plan. That was the whole plan, and he loved it the way you love anything you've built with your own hands: completely, and without once asking whether it loved you back.

The trouble with the plan arrived at half past four, wearing an apron, carrying a lamp.

* * *

"You've been down here three hours," Marta said. She stood at the bottom of the stairs with the lamp held high, and the light made caves of her face—the hollows under her cheekbones deeper than they'd been in autumn, the cough she thought she hid audible now even from across a room. "Dinner's at seven. You'll eat standing up and burn your mouth like an animal, I suppose."

"Probably."

She came closer. She was the only person in the house who came down here without flinching at the smell of ink and solvent; she'd grown up above a print shop herself, in another country, in another life, before her brother—Sef knew about the brother the way everyone in the house knew about the brother, which was to say: that there had been one, and that there wasn't anymore, and that the knowing was carried carefully and never set down.

Her eyes went to the covered forme in the corner. They stayed there.

"And what's under the cloth, printer?"

"Nothing."

"Mm." She set the lamp on the shelf beside the type cases, and the light swung, and the shadow of the covered forme moved on the wall like something breathing. "In my experience," Marta said, "nothing under a cloth weighs forty pounds."

Sef could have lied better. He'd had three years of practice at keeping secrets in this house. But he was tired—two nights of bad sleep and a day of arithmetic that kept ending with himself subtracted—and Marta was looking at him the way she looked at Lidiya when the girl lied about having eaten, which was to say: without anger, and without mercy either.

"It's a defense of Patrik," he said. "Eight pages. Two thousand copies. I drop them the night before the hearing—courthouse, papers, market, everything. By morning the whole city knows the evidence is manufactured, and they can't try him in the dark anymore."

Marta was quiet for a long moment. In the quiet, the house settled around them, beams ticking as the day's warmth left them.

"And then?" she said.

"Then what?"

"Then the city knows. And then the police know that someone in this house can set type fast enough and brave enough to do this. And then they come here with a warrant that writes itself, and they find—" she nodded at the press, the type cases, the reams of paper stacked against the wall like pale loaves, "—all of this. Every pamphlet we've ever run. Every name in every distribution list. Half the radical circles in this city, delivered to Inspector Rell in a single crate, tied with string." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Marta; she had been angry so long and so often that she'd worn the anger smooth, like river stone. "You want to save one man by drowning everyone he loves. That's not a rescue, Sef. That's arithmetic done backwards."

"They're going to come anyway!" It came out louder than he meant, and he was glad, suddenly, that it had; he'd been holding it at a civilized volume for two days and it had been crushing him. "You think the circular was politeness? You think Ulyana burning letters was tidiness? They're coming. The only question is whether we meet them as people who told the truth or people who hid. And hiding—" he heard his own voice crack and hated it and kept going, "hiding hasn't saved your brother, has it. Hiding hasn't saved anyone. It just decides who dies politely."

Marta flinched. It was small—a tightening around the eyes, a half-step back—and he saw it land and wished, immediately and uselessly, that he could take it back. But he also didn't take it back. Some things, once set, you printed.

"You're young," Marta said finally.

"That's not an argument."

"No. It's a diagnosis." She picked up the lamp. At the stairs she paused, and he watched her decide something, watch her weigh him the way she weighed everything—against the survival of the house. "Say it to the family," she said. "Tonight. Say it to all of us, not just to an old woman in a cellar. Let's see if it survives daylight."

* * *

The kitchen after dinner smelled of stewed apples and lamp oil, and it held all of them, which almost never happened: Patrik in the good chair that nobody else ever sat in, Marta at the table with her hands folded, Lidiya cross-legged on the floor with a book she was absolutely not reading, and Ulyana by the door, coat still on, arrived back from wherever she'd been at exactly the moment conversations like this required a witness who wouldn't speak.

Sef stood. His legs felt strange, too tall for the room.

"There's a press in the cellar," he said. Which was absurd, because they all knew; it was the worst-kept machine in the city. But you began somewhere, and he began where the truth began. "I've been setting a defense of Patrik for two days. Eight pages. What the newspapers won't print—the timeline, the witness, the money that doesn't exist. Two thousand copies, the night before the hearing. Courthouse steps, the dailies, the market. Make them try him in public."

Patrik's face did something complicated—the pride arriving first, Sef saw, the flush of a man who had spent his life being defended by lawyers and had never once been defended by a boy with ink on his knuckles—and then the fear arriving after it, slower, heavier, like luggage.

"Two thousand copies," Patrik repeated.

"In one night. Maybe two."

"With our press. From our cellar. Under our roof." Patrik looked around the kitchen as if seeing it for the first time—as if seeing the walls' actual thickness, the actual distance to the street. "My boy, do you understand what that press is? The moment that pamphlet exists, that press stops being a hobbyist's toy and becomes evidence. It becomes the entire justification for anything they want to do to us. They won't need to manufacture anything anymore. We'll have manufactured it for them, beautifully, in eight-point type." He rubbed his face. "They are building a case that says this household is a cell of conspirators. And your answer is to become one."

"To become one *that tells the truth,*" Sef said.

"The truth is not a defense!" Patrik's voice cracked upward, and in the crack Sef heard, for the first time, the man underneath the patriarch—not the patron, not the name on the letterhead, but a sixty-year-old man who had watched his own name turn into an accusation and discovered that money purchased silence but not innocence. "Don't you understand? I've spent two days learning this. Truth is what you have *after* you survive long enough to say it. Right now we have one berth and a winter and a police inspector who has already decided—or has already been decided for. You want to hand them a torch and call it a lantern."

"Then what's your answer?" Sef turned, sweeping the room. "Run? All right. Who runs? There's one berth. One. So we sit here and do arithmetic about which of us gets to live, and we call it prudence, and we wait for the door to come down. Or we fold—we hand over everything, cooperate fully, trust in the process, trust in courts that hanged Marta's brother on thinner paper than what they've got on you." He saw Marta go still at the brother, saw Lidiya's head come up off the book, and pressed on because stopping now was death. "Fight, flee, or fold. Those are the choices. I'm the only one who's named one."

"Fighting isn't printing," Marta said quietly. "Fighting is surviving. Ask me how I know."

"I'm not asking you to fight. I'm asking you to *say something.* Out loud. In public. Once." He looked at Ulyana, last of all, because he'd saved her for last the way you save the judge. "You read every letter that comes into this house. You know better than any of us how much of this city runs on things that were never supposed to see light. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me the truth stays safe in cellars."

Ulyana met his eyes. She had the grayest eyes Sef had ever seen, the color of the river in November, and she used them the way she used everything—sparingly, precisely, without waste.

"I don't tell people they're wrong," she said. "I tell them what I've carried."

"Then tell us that."

She was quiet long enough that the clock on the mantel got loud. When she spoke, her voice was level as ever, but slower, each word set down like type into a forme.

"I've carried letters that ruined marriages and letters that saved lives, and I could not always tell which was which until years later. I've carried a warrant that looked, from the outside, exactly like a love letter." A pause. "What you've composed downstairs is the truest document in this house. And it is also the match. Both of those things are true, and I will not pretend one of them away to make the night easier. That's my answer."

"That's not an answer," Sef said.

"It's the only kind I have."

Lidiya, from the floor, said nothing at all. But Sef caught her looking at him—not with the hero-worship he'd have expected, or the fear, but with something more unsettling: recognition. She was fifteen, and she was taking notes behind her eyes, and what she was noting, he understood suddenly, was *this is what it costs.*

Patrik stood. He crossed to Sef and put a hand on his shoulder, heavy and warm, and for a moment Sef let himself lean into it the way he had never leaned on anyone.

"If we do this," Patrik said, "we do it knowing what it purchases. It purchases a raid. It purchases everything Rell's warrant wishes existed, gift-wrapped. I won't pretend otherwise, and neither will you." His grip tightened. "But I will also not pretend, boy, that some part of me hasn't wanted, for two days, to shout. So. Leave it with me tonight."

"There's no time to leave anything—"

"There is always time to be deliberate. That is the entire difference between us and them." He released Sef's shoulder. "Tomorrow I'll give you my answer."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He went down to the cellar with a candle and uncovered the forme and read his own words by its small light, checking for errors he'd already checked for, because hands needed work or they shook.

Near midnight, the stairs creaked. Ulyana stood at the bottom, coat still buttoned—she seemed to live in that coat, a woman permanently halfway out a door—and she looked at the forme a long moment before speaking.

"Patrik will say no," she said.

"I know."

"But he'll say it tomorrow, and tomorrow there may be a reason it doesn't matter anymore. Things are moving faster than this house moves." She came forward and, to his astonishment, lifted the edge of the covering sheet, reading the first column upside down the way printers' families learn to. "If it comes to a choice between waiting for permission and losing the night," she said, still reading, "I would think about which regret you can carry. You strike me as someone who intends to carry things a long while."

She straightened the sheet, turned, and went back up the stairs. At the top she paused.

"One more thing. The clerk at the district court—Vaschenko. He owes me. Tomorrow, if you need to know which way the wind sits in the prosecutor's office, ask me, not the newspapers." A beat, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "Everyone argues for someone else in this house. Remember, when it's your turn, that you're allowed to argue for yourself."

Then she was gone, and Sef sat alone with his candle and his locked-up truth, and outside, far off toward the river, he heard—for the first time that year, faint as a bone shifting under skin—the ice begin to groan.
