## 8. Inspector Rell's Doubt

The file weighed less than a confession should.

Inspector Aldous Rell sat in his office at the Fourth Precinct with the Marchmont case spread across his desk like a hand of cards he suspected someone had stacked, and he did what he always did with evidence that arrived too conveniently: he read it twice, then a third time, looking for the seam.

Outside his window, November was settling over the city like wet wool. A constable coughed in the corridor. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing in the holding cell, badly, a music-hall tune about a girl who waited by the harbor. Rell had heard it four nights running. He found he preferred it to the silence of the file.

The file was too good. That was the trouble. Twenty-two years in the Service had taught him that real cases were ragged things—contradictory statements, witnesses who recanted, physical evidence that pointed three directions at once and lied about all of them. Real cases had loose threads you could pull until your fingers bled. This one lay flat and smooth as a pressed flower, every petal accounted for.

He laid out the photographs again. The bank draft, dated the ninth of October, drawn on an account traceable—through two intermediaries who had since emigrated—to Patrik Marchmont's personal funds. The fragment of receipt recovered from the rubble on Corvessy Street, where the bomb had gone off at half past eleven in the evening and taken four people with it: a tobacconist, his wife, a clerk walking home, a boy no older than fourteen whose name the papers couldn't even agree on. The testimony of a porter who swore he'd seen "a gentleman of quality" speaking with the known anarchist Voss outside the warehouse district nine days before the blast.

Each piece fit the next. That was what bothered him. In twenty-two years he had never once seen a case assemble itself this cleanly unless someone had assembled it.

Rell took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. He was fifty-three years old, a widower, a man who ate his supper at the same chophouse every Thursday and tipped the same waitress the same sixpence, and he had risen as far as he intended to rise precisely because he had never been ambitious enough to be dangerous. He solved cases. He closed files. He told himself—and mostly believed—that the truth and the verdict usually shook hands somewhere along the way, and that when they didn't, it was tragedy rather than design.

He looked at the bank draft again.

The date was wrong. Not visibly wrong—the ninth of October was a plausible date, a Tuesday, nothing about it rang false—but wrong in the way a forged painting is wrong, in the weight of it. Marchmont's household accounts, which Rell had subpoenaed quietly through the bank, showed a man who paid everything by draft and note, dozens of transactions a month, meticulous, almost fussy. And here was a single draft for eight hundred marks, drawn through intermediaries, unrecorded in any ledger Rell could find, for a sum that would have bought a great deal of pamphlets and powder.

A man that careful about his grocery accounts did not finance a bombing with an orphaned piece of paper.

He set the draft down and looked out the window at the rain beginning to stitch the street. He thought about who benefited.

That was the question he taught his juniors to ask first, before motive, before opportunity, before anything: *who benefits?* And when he asked it now, honestly, the answer did not point toward Marchmont. It pointed inward. It pointed down the corridor, past the constable's desk, up two floors to the office of Deputy Commissioner Harl, whose Special Political Section had been hungry for a headline conviction since the summer, since the Corvessy Street bomb made anarchists the terror of every breakfast table in the city, since Harl had stood at some function or other and said within Rell's hearing that *the public forgives us our failures but not our patience.*

Harl wanted a name in the dock by spring. Harl had a rival faction's favorite patron to burn—a suffragist house, a radical printer, a rich man who wrote letters to newspapers about the rights of the accused—and Harl had, Rell would have wagered his pension, a forger on salary who could produce a bank draft so clean it would make a bishop weep.

None of which Rell could prove. None of which, if he said it aloud in the wrong room, would leave him with anything but a smaller office and a smaller pension and a reputation for being difficult.

*The public forgives us our failures but not our patience.* He turned the phrase over. It was true, was the ugly thing. He had watched juries, he had watched newspapers, he had watched the faces of the bereaved outside coroner's courts, and they did not want patience. They wanted a man in irons and a sentence read aloud, and if the man was innocent then the truth was a luxury item, something for historians, something for men who could afford to wait fifty years.

Rell was not sure he could afford to wait fifty years. He was fairly sure he could not afford anything else.

* * *

He went down to the records room in the afternoon, ostensibly to check a warrant form, actually to pull the Corvessy Street physical evidence log under his own authority, which required him to sign for it and therefore to put his name on its trail like a man dipping his hand in ink.

The evidence room smelled of coal smoke and camphor. Sergeant Ives, who ran it, was a stooped man with a deaf ear and total recall, and Rell had learned long ago that Ives remembered everything that came through his hands and volunteered nothing.

"The Marchmont material," Rell said. "Corvessy Street follow-up. Who logged the receipt fragment?"

Ives consulted nothing. "Two officers. Pell and Crowther."

"Political Section?"

"Seconded." Ives said the word the way other men said *borrowed*, with the faint suggestion that the thing borrowed might not be returned. "They came the morning after. Before the scene team finished."

"Before?"

"Before." Ives wiped his hands on a rag that was more rag than towel. "Scene team wasn't done sifting. They walked the grid anyway, said they'd been sent direct. Logged the fragment themselves, bagged it themselves, carried it themselves." He paused, and his good ear tilted slightly toward Rell, though his eyes stayed on his inventory. "Bag was new. Seals were fresh. Paper inside was dry, mind, and it had rained that night like the sky meant it."

Dry paper. From a bombed building. Recovered from rubble doused by fire hoses and rain. Rell felt the cold settle into him, the particular cold of a man confirming something he had hoped not to confirm.

"Anyone else handle it?"

"Pell. Crowther. Whoever sealed the evidence tin upstairs." Ives finally looked at him, and there was something in the old sergeant's face—not warning exactly, but the flat compassion of a man who has seen inspectors walk into this particular weather before. "You signing out a copy of the log, sir?"

"I'm signing out the log."

Ives produced it without hurrying. Rell signed. His name went into the book in his small, upright hand, and he understood that he had just written himself into the case whether he liked it or not.

* * *

That evening he did something he had not done in years. He took the file home.

His flat above the chophouse was two rooms and a kettle, kept the way he kept himself: clean, spare, and faintly sad. He cleared the supper things from the table, laid the photographs in order, and worked until midnight with a magnifying glass and a lamp, the way he had worked as a young man before the work had learned to disappoint him.

It held. Of course it held. But holding was not the same as being true.

He examined the porter's statement next. The porter, a man named Gudik, swore he had seen Marchmont speaking with Voss on the night of the twenty-eighth of September, outside the warehouse district, at distance, at dusk, in fog. Rell pulled the weather tables. Fog, yes—heavy fog, the kind that swallowed a man at ten paces. And the description in the statement—"a gentleman of quality, grey coat, silver-topped cane"—matched, word for word, the description of Marchmont that had appeared in the *Evening Standard*'s society column three weeks earlier, down to the cane. Men did not remember canes through fog. Newspapermen did.

Someone had written the witness. Someone had handed Gudik his memory the way you hand a beggar a coin, and Gudik, who had a sister in a charitable ward and debts Rell could probably have traced to a bookmaker with Political Section connections, had repeated it faithfully and sworn to it solemnly and would very likely die believing it, because that was how manufactured memory worked. It became true to the man carrying it.

Rell sat back. The lamp hissed. Down in the street a cab horse clopped past through the wet.

He thought: *I could write all this down tonight. The dry paper. The fog and the cane. The seconded officers walking the grid before the sifters finished. I could put it in a report, sign it, and put it on Harl's desk, and by Friday I would be transferred to some coastal precinct counting smuggled brandy, and the report would be in a drawer, and Marchmont would hang anyway, because Harl's faction holds the Home Office contacts and I hold a magnifying glass.*

And then he thought: *or I could say nothing, close the file, and let the machinery do what machinery does, and sleep at night the way I've slept these twenty years—badly, but with my pension intact.*

There was a third door and he knew it. There is always a third door. You keep the doubt alive. You slow the machine down without standing in front of it. You lose papers, misfile things, ask questions in rooms where the questions will travel. You become the grain of sand, not the wall. It is coward's justice, but it is justice, and it keeps the man dispensing it employed.

Rell poured himself the last inch of the bottle he kept for occasions, drank it standing at the window, and admitted the truth he had been circling all evening: he wanted the truth, yes. He had wanted it all his life; it was the only romance left in him. But he wanted his pension more, and his quiet Thursdays, and the small warm certainty that he was a decent man in a foul trade doing what decent men in foul trades could manage.

Truth, but not at the price of burning. That was the arithmetic. He hated himself mildly for it, the way you hate a limp, familiarly, without hope of cure.

* * *

On Monday he took a step that was almost bold and carefully not quite. He requested, through channels, a routine verification of the intermediary accounts behind the bank draft—an audit query, the sort of thing that crossed forty desks and implicated no one. If the intermediaries were real men, the audit would find them and cost him nothing. If they were paper ghosts, the audit would come back thin, and thin was a word he could use later, in a room where it mattered, when it mattered—if it ever mattered.

By Wednesday the answer came back, and it came back wrong in the way he'd expected: the first intermediary existed, a shipping agent in the river quarter, deceased eleven months. The second did not exist at all. The account had been opened with a signature and a deposit and had sat dormant for a year like a gun in a drawer, waiting.

Waiting. That was the word that finished it for him. Evidence accumulated; traps were baited in advance. This account had been opened in the spring, months before the bomb, months before anyone could have known Marchmont would need framing—which meant either prophecy or preparation, and Rell had stopped believing in prophecy around the same time he'd stopped believing in the benevolence of superiors.

He locked the audit slip in his personal drawer, underneath the warrant forms where no clerk would look, and sat for a long moment with his hand on the drawer's cold brass handle.

He thought about Marchmont, whom he'd met once at some civic dinner, years ago—a big, warm, self-satisfied man, the kind who believed the world was a club that had elected him to membership. He thought about the household attached to him: the cook, the printer's boy, the driver everyone mentioned and no one described. He thought about the dead on Corvessy Street, the tobacconist and his wife and the clerk and the boy, who deserved a murderer caught far more than they deserved a convenient one, and who would get neither.

And he thought about Harl, two floors up, polishing a conviction the way a man polishes a boot, and about the spring assizes, and about the clock that had already started and would not stop for an inspector's conscience.

Rell opened the drawer once more, took out the audit slip, and did the only brave thing his arithmetic allowed: he copied it, hid the copy somewhere no raid would think to look—in the lining of his late wife's hatbox, which he had kept for eleven years for reasons he never examined—and returned the original to the file, unsigned, unremarked, a seed in frozen ground.

Then he wrote in his day-book, in the small upright hand, beneath the day's routine entries, a line that would mean nothing to any superior who skimmed it and everything to a man who knew how to read him:

*Marchmont matter — evidence review ongoing. Recommend delay of warrant application pending account verification. Verification may take some time.*

Some time. He underlined it twice.

It was not much. It was a grain of sand in the gears, a few weeks bought, perhaps a month. But as he blotted the page and buttoned his coat against the November damp, Inspector Aldous Rell told himself the story decent men in foul trades tell themselves: that the machine could be slowed, that slowness was mercy, that a man who could not stop the train might still throw a switch.

He did not yet know that within the fortnight the machine would send him to the Marchmont house itself—with a warrant, a list, and orders he had not written—to search, personally, the home of the man he believed innocent.
