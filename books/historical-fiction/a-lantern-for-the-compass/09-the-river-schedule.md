## 9. The River Schedule

The notice came folded inside the *Kronstadt Shipping Gazette*, which was itself folded inside a parcel of printer's ink Sef had ordered from a supplier in Reval. Three layers of innocence around one sentence of truth. Ulyana read it standing at the kitchen table with Marta's bread dough rising under a cloth beside her, and she read it three times before her hands stopped shaking enough to fold it closed.

*Ice expected to break on or about the 14th. Steamer *Varyag*, last northbound passage of the season, single berth held under reservation "A. Volkov." Present ticket at pier office by dawn of departure. No extension possible.*

Eleven days.

She put the notice face-down on the table, then picked it up again and put it in her apron pocket, then took it out and burned it over the stove flame, watching the ink curl to black flakes that Marta would later find in the butter if she wasn't careful. The words were already in her head. They would stay there. Everything stayed there; that was the whole architecture of Ulyana Iversen, a woman built like a strongbox with no key but her own memory.

"A. Volkov," she said aloud, testing it. The name tasted like a coat that had hung in a closet for six years—still good wool, still smelling faintly of the person who'd worn it last.

Marta looked up from the dough. "You've gone the color of the flour."

"Nothing."

"You're a poor liar for someone whose entire life is lying." Marta wiped her hands and crossed the kitchen, lowering her voice as though the walls had ears now instead of cracks. "Out with it."

Ulyana weighed it. There were things she told Marta and things she told Patrik and things she told no one, and the categories shifted daily like ice floes. But this—this belonged to all of them, and to none of them yet.

"There's a boat," she said. "North. When the river opens. Eleven days."

Marta's hands went still on the table. Cook's hands, scarred and broad, hands that had fed this household through two winters of scarcity and one summer of surveillance. "One boat."

"One berth."

* * *

The berth had been bought in 1907, in the bad spring after the executions, when everyone who could still think clearly understood that the Marchmont circle would someday need a door out and that doors out required money paid in advance to people who asked no questions. Patrik had arranged it himself, through a shipping agent in Reval with a taste for cognac and discretion. One berth, renewable annually, under a name that belonged to no living person: A. Volkov. A ghost's ticket, kept warm by yearly payments, waiting for a body.

For six years the arrangement had been a comfort simply by existing—the way a fire escape is a comfort even in buildings that never burn. No one had ever been meant to use it. It was insurance against the impossible, and the impossible had always politely declined to arrive.

Now the impossible had a schedule.

Ulyana walked the arithmetic through the house like a woman carrying water across a frozen yard, carefully, knowing every step might crack. She did it in the margins of her day: while polishing the motorcar that hadn't been driven openly in months, while sorting the mail into safe and safer and burn, while listening at the edges of rooms the way she always listened, gathering what people said when they thought they were alone.

Patrik said, in his study, to his own reflection more than to her: "If it comes to flight, obviously it's me. I'm the one they want. If I go, the pressure lifts from the rest of you." He said it with the brisk nobility of a man donating to charity, and Ulyana heard underneath it the other thing he didn't say, which was that he could not bear to be the reason anyone else suffered, and also could not bear to leave, and also believed—truly believed, because wealth is its own kind of naivety—that a man of his standing could talk his way back across any border.

Marta said, in the pantry, to the shelves: "I'm not going anywhere. My lungs are shot, my papers are worse, and if they stop that boat at the first lock I'll hang and take whoever's beside me down too." Then, softer, to Ulyana directly: "But Lidiya. Lidiya has to be on that boat if anyone is."

Sef said, in the cellar among the type cases, furiously, to the press: "Why should anyone go? We should stand. Running is an admission." He said it the way young men say everything, at full volume, and then later, quietly, washing ink from his hands at the scullery sink: "Unless it should be me. I'm the one with the record. Two arrests already. If they raid again, they'll deport me for certain, and my mother—" He stopped. His mother was in Helsingfors and had not answered a letter in a year. "Someone should go," he finished, which was not the same thing as volunteering, and both of them knew it.

And Lidiya said nothing, because no one had told her there was anything to say, which was itself a kind of arithmetic error the whole household kept making: they counted her as a child when counting her as a cost.

* * *

On the fourth evening after the notice, Ulyana sat in the cold conservatory with a ledger she kept for exactly this purpose—not money, but weights. She had learned bookkeeping from a smuggler on the Gulf coast when she was nine years old, and the man's first lesson had never left her: *everything weighs something; your job is to know what.*

She wrote the names and set their weights against them.

**PATRIK.** Weight of guilt: heavy, but guilt travels well. Weight of usefulness abroad: considerable—a man with his connections could raise funds, could advocate, could keep the family's cause alive from exile. Weight of danger here: highest. He was the named man. But—and she pressed her pencil hard on this—he was also the most visible man in the province. Put him on a steamer and half the pier would recognize him before the gangplank rose. His escape might fail, and a failed escape would confirm everything.

**MARTA.** Weight of illness: fatal within two years, the coughing said so even if Marta wouldn't. Weight of papers: none valid. Weight of what she carried: the floorboards, the pistol, her brother's letters—she would never board that boat without emptying the kitchen first, and emptying the kitchen was its own execution. And weight of will: Marta would refuse. Marta would refuse with such iron courtesy that refusing her would feel like striking a nun.

**SEF.** Weight of youth: enormous, twenty-two years of it, the whole argument in favor. Weight of recklessness: nearly equal. He would reach the north, find the printers' circles of Helsingfors, and have himself arrested within a season for printing something glorious and stupid. Still—young men survive their own stupidity more often than old women survive their lungs.

**LIDIYA.** Weight of age: fifteen, which was either the strongest argument for or the strongest against, depending on how you counted. A child alone on a long journey. But a child with a quick mind and a coded notebook and nerves like piano wire, a child the household had accidentally forged into exactly the kind of person who survives. Weight of what she'd be leaving: everything, and everyone. Weight of what she'd be carrying: the family's whole future, compressed into one small body like a seed.

Ulyana looked at the four names and understood that the problem was not arithmetic at all. Arithmetic resolved. This equation had no solution—only choices, and every choice was a wound dressed up as a decision.

Because here was the thing she had not written in the ledger, the thing pressing against the inside of her ribs like a fifth entry: there was a fifth weight in the house, and it was hers. Ulyana Iversen, thirty-four years old, healthy, papered under a name that was almost real, trained in engines and codes and border towns, a woman whom no inspector had ever bothered to learn the face of. By every measure in the ledger she was the best candidate. She was the ideal passenger. And she had not written her own name down because writing it down felt like theft—like reaching into the family's purse and taking the coin marked *survival* when she had only ever been the hand that carried the purse.

Her whole life she had earned her place by carrying things. Letters, secrets, contraband, other people's griefs. The carrying was the worth. To carry nothing—to be carried, to sit in a berth bought with someone else's foresight and let the river do the work—that was not humility. That was annihilation. She had been a stowaway once, at eight years old, curled in a fish crate while her mother stood on the dock and grew smaller. Being carried meant being disposable. Being carried meant someone had decided the space was worth more than you.

She closed the ledger.

* * *

The river announced itself that night.

Ulyana heard it from her room above the garage—a sound she had known since childhood on the gulf, the deep tectonic groan of ice beginning to think about letting go. Not cracking yet. Conversing. The river talking to itself in its sleep, saying *soon, soon,* the way it said it every year, and every year some people made it north and some people missed the boat and some people stood on the bank and watched the last smoke disappear and understood they had chosen wrong.

Eleven days became ten. Ten became nine.

On the ninth morning she found Lidiya in the conservatory, sitting where Ulyana herself had sat, with a notebook open on her knee. Ulyana saw the cover—plain, brown, unremarkable—but she saw also the way the girl snapped it shut, the way her eyes went to the door first and Ulyya second, cataloguing threat before greeting.

"What are you writing?" Ulyana asked.

"Nothing."

"You're a poor liar for someone whose entire life is going to be lying." She sat down across from her. Outside, the garden was mud and last year's stalks, and beyond the wall the river ran gray and grinding. "Lidiya. If I told you there was a boat. One seat. When the ice breaks. What would you say?"

Lidiya's chin came up the way it did when the adults were being indirect and she resented it. "Who's it for?"

"That's the question no one can answer."

"Patrik says it's him. Marta says it's me—I heard them, don't look surprised, I hear everything. Sef says nobody should go." She recited it flatly, a girl reading her own eavesdropping back as evidence. Then, quieter, with a crack in the flatness: "Nobody asked me what I want."

"What do you want?"

"I want to stay," Lidiya said, and her voice broke on it, fifteen years old for just one second before the steel came back. "And I know that's stupid. Everyone treats wanting to stay like it's childish. Like staying is what happens to people who lose. But this house is—" She stopped, jaw working. "You'd know. You've been here longest."

Ulyana did know. The house was the first place she had ever been that pretended she belonged, even in pretense, even as a servant, even invisibly—it was the first roof under which her name had ever been spoken without suspicion attached.

"The boat isn't about what we want," Ulyana said. "It's about who the future needs where."

"And who decides that?"

The question sat between them like a coin on a table. Ulyana looked at it for a long time. In her apron pocket, folded small, was the scrap of paper where she had finally, the night before, written a fifth name—written it and crossed it out and written it again, unable to commit to either stroke.

"Someone has to," she said.

"Then it should be someone who counts everyone," Lidiya said. "Not someone who counts themselves last."

Ulyana stood so quickly the chair scraped. For a moment she could not speak; the girl had put her finger precisely on the wound and pressed, innocently, the way children press bruises to see if they're real.

"Stay out of the council tomorrow," Ulyana managed. "Whatever you hear through whatever wall you hear it through—stay out of it."

"Yes," said Lidiya, in the tone of a person agreeing to nothing.

That night the river groaned again, louder, and Ulyana lay awake doing the arithmetic one final time, and arrived—as she had known she would—at the number that changed everything: eleven days minus nine was two. Two days until the family met to decide. Two days until love became competition and each of them argued for someone else, because that was what this household did with sacrifice, it passed it around like bread until no one was fed.

Two days. And in two days, unless someone in that room was honest enough to be selfish, the berth would go to whoever lost the argument loudest.

She got up, lit the lamp, and opened the ledger to the page with five names. Four were argued over nightly. The fifth she touched with one fingertip, not writing it, not crossing it out.

Outside, far off, the ice gave a long, low moan—the river counting down with her.
