## 10. Everyone Argues for Someone Else

The meeting was held in the drawing room, which was itself a small confession. In nine years Ulyana had known the Marchmont household to hold its councils in the kitchen—among the flour tins and the warm brick of the stove, where Marta could feed people while they fought and Sef could smoke out the window and no one had to sit in a straight-backed chair. The drawing room was where the Marchmonts received strangers. That they gathered there now meant they were, for one evening, strangers to themselves.

Ulyana arrived last, as she always did at meetings, because she had spent the hour before checking the doors, the lamps, the position of the carriage against the alley wall. She carried the tea tray herself, though Marta had tried to take it from her hands with a look that said *you carry enough*. She set it down on the low table between them like a border marker: here is what we can still share.

The room held five people and one absence. Marta sat nearest the fire, wrapped in a shawl even though the house was warm; she had been cold for two weeks now, a deep cold that blankets didn't touch, and everyone had noticed and no one had spoken of it. Patrik stood at the window with his back half turned, a posture he believed looked like command but which Ulyana recognized as a man arranging his face before it was seen. Sef perched on the arm of the settee, ink-stained fingers drumming his knee, twenty years old and vibrating with everything he wasn't allowed to say yet. And Lidiya sat cross-legged on the floor by the fender, fifteen and pretending to read, her coded notebook hidden inside a volume of Tennyson so poorly that Ulyana could have named the page.

The sixth chair—the one that should have held the answer—stood empty by the mantel. On its seat lay the ticket.

It was a plain thing. Grey paper, a printed number, a name written in faded iron-gall ink: *A. Voss.* A berth on the *Katarina*, the last steamer north before the river closed, sailing when the ice broke. Eleven days, perhaps ten, depending on the weather coming down from the fells. One name. One person. Purchased four years ago by Patrik Marchmont under a false name he could no longer remember inventing, kept in a drawer against exactly this night, and now lying in an empty chair like a corpse waiting to be claimed.

"Well," Patrik said. "We all know why we're here."

"We don't," Lidiya said, not looking up from Tennyson. "Nobody's told me anything since Tuesday. I live in this house too."

"You're fifteen," Sef said.

"I'm fifteen," Lidiya agreed, "and I count the knives when the police leave, same as you do."

Ulyana poured the tea. She had learned long ago that pouring gave her time, and that time given to other people's silences was never wasted. She filled five cups. She did not fill a sixth, because the sixth person at this table was not a person but a decision, and decisions drank nothing.

Patrik turned from the window. He had dressed for the occasion—she saw it and hated that she saw it—in his good grey coat, the one that made him look like what he had been born as rather than what he had become: a rich man's son who had spent thirty years giving the money away and been surprised, each time, that it bought him less safety than he'd imagined. He looked at the ticket. Then he looked at Marta.

"There's no discussion to have," he said. "Marta goes."

"No," said Marta.

"Marta—"

"I know what you're going to say." Marta's voice was gravel over a low fire, unhurried. She folded her hands on top of the shawl. "You're going to say you're the head of this household, that the berth was bought with your money, that your name is the one in the file, and that logic puts you nowhere near that boat and me somewhere on it. You've been rehearsing it since dinner. You did the thing with your cufflinks when you rehearse."

Patrik's hand went, involuntarily, to his cufflink.

"I'm not arguing," Marta continued. "I'm telling you how it lands. It lands like charity. And I've taken your wages and your roof and your protection for eleven years, Patrik, gladly, because we were building something together. But I will not be *sent* north like a parcel because a rich man needs to feel he's done the decent thing."

"It isn't charity. It's arithmetic. Your lungs are failing. Lidiya needs—" He stopped himself, but the room had already heard it.

"Lidiya needs," Marta repeated softly.

And there it was, Ulyana thought. There was the whole evening, laid out before anyone had argued anything. Everyone in this room would fight for someone else, and the fighting would be sincere, and that was what would make it unbearable. Love, in this house, had always expressed itself as competition—the competition to be the one who suffered, who stayed, who paid. The poor performed generosity because generosity was the only property they had. The rich performed sacrifice because sacrifice was the only currency that let them forget, for an hour, that they'd started life holding more chips than anyone else at the table.

She poured herself no tea. Her hands needed to be free.

* * *

Sef went first, because Sef always went first; patience was a language he was still learning to read.

"Look," he said, sliding off the arm of the settee, "someone has to say it, so I'll say it. It should be me."

"Sef," Marta began.

"No, hear it out. Hear the whole ugly thing." He was pacing now, three steps each way between the table and the bookshelf, a caged animal in a borrowed waistcoat. "I'm nineteen. I've got no family that claims me, no trade except the press, and the press is the first thing they'll smash anyway. If they take me, I'm a printer's boy with pamphlets in his floorboards—two years, maybe less if Rell wants a fast conviction to wave around. If they take any of you, it's worse. Marta's got a brother they already hanged; they'll hang the memory of him around her neck. Patrik's got a name, which means they'll make an example. And Ulyana—" he faltered, glanced at her, glanced away—"Ulyana's foreign-born with no papers that matter. They could deport her to a country she hasn't seen since she was six."

"So you've noticed," Ulyana said mildly, "that every reason you give for yourself is a reason for someone else."

"That's not—" He stopped pacing. Ran both hands through his hair until it stood up like a frightened animal's. "That's exactly what I mean. God. I sat down to argue for myself and every road out of my own mouth ends at one of you."

"Then walk it honestly," Ulyana said. "You want to live. Say it."

The room went quiet. Even Lidiya lowered Tennyson.

"I want to live," Sef said, and his voice cracked on *live*, and something in Ulyana's chest cracked with it, because he was nineteen and furious and brave and terrified, and wanting to live was the most honest sentence spoken in that room all night. "I want to live, and I want to print things worth living for, and I want the boat. And then I think about Marta's cough and Lidiya's notebook sitting in her mattress like a lit fuse, and I think—if I take the berth and any of you go to prison, I'll spend every morning for the rest of my life setting type knowing what it cost. So." He spread his hands, empty. "Recanted. Give it to Marta."

"You just recanted into the same argument Patrik started with," Lidiya observed.

"Yes, well." Sef dropped back onto the settee arm, deflated. "Good arguments are like good typefaces. There's only a few of them and everybody borrows."

* * *

Patrik tried again, and this time he did it properly, standing, addressing the room like a committee—which was, Ulyana thought, the tragedy of him. He had learned to plead only in the grammar of governance.

"The berth was purchased for a servant of this household," he said. "Not for me—I was never a candidate; my name is on their file precisely because they expect me to run, and running confirms it. Not for Sef, though God knows the boy deserves it. For whoever the household cannot afford to lose. And the household cannot afford to lose Marta."

"Because I cook," Marta said.

"Because you are the reason this family has held together for eleven years, and you are ill, and every week we pretend otherwise is a week we're stealing from you."

"Stealing," Marta repeated. "Listen to him. Eleven years I've fed this house and now I'm being robbed by kindness." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on her knees, and Ulyana saw that they trembled—not with age, not with fear, but with the effort of keeping her voice level. "Patrik. My brother was arrested in 'ninety-eight because someone had to stay behind and mind the press, and he drew the short straw, and they hanged him in the yard at Kessel with a sack on his head. I have spent twenty-six years being the one who stays behind. I am very, very good at it. Do not take the last thing I own—the last choice I own—and dress it up as my rescue."

"If you stay, they question you again. You saw what one night in custody did—"

"What it did," Marta said, "was show me that I can survive them. What it also showed me is that I cough blood when I'm frightened, which is information I could have lived without." She said it flatly, and the flatness was worse than weeping. "I am dying, Patrik. Slowly, but surely, and north of the ice or south of it, that doesn't change. What changes is whether I die having decided something, or having been decided for."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice, the old house settling its bones.

"And Lidiya?" Ulyana said.

Everyone turned. Ulyana had spoken perhaps six words all evening, which was her custom, and so the six words landed with the weight of a dropped anchor.

"She's fifteen," Patrik said. "This isn't hers to decide."

"She's counting the knives," Ulyana said. "She coded her notebook after watching me code my ledger. Everything this house knows, she learned by breathing our air. She is in this whether we put her in it or not. The only question is whether she hears us decide her fate from behind a closed door—again—or whether she sits at the table where it happens."

"I'm right here," Lidiya said.

"I know," Ulyana said. "I've known since Tuesday."

Lidiya closed Tennyson. Set it aside, notebook and all, deliberately visible. Sat up straight, knees together, hands in her lap, a schoolgirl presenting herself for examination. "I don't want the berth," she said.

"Lidiya—" Marta started.

"I don't. Listen. Everyone keeps saying my name like it's a trump card. Marta wants it to be mine because Marta thinks her lungs are already spent. Patrik wants Marta to have it because Patrik can't stand the idea that money can't fix this one. Sef wanted it himself for four minutes and then hated himself for it." She counted them off on her fingers, merciless, accurate. "Nobody has once asked what I want, so I'll tell you. I want to stay and finish school and keep my notebook and watch what you all do next, because whatever you do next is going to teach me how to be a person, and I'd rather learn it close up than safe at a distance. Also," and here her voice wobbled, the fifteen showing through the forty she was impersonating, "also I don't want to go north alone. I don't care whose name is on the ticket. A berth isn't a person. I don't want a berth. I want one of *you*."

Silence. The kind of silence that follows a child saying the true thing all the adults had arranged their lives to avoid saying.

Ulyana looked at the empty chair. At the grey ticket with its false name. *A. Voss.* Four years ago Patrik had bought it, and she had watched him buy it, and neither of them had ever said aloud the obvious thing: that a contingency plan with one seat in it was not a plan at all. It was a bet on who deserved to survive, placed years in advance, by a man who had never had to place such a bet himself.

"Patrik," she said.

He looked at her. And she saw it happen—the moment the evening tipped from a family's arithmetic into a wound. Because he knew what was coming. Some part of him had known since the raid, since the custody, since the night she had stood in his study and told him she held his alibi and his ruin in the same hand. He knew she was going to say the thing that couldn't be unsaid.

"You have something to add," he said. His voice was careful. Too careful. The voice of a man walking on ice he'd been told was thin.

"I've been listening all evening," Ulyana said. "To everyone argue for someone else. Marta argues for Lidiya. You argue for Marta. Sef argues for himself, then recants into arguing for Marta. Lidiya refuses the whole game. It's very moving. It's also a performance, and I've watched performances in this house for nine years, so allow me to review the casting." She kept her voice level, driver's voice, the voice she used on horses and inspectors. "The rich man performs sacrifice. The poor perform generosity. And nobody—nobody—has said the simplest fact in the room, which is that the ticket exists at all because *you* bought it, *you* chose who it was for, and *you* have assumed since the day you pinned it in that drawer that the choosing was yours."

"That's unfair—"

"Is it? Whose name did you write on it? Not *any servant*. A. Voss. A name you invented. A person who doesn't exist. You bought a berth for a ghost, Patrik, and now you're holding séances trying to decide which of us gets to haunt it."

Sef made a sound that was half laugh, half horror, and strangled it.

"I bought it to save a life," Patrik said, and now the care was gone and something rawer showed through, and Ulyana understood she had cut him somewhere real. "What have you ever risked, Ulyana? You read our letters. You drive our roads. You've built yourself into the walls of this house so thoroughly that no one can see you—and fine, that's survival, I respect it—but don't stand there tonight and lecture *me* about choosing, when your entire method is to never be the one who chooses. You decide nothing. You carry everything. There's a difference, and the difference is that carrying lets you keep your hands clean."

The words hit the room like a dropped glass.

Because it was true. That was the obscenity of it—it was true, or nearly true, or true enough that it didn't matter. Nine years of carrying letters and secrets and alibis and other people's guilt, and yes, yes, she had told herself it was service, it was love, it was the work of invisible hands—but Patrik had just said aloud the thing she whispered to herself at three in the morning: that invisibility was also a fortress, that the woman who carries everything can always claim she never chose anything.

Marta was staring at her. Sef had gone very still. Lidiya's eyes moved between them like a spectator at a tennis match played with knives.

"You're right," Ulyana said.

Patrik blinked.

"You're right that I hide in the carrying. You're wrong about why." She crossed to the mantel, took the ticket from the empty chair, and held it up between two fingers. "You bought this berth for a ghost. Fine. Here's what ghosts are for: they're for the moment when the living run out of arguments. Nobody in this room can agree who deserves to live—that's tonight's finding, and it's an honest one, and it took us a war council to reach it. So the ticket goes back in the drawer for one more day. And tomorrow I'm going to do the thing you say I never do."

"Which is?"

"Choose." She slid the ticket into her apron pocket, where it weighed nothing and everything. "There's a woman across town who watched you commit no crime, Patrik, and who could prove it tomorrow, and who won't speak because speaking would burn her own family down. While the rest of you spent this evening performing nobility over a single boat seat, I'm going to go ask her to trade her silence for her shame. Because that's the actual arithmetic. Not one berth. One statement. And if I come back with it, we may not need the boat at all—and if I come back without it, then at least we'll choose who sails knowing we tried truth before we tried tickets."

She untied her apron as she spoke, folding it over her arm—a driver does not wear her uniform into another man's parlor. At the door she paused.

"For the record," she said, without turning, "I have chosen something every day for nine years. I chose this family. Tonight is simply the first time I'm doing it where you can see me."

The door closed behind her on a room gone absolutely silent.

Outside, the wind had come around from the northeast, smelling of river and iron. Down at the docks, eleven days early, the ice on the channel groaned in its sleep—a long, subterranean complaint, like the earth turning over before waking.

Elena Vasilievna lived on Molot Lane, above the milliner's, behind a green door. Ulyana turned up her collar and walked.
