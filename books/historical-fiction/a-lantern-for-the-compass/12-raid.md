## 12. Raid

The dogs started first.

Ulyana heard them before the knock—two of them, maybe three, their voices carrying up the frozen street with that flat, official excitement she had learned to read from a distance. She was already awake. She had been awake since three, lying in her narrow room above the carriage house with her boots on, listening to the river grind its teeth against the ice. She had known for two days, the way you know a storm is coming: not by any single sign but by the accumulation of small wrongnesses. The clerk at the records office had been too polite when he took her money. A boy she'd never seen before had walked past the house four times on Tuesday, whistling the same eight bars over and over like a man rehearsing his testimony. And last night, the lamplighter had skipped their street entirely, leaving it dark—a courtesy or a stage set, depending on who had paid him.

She sat up. Pulled on the rest of her clothes in the dark. Checked the time by the kitchen clock she could hear faintly through the floor: twenty minutes past five.

Then the knock came—not a knock at all but a fist, the kind of blow meant for doors that belonged to no one.

Ulyana went down the back stairs the way she always did, counting the steps out of habit, and crossed the cold kitchen where Marta was already standing by the stove in her shawl, hands folded, as though she had been waiting all night for this particular visitor and had prepared tea.

"They're early," Marta said.

"They're punctual," Ulyana said. "It's worse."

Sef appeared in the doorway of the scullery, hair wild, eyes already too bright. Lidiya came behind him, dragging her blanket, and Ulyana saw the girl's gaze go straight to the loose board under the bread shelf—the one that hid nothing now, nothing at all, just flour and turnips—and then away again, composed. Fifteen years old and she could arrange her face faster than Sef could. The family had made her well.

"To your places," Ulyana said quietly. "Sef—the stories, once more."

"I'm a printer's boy. I work for Herr Vogel on—"

"Faster."

"—Kanalstrasse, I sleep here because it's cheaper, I've never seen Patrik Marchmont write anything but letters to his sister." He recited it flat, bored, the way she'd drilled him. Good. Boredom was the hardest thing to fake and the most convincing thing to wear.

"Lidiya?"

The girl lifted her chin. "I'm the ward. My parents died. I do lessons with Miss Anselm on Thursdays. I don't understand politics, sir, I only want my breakfast." She delivered it with a child's sullenness, perfectly pitched, and Ulyana felt something twist in her chest—pride and grief in the same knot, indistinguishable.

"Marta," Ulyana began, and stopped, because Marta was looking at her with an expression Ulyana had never seen on her before. Not fear. Something older and calmer than fear. Resignation worn smooth as a river stone.

"Say it anyway," Marta said. "For the practice."

"You are the cook. You have been here eleven years. The kitchen is yours and everything in it is yours and you know nothing about anyone's correspondence."

"Everything in it is mine," Marta repeated, and touched the floor once with her slipper, so lightly it might have been a blessing.

Patrik came down last, dressed properly, jacket buttoned, grey at the temples combed flat. He looked, Ulyana thought, exactly like what he was supposed to look like: a gentleman inconvenienced before breakfast. Only his hands betrayed him, working at each other behind his back, and only she knew to look.

"Ulyana," he said softly. "If they take me—"

"They won't today. Today they're fishing. Today they want us frightened enough to make mistakes." She met his eyes. "So we won't be frightened."

The door shook under the second blow, and somewhere above them a window rattled in its frame, and the morning began.

* * *

There were nine of them. Ulyana counted them through the frosted glass of the front door before she opened it—an old habit, counting—and noted the details the way she noted everything, filing them where she filed things: six uniformed men, two plainclothes, and a third man in a good coat standing slightly apart from the others with his hands in his pockets, watching the house rather than the door. Not Rell. Someone else, younger, with a neat moustache and the impatient posture of a man who expected to be promoted for this morning's work. She memorized his face the way she memorized faces: a habit born of protecting the household from informers, useful now in ways she wished it weren't.

She opened the door on the second blow.

"Yes?" she said.

The warrant was thrust at her—a printed sheet, dense with clauses, signed by someone whose signature looped like a bird track. She read it in four seconds, which was three seconds longer than she needed. *Premises occupied by P. Marchmont and associates. Search for seditious literature, arms, correspondence pertaining to* —and there it was, the bombing, the word *explosive* sitting on the page like something dead. Her face did nothing at all. She had spent forty years making her face do nothing at all, and it had never once failed her until now, and it did not fail her now.

"The master of the house is dressing," she said. "You'll wait in the hall."

"We'll wait where we like."

"You'll wait in the hall," she repeated, in the voice she used for horses and drunk men and officials of every rank, "or you'll explain to the magistrate why the household's papers were handled before the master could witness it. It's in your warrant. Clause six."

The man with the moustache glanced at the paper, then at her, recalibrating—she watched him do it, watch him decide she was a servant who had read a warrant before, which was either alarming or useful to him. "Name?"

"Iversen. Driver and secretary." *Secretary.* She gave them the word herself before they could find a worse one. Control the story; give them the smallest true thing and let it stand in for all the rest.

They poured into the hall in a smell of wet wool, boot polish, and cigarette smoke, and then the house that had hidden everyone began to be taken apart room by room.

* * *

Terror, Ulyana discovered, rendered procedurally, looks like this: a constable in your bedroom turning your shirtsleeves inside out one by one, holding each cuff to the light. It looks like another man running his thumb along the spines of Patrik's books while Patrik stands in the doorway reciting titles as if the man might steal them rather than read them. It looks like Marta being made to open every jar in her own pantry while a boy barely older than Sef sniffs each one—pickled cabbage, rendered fat, the plum preserve she saved for Christmas—with the gravity of an apothecary.

It sounds like drawers. That was the sound of the morning, Ulyana thought, standing at her post in the hall with her hands folded: the sound of drawers, opened and closed, opened and closed, wood on wood, the house speaking in a language it had never used. Underneath it, the scrape of furniture moved by strangers. Once, the crack of a chair leg breaking and the small pause afterward in which everyone waited to see if anyone would be punished for it.

Her preparations held. She walked the house behind the searchers—not obstructing, never obstructing, simply present, the way a servant is present—and checked off her inventory as they went.

The letters were gone; she had burned them weeks ago, and the grate held only ordinary ash, and ash was ash. The pamphlets were under Marta's floor, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a layer of stone shards and broken crockery that had been laid down in September for exactly this, and the searcher who knelt in the kitchen pried up two boards, found rubble, found nothing, and moved on. The press was not in the house—it had never been in the house; Sef kept it disassembled in three crates at a coal merchant's on the far side of the canal, and the receipt was written in a name that did not exist. The pistol—she felt her stomach tighten when she remembered the pistol—but no. The pistol had gone into the canal in November, wrapped around a brick, on a night so cold the water had tried to keep it. Gone was gone. She had made sure of gone. That had been her whole life's profession, it seemed this morning: making sure of gone.

What they found: a suffrage pamphlet, openly displayed on the parlor table because hiding it would have been worse than showing it. Letters from Patrik's sister, full of weather and rheumatism. Lidiya's schoolwork. Marta's account book, columns of eggs and flour in a hand like iron fencing. Sef's copy of *Das Kapital*, which drew a grunt of satisfaction from the moustached man and which Sef defended with the perfect indignation of a youth who has read forty pages.

"That's a library book," Sef said hotly. "From the lending room on Kanalstrasse. There's a card in it, look—"

There was a card in it. Of course there was a card in it. Ulyana had put it there herself, aged it under the stove, and written Herr Vogel's lending stamp on it with a pen nib she had ground down to imitate his hand. The plainclothes man turned it over twice, sniffed it, and pocketed it anyway. Everything was evidence if you were determined; she understood that now in her bones. Innocence was not a defense. Innocence was only a slower way to be convicted.

Through all of it, Ulyana stood and answered questions in her flat, unaccented-as-possible voice—*no, sir, I handle the correspondence, yes, sir, I read it aloud to the master on account of his eyes, no, sir, I don't recall the contents, sir, it's not my place to recall*—and each lie was a small stone laid in a wall, and the wall held, and the wall held, and she watched Marta across the length of the kitchen and saw the wall begin to fail.

* * *

It was the strain, afterward, that everyone called it. But Ulyana knew better. Strain was what happened to other people. What happened to Marta was older than that—a debt coming due, a body presenting its invoice after years of deferred payment, and the morning's terror merely the collector at the door.

Ulyana saw it start. They had been at it two hours. Marta had opened every jar, lifted every pot, knelt twice on the cold flags for the men going through her cupboards, and each time she rose Ulyana marked the pause—the half-breath before her legs agreed to hold her, the hand that found the table edge a fraction later than it should have. Her color had gone wrong. Not pale; grey, a specific grey Ulyana recognized without knowing from where, the grey of tallow, of winter ice with snow pressed into it.

"Sit down," Ulyana murmured, passing her. "Let them dig. Sit."

"And let them think I'm hiding something under my skirts?" Marta whispered back, almost smiling. "I'm fine, little wolf. I've been searched before. In 'ninety-one they took the whole street apart looking for my brother." Her brother. The executed one. The letters under the floor that Ulyana had promised to burn and hadn't, because some things even a woman who burned everything could not burn.

The third time Marta knelt, she did not get up quickly.

She got up slowly, and then not at all—her hand slid off the cupboard edge, and she sat down hard on the kitchen floor among the searchers' boot prints, and the sound she made was not a cry but a long exhalation, as if the breath had finally been taken out of her for good.

"Marta—" Ulyya was across the room before she had decided to move, on her knees beside her, one arm braced along the cook's spine. Marta's skin through the shawl was cold and damp and her breathing had a whistle in it now, high and thin, like wind through a cracked window. The whistle was new. Or it had been new for months and Ulyana had refused to hear it, and hearing it now, in front of nine witnesses, she felt shame arrive ahead of grief.

"She needs a doctor," Ulyana said. Flatly. To the room. To the moustached man, who had come to stand over them with the expression of a man inconvenienced by weather.

"What she needs is to answer some questions." He crouched, unhurried. "At the district office. There are irregularities in this household's accounts. Persons will assist inquiries."

"She can't walk to the corner."

"Then persons will be carried."

Something went through Ulyana then, cold and totalizing, a clarity like the moment ice gives way. She looked up at the man and let him see, for one unguarded second, exactly what was looking back at him—and he actually stepped back, half a step, the instinct of any creature meeting something wilder than expected—before she smoothed it away and became the secretary again, the driver, the invisible woman, because becoming visible right now, in this room, would cost Marta everything.

"I'll pack her bag," Ulyana said. "Medicines. She has a heart condition. It's documented."

"It isn't documented anywhere."

"It will be," Ulyana said, "when her doctor writes it tonight. Unless you'd prefer to be the officer who arrested a dying woman and lost her in custody. I imagine that makes a different kind of career."

The moustache twitched. Somewhere behind him, one of the uniformed men shifted his weight, and Ulyana understood that even machinery has men inside it, and men can be spoken to. It was the only lever she had, and she leaned on it with everything she had.

They let her pack the bag. She packed it slowly, deliberately, in full view—flannel nightgown, the brown shawl, a tin of the plum preserve because Marta's eye flicked to it and Ulyana read her like a letter. And underneath the flannel, folded so flat it was a whisper, a note she wrote in ten seconds at the dresser with her body blocking the view: *Say nothing. Own nothing. We are coming. — U.*

Marta's fingers closed over the flannel and felt the paper through it, and the two women looked at each other, and Marta nodded once—the smallest nod, a queen's nod from a kitchen floor—and said, loudly, for the room: "Mind the starter jar by the window. Feed it daily or it dies. Eleven years I've kept it alive and I'll not have it die because of *this lot*."

"Yes, Marta," Ulyaya said, and her voice cracked on the name, only slightly, only visibly to someone who knew her, and Lidiya—God, Lidiya—stepped forward and took Marta's other arm as if this had been assigned to her, as if fifteen-year-olds were permitted, and together they brought the old woman upright.

At the door, Marta paused. Turned her head. Her breath whistled like a kettle about to sing.

"The floor," she said to Ulyana, clearly, in front of everyone, "needs re-laying. Those boards are loose as anything. Anyone could hide anything under there." And her eyes glittered, fierce and wet and laughing all at once, because it was the truest sentence in the house and therefore the safest: tell the police a truth they won't believe and it becomes armor.

Then the door closed on her, and the boots went down the steps, and the wagon wheels ground away over the frozen ruts, and the house stood empty of her for the first time in eleven years.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, the terrible kind, the kind with a shape. The searchers finished by noon—found nothing, took the pamphlet, the book, and Patrik's passport *for verification*—and left a receipt for seized property that Ulyana read twice and filed in her memory, clause by clause. The broken chair leg lay in the parlor like a bone. Sef sat on the stairs with his head in his hands. Lidiya fed Marta's starter jar with a solemnity usually reserved for sacraments. Patrik stood at the parlor window watching the empty street and saying nothing, which was itself an accusation, though of whom Ulyana couldn't yet say.

Ulyana washed the kitchen floor on her knees. It didn't need washing. She did it anyway, because her hands required occupation or they would shake, and because Marta would have done it, and because the floorboards under her knees were loose as anything, anyone could hide anything under there, and she pressed her palm flat against the gap between two boards and felt the oilcloth-wrapped bundle of a dead man's letters, warm somehow, or seeming warm, the way the dead stay warm in the people who refuse to let them go.

They had taken the wrong woman. That was the arithmetic she kept arriving at, circling it like a hawk. Every calculation, every contingency, every rehearsed answer—she had built the architecture, she had drilled the stories, and the state had simply reached into the middle of it and taken the person whose only crime was standing on top of a secret. Someone always pays for someone else's safety. She had known it since she was a stowaway's daughter. She had never before been the someone who chose who paid.

In the afternoon she put on her coat and told Patrik she was going to see about feed for the horses, which was true, in the sense that she intended to end up near the stable district eventually. What she meant was the records office, the clerk, the money she had left, the corridor where information changed hands for coins and favors. What she meant was: I am going to find out where they keep her, whether she has a blanket, whether the whistle in her chest has gotten worse, and what her interrogator's face looks like, because I will need it later, when I know what to do with it.

At the door, Lidiya caught her sleeve.

"Take my notebook," the girl whispered, pressing it into Ulyana's coat pocket before Ulyana could refuse—small, coded, dangerous, the child's imitation of everything Ulyana had taught her without meaning to. "If they come back, it shouldn't be here. And—" Lidiya's chin trembled once and steadied. "Bring her home."

Ulyana walked out into the blue afternoon with the notebook riding against her hip like a second heartbeat, toward the office, the clerk, the corridor, the machinery—already composing in her head the first of the lies she would need, already spending herself like currency, and telling herself, as the ice groaned somewhere far off beneath the city, that she would bring Marta home.

She did not yet know the price the machinery would ask.
