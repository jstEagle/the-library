## 13. Marta in Custody

They took her at half past six in the morning, and by seven she had already decided how she would be treated, because a woman who cannot choose her circumstances can still choose her posture.

The room they gave her was not designed for comfort. A wooden chair bolted to the floor, a table scarred with initials and cigarette burns, one window set so high that the light came down like water through a grate. Marta Voss had cooked in worse rooms. She had stood twelve hours over stoves in kitchens with no windows at all, in houses where the mistress counted the sugar. This room held no terror for her. It was only another kitchen where she would be the ingredient.

The man across the table introduced himself as Examiner Dorn, second rank, and laid out his papers with the care of a man setting a table he expected to dirty himself. He was younger than she'd expected. Soft around the mouth. He would have a mother somewhere, or a wife who told him he looked tired.

"Name," he said.

"Marta Voss."

"Age."

"Fifty-three years, and old enough not to enjoy being asked twice."

He looked up. She gave him nothing—not a smile, not a scowl, only the level patience of a woman waiting for bread to prove. He made a note. She watched him write it and understood that everything now was notes: her words becoming ink becoming evidence becoming a file that would outlive them all if they let it.

"You are employed as cook in the household of Patrik Marchmont," Dorn said. "How long?"

"Eleven years."

"Eleven years is a long time."

"It's a long time for soup too," Marta said. "It doesn't make soup criminal."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—annoyance, or perhaps its cousin, respect. He smoothed it away and turned a page. "During this morning's search of the premises, certain articles were recovered from beneath the floor of the kitchen. You will want to hear what they were."

"I imagine you'll tell me regardless."

"Pamphlets. Forty-one in number, seditious in content. One pistol, unregistered. And a packet of letters addressed to a *V. Aldis*, deceased, executed by firing squad in 1906 for crimes against the state."

Marta folded her hands on the table. Her knuckles had gone the color of candle tallow in the gray light, and she noticed this with a strange detachment, as though her hands belonged to someone else being observed. Under the table, where no one could see, her thumb found the ridge of an old burn scar and pressed against it. The pain kept her here. Kept her steady.

"Those are mine," she said.

Dorn's pen stopped. "I beg your pardon?"

"The pamphlets. The pistol. The letters." She said it the way she announced dinner: plainly, completely, without apology. "All of it mine. Every article under my kitchen floor belongs to me and no one else."

"That seems—" He consulted his papers. "That seems improbable, madam. Forty-one pamphlets. An unregistered weapon. Correspondence with an executed anarchist. In eleven years, no one else in that house ever—"

"Eleven years is a long time," Marta said. "Long enough to bury anything."

She watched him try to fit her confession into his theory of the world. She could see the machinery working behind his eyes—the same machinery that ran every kitchen she'd ever worked in, every great house, every ministry: the assumption that servants were furniture, that furniture could not think, that a woman with flour still ghosted into the creases of her knuckles could not possibly have convictions. Let him keep the assumption. It was the most useful thing she owned.

"The Marchmonts knew nothing of these items," she said. "Mr. Marchmont is a man who reads novels and writes letters to newspapers about drainage reform. Mrs. Marchmont, God rest her, couldn't load a pistol if you handed her one barrel at a time. The girl Lidiya is fifteen and afraid of thunder. The driver Iversen drives. The apprentice prints wedding invitations and is bad even at that." Each name she gave them small, harmless, diminished—and each diminishment cost her something she did not let show. "The radical in that house, Mr. Dorn, has been standing at your elbow for eleven years, cooking your suspects their dinners. Me."

"Why?" he asked, and it came out almost genuinely puzzled. "Why hide such things? Why keep them at all?"

"Because my brother wrote those letters," Marta said, "and I was not going to let the state take him twice."

The silence after that was long enough that she heard the building breathe around them—a pipe ticking somewhere, boots in a corridor, the city going about its morning beyond the high window while she sat in it like a stone at the bottom of a well.

Dorn wrote for a while. When he spoke again his voice had shifted, softened into something more dangerous than menace: sympathy. "You understand that claiming ownership of these materials carries consequences. Serious ones. You're not a young woman, Frau Voss. There are arrangements we could make instead. Cooperation. Names."

"There is soup," Marta said, "and there is the water the soup was boiled in. You've got the water, Mr. Dorn. Drink all of it you like. You'll get no names out of me because there are none to give. I acted alone. I buried alone. I will hang alone, if it comes to hanging, and I will do it politely."

"We don't hang women anymore."

"What a modern age," said Marta, "to have invented so many alternatives."

* * *

Ulyana learned of the arrest the way she learned most things—by listening at the edge of things, in this case to the milk boy, who had seen the black carriage leave the Marchmont square at dawn with Marta sitting upright inside it "like a duchess going to her own funeral, miss, begging your pardon."

Begging pardon was correct. Ulyana thanked him, paid him double, and stood in the cold courtyard doing arithmetic.

The search had taken three hours. She knew because she had watched from the window of the cobbler's shop across the lane, having risen before dawn on instinct—the particular animal instinct of a woman who had spent thirty years reading other people's mail and could feel a raid coming the way sailors feel weather. She had watched them carry out boxes. She had watched Inspector Rell stand in the doorway with his hat in his hands, wearing the expression of a man attending a ceremony whose meaning he doubted. She had watched them bring Marta down the steps, and Marta had paused on the last stair to say something to Rell that made him look away.

Now the house was quiet in the wrong way. Quiet the way a body is quiet when the heart is deciding.

Sef was in the print room pretending to inventory type. Lidiya sat in the window seat with a book she was not reading, her coded notebook burned two nights ago on Ulyana's instruction, its ashes indistinguishable from the ashes of everything else. Patrik had shut himself in the study with the brandy he pretended he didn't drink before noon. The household moved through its routines like actors who had lost the play but remembered the blocking.

Someone had to know what was happening to Marta. Not rumor—fact. What she had been asked. What she had answered. Whether she had slept, whether she had eaten, whether her lungs—which had rattled all winter like a door in wind—were holding.

There was exactly one channel for fact in this city, and Ulyana had spent years learning its price.

The records office of the Fourth Precinct occupied a narrow building wedged between a pawnbroker and a shop that sold religious candles to both churches equally. Inside, behind a counter of varnished oak gone cloudy with age, sat clerks. Dozens of clerks. And among them, third desk from the window, a young man named Piotr Havel with a widowed mother, four sisters, a gambling debt he believed was secret, and a salary that covered none of it.

Ulyana entered at eleven, when the morning queue had thinned. She wore her plainest coat and carried a market basket containing, beneath the cabbage, an envelope containing, beneath its innocent paper, sixty crowns in mixed notes. She had withdrawn it from the household emergency fund that morning, writing the entry in the ledger herself: *repairs, kitchen*. The lie sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She was stealing from the family to save a piece of the family, and she suspected that all love, examined closely, looked something like embezzlement.

"Good morning," she said to Havel, pleasant, forgettable, the voice of a woman buying stamps.

"Good morning. How may—"

"Your sister Katya," Ulyana said quietly. "She plays piano at the Café Aurora on Thursdays. Lovely player. It would be a shame if the café's owner were visited regarding his licensing. He owes money to people who would be very interested in a word from the police."

Havel went pale in the specific way of men realizing the ground was not where they'd left it. "I don't—I don't know what you—"

"Second desk from the window," Ulyana continued, in the tone of someone reciting a recipe. "Four sisters. Your mother takes in sewing. You owe nine hundred crowns to a man named Ferenc, who is not a patient man, and you have been covering it by selling information to a journalist at the *Daily Courier*, which is also a crime, though nobody much cares about that one." She set the basket on the counter. "I care about one thing only. Marta Voss, brought in this morning. Interrogation notes, medical observations, meal records—yes, they record meals, don't look surprised, bureaucracy records everything, that's its whole genius. I want to know what she says, what she eats, whether she coughs blood. Daily. Left in the loose brick behind the candle shop, wrapped in oilcloth. In exchange, this basket, monthly, and my permanent silence about Katya and the *Courier*."

"This is blackmail," Havel whispered.

"It's employment," said Ulyana. "With excellent terms. Consider that the alternative is prison, and that I read letters for a living, which means I know precisely how many people in this city could be ruined by their own correspondence. You would be amazed how safe you are with me, Mr. Havel. I'm the safest person you'll ever meet."

He stared at her. Behind the fear, something else surfaced—relief, almost, the terrible relief of a drowning man who finds the thing pulling him under has hands. "The Voss woman," he said slowly. "You're with her. With the Marchmonts."

"I drive for them," Ulyana said. "And carry things. That's all anyone needs to know."

He took the basket. His hands shook. "First report tonight," he said. "If there's—if she's—"

"She's stronger than your whole precinct," Ulyana said, and walked out into the cold with the empty basket against her hip, hating herself a little, loving Marta a great deal, and understanding with perfect clarity that these were the same feeling.

* * *

In the evening, the first report came, written in Havel's cramped clerk's hand on the back of a laundry list:

*S.V. interrogated twice today, 9–11 a.m., 2–5 p.m., Examiner Dorn presiding. Subject claims sole ownership of all recovered materials. Repeated claim, consistent under pressure. Refuses counsel ("I'll speak for myself; God knows no one else will"). Declines to elaborate on pamphlet distribution. Ate full supper. Coughing at intervals; requested honey; request denied, then granted after subject remarked that dead witnesses inconvenience everyone. No charges filed yet. Dorn requesting authorization to extend detention.*

Ulyana read it three times by lamplight in the coach house, then fed it to the stove and watched the paper curl.

*Sole ownership.* She could hear Marta saying it—could see the set of her jaw, the folded hands, the iron courtesy that would make Dorn feel like a rude guest at his own table. Marta had claimed the pamphlets, the pistol, the letters. All of it. Eleven years of buried history gathered up into one old woman's apron like spilled flour swept into a dustpan.

It should have been a relief. It was a relief, and Ulyana despised the relief, because she could feel what it was made of underneath.

It was made of Marta's lungs. Of fifty-three years and a chest that rattled. Of interrogation rooms and thin blankets and the state's genius for grinding down whoever it could reach. Marta had stepped in front of the family the way a cook steps between a falling pot and a child—without thought, without hesitation, because that was simply where she stood in the order of things. And the family had let her. Had needed her to. Would go on needing her to, and call it gratitude, and sleep at night.

Ulyana sat in the dark of the coach house and did the arithmetic she was becoming famous for among no one.

Sef had wanted to fight. Patrik had wanted to endure. Lidiya was too young, Marta too ill, Patrik too marked. Everyone in that house had argued, in chapter after chapter of their shared catastrophe, for someone else to be saved. And Marta had solved the whole insoluble problem by simply declaring herself guilty of everything, the way she declared dinner served—finally, completely, no discussion.

But guilt was not a coat you could put on for someone. It went on like a fever, and it burned whoever wore it, and the warmth everyone else felt was borrowed heat.

*Someone always pays,* Ulyana thought. That was the secret ledger beneath all ledgers. Someone always pays for someone else's safety, and the paying is never voluntary for long, and the ones who pay are always the ones the world has already decided are cheapest: the servant, the woman, the old, the invisible. Marta was paying in lungfuls. Somewhere a clerk was paying in fear. Ulyana herself was paying in sixty-crown increments and lies in the household ledger, and the interest on all these debts was compounding in the dark.

She went inside, past Sef's door, past Lidiya's, to Patrik's study, where the brandy bottle stood open like a confession.

"They're extending her detention," Ulyana said without preamble. "No charges yet. They're building toward charges. Claiming sole ownership buys time, not freedom—Dorn wants a distribution network, and when he can't find one he'll invent her accomplices, and when he can't invent them he'll charge her with possession alone, and possession alone is still years."

Patrik looked up. He had aged a decade in four days; the silver in his beard had gone white at the chin. "Then we buy her out. Lawyers. I'll sell the—"

"Lawyers need a client who isn't confessing to terrorism out of politeness," Ulyana said. "And your name is in a file that says *bombing*. Every lawyer you hire becomes another thread they pull. You know this. I know you know this."

"Then what, Miss Iversen? Tell me what, since the household appears to have decided you're the one who knows things." There was an edge to it—the first edge he had ever shown her, and she noted it without flinching, because fear makes patriarchs petulant and she had watched better men than him do worse.

What she knew was this: there existed, somewhere in the machinery of the Fourth Precinct, an inspector named Rell who had stood in a doorway holding his hat like a man at a funeral he distrusted. Who had looked away when Marta spoke to him on the steps. Who had, three weeks ago, in a corridor of this very house during the first search, paused beside Ulyana and said, in a voice pitched below the hearing of his own men: *"The evidence against your employer is very tidy. I distrust tidy."*

She hadn't understood it then. She was beginning to understand it now.

"I know who doubts the case," Ulyana said. "And I know doubt is the only currency in that building worth anything. Give me until tomorrow night."

Patrik studied her—the driver, the letter-reader, the woman who had stood at the edge of his family for eleven years carrying things—and whatever he saw in her face made him nod, slowly, the way men nod at seas they've decided not to argue with.

After he slept, Ulyana sat alone with the lamp turned low and wrote in her own notebook, in her own cipher, the sentence she would build the next day around:

*Rell. Tidy. Find out what he wants. Be prepared to pay it.*

Below it, after a long pause, she added:

*Marta is paying for us. Find out what we owe.*

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice groaned in its sleep—a long, low sound like the earth clearing its throat, like something vast beginning, very slowly, to move.
