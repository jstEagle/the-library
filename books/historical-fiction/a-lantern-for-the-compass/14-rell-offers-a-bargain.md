## 14. Rell Offers a Bargain

The summons came the way winter came—gradually, then all at once. A constable in a plain coat appeared at the Marchmont kitchen door on a Tuesday of grey sleet, handed Marta a folded card, and touched his cap as though delivering a laundry list. *Inspector Rell requests the presence of Miss Iversen at the Fourth Precinct, Thursday, eleven o'clock. A matter of documents.*

Documents. Ulyana turned the word over on her tongue all Wednesday while she greased the axles and checked the carriage springs and did everything else a driver does with hands that wanted to be doing something else. Documents meant they had something with writing on it that concerned her. In her experience, the state's interest in a woman's documents was never benign. Papers were how they counted you, and once you were counted you could be filed, and once you were filed you could be lost.

Marta watched her sharpen the kitchen knives that evening—not for use, just for the comfort of the rhythm—and said nothing until the whetstone had made its full circuit twice.

"He'll offer you something," Marta said. "Men like that don't summon; they trade."

"And what would I have to sell?"

"Yourself, most likely. It's the only thing they think we're carrying." Marta coughed, a small dry sound she'd been making for a week and pretending was nothing. "Sell it dear, girl."

---

The Fourth Precinct smelled of wet wool, ink, and boiled cabbage—a smell that managed to be institutional without being clean. The desk sergeant took her name without looking up, wrote it wrong—*Iverson*, one s where there should be two—and she let him. Let them get it wrong. A woman whose name was misspelled was a woman who hadn't been worth the effort of looking at closely.

Rell's office was at the top of three flights of stairs, small, cold, dominated by a window that looked out onto the yard where prisoners were walked between buildings. He stood when she entered—an unexpected courtesy—and gestured to the chair across from his desk without sitting down himself.

"Miss Iversen." He pronounced it correctly. She noted that too. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much choice, Inspector."

"No," he agreed. "I didn't." He sat, and she saw for the first time how tired he looked—not the theatrical exhaustion of a man performing diligence, but the real kind, ground into the face like soot into a collar. His eyes went to the file on his desk, brown and thick and tied with the regulation red string, and something in his expression flickered, quick as a fish turning under ice.

"I'm going to speak plainly," he said, "because I believe you're a woman who deals in plain speech. I've read every page in this file. Twice."

Ulyana kept her hands folded in her lap and her face arranged into polite blankness—the face she wore for officials, for landlords, for anyone whose power over her depended on thinking her simple.

"The evidence against Mr. Marchmont is very thorough," Rell continued. "Dates matched to travel ledgers. A signature on a delivery docket. Witness statements from two men who claim to have seen him at the warehouse district on the night of the eleventh. Bank withdrawals timed suspiciously close to the purchases of materials." He laid one hand flat on the file. "It is, as I say, thorough."

He paused. She waited. Waiting was the oldest skill she had.

"It is also," Rell said quietly, "too good."

Outside, the sleet ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, a cell door clanged and a voice complained about the cold in the flat accent of a man who had complained about it every day of his life.

"Real guilt is messy, Miss Iversen. Real guilt has gaps, contradictions, a witness who remembers the weather wrong. This file has none. Every document corroborates every other document like a choir that's been rehearsing. And I have been an officer of this police long enough to know what a rehearsed choir sounds like." He looked at her directly. "Someone built this case. Built it brick by brick, with access to our own records and a grudge I haven't yet located. I don't believe Patrik Marchmont killed four people. I believe he was chosen to hang for it because he is rich enough to be a prize, radical enough to be plausible, and isolated enough to have no one who can swear otherwise."

The words landed in Ulyana like stones dropped down a well—one after another, each striking deeper than the last. She had spent weeks assuming the man across this desk was the wall. It had not seriously occurred to her that he might be a crack in it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that.

"Because I need you to understand that what I'm about to offer isn't a trap. Or rather—" the ghost of a bitter smile, "—it's a trap we're both already caught in, and I'm offering us both the same way out."

* * *

He opened the file.

For a moment she thought he was going to show her its contents, and her mind raced ahead through consequences—what could be gleaned, what could be denied—but instead he simply held it up, the red string dangling, and said:

"There is a version of events in which this investigation continues. In which I pursue the faction inside this department that manufactured these documents, in which I subpoena bank clerks and question witnesses and drag this whole rotten arrangement into the light. Do you know what happens then?"

"The family is destroyed either way."

"The trial takes a year. The newspapers take turns dissecting every member of your household—the cook's floorboards, the printer's press, the girl's notebook if it surfaces, your habit of reading other people's mail. Even acquitted, even vindicated, the Marchmonts would be finished in this city. Acquittal is not survival, Miss Iversen. I've seen men walk free from courtrooms straight into ruin."

"And the other version?"

He untied the string slowly, deliberately, letting her watch him do it.

"The other version, the file goes into the bottom drawer of my desk. Marked pending. Pending is a beautiful word—it means the machinery stops moving without ever admitting it stopped. The case against Marchmont starves quietly. No trial. No newspaper circus. Your household packs whatever it wishes to pack and departs the city before spring traffic makes departure conspicuous. I lose my best chance at the men who used my department as their instrument, and you lose—" he weighed it, "—everything except each other. That's the bargain. Silence for silence. Disappearance for disappearance."

Ulyana felt the temptation arrive the way heat arrives from a stove—before thought, before permission, warming her from the outside in. One drawer. One red string retied. And Patrik safe, the house quiet, Lidiya never seeing the inside of a courtroom, Sef keeping his hands, Marta—

Marta.

The warmth curdled.

"Marta Voss is in your custody," Ulyana said. "In a cell on your second floor, coughing blood into a blanket that hasn't been changed in four days—I've paid your clerk enough to know. You're offering to bury a file while you keep a sick woman locked in the dark. That's not silence, Inspector. That's a hostage."

Something moved behind Rell's eyes—shame, maybe, or the memory of shame. "Her release depends on cooperation. That's not my rule."

"Whose rule is it?"

"The same people who built the file. They want a confession from someone in the household. Any someone. It gives their manufactured case a spine. Once there's a signed statement, no one above me will look at the evidence again—they'll have what they want and the drawer stays shut forever." He leaned forward, elbows on the desk. "I can manage the file. I cannot manage her release by myself. But cooperation from the household—a statement, a name offered, anything that lets her walk out the front door with her confession accepted—that I can arrange within a week."

"A confession to what? She owns nothing. She's claimed the contraband as hers alone precisely so that no one else touches it."

"Yes. And it's nearly enough. What's missing is motive. Why would a cook keep pamphlets and a pistol under her floor unless she served a cause or a master? Give the statement a reason and it becomes credible, and once it's credible, she's a closed matter—a fanatic, punished, done. Not a thread leading anywhere."

Ulyana sat very still.

She understood now, fully, what he was asking. Not for a lie exactly—he hadn't asked for a lie, which was somehow worse. He was asking for a shape. A story with a motive in it, fitted around Marta like a dress made to her measurements, and any tailor knows that a garment fitted to one body will hang wrong on another, and the wrongness would fall on Patrik. A servant's fanaticism needed a source. A cause needed a patron. Every road out of Marta's cell ran through the name Marchmont.

"You want me to build the confession," Ulyana said slowly, "out of the man you just told me you believe is innocent."

"I want Marta to go home alive." Rell's voice was flat and unapologetic. "You asked why I'm telling you the file is false. Because I want you to make this choice knowing the truth. I'm not asking you to save an innocent man by condemning a guilty one. I'm asking you to decide whether a guilty system gets to keep its fiction, if the price of refusing is a dead cook and a ruined family." He pushed back from the desk. "That's the arithmetic. I've done it myself, more times than I'll admit. Most days I get the answer wrong."

* * *

She walked home through the sleet because she couldn't bear to sit still in a cab.

The city slid past her, grey and indifferent: shopgirls huddled in doorways, a milk cart's horse steaming, a newsboy shouting something about the budget that nobody bought. Ordinary life, proceeding as if the world were a place where documents told the truth and inspectors were walls and cooks were only cooks. She had lived inside that ordinary world her whole career—had built her livelihood on the gap between what things appeared to be and what they carried. Now the gap had opened beneath her own feet.

*Bury the file.* She turned it over. It was, on its surface, mercy. Patrik freed without trial, the household dispersed to safety, Lidiya's future unbloodied. All of it purchased with one bottom drawer and one shaped confession and Marta's name attached to a crime of conviction she had committed only in part—the pamphlets were hers, yes, but the motive would be sewn onto her like a label, and labels outlive garments.

And the berth. God, the berth. One seat on the last steamer north, secured years ago under a name that belonged to no one. Rell's bargain required the family to vanish quietly—and vanishing quietly required choosing, finally, the impossible arithmetic they'd all been circling for weeks. If she took the bargain, she wasn't only trading Marta's confession. She was deciding, in the same motion, who left and who stayed. Who got the north and who got the wreckage.

She stopped on the bridge over the river. Below, the ice lay whole and white and silent, holding, the way it had held all winter. Eleven days, maybe fewer. Under that crust moved water that had somewhere to be.

The lawful path and the just path had always been strangers to each other—she'd known that since she was nine years old watching her mother pay a harbormaster with money they didn't have. But she had believed, in some stubborn cellar of herself, that they at least pointed the same direction, that law was just justice walking slowly. Rell had shown her the fork in the road, and the fork went opposite ways, and both roads were paved with someone's sacrifice.

If she refused the bargain, she could fight—pursue the forged evidence, expose the faction, drag the rehearsed choir into court. And Marta would cough her lungs out in a cell while the machinery ground through its year of newspapers. If she took it, Marta came home to die in her own bed among her own pots, and Patrik's name went into her confession like a nail, and the family scattered north on the strength of a lie Ulyana had helped stitch.

There was a third thing, of course. There was always a third thing, the thing she couldn't see yet, standing slightly off the road like a lantern in fog. But she couldn't carry it tonight. Tonight she could only walk.

When she came through the kitchen door, smelling of sleet and precinct smoke, Marta looked up from her mending and read her face in one glance, the way she read everything.

"Well?" Marta said. "What did he sell you?"

Ulyana hung her wet coat on its hook. Her hands were steady. Her voice would have to be.

"He sold me a way out," she said. "For everyone. Almost everyone. And the price—" She looked at the floorboards, at the place where the boards lifted, where a pistol and a brother's letters slept in the dark under Marta's feet. "The price is a story. And I have to decide tonight whether I'm the one who tells it."

Marta set down her mending. Outside, faint under the wind, came a sound neither of them named—the river, far off, beginning to talk in its sleep.
