## 16. Lidiya's Notebook Found

The second search came on a Thursday, nine days after the first, and it began with a knock so polite it was almost an apology.

Ulyana answered the door herself because Marta was still in custody and someone had to, and because she had learned in fourteen years of driving and carrying that doors answer better when a woman opens them than when a man does. Two constables stood on the step with their caps in their hands like men calling about a debt, and behind them, at the gate, Inspector Rell sat in an unmarked motorcar with his hat brim low, watching the street both ways.

"Routine follow-up, miss," said the taller one. "Warrant's in order."

She read the warrant over his shoulder before he could stop her—she read everything, that was her nature—and saw the new language in it: *expansion of scope*. The first search had been for pamphlets, presses, correspondence. This one searched for *instruments of conspiracy*, which meant anything, which meant everything.

"You've had your look," she said. "The house hasn't changed since Tuesday."

"Houses change, miss." He stepped past her, gently, the way a tide comes into a room.

* * *

They were thorough in the way of men who had been embarrassed once already. They took up the loose brick in the scullery where Marta kept her good knives. They measured the coal cellar against the exterior wall. One of them lay full-length on the kitchen floor with his cheek against the boards, listening, as if the house might confess through its joists.

Ulyana stood in the doorway and made herself useful in the only way left: she narrated. "That floor was laid in 1906 by a carpenter named Voss who drank. There's a hollow under the third flagstone but it's only where he skimped on rubble. The trunk in the attic is Patrik Marchmont's university things. Latin primers. A rowing medal. You're welcome to read his essays; they're very dull."

The men laughed despite themselves. That was the trick, the old trick: be the most competent person in the room and the room forgets to fear you. She had built this house's invisibility out of such moments, one dull essay at a time, and she watched them now move through it like tourists through a museum she curated.

Sef had gone to the print shop at dawn with instructions not to return until dark. Patrik was at his solicitor's, sitting in a waiting room he hated, being told again that patience was a strategy. The house held only Ulyana and Lidiya, and Lidiya was supposed to be at her lessons across town.

But Lidiya came home early.

Ulyana heard the gate before she saw the girl—heard the particular hesitation in the latch, the half-second pause of someone deciding whether to run. She moved to the hall window and saw her coming up the path, satchel on her shoulder, fifteen years old and wearing the expression Ulyana knew too well from her own mirror: the face of a person calculating whether the thing behind the door can still be protected or must simply be survived.

Lidiya saw the constables' caps hanging on the hall pegs and stopped in the entrance like a bird that has misjudged glass.

"Afternoon, miss," said the tall constable, pleasant as milk. "You live here?"

"She lives here," Ulyana said, stepping between them. "She's fifteen. She has lessons. She knows nothing."

"I didn't say she did, miss."

But he looked at her a moment too long, and Ulyana felt the search change temperature. It was no longer about finding something. It was about the girl standing in the hallway with rain on her collar, and what a girl like that might keep in a room like hers.

They went up together—the tall one, his partner, and Ulyana climbing behind them saying, *her books are schoolbooks, her desk is a school desk,* and hating how quickly the words turned to begging, how competence abandoned her the moment the arithmetic involved a child.

Lidiya's room was small and fierce, the way its occupant was. A bed made with military corners Marta had taught her. A window box with dead herbs she refused to remove because *they might come back.* On the desk: Latin grammar, a tin of pens, a copy of *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* with its spine broken from rereading.

And beneath the false bottom of the pen tin—Ulyana knew about the false bottom, had helped cut it herself, God forgive her—a notebook bound in grey board, small enough to vanish in a coat pocket, dense enough to hang them all.

She watched the constable's hand go past the Latin grammar. She watched it rest on the pen tin. She had perhaps two seconds, and in those two seconds she ran the whole ledger of her life—the letters steamed open, the three burned in winter, the brandy shared with Marta over a pistol under the floor, the forged statement folded against her ribs at this very moment—and understood that every skill she owned was useless against a hand that was already moving.

He lifted the lid. The false bottom tilted. Grey board showed white at the edges.

"That's mine," Lidiya said. Her voice did not shake, which was somehow worse. "It's my diary. It's private."

"A diary." The constable weighed it in his palm like a man judging a fish. "Mind if we have a look at your diary, miss?"

"You need—" Lidiya started, and Ulyana finished for her, flat and final: "He doesn't. Not with a warrant of this breadth. I've read it."

Both men looked at her. She had said too much; she knew it even as the sentence landed. But there was no help for it now. The truth of the household was that everyone in it had learned to read law the way other families learned scripture.

The tall constable opened the notebook to the middle pages, and Ulyana watched his eyes move, and watched the pleasantness drain out of his face by degrees, replaced by the flat satisfaction of a man whose embarrassment has just been paid back with interest.

Because it wasn't a diary. It was a cipher book. Lidiya had invented it herself—not copied, *invented*—a substitution scheme keyed to the dates of suffrage processions, with columns of entries Ulyana could read at a glance because she'd taught the girl half the method: dates of police movements observed from the kitchen window, names abbreviated to initials, sums of money, the schedule of the printing press, and—one entry, dated eleven days ago, and Ulyana's blood went cold reading it upside down—the berth. The steamer. The false name. Written in a child's careful hand because the child had listened at doors the way Ulyana listened at envelopes, because the family had made her exactly what she was.

"There's nothing in that but school exercises," Ulyana said.

"It's coded, miss." He said it almost kindly. "I can't read it, but a man at the office can."

"It's a game. Children make games."

"Yes, miss." He tucked the notebook inside his coat. Then, to Lidiya, with the terrible gentleness officialdom reserves for its smallest prey: "We'll need you to come along and help us with it, miss. Just as a witness. Material witness. You'll be looked after."

"I'm not going anywhere," Lidiya said, "without her." And she pointed at Ulyana.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way collapses do. There was no dramatic moment, no raised voice. Rell came up the path when the constable signalled from the window, and he stood in Lidiya's bedroom among the dead herbs and the broken-spined Wollstonecraft, and he would not meet Ulyana's eyes.

"The magistrate will want her statement," he said. "It's out of my hands. If the book decodes clean—if it's really just observations—I can have her home by tomorrow night."

"And if it doesn't decode clean?"

"Then she's involved, Miss Iversen. However young." He said it like a doctor delivering a prognosis he didn't believe. "You should have sent her away months ago. Any of you. You keep people in houses like this the way misers keep coins, and then you're surprised when the tax man counts."

"We don't keep her," Ulyana said. "She chose us."

Rell finally looked at her, and there was something raw in his face, something almost like grief. "So did I choose this job. It doesn't mean I'm not held by it." He turned to the constables. "Take her round front. No handcuffs. Blanket in the motorcar—it's cold."

Lidiya went down the stairs ahead of them, spine straight, chin level, performing courage she had learned from watching adults perform it. At the door she stopped and turned around, and Ulyana braced herself for tears, for accusation, for the cry of *do something* that would break her heart.

Instead the girl looked at her steadily and said, "The key to the cipher is the procession dates. Tell them the fourth of June and they'll have it wrong forever." A pause. "Don't tell them right."

Then she was gone, into the motorcar, into the machinery, a small figure under a blanket in the back seat, and the gate clicked shut behind her with the softness of a thing done politely, which was how the state did all its worst work.

* * *

The house after they took her was the quietest Ulyana had ever known it, and she had lived through funerals in it.

She walked through the rooms doing the rounds without meaning to—kitchen, scullery, stairs—touching surfaces the way a blind woman reads. Marta's absence had hollowed the ground floor; Lidiya's absence hollowed the upper one. In the girl's bedroom the pen tin still lay open on the desk, its false bottom sprung, and beside it the Latin grammar with a ribbon marking page forty. Homework unfinished. The ordinary debris of an interrupted childhood, more damning than any pamphlet.

When Sef came home at dusk and read the empty room, he put his fist through the pantry door, and Ulyana let him, because a boy needed somewhere to put it and the door could take it better than the world could.

"They took a *child*," he said. "For writing in a *book*."

"For writing down what we did in front of her," Ulyana said. "Don't mistake the crime. The crime is ours. They're only billing the cheapest account."

Patrik arrived an hour later, still wearing his overcoat, and when Ulyana told him he sat down slowly on the hall bench like a man twice his age and put his head in his hands, and for a long time the patriarch of the Marchmont household said nothing at all.

"We get her back," Sef said. "Whatever it takes. We burn the shop, we burn the press, we give them everything—"

"Burning things is how we got here," Patrik said wearily.

"Doing nothing is how Lidiya ends up in front of a magistrate!"

"Enough." Ulyana heard her own voice come out with an edge she hadn't authorized. Both men turned. She stood in the doorway with her coat still on, because some part of her had been ready to leave since morning—to walk to the station, to the courthouse, to wherever they were keeping the girl—and the coat had become a kind of argument. "Neither of you decides what it takes. Because it won't be your name on the paper, or your hands in the machine, or your body in the cell. It will be mine. It always is. So sit down, both of you, and let me think."

They sat. It was, she thought distantly, the first time either of them had ever simply obeyed her, and she found no pleasure in it whatsoever.

* * *

She thought best while working, so she worked. She banked the kitchen fire. She laid out bread and cold meat nobody would eat. She climbed to Marta's room and stood a while among the lavender and the medicines, counting the bottles, doing the grim arithmetic of how long Marta's lungs had left and whether Marta knew it better than any of them did. Under the floor of the scullery, the pistol and the pamphlets waited in their hiding place like organs of a body the surgeons hadn't found yet.

At some point she sat at the kitchen table with a candle and a sheet of plain paper, and she wrote, in her own hand, unencoded:

*Lidiya. Fourth of June is wrong. Everything you wrote down was true. That is the whole of your guilt—that you paid attention. Hold steady. We are coming.*

She had nowhere to send it. There was no address for a cell. But she sealed it anyway, and held it, and understood that she was holding the shape of the next thing, though not yet its contents.

Because here was the truth she had arrived at, sitting alone at midnight in a house stripped of its women: the state had taken Lidiya's notebook and found in it, encoded in a child's invented cipher, a complete map of the family's survival—the berth, the false name, the plan entire. Which meant that whatever bargain Rell had offered, whatever fragile architecture Patrik's solicitors were building, whatever lie Sef had forged in Elena's name—all of it now passed through a magistrate's decoding table in the hands of men looking for a reason.

Unless the map became unreadable. Unless the key changed. Unless someone gave the court a version of events so complete, so documented, so *witnessed*, that the notebook became a schoolgirl's game and the real record lived somewhere else entirely—in a woman who had spent her whole life invisible and was beginning to suspect that invisibility was the one weapon they couldn't confiscate.

There was a knock at the kitchen door. Three knocks, spaced evenly—the clerk's knock, the bribed man's knock, the sound of news bought and paid for.

Ulyana blew out the candle, folded the letter for a daughter who wasn't hers into her coat beside the forged statement, and went to answer it.

The clerk stood on the step with his hat dripping and a paper in his gloved hand, and his face told her before he spoke that the arithmetic had changed again.
