## 17. The Forged Statement

The pen was the first problem.

Ulyana had spent three days acquiring everything else. A sheet of good laid paper, bought loose from a stationer on Voznesensky who asked no questions because she tipped him not to. The ink—iron gall, mixed fresh, because Elena Volkonskaya's letters to Patrik Marchmont had been written with a bottle that sat on a desk by a window facing east, and iron gall browned as it aged, and a statement delivered tomorrow could not look like ink mixed tonight. She had learned that from years of reading other people's mail: paper lies in its texture, ink lies in its color, but time only lies if you help it.

The pen was the problem because Sef's hands shook.

Not always. When he set type his hands were surgeon-steady; she had watched him pull proofs at four in the morning, letters no larger than a thumbnail, and never once a smudge. But put a pen in those same fingers and ask him to imitate a woman's hand—a woman he had never met, whose writing existed for him now only in the two samples Ulyana had stolen from the drawer of Patrik's desk—and the steadiness went out of him like heat from an open door.

"I can't," he said, and then, furious at himself, "I *can*, I just—give me a minute."

They were in the back room of the print shop, which had stopped being a print shop eleven days ago when Sef pulled the press apart and buried its heart under the coal cellar. What remained was a workbench, a lamp turned low, and the smell of solvent that clung to Sef's sleeves the way incense used to cling to altar boys. Ulyana stood at his shoulder. She did not sit. Sitting would have meant this might take all night, and she needed it not to take all night, because the clerk at the courthouse opened his window at eight and closed it at nine, and her bribe bought exactly one envelope's passage into Inspector Rell's morning tray.

"Again," she said.

Sef wrote *Elena Andreyevna Volkonskaya* beneath the two genuine signatures. The capital E looped too high. In the genuine hand, the loop bent forward, urgent, as though the writer were already reaching for the next word.

"Again."

He wrote it seven more times. On the ninth attempt the loop bent correctly, but the *v* in Volkonskaya came out angular where Elena's was soft, a woman who wrote her consonants the way she presumably entered rooms—apologetically, taking up less space than offered.

"You're thinking about the letters," Ulyana said. "Don't think about them."

"What else is there to think about?"

"The weather. Marta's cough. Whether Lidiya has eaten." She heard her own voice come out flat as a ledger line and made herself soften it. "You're not forging a signature, Sef. You're being Elena for thirty seconds. She was frightened when she wrote those letters—frightened and happy, both at once. Write like someone who wants to be believed and hates wanting it."

He looked at her. In the lamplight he looked fifteen instead of nineteen, the age Lidiya was, and something in his face made her want to take the pen away from him and carry this weight herself, the way she had carried every weight since she was six years old standing on a dock watching her mother's ship pull out without her.

But she couldn't. This was the one thing she could not carry. Her hand was known—her hand appeared in a hundred household accounts, delivery receipts, the neat Cyrillic of a woman who kept other people's secrets in her own script. If the statement was examined and the hand matched hers, they would hang the forgery on her before lunch. It had to be Sef's crime or no one's.

On the eleventh try, the signature was right.

She held the sample beside it until her eyes watered. The loop. The soft v. The small, hurried period after the surname, as if even signing her name Elena could not quite commit to the full stop.

"That's her," Ulyana said quietly. "God forgive us. That's her."

Sef put down the pen the way you put down a knife after slaughtering something. "Do you want to know what I keep thinking?"

"No."

"That it's the first true thing I've ever printed."

She looked at him. He wasn't being clever. His face had gone past cleverness entirely, out the other side, into the country where the young live when they discover that the world requires dirty hands and their elders have been lying about it.

"The statement is true," she said. "Every word of it. Patrik was with her. Four people are dead and an innocent man is going to be tried for it, and the one person who can stop it won't lift a pen because she's ashamed. So we lift the pen for her. That isn't a lie, Sef. That's a translation."

"It's a forgery," he said. "It's a forged document submitted to a magistrate, and if Rell's people compare it against her real hand—" He stopped. He didn't have to finish. They had both done the arithmetic; the numbers hadn't changed overnight.

"If it's detected," Ulyana said, "it destroys Patrik beyond saving, it destroys you, it destroys me, and it gives the prosecution everything they've wanted since the day the bomb went off. Yes. I know. I've read enough confessions to know how they end." She gathered the papers—the practice sheets, the drafts, every false start—and fed them one by one into the stove. The iron-gall ink flared briefly green before it blackened. "Which is why it will not be detected. Watch the fire until it's ash, then grind the ash into the cinders. I'll be back before dawn."

"Where are you going?"

"To do the last thing I'm good at," she said. "Reading someone's mail."

* * *

Elena Volkonskaya lived on the third floor of a building that had once been respectable and was now merely occupied. Ulyana climbed the stairs with a basket on her arm—eggs, a jar of honey, the props of a neighborly call—and rehearsed nothing, because she had learned long ago that rehearsed words came out sounding rehearsed, and the whole art of her life was sounding like she belonged wherever she stood.

Elena opened the door on the second knock. She was younger than Ulyana had pictured—not a girl, but near it, thirty perhaps, with the kind of pale, careful beauty that looks like it has been instructed to stay still. She recognized Ulyana immediately. That was the first surprise. The second was that she stepped aside to let her in without a word, as though she had been expecting this visit the way you expect a tooth to ache.

"I brought honey," Ulyana said, foolishly, and then abandoned the basket and the pretense together. "You know why I'm here."

"I know why you're here." Elena stood by the window, arms folded, not offering a chair. East-facing window. Morning light would fall across that desk. Ulyana noted it the way she noted everything now, uselessly, cataloguing details for a future that might not include her. "Patrik sent you."

"Patrik doesn't know I'm here."

That landed. Elena's chin lifted a fraction—the pride of a woman discovering she mattered outside her arrangement, and hating how much she liked it.

"He doesn't," Ulyana pressed, "and he mustn't. I came alone because what I have to say is mine, and so is what I'm about to ask, and neither of us can give him to anyone. Not to the court. Not to each other." She took a breath. This was the part she had built in her head forty times on the walk over, and now that she stood inside it, the architecture felt thin. "Madam. They will try him in three weeks. The evidence is manufactured—I know it is, you know it is, and there exists exactly one sentence in your voice that can prove it. One sentence, signed. No one will ever learn why you wrote it. I will see to your protection myself, with my own name attached to the promise, and my name is worth very little except that I have never once broken it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then he hangs for a thing he didn't do, and you live the rest of your life knowing the exact date it became true that you let him."

The words came out harder than she'd intended. She watched them strike—watched Elena go white above the collar of her dress—and understood, with a sick lurch, that she had just done to this woman what Inspector Rell's machinery did to everyone: mistaken leverage for persuasion.

But Elena didn't order her out. She turned to the window. For a long moment the only sound was the building settling around them, pipes knocking somewhere below like a slow heart.

"Do you know why I won't sign?" Elena said finally. "It isn't fear. Fear I could manage; I've managed it for two years. It's that the statement would be true, and the signature would be a lie, and I cannot—" Her voice caught and steadied, caught and steadied, a woman holding a door shut against weather. "I have spent my whole life being one kind of false or another. The wife of a man who barely sees me. The mistress of a man who sees me too well. This—" she touched the windowsill, the desk, the small ordered territory of her compromise— "this is the one room where I am exactly what I appear to be, which is nothing. Nobody. And you're asking me to become somebody in front of a magistrate, in front of my husband's lawyers, in front of *everyone*, for a man who will be grateful to me for exactly as long as gratitude costs him nothing."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "I am."

"You don't understand—"

"I understand better than you think." And she found, to her own surprise, that she was going to say the next thing, the true thing, the thing she had told no one in this city. "I have spent twenty-two years being nobody. I open other people's letters. I drive other people's carriages. I am so good at not existing that the state itself forgot to watch me, and do you know what that is worth? Everything, until the day you need to be somebody. Then it's worth nothing, and there's no way back." She stepped closer. "You are protecting the last scrap of your invisibility, madam. I am begging you to spend it, because I promise you—invisible things get confiscated eventually anyway. At least his life will buy yours back."

Elena looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes—not agreement, not refusal. Recognition, maybe. Two women measuring each other across the same narrow bridge.

"I can't," she said at last. It came out almost gently. "Tell him—no. Don't tell him anything. Tell him nothing."

Ulyana picked up her basket. At the door she paused, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "he has never once spoken of you carelessly. Not drunk, not frightened, not to save himself. In my experience, that is rarer than honesty."

She heard Elena breathe in behind her, and left before either of them could ruin it with another word.

* * *

The print shop again, past midnight. The stove still warm. Sef asleep with his head on his arms atop the workbench, and the finished statement lying beside him under a clean sheet of blotting paper—waiting, patient as a loaded gun.

Ulyana woke him with two fingers on his shoulder. He came up blinking, saw her face, and knew.

"She refused."

"She refused."

Sef sat with that for a moment. Then he reached under the bench and brought out the sheet he'd prepared hours ago, before she returned—because he had known too, some deep boyish part of him had known how these stories go—and smoothed it flat with both palms.

"Then it's this or nothing," he said. "I've practiced until I can do it in my sleep. Look at it. Look as hard as you can. If there's one thing wrong, tell me now and we burn it and think of something else."

She looked. She looked longer than she had ever looked at any document, including the sealed dispatch that had started all of this, because that letter had only named a liar and this one *made* one. The loop bent forward. The v was soft. The ink was two days old by lamplight trickery—diluted, blotted, breathed on. The period sat after the surname like a held breath released.

And the sentence itself, the single sentence, the true lie: *I confirm that Patrik Marchmont was in my company on the night of the fourteenth, from evening until morning, and can speak to it.*

True in substance. False in origin. A translation, Sef had called it. She turned the page over as if the truth might be hiding on the back.

"It's right," she said. "It's perfect. God forgive me, it's perfect."

"So we deliver it."

She didn't answer at once. She stood in the yellow circle of the lamp and felt, with terrible clarity, the exact spot where her line lay—the line she had drawn at fourteen when a customs officer searched her mother's trunk and Ulyana lied about what was inside, the line she had rebuilt every year since, brick by brick: *I will protect people, but I will not manufacture truth.* Twenty-two years of carrying other people's real letters, real words, real sins, and never once adding to them. It was not much of a principle. It was the only one she owned outright.

Marta's cough floated up through the floorboards from the house, two streets away in her memory if not in fact. Lidiya in a cell, learning what the state thought of children who kept notebooks. Patrik in his study, aging ten years a week, practicing dignity the way a drowning man practices strokes.

Principle, she thought, is what you call the cargo you refuse to put down—even when putting it down would save the people who taught you to carry.

"Fold it," she said. "Envelope. Plain wax, the gray seal, the clerk's office style. You know it."

Sef worked fast, hands steady now, steady as they'd ever been over type. When he handed her the finished envelope it weighed nothing at all, less than an egg, less than a moth, and it contained the destruction or salvation of five people and possibly six, counting Elena, who would wake tomorrow to find her name doing work she had refused to do.

"If they catch this," Sef said quietly, "they'll charge you with it. Not me. Your name will be on the courier log—you bribed the clerk yourself. Ulyana, they'll *hang*—"

"They won't hang women for forgery. They'll just take everything else." She buttoned her coat. Outside, far off, the river made a sound she had been hearing in her sleep for days—a deep, grinding groan, like a giant turning over. The ice was thinking about breaking. "Go home to the house. Sleep. Tomorrow, whatever happens, you're a printer's apprentice who knows nothing about envelopes."

"And you?"

She paused at the door, the envelope inside her coat, over her heart, where a lantern would hang.

"I'm a driver," she said. "I make deliveries."

* * *

The courthouse steps at ten minutes to eight were slick with the season's first refrozen melt, and Ulyana climbed them slowly, an unremarkable woman in a gray coat, carrying among a hundred other errands one gray-sealed envelope addressed in a clerk's bland hand to the Office of the Investigating Magistrate. The bribed clerk—Pavel, damp-palmed, terrified, paid enough to feed his family for a year and hating himself in the specific way that men hate themselves when money works—was already sorting the morning tray.

"Festival deliveries," she said, loud enough for the guard by the door to hear. "From the merchant Iversen's office. Routine correspondence, he said to tell you, nothing requiring receipt."

Pavel took the envelope without looking at it, which was the entire art of his profession, and slid it into the second row of the tray—the row Rell's secretary collected at half past eight, before the junior clerks got their hands on anything.

"There's a steamer leaving when the ice breaks," Pavel whispered, staring fixedly at his papers. "My cousin says they're loading extra coal. Whatever you're doing, woman—finish it fast."

"Almost finished," Ulyana said.

She walked down the steps into the white morning. Behind her, in a building full of laws, a lie was traveling upward through the machinery toward the one honest man inside it, and ahead of her the river lay under its ice like a held breath, groaning once more, so deep she felt it through her boot soles—groaning the way a door groans just before it opens, or just before it breaks.
