## 18. Ice Cracking

The river spoke first.

It had been speaking for days, if Ulyana was honest—a low complaint beneath the ice, like a man turning in his sleep—but on the morning after the forged statement left her hands, it woke properly. She stood at the kitchen window with a cup of tea going cold and watched the surface of the Neva shudder. A crack ran out from the middle of the channel, quick as a struck match, and stopped at the far bank as though embarrassed by its own reach.

Tomorrow the steamer would load.

She counted it the way she counted everything now: not in days but in what remained to be done inside them. One berth. One name. One girl in a holding cell who did not yet know she was leaving.

Marta came down the stairs before six, and Ulyana heard her before she saw her—the new sound, the one that had arrived with her release and would not leave. A whistle in the breath. A small torn-paper sound at the top of each inhale, as though Marta's lungs had been folded and refolded too many times by hands that didn't care about creases.

"You're up," Marta said.

"The river's breaking."

"I heard it from my bed." She lowered herself into a chair, and Ulyana pretended not to notice how carefully she did it, how she chose the chair nearest the stove without appearing to have chosen anything at all. "Eleven days they kept me in that room with the wet walls, and now the ice wants to go before I do."

Ulyana set fresh tea in front of her. Under the floorboards overhead—under the kitchen floor itself, where the pamphlets had been and the pistol and the letters from Marta's brother—the house was quiet in the way of a thing that has been emptied on purpose. Sef had slept three hours and was already at the press next door, printing nothing, printing anything, keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't do the arithmetic he couldn't afford. Patrik Marchmont had not come down at all. She'd heard him pacing his study past two o'clock, the floorboards giving him away the way they gave everyone away; she knew this house's confessions better than any priest.

"They'll bring Lidiya to the trial?" Marta asked.

"As a material witness." The words tasted official. Ulyana had learned them from the clerk she bribed, along with the visiting hours and the name of the matron who took sugar in her tea. "Fifteen years old, and they've decided she's material."

"She learned it from us." Marta wrapped both hands around the cup. Her knuckles were worse than they'd been—swollen, the joints of a woman who had knelt on cold stone floors for thirty years and been thanked in coin. "We taught her to listen. We taught her to keep things written down. And now—" The whistle in her breath caught. She rode through it, jaw set, the way she rode through everything. "Now the state has enrolled her in our school and marked her present."

"Rell says—"

"Don't tell me what Rell says. Tell me what Rell will *do*."

Ulyana looked out at the river. The crack had widened; open water showed black between the floes, moving, alive, indifferent. "He'll do nothing more," she said. "He told me so himself, in the way men tell you things when they want credit for telling you. The statement worked. The file on Patrik is buried. But there's no protecting anyone further. His words. He's spent whatever he had buying our silence, and now we're expected to spend ours."

"And Lidiya?"

"Lidiya is the price that doesn't concern him."

Marta drank her tea. Outside, the ice groaned again—a long, deep note, like a ship's cable taking strain—and somewhere downriver a dog answered it, absurdly, faithfully.

"Then tomorrow," Marta said, "one of us puts her on that boat."

"One of us," Ulyana agreed, and did not say which one, because the decision had already been made, had perhaps been made years ago in a railway yard in Finland by a girl hiding in a coal truck, and all this time had only been waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

* * *

The clerk's name was Osip Vasilyevich, and he met her at half past ten in the archive annex, among shelves of files that smelled of dust and mildew and other people's ruined lives. He was a soft man with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent apologetic hunch of someone who had spent twenty years being shouted at by superiors, and he took her money the way he took everything—with a wince, as if hoping the transaction would conclude before anyone noticed him participating in it.

"The girl is in the west wing," he murmured, eyes down. "Matron Kessler has her. Visiting is permitted Thursdays, but—" he glanced at the door, then at the banknotes disappearing into his ledger drawer, "—Thursdays are a formality. The matron owes me. Her nephew's exemption papers passed through my desk last autumn, and they were not entirely... accurate."

"Today isn't Thursday," Ulyana said.

"No. Today isn't Thursday." He almost smiled. It was the closest he would ever come to heroism, and both of them knew it. "You have until the afternoon count. Two hours, perhaps less. If anyone asks, you're a laundress delivering linens. There's a basket by the door. Take the basket."

The corridor smelled of carbolic and boiled cabbage. Ulyana walked it with the laundry basket on her hip and her face arranged into the blankness she had spent a lifetime perfecting—the face of a woman whose business is sheets, whose opinions are none, whose presence registers on no one. Doors opened off either side. Behind one she heard singing, tuneless and defiant; behind another, silence of the kind that is practiced rather than empty.

Lidiya sat on a narrow bed in a room with a high barred window, writing.

Of course she was writing. She had her notebook on her knee—the replacement, the third, because the first two had been confiscated and read by men who understood neither the code nor the child—and she looked up when Ulyana entered with an expression Ulyana recognized from mirrors: watchful first, relieved second, relieved only after it had checked whether relief was safe.

"You shouldn't be here," Lidiya said.

"No. I'm a laundress."

"You're a terrible laundress. You hold baskets wrong."

Ulyana sat down beside her. Up close the girl looked thinner, the sharpness of her cheekbones newly architectural, but her eyes were steady and her notebook was full. Fifteen years old, detained as a material witness in a case that no longer existed, held anyway because the machinery, once fed, does not voluntarily spit anything back out.

"How are they treating you?"

"Boringly. That's their whole strategy. Matron Kessler thinks if nothing happens long enough, I'll forget what I'm protecting." Lidiya closed the notebook. "She doesn't know that forgetting is the one thing this family never taught me."

Ulyana looked at the barred window, at the grey light coming through it, at the river visible beyond—she could see the ice from here, could see the dark seam of open water widening like a wound. Tomorrow the steamer. Tonight, arrangements.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to listen without arguing, which I know is difficult for you, because you argue the way your mother must have argued, may God rest her, and Marta argues, and Patrik argues, and I am the only person in that house who has ever simply done the thing while everyone else discussed it."

Lidiya went still. She was smart enough to know when the ground was shifting under a conversation.

"There's a berth," Ulyana said. "On the steamer north. It's been waiting under a false name since before you came to us—it was bought for exactly the night that's coming, and there is one ticket, one name, and the name goes on tomorrow morning at the dock." She turned and looked at the girl directly. "And the name is yours."

The silence lasted four seconds. Then Lidiya said, "No."

"Yes."

"*No.* You bought it for—for whoever needed it most, that was always the point, and Marta's lungs are ruined, you can hear her breathing from the hallway, she needs—"

"Marta cannot survive the voyage and we both know it. She knows it better than anyone; she'd never say it aloud, but she told me with her hands yesterday, holding her own chest like something borrowed. Patrik stays because he's the patron and his name is his armor even now, especially now—cleared men don't flee, fleeing un-clears them. Sef won't go while the press is running and I couldn't make him and wouldn't try. And me—" Ulyana paused, and found the truth sitting there, patient, enormous. "I have testimony to give. Or I will have. There are things I know that only I can say out loud, in a courtroom, on the record, where they become real. Someone has to stand up in that building and refuse to disappear, or everything we did—all of it, the burning and the lying and the forging—was just survival, and survival alone isn't worth what it cost."

"You'd stay for *that*? For a courtroom?"

"For you," Ulyana said. "The courtroom is how."

Lidiya looked down at her notebook. Her thumb moved over its cover, back and forth, the way it did when she was thinking hard enough to hurt. When she spoke again her voice was smaller, and younger, and fifteen.

"What if the boat sinks."

"It won't sink."

"What if they stop it. What if they ask for papers and my name isn't—"

"The name on the ticket will match the name in the manifest, because I will make it match. That is the entire profession I have built, Lidiya. Making names match. You are getting on that boat with papers so clean they'll apologize to you."

"And after? After I'm north? What do I—"

"You write to us." Ulyana took the girl's chin gently and turned her face up. "Under a name we agree on tonight, through an address Marta will give you, and every letter gets read by me first and delivered second, and it will be the first correspondence in the history of this family that travels honestly, because I'll already know everything in it." She smiled, and felt her face do something unfamiliar with the expression, as though the muscles had gone stiff from disuse. "You'll write to me especially. You'll complain about the cold and the food and the boredom, and I will read every word twice."

Lidiya's eyes were bright and furious. "I hate this plan."

"I know."

"I hate that it's a good plan."

"That's the worst kind," Ulyana said, and pulled the girl against her shoulder, and let her shake there for exactly as long as the afternoon count allowed, which was not nearly long enough.

At the door, Lidiya caught her sleeve. "Ulyana. The code in my notebooks. Did you ever—"

"Page forty-one," Ulyana said. "The substitution shifts by the date. I figured it out in November. You double-encrypted the names but not the places." She paused. "You'll fix that before you fill the fourth one."

The girl stared at her, betrayed and admiring in equal measure, which Ulyana supposed was the truest portrait of their relationship anyone would ever draw.

* * *

Rell came at dusk.

Not to the house—to the corner of the embankment where Ulyana walked every evening, a habit she had kept precisely because it was known, because a woman with predictable habits looks like a woman with nothing to hide. He stood by the railing in his good coat, hatless despite the cold, watching the ice with the expression of a man auditing a demolition he'd authorized.

"Frau Iversen."

"Herr Inspektor."

They watched the river together. A floe the size of a chapel groaned loose from the bank, turned, and began the long slow journey toward the sea.

"The statement was accepted," Rell said. "The file on Marchmont is closed. Quietly. There will be no announcement, because an announcement would invite questions about who manufactured the evidence in the first place, and certain colleagues of mine prefer that those questions sleep." He exhaled, breath smoking. "Your forgery was competent. Better than competent. The ink aging was a nice touch—I assume that was the printer boy."

"Sef has many talents."

"He'll want to be careful with them. Talents are how people end up in my files." He turned to look at her then, and in the failing light his face was harder to read than usual—not cruel, not kind, just tired in the specific way of a man who has looked into the machine and seen the gears. "I want to be clear about what I've purchased and what I haven't. I've buried a false case. I have not, and cannot, protect this household from further attention. The raid found nothing, thanks to you, but raids recur. Custody recurs. If the girl is detained again, there is nothing I can do, and I need you to understand that I am not saying it to threaten you. I'm saying it because it's the truth, and truth is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit."

"Then spend it plainly. What happens to her?"

"The trial is a formality for a formality. Material witness in a collapsed case—the prosecution wants her on record and forgotten. If she's gone before the date, the file closes itself." He looked back at the river. "Gone, Frau Iversen. Do you understand me? North, across the border, beyond the reach of my colleagues' paperwork. I am not advising you. I am describing weather."

"The ice is breaking," Ulyana said.

"So it is." He put his hat on. At the edge of the lamplight he paused, and without turning back: "For what it's worth—which is nothing, which is less than nothing—I believe you when you say Marchmont is innocent. I believed it from the start. That's why the file bothered me." A beat. "Testimony is a strange weapon, Frau Iversen. It cuts the hand that holds it. Make sure the hand is yours by choice."

He walked away along the embankment, a dark shape diminishing against the lamps, and Ulyana stood at the railing and listened to the river tear itself apart.

* * *

That evening the household gathered in the kitchen, because kitchens are where households gather when drawing rooms have become dangerous furniture. Marta presided from her chair by the stove, wrapped in two shawls, supervising a soup she was not cooking. Sef sat on the floor with his back against the cupboard, ink to his elbows, having come straight from the press. Patrik Marchmont stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, a patriarch-shaped silhouette, and when he finally turned, his face carried the particular devastation of a powerful man confronting a problem power cannot solve.

"The boat loads tomorrow," he said. "One berth. We settled this."

"We settled nothing," Marta said. "We argued beautifully and decided nothing, which is our national art."

"The berth is Lidiya's." Ulyana said it flatly, the way she announced departures and arrivals. "It's decided. I visited her today. She'll be brought to the dock by Osip Vasilyevich—he owes the matron, the matron owes him, the chain holds. She boards under the name on the ticket, and the boat leaves at eleven, and by the time anyone thinks to check a witness list, she'll be water and distance away."

Patrik crossed to her. Close up, she could see how the weeks had worked him over—the greying at the temples gone fully grey, the fine hands unsteady. "And you," he said. "You walk into that courthouse the day after and hand them your confession. Reading their letters. Forging a signature. You'll stand in front of God and the Ministry and describe your crimes in detail, for *her*, for a girl already safe—"

"Not already. Safe when the boat clears the harbor. Until then, everything is fuel."

"You'll lose everything. Your position, your papers, possibly your freedom—they'll suspend the sentence or they won't, and either way no household in this city will employ a driver who reads mail and testifies about it—"

"I know exactly what I'll lose." Ulyana held his gaze. She had rehearsed this, not the words but the stance, the way one rehearses a bridge before crossing it. "I have spent my whole life carrying things for other people, Patrik. Letters, secrets, blame, children. Carrying is how I earned my place, and carrying is also how I stayed invisible, and invisibility seemed like safety until I watched what it cost—watched Marta pay for us in lungs, watched Lidiya learn to hide at fifteen because we taught her nothing else. So yes. I'll stand in that courtroom and be entirely visible, once, on purpose. Because justice outside the law begins with refusing to disappear, and I have been disappearing professionally for thirty years, and I am very good at it, and I quit."

The kitchen was silent except for the stove and, faintly, from outside, the river—a deep grinding exhalation, the ice surrendering a little more of itself to the water.

Sef spoke first, from the floor, grinning up at her with soot on his cheekbone. "She quits."

"She quits," Ulyana confirmed.

Marta laughed—a short, rusted sound that turned into coughing, and Ulyana was at her side before the coughing ended, one hand braced against the thin back, feeling the ribs move like something fragile in a paper bag. When it passed, Marta gripped her wrist hard, harder than her strength should have allowed, and said quietly, for Ulyana alone:

"You take care of that girl's letters until mine come. And you write to her yourself, not just deliver. A woman who reads everything deserves to write something."

"I will."

"And Ulyana—" Marta's eyes were wet and fierce. "When you testify. Say my brother's name. They never printed it. Not once, in any of the papers. Say it where it lands."

"I will," Ulyana said again, and meant it the way she had never meant anything delivered secondhand.

Patrik stood apart, defeated by the arithmetic of love, and at last he bowed his head—an aristocrat's concession, formal and genuine—and said, "Then we do it your way. God help us all."

Outside, the ice gave a great cracking report, sharp as rifle fire, and every one of them flinched, and then, absurdly, Sef laughed, and then Marta, wheezing, and then even Patrik, the brittle shared laughter of people standing on a floor that is about to become water.

Later, when the house had gone dark, Ulyana climbed to her narrow room under the eaves and laid out tomorrow on the bed like instruments on a tray: the ticket, the envelope of clean papers, the clothes Lidiya would travel in, the letter—unsealed—that the girl would find in her pocket halfway up the river, written in Ulyana's own hand, the first letter she had composed in thirty years of reading everyone else's.

From the window she could see the dock, the lamplit gangway, the steamer breathing smoke into the freezing air. The river ran black and broken beneath the ice, moving at last, going somewhere.

Tomorrow, she thought. One name on a ticket. One girl on a gangway. One courtroom waiting with its doors open like a mouth.

She blew out the lamp and lay down fully dressed, and listened to the ice break all night, closer and closer, like footsteps coming up the stairs.
