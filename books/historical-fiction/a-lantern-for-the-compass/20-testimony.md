## 20. Testimony

The steamer's horn went off at six, and Ulyana heard it from the courthouse steps.

She had meant to hear it from the dock. That had been the plan all night as she'd sat in the clerk's back room with Lidiya's cold hand in hers, waiting for the constable's rounds to shift, for the door to open, for the girl to be passed to her like contraband—because that was what Lidiya was now, in the eyes of the state: a parcel of testimony to be held until trial. But the plan had ended at the dock. The plan had never accounted for what Ulyana would feel, standing there on the quay with the river ice groaning like a great animal turning over in its sleep, watching the *Vera* take on coal, and understanding that if she put her own name nowhere—if she simply stepped back into her coat and her cap and her invisibility—the machinery that had swallowed Marta and Lidiya and Patrik Marchmont would go on swallowing, and she would have spent twenty years learning every gear of it only to let it run.

"You're not coming," Patrik had said at the dock. Not a question. He had seen it in her face before she'd known it herself.

"No."

"Ulyana." His voice cracked on the second syllable, the patriarch becoming mortal again in a single word. "They will eat you."

"Then they'll choke," she said, "on everything I've read."

She had kissed Marta's cheek—Marta smelled of camphor and defeat, her lungs whistling like the wind through the kitchen window—and pressed the ticket into Lidiya's fist and closed the girl's fingers over it and said, in Russian, so no one on that English-speaking dock could follow: *You are the family now. Write to me.* And then she had walked away before her legs could learn cowardice, up the hill through the fish-smell and the gull-cry, past the shuttered shops, to the grey bulk of the assize courthouse, where the doors opened at seven and justice was conducted, they said, in daylight.

The horn sounded again behind her, longer, and then faded, and she did not turn around.

* * *

The courtroom smelled of floor wax and wet wool. She had expected marble; it was wood, scarred, human-sized, and this steadied her somehow—that the place where four people had died on paper was only a room where men got tired.

She had dressed carefully. Not her driving clothes. Not the cap and the coat that made her furniture. Marta's black dress, taken from the wardrobe Marta would not need this spring, buttoned to the throat, and her hair pinned up in a way that made her look like what she was: a woman who intended to be seen.

The usher asked her business. "I'm here to testify," she said, and the words came out of her mouth sounding like someone else's, someone loud, someone who had never once stood in a doorway waiting to be noticed or waved through. "In the matter of the Crown against Marchmont. Tell them Ulyana Iversen is here, and tell them I won't wait."

They made her wait anyway, two hours on a bench between a woman with a basket of eels and a boy chewing his sleeve, but she had waited her whole life in one form or another, and these two hours were the last ones she would ever spend being patient, so she spent them memorizing the room: the judge's high chair, the jury box with its nine bored faces and three attentive ones, the dock where Patrik sat in a borrowed suit, grey-faced, hands folded like a man at his own funeral. When he saw her come up the aisle he half-rose, and the bailiff pushed him down, and across the whole width of the room his eyes said what his voice could not: *you fool, you brave, impossible fool.*

Inspector Rell sat at the prosecution table and did not look surprised.

That was the thing she noticed first, and it frightened her more than anything else that morning: he had expected her. There was a folder in front of him that had not been there yesterday—she knew because she bribed clerks, because knowing things was the only country she had ever held citizenship in—and when the prosecutor called, late in the morning, after the handwriting experts and the railway timetables had done their careful lying, "Does the Crown have further witnesses?" Rell leaned over and murmured something, and the prosecutor's face went through a small storm of its own, and then he said:

"The Crown calls Ulyana Iversen."

* * *

They swore her in and she gave her name clearly, spelling it, letter by letter, the way she spelled it on envelopes she had no right to open: *I-V-E-R-S-E-N*. Her voice did not shake. Later she would wonder at that. At the time it seemed the most natural thing in the world, as though her voice had been kept in a drawer all these years for exactly this occasion and came out oiled.

"What is your occupation?" the prosecutor asked.

"I was driver and secretary to the Marchmont household. I also read their letters."

A stir. The prosecutor blinked. "Read them?"

"All of them. Before delivery. For eight years." She let the number sit in the room. "It began as protection. The household received threats—arson, worse. Someone had to know what was coming through the door. I steamed the seals, read, resealed, delivered. No letter reached that house unread, and no letter left it unwitnessed. I am telling you this freely. No one has accused me. I accuse myself."

The judge leaned forward. "Madam, you understand you may incriminate yourself."

"I understand, my lord, that everyone in this court has already decided I incriminate myself by existing. I would rather do it out loud."

Someone laughed—a juror, quickly smothered—and the laugh broke something loose in the room, some tension that had been holding the proceedings shut like wax on an envelope.

"Then tell the court," the prosecutor said slowly, "what you know of the bombing on Kessel Street."

And she told them. All of it, in order, because order was her native language. She told them about the circular in winter warning of raids on radical households, and how she had burned three letters rather than let them be found. She told them about the sealed dispatch in wax she did not recognize—official, not personal—and how she had held it over the kettle and watched the seal surrender, and read the words that named Patrik Marchmont financier of a murder he had not committed, and how even then, even with dread sitting on her chest like a cat, she had noticed the wrongness of the paper. "The watermark was right," she said. "The ink was right. But the folding was wrong. Government dispatches fold twice lengthwise. This one folded once, crosswise, the way an amateur folds a letter to fit an envelope he bought in a hurry. I fold letters for a living, my lord. I have folded ten thousand. I know how paper travels."

She watched the jury watch her. Nine bored faces had become twelve attentive ones.

She told them about Elena—not her surname, she refused that, and the judge, after a long moment, allowed it—"a woman whose presence with Mr. Marchmont on the night in question would ruin her own family, which is why she would not sign. And so—" and here her voice finally caught, and she let it catch, let the jury see it, because a witness who does not flinch is a witness who has rehearsed, and she wanted them to believe her precisely because she hurt— "and so we forged her signature. I carried the statement to the police. I am guilty of forgery, of perjury, of eight years of reading mail that was not mine. I will plead to all of it. But I will not plead to silence."

"Why?" The prosecutor again, softly, almost gently, as though he too had been waiting years for someone to answer this question. "Why stand here and burn yourself?"

She looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the jury, at their faces, at the eel-woman who had followed her in and stood at the back with her basket.

"Because I have spent my whole life invisible," she said. "I was born below decks on a ship that wasn't ours. I learned early that the ones who carry things are the ones who get thrown overboard when the cargo gets heavy, and that the safest place in any house is the doorway, where no one looks. I made a profession of it. I made a *virtue* of it. And then the state took a fifteen-year-old girl out of my house because she wrote down what she saw in a notebook, and it took a dying woman because she wouldn't give up anyone else, and it built a case out of folded paper against an innocent man, and it did all of this in daylight, in rooms like this one, counting on the fact that the people it crushed were people no one had ever bothered to see." She set both hands flat on the rail of the witness box, and the wood was cool under her palms, and she thought: *remember this. You will want to remember this.* "So here I am. See me. My name is Ulyana Iversen. I read your letters. I know how your paper folds. And I am telling you that the evidence against Patrik Marchmont was manufactured—and I can prove it, because the men who manufactured it were careless, and I have spent eight years being paid to notice what careless men leave behind."

* * *

They adjourned for an hour, and she sat in the witness box because no one told her to leave, drinking water a kind bailiff brought, and across the room Inspector Rell watched her with an expression she had never seen on a policeman's face and could not, at first, name.

It was relief. It was a man setting down something heavy.

When court resumed, the defense counsel—hired that morning with money Sef had raised by selling the press, the beautiful reckless press, to a rival printer at a loss that made him weep—stood and said, "My lord, the defense recalls Inspector Rell."

Rell took the oath in a voice like gravel settling. And then, while the prosecutor stared at him as though he had grown a second head, Rell turned to the jury and did the thing Ulyana had gambled everything on, the thing he had promised her on the prison steps two nights ago with his hat in his hands and his career in ruins around his ankles: he corroborated.

"I have been nineteen years in the force," he said. "I built the file against Marchmont myself, and I believed it—for a week. Then I checked it. The informant who supplied the ledger naming Marchmont cannot be produced because he does not exist under the name given. The bank confirmation of the transfer arrived at my office before the bank records show the request was made. I flagged these discrepancies"—his jaw worked—"to my superiors, and was instructed that discrepancies were 'administrative noise.' I was instructed, in effect, to stop noticing." He looked down at his hands. "I have been paid my whole career to notice things. When I was ordered to stop, I should have resigned. Instead I did what she says she did—I waited in doorways. I am done waiting in doorways. The evidence against Patrik Marchmont was fabricated within the force, by persons unknown to me, and I believe it was fabricated to destroy him in favor of parties whose names I do not yet have—but I know where to look, and I am looking, and I am saying it here because here is the only place left where saying it costs me less than silence."

The courtroom did not erupt. Courtrooms, Ulyana learned that day, are quieter than churches at the moments that matter most. The silence had weight and temperature. In the dock Patrik Marchmont bowed his head over his folded hands, and his shoulders moved once, twice, and were still.

* * *

The verdict came the next noon: not guilty, returned inside ninety minutes, and the crowd outside—there was a crowd now, eel-women and printers' boys and women wearing the green and white of a movement the newspapers had spent a decade calling fringe—made a sound like the ice going out on the river, long and cracking and unstoppable.

But Ulyana was not outside for the cheering. She was still in the building, at a smaller table in a smaller room, pleading guilty to forgery, to perjury, to the interception of correspondence, while a magistrate who had watched her testify read out the charges in a voice that kept softening, as though embarrassed to be harsh with her. Suspended sentence. Two years' good behavior. Loss of whatever licenses and references her old life ran on—no employer would touch a letter-reader who confessed to reading letters; no household, no firm, no shipping office. The law, having failed to hang an innocent man, settled comfortably for making sure she would never again be useful.

She signed her name at the bottom of the last document, and the pen scratched, and she thought: *that is the sound of my old life ending,* and discovered that it sounded almost exactly like the ice breaking.

Rell walked out with her, down the corridor, past the clerks who would not meet her eye and the one young one who did, and at the doors he stopped.

"They'll come for me eventually," he said. "The faction. Men like that don't lose gracefully."

"I know."

"And you'll be unemployable. Penniless, probably. Marta's house—"

"Is Marta's house until Marta doesn't need it, and after that it is nobody's and everybody's." She pulled on her coat—Marta's coat, black, too big in the shoulders—and looked up at him, this tired, ruined, honest man, and found she was smiling. "Inspector. Do you know what I've decided to do? Now that no one will hire me to be invisible?"

He didn't, and she told him, and his face did something complicated, and she pushed open the courthouse doors into the light.

The river was running free at last. From the top of the steps she could see it—all winter locked, and now broken into travelling pieces, carrying itself north toward the sea, toward the town where a fifteen-year-old girl was stepping off a steamer with a ticket in another person's name and a family's whole future folded into her pocket like a letter no one had read yet.

Ulyana went down the steps and did not look back at the courthouse.

There was a letter waiting for her at Marta's house. It had come that morning, postmarked from a northern port, the handwriting young and careful, the envelope addressed in block capitals learned from watching someone address hundreds of them. Ulyana held it in both hands a long moment on the doorstep.

Then she opened it honestly, the way she had promised herself she would open every letter for the rest of her life—and began, already, to compose her reply.
