## 1. The Weight of Small Things

There is a particular honesty in tar that Dominic Winterbourne had forgotten during his years of carrying secrets. Tar does not lie about what it is. It sticks to your hands, it stains your knuckles black to the first joint, and it announces itself in everything you touch for a week afterward. A man smeared in tar is a man who has plainly been doing honest work, and no one looks twice at him.

Which was, of course, the entire point.

"Mind the seam," called Bosun Ferro from above, his voice carrying the particular rasp of a man who had been shouting into salt wind for forty years and had long ago stopped caring whether anyone obeyed. "Mind the seam, or we'll be bailing the harbor out of our boots by Vellamark."

Dominic minded the seam. He pressed the hot pitch into the gap between planking with a wooden spatula, smoothed it flat, and stepped back before it could claim his boot as well. The *Meridian* lay against the quay at Corvassa like an old dog leaning on its master — a merchant ship of some three hundred tons, broad-beamed and patient, never built for passengers and now, in the last week of the dying year, asked to carry four hundred souls across a winter sea to a neutral harbor whose name everyone aboard pronounced carefully, as if saying it too loudly might make it close its gates.

His hands knew the work. That was the strange mercy of it. Three days aboard as a hired deckhand under the name Tomas Reel — a name he had taken from a gravestone in a fishing village he'd once passed through — and already the labor had settled into him like an old coat. Hauling rope. Coiling rope. Being shouted at about rope. There was no room in any of it for the thing wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the tar barrel in the forepeak store, beneath three coils of spare hawser that weighed more than he did.

The satchel. He did not let himself think of it as anything so grand as *the codes*, though that was what they were: forty pages of cipher, diplomatic credentials, seals of a republic that had perhaps eleven days left to live. Forty pages that could make a government-in-exile real instead of a rumor. He thought of them as *the tar barrel* sometimes, deliberately, the way a man might call a wound by a silly name to make it smaller. The tar barrel was doing fine. The tar barrel smelled awful. The tar barrel was nobody's business.

"Reel!" Ferro again. "Passengers coming up the gangway. Get down there and keep them off my working deck."

"Aye, bosun."

He went down the ladder two rungs at a time, because a man named Tomas Reel moved like a deckhand, and deckhands took ladders quickly.

---

The quay at Corvassa had been a place of order once. Dominic remembered it from a diplomatic visit six years ago — flags, brass bands, merchants in good wool discussing freight rates with the serene confidence of men who believed next year would resemble this one. Now the same stones held a tide line of abandoned luggage, and the flags were gone from their poles except one, torn halfway down, snapping in the wind like a man waving with half an arm.

They came up the gangway in families. That was what struck him, every hour of every day, the way it struck him fresh each morning: they came in families, holding onto each other, because everything else they'd owned had been left behind or carried off, and a family was the one possession the purge hadn't figured out how to confiscate yet.

An old woman climbed the planks with her hand on her son's shoulder, though from the set of her jaw it might have been the other way around. A young man carried a birdcage with no bird in it. When Dominic looked at him too long the young man said, defensively, "It was hers," and walked past, and Dominic touched his cap and said nothing, because Tomas Reel was not a curious man. Curiosity was a luxury for people with safe names.

A child fell on the gangway — a small girl in a coat too big for her, tripping over a hem someone had meant to let down in a better year — and Dominic caught her before the cry finished leaving her. For a moment she looked up at him with enormous gray eyes, and he braced himself for the question children ask, the terrible simple one: *Are we going to be all right?*

Instead she said, "You have tar on your face."

"So I do," said Dominic gravely. "It's how you can tell I'm important."

She considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate, decided it was acceptable, and ran on up the deck after her mother. Dominic stood a moment watching her go, and something in his chest did the thing it had been doing all week, the slow twisting that he had learned to ignore the way a sailor ignores weather — noting it, naming it, and getting on with the work.

The republic had fallen the way ice goes out of a river in spring. Not all at once. Not even dramatically, at the end. First the cracks, then the groaning, then a whole season of watching pieces drift past and telling yourself the rest would hold. He had been a courier through all of it — riding through the provinces with sealed dispatches while ministries emptied, while friends stopped answering letters, while the word *purge* went from being an ugly abstraction to a list of names he knew. His sister Lys had been on such a list, two years ago. Executed, they said. There had been no body to bury, but there was never a body anymore; the new regime considered bodies an extravagance. He had grieved her the way you grieve in wartime, standing up, in motion, between errands.

And then he had been handed the satchel — because he was reliable, because he was forgettable, because the last minister of a dying republic had looked down a corridor full of ambitious men and chosen the quiet courier who fixed the latch on his door without being asked — and told: *get these out. Get them to a neutral shore. Whoever holds these can speak for us. Don't be caught.*

So here he was, with tar on his face and a dead man's name, minding seams on a ship full of strangers.

---

At midday he carried crates of hardtack from the hold and stacked them under the watchful eye of the quartermaster, a woman named Odile Fenn who counted everything twice and looked at people once, thoroughly, the way an appraiser looks at silver. She had accepted "Tomas Reel" onto her crew list without a flicker, which either meant she believed him or found him uninteresting enough not to matter, and Dominic honestly could not decide which reassured him more.

"Third crate is short," she said.

"Third crate is what came aboard."

"I know what came aboard. I'm noting it." She wrote in her ledger with a pencil worn to a stub, and added, without looking up, "You coil a line better than most men who've done it longer."

"My father fished."

"Did he." It was not quite a question. Odile Fenn had the gift of making statements sound like open files. Then she closed the ledger and said only, "Lunch is at the bell. Don't be last; the profiteers eat fast."

The profiteers. That was the other cargo the *Meridian* carried, the kind that wasn't listed anywhere: the passengers who had bought their berths with gold extracted in the last desperate months, jewelers of the collapse, men whose fortunes had grown precisely in proportion to everyone else's ruin. They kept to the aft cabins and wore good coats and spoke quietly, and the refugees forward of the mast watched them with the particular hatred of the exhausted. Somewhere between those two populations — the ones who had fled with nothing and the ones who had fled with everything — the old loyalists drifted like sediment, former officials and officers who still spoke of *the government* in capital letters, as if capital letters could raise the dead.

A republic, Dominic thought, hauling the empty crate back toward the hold, does not die of its enemies alone. It dies of the arithmetic. Of the moment when enough people conclude that the ship is sinking and start counting lifeboats. He had seen the arithmetic happen in faces, in corridors, in the pause before a friend answered a question. Everyone had done the sums. The sums were bad.

---

In the afternoon the wind backed around and the loading grew urgent, and there was no more time for thinking. This was the third mercy of honest work, after the tar and the tiredness: urgency. A body given a task has no spare hands for despair.

He worked the cargo derrick with Ferro and a silent Gellishman named Brakk, swinging crates of water casks aboard while the crowd below pressed closer to the barriers. Soldiers of the port guard — the last soldiers of the last authority that still answered, loosely, to the idea of the republic — held a line that frayed a little more each hour. Every time the derrick swung, the crowd surged, and every surge pushed a few more people against the ropes, and every person pushed against the ropes was somebody's mother or brother or whole remaining world.

"Last light!" someone shouted from the quay. "Last light for boarding!"

And the sound that rose from the crowd then was not a riot and not a wail but something worse, something orderly — hundreds of people simultaneously compressing themselves, folding their desperation smaller, becoming polite, because politeness was the last currency anyone would still accept. *Please. After you. My daughter — please, she's small, she'll take hardly any room.* Dominic heard a man offer his coat to a guard for the privilege of stepping over a rope. He heard a woman recite, quietly, over and over, the name of a harbor town as if it were a prayer and a destination at once.

He kept his head down and his hands moving. That was the discipline. Grief cracked a man open, and everything leaked out through the crack — hope, mostly, hope and the wild urge to promise things — and a courier who promised things was a courier who got caught. So: head down. Hands moving. Be tarred, be tired, be Tomas.

Near dusk, Ferro sent him forward to sort out the rope locker, and he went gladly, because the rope locker was beside the forepeak store, and the forepeak store held the tar barrel.

He made sure the passage was empty. He tipped the barrel slightly with his knee — it was heavy, tarred, unremarkable — and felt through the staves the shape beneath the hawsers, the flat rectangular certainty of the oilcloth bundle. Still there. Still cold. Still just a tar barrel.

He had opened it exactly twice in three days, at night, by the light of a shuttered lantern, counting the pages the way a miser counts coins — not because the count changed but because the counting was a form of prayer. Forty leaves. Cipher tables, credential seals, the authorization formula drafted in the ministry's last lucid week: *the bearer, duly empowered…* He had not read past the formula. Reading it fully felt like opening a door he couldn't close. He knew enough. He knew that whoever presented those pages, intact and credentialed, could stand up in a neutral city and say *I am the republic,* and be believed.

Forty pages. He had carried them through checkpoints where soldiers' hands had been six inches from the barrel. He had slept with his boot hooked through the hawsers above them. And now his job, absurdly, was to do nothing at all — to remain a deckhand named Reel for nine more days until the *Meridian* reached Vellamark, and then walk down a gangway and become a ghost with a mission.

Simple. He told himself it was simple. He was getting very good at telling himself things.

---

Supper was barley and salt fish, eaten on deck in the lee of the deckhouse because the 'tween decks were already packed shoulder to shoulder with the last hundred passengers, bedding laid directly on planking, a city of blankets with lanes between them like streets. Dominic ate with Brakk and two younger sailors, and listened, as Tomas Reel listened, to the talk flow around him.

They talked of the sea, mostly, because the sea was safer than the subject underneath everything. Ferro's opinion of the storm season, delivered in full. Whether Vellamark would actually take them — "they took the *Coriander* in autumn," said one young sailor, "and turned back the *Salt Rose*" — "the *Salt Rose* was carrying arms, fool, you don't turn away women and children" — "you turn away whoever's inconvenient, that's what neutral means—" And underneath it, when the wind dropped, from the passenger deck, another conversation in lower voices: names. Lists of names. Who had escaped, who had been taken, who had signed, who had refused to sign.

Dominic ate his barley and memorized without meaning to. Old habit. A courier's ears were the second satchel; you couldn't put them in a barrel.

It was near full dark when Odile came along the deck with her lantern and her ledger, doing the final count of the day, and paused beside the deckhouse where he sat.

"Reel. You've a head for detail, I've noticed."

"Is that a crime aboard this ship?"

"It's a qualification." She lowered her voice, and for the first time since he'd known her something in her face was less than certain. "Final passenger manifest came up from the purser's clerk. It wants copying clean for the harbormaster at Vellamark — they'll want it ready before we make the roads. I'd do it myself, but I've the stores inventory and the water rationing both outstanding, and Ferro tells me you write a fair hand for a fisherman's son."

Dominic felt the small, cold click of a trap that wasn't a trap — or wasn't one yet. Copying the manifest meant reading the manifest. Every name. Every berth. Everything.

"Tomas Reel can copy what's put in front of him," he said carefully.

"That's all I'm asking." She pressed a folded packet of papers into his hand along with a pen and a stub of ink. "Fair copies by morning bell. Names as written — don't correct the clerk's spelling, whatever you do; the harbormaster matches against arrival papers, and a corrected name is a discrepancy." She straightened, and her lantern threw her shadow tall across the deckhouse wall. "Some of them are strange, mind. Names on that list. Clerk swears he copied them true off the boarding papers, but I say he was tired, and tired men copy wishes instead of words."

"What kind of strange?"

Odile looked at him a moment longer than she needed to, the appraiser's look, silver held to the light.

"You'll see," she said, and walked on, her lantern bobbing away down the deck toward the packed dark of the 'tween decks, where four hundred people breathed in their sleep and dreamed, if they dared, of harbors.

Dominic sat a long while with the packet on his knee, untouched, while the ship creaked and the water slapped the hull and somewhere aft the profiteers' lamps burned behind curtained glass.

Then he unfolded the first page, and read the first name on the list of the dead-and-gone that should never have been there — a name he knew, a name he had mourned, a name that stopped his breath like a hand closing around it —

— and out on the black water, far off toward the harbor mouth, a signal lamp began blinking.
