## 2. Manifest of the Departing

Odile Marchetti had kept the Meridian's books for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one iron law of the sea: everything that goes aboard must be written down, and everything written down becomes a kind of promise. Flour promised to become bread. Rope promised to become rigging or a noose, depending on the week. And a manifest—God help you—a manifest promised that every soul sleeping in your hold was exactly who they claimed to be.

She sat at her little desk in the quartermaster's cabin with a stub of pencil behind each ear and a third in her hand, tallying barrels by lamplight while the ship rocked at anchor off Pointe-Sarrazine like a tired horse waiting to be loaded. Outside, through the salt-fogged glass, she could see the boats coming. They came all night now, little lanterns bobbing on black water like fireflies that had lost their way home.

"Names," she said to the deckhand who ducked through her door with the evening's passenger list. "I want names spelled properly this time. Last boat gave me three men named Jean and not one of them could tell me which was which."

The deckhand was the quiet one—the new hire from the Sarrazine docks, Winterbourne, big hands, careful with rope, said less than any man she'd ever signed onto a crew. He set the papers on her desk without a word.

"You could say 'here you are, madame,'" Odile suggested. "It's a whole sentence. Only five words."

He almost smiled. She'd caught it once before, the ghost of one, like a fish glimpsed under ice. "Here you are, madame," he said, and his voice was lower and better-bred than a dockhand's ought to be, but she'd decided weeks ago not to wonder about it. A man who wanted to be nobody had his reasons, and Odile had lived long enough to know that reasons were cargo too—you didn't go poking through other people's holds.

"Off with you, then. I'll have your wages chit ready by morning."

He left, and she bent over the manifest with her pencil, and that was when the night turned strange.

* * *

The list ran four pages. Odile read every name twice, as she always did, because a name entered wrong was a soul uncounted, and souls uncounted had a way of becoming corpses unexplained. Most of it was what she expected: merchants with their gold sewn into coat linings, clerks, two midwives, a violinist who insisted his instrument counted as a dependent. Ordinary flight. The republic was dying the way old animals die—slowly, then all at once—and its people were pouring toward any hull that would float.

Then, on the second page, halfway down:

**Vasseur, Annelise — widow, age 51 — embarkation: Pointe-Sarrazine.**

Odile put down her pencil.

Annelise Vasseur had been dead for three years. Everyone knew it. There had been a notice posted in the harbor office, official seal and all, when the purges reached the customs service—Vasseur, Annelise, executed for falsifying shipping manifests. Which had struck Odile at the time as darkly funny, in the way things were funny when they weren't, because Annelise Vasseur had taught Odile half of what she knew about manifests, back when they were both young women counting crates in the old free port. Falsifying them was the last thing she'd have done. If Annelise Vasseur died over a shipping document, the document had been a lie.

And here she was, boarding tomorrow's tide, alive and widowed and fifty-one years old, which was about right, actually, give or take an execution.

Odile poured herself a finger of plum brandy, purely medicinal, and kept reading.

**Dorn, Emil — physician.** Dead too, or supposed to be—hanged in effigy in the capital square two winters past, though effigies were notoriously easy to substitute for flesh. **The Renard brothers, fisherfolk.** Their boat had gone down with all hands; she remembered the collection taken up at the quayside, the little tin box rattling with coppers. **Madame Sorel and child.** Madame Sorel's name had been read aloud on the radio last spring among the disappeared.

Five names. Six. Seven. Names that should be dead, walking up her gangplank with their papers in order and their eyes doing that particular thing refugee eyes did—looking at you, then past you, never quite at you.

Now, Odile was not a fool, and more importantly she was not a romantic. There were explanations. Some of the names might be cousins borrowing identities; some might be bribes purchased from a clerk with loose morals and tight finances. In a collapsing republic, death notices were sometimes just another kind of paperwork error, and paperwork errors could be corrected—for a fee.

But seven? On one boat? Hers?

She thought about the harbor office, and the men who had come around last month asking, very politely, whether the Meridian would be sailing on schedule, and whether she would be so good as to file her final manifest with the Port Authority *before* departure rather than after. Very polite men. Polite men with good memories. A manifest filed in advance was a gift wrapped in ribbon: here is everyone aboard, here is where we sleep, here is what we are worth.

She looked at her own copy of the list, sitting there under the lamp, promising things.

"Well," Odile said aloud to the cabin, because talking to yourself was one of the few liberties still legal, "we're not going to do that, are we."

* * *

She worked until nearly dawn, because if she couldn't trust the manifest she could at least rebuild it. She went down into the hold with a lantern and counted bunks herself. She interviewed the watch. She cross-checked the passenger rolls against the crew's own gossip—which was more reliable than any government record, since sailors lied only about money and love, never about people.

By the time the sky went grey, she had a private list of her own, penciled in shorthand only she could read, tucked inside her boot where she kept the ship's real accounts and her late husband's photograph. Seven names that should be dead. One ship full of secrets.

And one thing more. Because there was a second copy of the manifest—there always was, duplicate, carbon, the clerk's habit of a lifetime—and the deckhand Winterbourne had left his copy on her desk by mistake when he brought the papers. Or not by mistake. She honestly couldn't decide which possibility interested her more.

She found him at first light forward, coiling line with those enormous careful hands, working while the passengers began to come up out of the steerage section to see the sun. He didn't look up when she approached, but his shoulders changed—just slightly, just enough—like a man listening.

"Winterbourne. You dropped something." She held out the folded pages.

He took them without looking at them. "Thank you."

"Mm." Odile crossed her arms and regarded him with the expression she used on cargo that smelled wrong. "Funny thing about lists. You read them long enough, they start reading you back."

He said nothing. Coiled line.

"I had an aunt," she went on conversationally, "who said you can tell everything about a man by how he handles paper. Hoarders clutch it. Liars smooth it. Guilty men fold it small, so it fits in a pocket near the heart." She nodded at his jacket, where—she happened to know, because she had eyes—the folded pages now resided. "You've folded yours very small indeed."

For a moment the only sound was rope and water and the gulls fighting over something dead. Then Dominic Winterbourne—or whoever he was—looked at her directly for the first time since he'd signed on, and Odile saw what she'd suspected for weeks: not a dockhand's eyes at all. Eyes that had read things. Eyes that had carried things.

"What will you do with your list?" he asked quietly.

"That depends. What will you do with yours?"

A pause. Then he did the most interesting thing she'd seen a man do in eleven years of manifests: he walked with her, unhurried, to the rail, and there, with the grey morning wind pulling at the pages, he unfolded his copy of the passenger list, held it over the water a moment—

and burned it instead. From a twist of paper and a match struck against the rail, cupped against the wind, flame taking the names one by one until only ash spun away astern. Annelise Vasseur's name went first. Odile watched it go and felt something in her chest turn over, some old door swinging open onto a room she'd shut years ago.

"Why that one?" she asked, when he'd doused the flame and ground the ash between his fingers like a man finishing a prayer.

"Because someone on that list is going to ask me for something," he said, "and I haven't yet decided what I'm willing to pay."

"Ah." Odile nodded slowly. "One of those."

"And your copy, madame?"

"My copy," she said, patting her boot, "is written in shorthand, misspelled on purpose, and lies about three ages. Let the polite men from the Port Authority read theirs. Mine is for me." She started back toward her cabin, then stopped, because fairness was fairness. "Winterbourne. Whatever name is folded next to your heart—it's safe as far as I'm concerned. I keep books. I don't keep files."

Behind her she heard him exhale, long and slow, like a man putting down weight.

"One more thing," she called over her shoulder. "The Vasseur woman boards with the morning tide. If she asks for me, tell her—" Odile stopped, surprised by her own voice going rough. "Tell her Marchetti kept the tin box money in trust. All hands. She can collect."

* * *

The morning tide brought the boats, and the boats brought the dead.

Odile stood at the head of her gangplank with her ledger open and checked each soul aboard against her doctored list, and one by one the impossible names stepped past her. Emil Dorn, the hanged-in-effigy physician, was a stooped man with a medical bag and a smell of carbolic. The Renard brothers were broad, weathered, silent, and could not meet her eye. Madame Sorel's little girl waved at Odile, and Odile waved back, and wrote down a false age in a steady hand.

Last came a woman in a widow's grey shawl, fifty-one or thereabouts, carrying one carpetbag and twenty-six years of shared history in her spine. She stopped at the top of the gangplank. Looked at Odile. Odile looked back.

"Passenger name?" Odile asked, formal as a funeral.

Annelise Vasseur smiled, and it was the same crooked smile from the counting-house, thirty years younger. "You always did count twice, Marchetti."

"Someone has to." Odile made the entry. Widow, age 51, embarkation Pointe-Sarrazine. A promise. "Berth twelve, lower steerage. Supper's at six, complaints at six-thirty."

When the woman had passed below, Odile closed the ledger and stood a moment in the cold bright air, watching Pointe-Sarrazine shrink behind them and feeling the peculiar double weight of a ship well-run in bad times: pride and dread in equal measure, ballast for the soul.

Seven names that should be dead, and hers the only ship reckless or merciful enough to carry them. That meant the Meridian wasn't an evacuation anymore. It was a message. And somewhere aboard—she thought again of the quiet deckhand, of the pages folded small against his heart—somewhere aboard was whatever the message was *about*.

She'd find out. She kept books, after all. Everything that goes aboard gets written down.

That evening, making her rounds, she passed the foredeck and saw Winterbourne standing at the rail in the last light, watching the coastline burn orange in the distance—not sunset, she realized, but fires—and beside him, uninvited, sat the stowaway boy the cook swore wasn't there, sharing something out of a paper twist.

Two figures against the darkening water. One large and guarded, one small and quick. Between them, held carefully in the big man's scarred hands, a strip of dried fish divided exactly in half.

Odile noted it, the way she noted everything, and wrote nothing down at all.

Some entries, she was learning, you kept in the heart's column only.

But it was later that same night—lamplight low, ledgers balanced, the ship running dark along the coast—that the knock came at her cabin door. Three raps. Soft. Wrong.

Not a sailor's knock. Not a passenger's either.

Odile slid her boot-list deeper into her boot, picked up her lamp, and opened the door onto a tall stranger she had never signed onto any manifest, rain-wet from a boat that must have come alongside in the dark, holding up a leather wallet of letters tied with string.

"Forgive the hour, madame," he said, with a smile like a key turning. "I'm looking for a passenger who may not exist. Perhaps you could help me find him."

Behind him, far aft, Odile heard footsteps stop—one pair of feet, going very still on the quarterdeck.

She recognized the stillness. It was Winterbourne's.
