## 3. A Hand He Knew

The last port was called Serevane, though the sign at its head had been shot through sometime in October and now read only *SER*, which Odile said was fitting, since that was what everyone there was trying to do.

We took on water, coal, and forty-one more souls. The coal came first, because the Meridian's heart was failing and Captain Voss had made it plain that sentiment could burn later. Then the water, hauled up in creaking barrels by men whose hands shook from cold or fear or both—I had stopped trying to distinguish between them, having long since concluded they were the same substance in different containers. The souls came last, because souls always do. They queue. They bargain. They look back.

I worked the gangway with a rope over my shoulder, steadying passengers as they came up out of the lighter. It is remarkable what a man can learn to be when his life depends on being unremarkable. Two years ago I had carried dispatch cases under diplomatic seal; now I carried crates of salt pork and answered to "you there" as naturally as to my own name—which was not my own name, but that is another matter, and one I had grown practiced at not thinking about. My hands were the best part of my disguise. Hands like mine, blistered and tarred and split at the knuckles, belonged to nobody important. A courier's hands are soft. These were a deckhand's hands, and I loved them for their treachery against my former self.

The passengers came up in ones and twos: an old woman clutching a birdcage with no bird in it, on the grounds, she announced to anyone who would listen, that the bird deserved a better country and she intended to provide it; a clerk of some ministry, still wearing his brass-buttoned coat as if the republic might be restored by force of lapels; a family of five who were somehow seven by the time they reached the deck, children appearing out of nowhere like coins from behind an ear. Odile counted them all twice and got different numbers both times and wrote down neither, on the principle, she told me, that the manifest was a work of aspiration rather than record.

I kept my head down. I had gotten very good at keeping my head down. It is a skill, keeping your head down—people imagine it is cowardice, but it is actually craftsmanship. You must know exactly how low to go. Too low and you draw the eye of whoever is looking for the man hiding lowest. Not low enough and you catch the light. There is a narrow band of obscurity, a kind of human fog, and I had spent two years learning to live in it, comfortable as a fish in weather.

Then Corvane came aboard.

*

He came up the gangway the way no refugee comes anywhere: unhurried. Everyone else arrived at the Meridian as though the sea itself were chasing them—and it was, in the form of the armies behind us—but this man climbed as though he were paying a social call on the ocean and expected it to receive him well. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned, wearing a good wool coat that had been deliberately mended in three places so that it would appear poor. I knew that trick. I had done the same thing to my own jacket. There is a whole economy among the fleeing built on looking slightly less desperate than you are and slightly more desperate than you can afford.

He carried a leather satchel, worn soft, held close to his body with one hand—not clutched, the way the frightened clutch things, but resting, the way a man rests a hand on a dog he trusts.

Odile stood at the head of the gangway with her ledger. "Name?"

"Corvane." He said it the way you set down a card. "Passage for one. I have funds."

"Funds are lovely," Odile said. "Occupation?"

"I was a teacher. Of languages."

"Of course you were." She wrote something. I have never learned what, because Odile's ledger entries were less records than small works of fiction, and she guarded her authorship jealously. "Anything to declare?"

"Only letters." And he smiled—a small, closed smile, the smile of a man who has just declared precisely what he came to declare and nothing more. "Letters for a family aboard. I'm told there is one."

My rope went slack in my hands. Some instinct older than thought pulled it taut again before anyone noticed, and I bent over my knotwork as though the fate of the ship hung on a bowline, which in a sense it did, though not the one in my fingers.

A family aboard. Letters for a family aboard.

There were four hundred people on the Meridian and every one of them had left someone behind—that was practically the definition of a passenger—but Corvane's eyes did not sweep the deck searching faces the way a letter-carrier's would. His eyes went where he wanted them to go. They rested on the crowd without reading it, the way a fisherman rests his line on water he already knows is full.

I told myself I was imagining it. I was very busy telling myself things that afternoon. That the cold was bearable. That the coal smell meant progress. That the knot I was tying needed tying. That a stranger with a satchel of letters was none of my business and could not possibly be mine.

But then, at dusk, I saw him on the afterdeck, and I saw him open the satchel.

*

I want to explain about Lys's handwriting, because you cannot understand what happened to me on that deck unless you understand the handwriting.

It leaned. Everything my sister ever wrote leaned to the right, as though hurrying toward whatever came next—the ends of sentences, the next page, the rest of her life. Her letters crossed their t's with a single slashing stroke, impatient, and her capital L's had a flourish at the bottom like a little boat riding a wave, because when she was eleven she had decided that her initial should look like the harbor we grew up on, and she had never once, in thirty years of writing grocery lists and love letters and smuggled political pamphlets, broken the habit.

They shot her two winters ago. In the courtyard of the Ministry of Justice, according to the lists, which were published, because the new regime believed in thoroughness even in murder. Name, crime, disposition. *Lysandra Winterbourne, sedition, executed.* I read it standing in a bakery queue in a city already dying, and I bought my bread, and I walked home, and I ate the bread, because the body is a stupid animal that goes on requiring things, and I remember thinking that this was the true cruelty of executions—not the wall and the rifles, but the bread afterward, the fact that your hands keep working, that the world keeps selling you loaves, that grief arrives with no provision for pause.

For two years I had carried her death folded flat inside me like a document too important to crumple.

And now, across the width of the afterdeck, lamplight falling on an open satchel, a stranger was holding a page of paper covered in handwriting that leaned to the right.

I did not go to him. Understand that. Every nerve I owned voted to go to him, marched in formation, presented arms, and I countermanded the order, because I had survived two years by understanding that the thing you most want to do aboard a ship full of spies is exactly the thing you must not. Instead I finished securing the aft cargo netting—it genuinely needed doing; even my terror had a work ethic—and I moved along the deck in the manner of a man with errands, and I passed within six feet of Corvane, and I let my eyes flick sideways the way any bored sailor's would.

The page was angled away from me. But the lamp caught the top corner, and there, riding the margin like a toy boat on dark water, was the little flourish. The L with the wave beneath it.

The rope in my hands was suddenly the only solid object in the universe. I gripped it the way drowning men grip driftwood, and I walked on, and my legs performed their duties flawlessly while the rest of me stood in a courtyard two winters ago hearing rifle fire.

*She's dead,* I told myself. *You read the list. You memorized the list. Lists don't lie; lists are the only honest literature left in the country.*

But the dead have been known to correspond before. The regime's lists had errors—we all knew of a name or two struck through in error, men reported shot who turned up in fishing villages, women reported shot who turned up as somebody's wife in another life entirely. Errors happened. Hope is a scavenger; it will live on anything, even a typo.

And besides—if it was a forgery, that was almost worse, and infinitely more dangerous, and either way I needed to see that page.

* * *

Opportunity came the way opportunity always comes aboard a working ship: disguised as labor.

That night the wind backed around to the north and freshened, and word came down that everything on deck not nailed down should be lashed down, and everything not lashed should come below. I volunteered for the afterdeck detail, which surprised no one, because volunteering was my camouflage the way dirt was. A man who always takes the miserable job is invisible; misery is a kind of fog too.

Corvane had gone below to the passenger quarters—or appeared to. When I came past his corner of the deck, where he'd been reading by the binnacle lamp earlier, his satchel sat tucked under the coil of the mizzen sheet, half-hidden, waiting for a master who assumed a rolling ship was safer than his own cabin. People trust decks. It's a mistake sailors make once.

I stood in the lee of the deckhouse, out of the lamplight, and I told myself I was only going to look. Looking is not theft. Looking is not confession. A sailor pausing beside a coil of line is the most natural sight on God's gray sea.

The satchel opened without resistance. Inside: papers, a few coins wrapped in cloth, a small pistol that I pretended very hard not to notice, and letters. Six of them, tied with string, each addressed in that leaning hand, each sealed with wax—plain wax, unmarked, because Lys had always said seals were confessions looking for a place to happen.

I took out the topmost letter. I did not break the seal. I am a courier; I know how to hold a folded page up to lamplight and read the ghost of what's written inside, the pressure-marks the pen leaves in the paper, and I am not proud of what I did next, but pride is a luxury that sank with the republic.

*Dominic.*

Not the name on my forged papers. Not the deckhand's name. *Dominic*—in ink, in her hand, pressed deep enough into the paper that I could read it backward through the fold like a message from the other side of the world, or the other side of death.

The deck rolled. The mizzen sheet creaked above me. Somewhere forward a hatch cover banged, and a voice cursed it with real feeling, and the ordinary world went on insisting on itself while I stood holding six words of my sister's living hand and shaking like a man in a fever.

She was alive. Or someone wanted me to believe she was alive, badly enough to forge thirty years of habit into ink—the lean, the slash, the little boat riding the wave. Either answer required me. Either way, the letter was bait, and I was the fish, and Corvane was the patient man holding the line.

I put the letter back exactly as it had lain. I retied the string with the same knots—I checked them twice; muscle memory is a faithful servant. I tucked the satchel back under the coil until it showed just the amount it had shown before, which is the true art of concealment: not hiding a thing, but restoring it to its proper degree of visible.

Then I stood in the dark and had the conversation with myself that I had been avoiding since the gangway.

If I revealed myself, I revealed the codes. A man asking after Dominic Winterbourne, courier, might as well hang a lantern on the satchel of cipher papers hidden in my tar barrel below. If I stayed hidden, the letters would sit in that satchel, and Corvane would grow tired, or suspicious, or would begin asking questions aloud—*does anyone know a Winterbourne? does anyone know a courier?*—and every hour I delayed made the eventual collision worse.

And underneath all the strategy, quieter than the wind, a child's voice that was not a child's at all but simply mine, thirty years old and permanent: *she's alive, Dom. She leaned her letters toward you. She's alive.*

I heard footsteps on the companionway ladder—unhurried footsteps, the footsteps of a man who pays calls on oceans.

I stepped back into the shadow of the deckhouse, heart hammering out some old marching song, and watched Corvane emerge into the binnacle light. He paused at the coil of line. He looked down at his satchel. And then, without touching it, without lifting his head, in a voice pitched exactly loud enough to carry six feet into the shadows—in a voice that proved he had known precisely where I was standing the entire time—he said:

"The letters are genuine, you know. She writes to you every month. She's grown impatient." A pause, delicately measured, like sugar weighed by an apothecary. "She asked me to tell you: the flourishes were your idea first. You drew the little boat for her. She only borrowed it."

The deck rolled. The world tilted with it.

Because that was true. God help me, that was true—I had drawn that wave in the margin of a schoolbook when I was nine years old, a joke between siblings, a secret shared by no one else alive. No forger could have known it. No intelligence service could have mined it from the rubble of our family.

Which meant the letters were hers. Which meant she lived.

Which meant that the man smiling at the darkness where I hid was either the bearer of a miracle or the maker of a trap—and had arranged matters so that I could no longer tell the difference, and perhaps never would.

"Whenever you're ready," Corvane said pleasantly, to the empty air, to me, and went below, leaving the satchel where it lay, leaving the letters where they lay, leaving me alone on a pitching deck with a dead sister's handwriting and a living sister's demand, and the certain knowledge that whatever I decided next would cost me everything I had left to spend.
