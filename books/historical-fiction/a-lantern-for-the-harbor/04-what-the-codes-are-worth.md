## 4. What the Codes Are Worth

The Meridian kept two clocks: the ship's bell, and the coughing of old Merrick three berths down. Somewhere past the second bell after midnight, both went quiet enough for Dominic to work.

He had chosen the coil room deliberately. It was the one place aboard where a man crouched among rope was invisible — deckhands slept there by right, and no one looked twice at a sailor who sat up late with his hands busy. Rope-work explained itself. A man fiddling with tarred twine could be doing anything, so far as officers cared, provided the twine kept moving.

The satchel had not left his body since Harwick. He wore it flat against his ribs beneath the jersey, where it rode like a second heartbeat, and he had begun, in the last few days, to hate it with an intimacy he usually reserved for people. He unslung it now, worked open the false seam along the bottom — a courier's trick, the first thing they taught you: never hide anything where a thief would search for it; hide it where a thief would get bored — and drew out the oilcloth packet inside.

Three layers. That was what the Foreign Ministry's cipher school had drummed into him, back when the Ministry still had walls to drum against. The outer packet was ordinary correspondence, dull enough to survive inspection. The middle layer was operational traffic — fleet movements, safe houses, the names of men who were already dead. And the innermost layer, the one sealed under wax stamped with the Chancellor's own signet, was the thing they had trusted to him because he was nobody: quick, forgettable, good at being overlooked.

*You are a lantern,* his instructor had told him, lighting the metaphor with evident satisfaction. *A lantern does not decide where the ships go. It only burns.*

Dominic lit a stub of candle, shielded it behind a coil of hawser, and set about finding out whether lanterns could lie.

* * *

The outer layer took twenty minutes and told him nothing he didn't know, which is to say it told him everything was lost. Dispatches from ministries that no longer existed, addressed to ambassadors who had fled or turned, written in the brisk official prose of men who still believed the republic had a next week. One letter ordered him to deliver his cargo to the Consulate at Vell. Vell had fallen in September. The letter was dated October. Bureaucracy, he thought, was the true immortal: it would go on issuing instructions long after the last human hand was gone to receive them.

He fed the pages back into their sleeve and sat for a while listening to the ship breathe — water against hull, the groan of timber finding its opinions about the weather. Then he took out the middle layer.

This was the part that frightened him properly, and he was a man who had crossed three checkpoints in a dead woman's coat, so fright had to work hard to impress him.

Operational traffic. He spread it out on his knees like a card sharp and went to work with the running key — a passage from the Founding Charter, of all things, which the cipher men had considered clever and which Dominic had always considered sentimental. *We hold these shores in trust.* Every time he ran the key through a fresh page, some dead founder's voice came muttering up through the arithmetic, insisting on things that were no longer true.

Page by page, the war assembled itself in miniature. Fleet dispositions, half of them stale. The names of forty-one persons marked for extraction, thirty-three of whom he knew to be dead, because he had carried the news of several himself. Safe houses burned. Funds frozen, then unfrozen, then frozen again by a treasury that changed hands the way a bad coin changes hands.

And then, near the bottom of the stack, a single sheet that stopped his breath.

It was unsigned. It was addressed, in effect, to whoever held it — a contingency document, drafted in the last sane week of the government and then, apparently, forgotten or abandoned, because the courier assigned to it had been Dominic Winterbourne, third-grade, and nobody had ever thought to recall him.

He read it four times.

It was a protocol of legitimacy. Not a treasure map, not a weapon, though it would function as both. It was simpler and worse than either: a chain of succession, sealed and countersigned, together with the authentication schedule by which any bearer could prove the chain genuine. Whoever held these papers held, in effect, the republic's signature. A government-in-exile formed around them would not be a committee of refugees shouting in a rented hall. It would be *the* government — recognized, fundable, able to sign treaties, able to command loyalty from every garrison and customs house and provincial clerk still pretending to wait for orders.

Any bearer.

He read those two words until they blurred. The Ministry had written them assuming the bearer would be a minister. Ministers did not run. Ministers stayed at their desks and were shot with dignity. The drafters had never imagined the chain of custody passing through the hands of a deckhand crouched in a rope room smelling of tar and other people's laundry.

Dominic put the papers down very carefully, as if they might notice trembling.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about power: it does not feel like power. It feels like weight. It sits on the chest at night. It makes the hands stupid.

He had spent two years being light. Lightness was the whole discipline of his trade — travel light, speak light, be the sort of man whose name slid off memory like rain off oilcloth. He had been robbed once in Corvane's country — no, not Corvane's, that was later — in some border town whose name he'd drunk away, and the thieves had gone through his pockets and found nothing worth taking, and he had lain in the ditch afterward feeling genuinely proud. *Nothing worth taking.* It had been the highest praise his profession offered.

Now he was the most valuable object between two horizons, and he had a sudden, sickening understanding of how the coin felt.

Because it wasn't only the new regime that would want him. That was the naive version of the danger — soldiers, agents, a quiet knife in a dark passageway. He could price that risk; he'd been paying it for years. The real danger was friendlier. It wore the faces of the people sleeping ten feet above him.

The loyalists aboard — old Admiral Cade's circle, who gathered each evening in the saloon to drink toasts to a republic that had stopped returning their letters — would need only to learn what he carried to make him their relic, their ark, their excuse. They wouldn't hurt him. They'd do something worse: they'd *need* him, publicly, permanently. He would spend the rest of his life being the vessel of other men's restoration, handled like the Crown jewels, consulted like an oracle, never again permitted to be a man who liked his coffee sweet and cheated mildly at cards.

And the profiteers — the Vassek brothers and their kind, with their manifests full of phantom cargo and their cabins suspiciously well stocked — would price the protocol in hard currency before the candle burned down an inch. There were governments enough in the world, neutral and otherwise, who would pay a great deal to be the ones holding the republic's signature. Or to be the ones holding the men who wanted it.

And the exiles — the quiet ones, the families with their bundled photographs, the clerk with the cough, old Merrick shuffling his rescued papers through the corridor like a man carrying embers in cupped hands —

Merrick.

Dominic closed his eyes. Of everyone aboard, the archivist was the one who might actually understand what the papers meant — and the one most likely to say something wise and terrible about them, in that dry voice, over tea.

*Memory must survive even if men do not.* He hadn't heard Merrick say it yet. But he could hear it anyway, pre-echoed, the way you sometimes heard a sentence coming before a friend spoke it, because by then you were becoming the same sentence.

* * *

The trouble — he admitted it to the candle, since there was no one else awake to admit it to — was that the temptation worked from the inside.

He did not want a government. He had never wanted a government; he wanted his sister alive and the war over and someone else responsible for the weather. But sitting alone in the dark with the republic folded in his lap, he caught himself doing the arithmetic anyway. *If I chose the men. If I picked carefully. If the exile council included Cade for honor and Merrick for memory and someone young and unsentimental for spine —* He could see it, whole and shining, the way you can see a harbor light from a long way off at sea: small, certain, promising home.

That was the corruption, he realized. Not greed. Greed he could refuse; he'd refused it professionally his whole life. It was *usefulness*. The seduction of mattering. Two years of being nothing worth stealing, and now the whole future of a nation could plausibly be arranged by his discretion — his taste, his judgment, his lone anonymous self — and some traitorous chamber of his heart whispered that this was not a burden at all but a promotion.

*A lantern does not decide where the ships go.*

No. But a lantern, he thought, wretchedly, could decide which ships wrecked.

He blew the thought out like the candle stub and sat in the dark feeling the ship roll beneath him, and understood that this was the actual test, and that he was probably failing it, and that the fact that he noticed he was failing it counted for approximately nothing.

* * *

He repacked the papers with hands that had steadied by sheer professional spite. Outer layer, middle layer, innermost layer wrapped separately in its oilcloth, the whole business pressed flat into the satchel's false seam. His fingers moved without consulting him, which was the point of training: the body keeps the faith even when the mind has started negotiating.

He was cinching the buckle when the bell above him rang the hour, and a voice said, conversationally, from the darkness by the door:

"You burn candles like a man who can afford them."

Odile. The quartermaster stood with a lantern hooked over her arm and a ledger under it, which as far as Dominic could tell was simply how she existed — the woman and her book were a single organism, like a snail and its shell.

"Tar," Dominic said. "Mixing it. For the rigging."

"At three bells."

"The rigging doesn't keep hours."

"No," she agreed pleasantly, "it doesn't." She let the silence do a lap of the room. She was very good at that; he'd watched her make grown sailors confess to smuggling cheese purely by standing near them. "You've been aboard nine days, Winterbourne. In nine days I've learned your knots are navy-taught, your hands are too clean for a deckhand, and you flinch — just slightly, just once — whenever anyone mentions the Ministry. I mention this not as accusation. Half my passengers flinch at something. The Vasseks flinch at mirrors." She adjusted the lantern. "I mention it because tomorrow we make the last port before the deep water, and I'll be signing names into my manifest, and I prefer to know what I'm signing for. Whatever you're mixing down here besides tar—" she tapped the ledger once, softly, "—make sure it's mixed by morning. Ships sink from the inside out, Mr. Winterbourne. I've seen it done."

She went out. Her footsteps climbed the ladder and faded into wind, and Dominic sat in the cold with his heartbeat going like a fist on a door.

She knew something. Not everything — if she knew everything, she'd have thrown him to Cade's loyalists within the hour, or sold him to the Vasseks, whichever paid better. But she had smelled the shape of him, the way dogs smell the shape of fear.

He looked at the satchel. Nine days of careful anonymity, and the truth was he'd never once been light, not really. He'd been a lantern the whole time, walking around with his shutter cracked, and the whole world was a dark sea full of ships steering by whatever glow he couldn't help giving off.

Tomorrow, the last port. Tomorrow, whatever boarded at that pier would board knowing the Meridian was the last boat out — which meant the desperate, and the hunted, and the sent.

Somewhere above him a hatch banged, and through the gap came a smell of bilge and, faintly, something else — small feet, perhaps, or small breathing, or only his nerves inventing company because the alternative was to sit alone with what he now knew he carried.

He banked the candle, pulled the tarpaulin over his shoulders, and lay down on the rope with the satchel against his ribs, the way you sleep beside a sleeping animal you're not entirely sure is tame.

By morning, he promised the dark, he'd have decided what he was.

The ship, having no interest in promises, rolled on toward the last port.
