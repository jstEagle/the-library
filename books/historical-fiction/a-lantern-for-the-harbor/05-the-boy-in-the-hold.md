## 5. The Boy in the Hold

The trick to being nobody, Tobias had learned, was to be smaller than the smallest thing anyone might look for.

He was very good at it now. Smaller than a crate of salted herring. Smaller than the gap between two bales of wool, which he had widened by exactly one boy's width over the course of an entire shivering night while the Meridian sat at her berth and men with lanterns walked the quay above him, their boots knocking like slow drums on his ceiling.

Now the boots were gone, the ropes were cast off, and the whole world had begun to move.

That was the other thing nobody told you about stowing away: the leaving itself was wonderful. For the first hour, before the cold came back and the hunger came back and the fear remembered where it lived, there was just this — the deck trembling under his spine like a giant cat purring, the groan of timber, water slapping the hull in that patient way water had, as if it could afford to wait for anything.

*We're going somewhere,* Tobias thought. *I don't know where, and I don't know how I'll eat when we get there, but we're going.*

It was more than most people in the city could say anymore. The city was behind them now — the smoke of it, anyway, a gray smudge through the aft hatch, sitting over the harbor like a lid on a pot someone had forgotten about. He'd watched the purge from a rooftop with the pigeons. He'd watched men who talked too loudly get taken away, and men who stayed silent get taken away too, because silence apparently sounded guilty if you were the wrong sort of listener. He had learned to walk like his shadow was heavier than other boys' shadows. He had learned that names were dangerous things, so he kept his folded up small inside him, next to the memory of his mother's hands, and took it out only at night.

*Tobias.* It still fit. That was the miracle of it. Everything else had gotten too big or too broken, but the name still fit.

The hold smelled of tar, rope, brine, and — this was the good part — apples. Somewhere among the crates there was a barrel of apples. He had smelled them since the second hour and spent the third hour conducting a careful survey, crawling along the cargo lines on his belly, reading stencils by the thin light of the hatch grating. *FLOUR — QUARTERMASTER'S SEAL. SALT PORK — QUARTERMASTER'S SEAL. WOOL — no seal, probably nobody's.* And then, tucked in the corner like something smuggled aboard by a sentimental sailor: *APPLES, WINTER VARIETAL*, with a chalk mark that looked almost like a little heart, though that was probably just sloppy chalking.

Tobias made himself a rule, because rules were how you stayed alive: one apple every day and a half. Not two. A boy who ate two apples a day got caught, because a boy who ate two apples a day got greedy and greedy boys got careless and careless boys got marched up on deck to face whatever a ship's captain did with stowaways. His mother's cousin had been flogged off a grain ship once. Or so his mother had said, in the voice she used for stories that ended badly, which by the end was all her stories.

So: one apple every day and a half. Water from the cask tap in the corner, one cup at a time, wiped dry after. Sleep curled in the wool, which held heat the way nothing else in the world held heat, and dream of things that didn't hurt, or failing that, of things that hurt only a normal amount.

It was a good plan. It lasted four days.

* * *

On the fourth day, Tobias learned that ships have ears.

He had heard footsteps come down into the hold twice already — once a pair, heavy, unhurried, belonging to some crewman checking lashings; once three pairs, quick, belonging to men whose voices argued in low tones about *berths* and *lists* and a word that made the air go strange: *codes*. Tobias had flattened himself between the wool bales both times and held his breath until colors swam, and both times the boots had gone away again, and he had awarded himself half an apple for bravery.

But on the fourth day the footsteps came down and did not go away.

They stopped near the wool. They were quiet footsteps, deliberate, the kind that belonged to a man who had done a lot of walking and thought about each step. Then there was a sound Tobias knew from the market streets, from alleyways, from every bad afternoon of his old life: the sound of someone noticing.

"Hello," said a voice.

Tobias did not answer. Answering was for people who existed.

"I can hear you breathing," the voice said. It wasn't unkind. That was somehow worse. "Behind the third bale. The wool one. You've been eating my apples."

*I have not,* Tobias thought indignantly, *they're nobody's apples, they have no seal—* and then, horribly, he sneezed.

The wool dust. Four days of it. It had been building up in his nose like a debt coming due, and it chose that moment — that exact, treacherous moment — to collect.

Silence. Then the voice again, closer, and now Tobias could see boots past the edge of the bale, worn boots, deckhand's boots, and above them a man crouching down to boy-height, which no one ever did, which everyone should do. He had dark hair going gray at the temples and eyes that looked like they were used to reading things from far away.

"There you are," the man said. "You're the smallest stowaway I've ever caught. You're barely a stowaway at all. You're more of a stowaway-in-progress."

Tobias pressed further into the wool and prepared to die, or be flogged, or be put off the ship at the next port, which amounted to the same thing, since the next port was the whole reason he'd hidden in the first place. "I'll work," he said, because it was the only coin he had. "I can haul. I can coil rope. I can bail. I know knots."

"Do you," the man said.

"Overhand. Figure-eight. Bowline." His mother's father had been a fisherman before the fish left and the tax collectors came. Some knowledge stays in the blood even after the blood has nowhere to live.

"Hm." The man sat down fully on a crate, which was either a very confident thing to do or a tired one, and Tobias suspected both. "And what's your name?"

Here Tobias performed the great magic of his life so far: he became nobody. He let his face go blank and stupid, the face of a boy who had never been called anything, the face of driftwood. "Don't have one."

The man looked at him for a long time. Tobias looked back, blank as flour, and waited for it not to work.

"That's a shame," the man said finally. "Everyone should have a name. Even a borrowed one." He reached into his coat — Tobias flinched; hands coming out of coats were how trouble arrived — and produced not a knife or a chain but a hard little apple, winter-varietal, with a chalk mark near the stem that really was almost a heart. "You've been stealing these. So you may as well steal this one properly, out in the open, like an honest thief."

Tobias took it. His hand moved before his brain finished voting. The apple was cold and dense and perfect, and he ate the first bite too fast, the way starving people do, and hated himself for it, and the man pretended not to notice, which was the kindest lie Tobias had ever been told.

"You'll be reported, of course," the man said. "Captain's rules. Stowaways go ashore at the next port, or work double watches till then, depending on the captain's mood and the quartermaster's arithmetic." He paused. "Unless."

"Unless what?" Tobias asked around a mouthful of apple, which ruined the effect entirely.

"Unless whoever found you forgot to report you." The man stood, brushing wool dust off his knees. "Which would make him a poor sailor. Rules exist for reasons." He looked down at Tobias for another long moment, and something crossed his face — quick, like a bird across a window — that Tobias couldn't read yet, though he'd spend weeks trying. "There's a coil of line by the mainmast foreward that wants coiling. If a certain person with no name happened to be coiling it, no one could report him for anything except untidy work. So make it tidy."

Then he climbed the ladder and was gone, and the hatch light swung, and Tobias sat alone in the dark holding an apple core, feeling like a door had opened somewhere that he hadn't even known was a door.

* * *

His name, when he risked using it aloud, was Tobias, and the deckhand — Dominic, the others called him, though he answered to nearly anything — never asked again after the first day, which was its own kind of courtesy.

Work, it turned out, was better than hiding. This surprised Tobias profoundly. Hiding had been his profession, his art, his religion. But coiling line in plain sight, hauling on a halyard when someone shouted for hands, learning that the galley cook, Brann, would trade a heel of bread for someone small enough to scrape out a pot nobody else could reach — this was a different country, and in this country people nodded at you. Said *morning*. Once, a huge tattooed able seaman named Kessler lifted Tobias bodily out of the way of a swinging block without breaking conversation, the way you'd move a chair, and set him down and said, "Watch yourself, lad," and Tobias walked away floating about six inches off the deck.

Nobody is ever too old, he decided, to be moved like furniture by someone enormous. It counts as affection.

Dominic taught him things without seeming to teach them — corrected his bowline by tying a worse one and saying, "Does this look right to you?", which was a trick, obviously, but an effective one. Showed him how to read weather off the water. Never touched him, never ruffled his hair the way adults in stories did, and Tobias understood dimly that this restraint was also a kindness, the way leaving a stray cat's food at arm's length is a kindness.

At night, Dominic let him sleep in the wool. "Officially you're a volunteer deckhand sleeping on duty," he said. "Unofficially, if Odile asks, you're my nephew."

"Am I?"

"No. But she won't check. She's got a manifest to worry about, and manifests are worse than children."

Odile, the quartermaster, turned out to be a narrow, sharp-eyed woman with ink permanently on her fingers and a voice like a ledger closing. She looked at Tobias exactly once, head to foot, the way she probably looked at crates, assessing weight and value.

"He eats," she said to Dominic. It was not a question.

"A bit."

"Everything eats. That's the whole problem with everything." She wrote something in her book — Tobias craned; it might have been his existence, entered in ink, which felt enormous — and snapped the book shut. "There's a torn sack of rice behind the flour. Rice that's already spilled isn't stolen, it's salvaged. See he understands the distinction."

Tobias understood the distinction completely and loved her for it.

* * *

But holds keep secrets, and so do passengers, and Tobias, who had spent years learning to hear things through walls, began to hear things through bulkheads.

There were factions aboard. That was the grown-up word he pieced together later; at the time it was just *the loud ones in the saloon* and *the quiet ones by the rail*. Men in good coats that had gone shiny at the cuffs, talking in corners about legitimacy and recognition and a word dropped soft as a stone: *codes*. Women counting children with the grim arithmetic of mothers. An old man with white hair who went every evening to a tiny room near the stern that Tobias was fairly sure had once been a paint locker and was now full of paper — paper, of all things, saved from a burning country like someone rescuing photographs from a house fire.

"They say there's something aboard worth a kingdom," he heard a passenger whisper to another, the night the storm glass began to cloud. "Something in a courier's keeping. Something that decides who governs when we land."

"And they say the man carrying it doesn't look like much," the other whispered back. "A deckhand, someone said. Quiet fellow. Good with knots."

Tobias lay in the wool that night doing sums he didn't want to do. Apples that appeared from nowhere. A man who crouched down to boy-height. A stranger with no history aboard a ship where everyone else had too much. *Good with knots.*

He didn't feel betrayed. That was the strange part. He felt — chosen, maybe, or trusted, or simply *kept*, the way you keep something valuable in a drawer instead of on a shelf. Whatever Dominic was carrying, whatever danger came with it, the man had fed him instead of reporting him, and in the mathematics of Tobias's short life that sum outweighed nearly everything.

Still. In the morning, when Dominic found him already coiling line, tidy as scripture, Tobias said, casually, to the rope:

"If somebody was looking for something on this ship. Something important." He pulled a loop tight. "Where would they look first?"

Dominic's hands went still for exactly one heartbeat. Then they resumed, smooth as tide.

"Why do you ask?"

"Men talk in the saloon," Tobias said. "Loud ones. They talk about codes." He glanced up, and let a little of his mother's sharpness show through his face, the inheritance he was proudest of. "And I'm small, and nobody looks at me, and I hear everything. You should know that about your nephew before you decide how useful he is."

For a moment Dominic just looked at him, and the morning wind came over the rail and filled the sails above them with that deep fabric sigh, like the whole ship drawing breath.

"Well," Dominic said at last. "First lesson, then. The most important cargo is always hidden in the least interesting place." He tossed Tobias the end of the line. "Coil that. And afterward — there's a man aboard named Merrick who keeps papers in the old paint locker. He'll want to meet the boy who hears everything."

"Why?"

"Because," Dominic said, turning toward the bow, and Tobias would remember the smile he threw back over his shoulder — tired, crooked, real — for the rest of his life, "he collects the things everyone else forgets to save."

Above them the gale flag was already climbing the halyard, black and snapping like a warning, and far off across the gray water the sky was stacking itself into mountains.
