## 6. Corvane's Bargain

There was an art to being unremarkable, and Corvane had spent eleven years perfecting it.

The trick, he had learned in the first month of his imprisonment, was not to be invisible—guards noticed invisible men—but to be *slightly disappointing*. The man whose face you couldn't quite summon later. The one who always seemed about to say something and then didn't. He cultivated it like a garden: a stoop that wasn't quite a stoop, a cough deployed at strategic intervals, a way of standing near walls as though he owed them money.

He was practicing this art now, third day out from Vellermont, wedged into a corner of the Meridian's aft deck where the tar barrels met the rail. From here he could watch the passengers without seeming to watch anyone: the loyalists who dined at dusk with linen they'd smuggled aboard; the profiteers who counted crates by lamplight and lied about their weights; the exiles, gray-faced and patient, who watched the wake as if the republic might surface in it like a drowned man.

And among them—the courier.

Oh, he thought he was clever. Deckhand's coat, rope-burned hands he'd earned honestly enough in three days of hauling line, a satchel hidden somewhere below with the devotion most men reserved for mistresses or gods. But Corvane had spent two years sharing a cell block with people who were very good at being other people, and he knew the tells. A true sailor spat over the leeward rail without thinking. Dominic Winterbourne *remembered* to spit over the leeward rail, and there was a small, private satisfaction in his face each time he got it right, like a boy reciting his catechism without stumbling.

Also—and Corvane permitted himself a thin smile—he still walked like a man carrying something valuable. Not in his arms. In his spine.

*Soon*, Corvane thought. *But not yet.* A bargain made too early could be refused. A bargain made too late was a threat. The art was in the interval between, when a man's loneliness did the negotiating for you.

He took out his pocket watch—a habit, not a necessity; the thing had run four minutes slow since Vellermont and he'd stopped correcting it—and clicked it shut again. Somewhere below, the ship groaned through a swell, and above him the gulls argued over the galley scraps with the moral fervor of parliamentarians.

*Patience*, he told himself. He had been patient for two years in a cell six feet wide. He could be patient on a ship with a horizon.

* * *

The opportunity came sooner than patience required, which Corvane had learned to treat as either luck or providence, depending on how much wine he'd had.

It was the boy.

Corvane had marked him early—a stowaway, obviously, all elbows and guilty eyes, haunting the hold like a small ghost with an appetite. He'd assumed the ship would deal with him in the usual manner: rations docked, dignity docked, tossed at the first port like ballast no one wanted. Instead he had watched the deckhand—the courier, though neither of them knew Corvane knew—catch the boy stealing bread from the galley stores and, instead of ringing the quartermaster's bell, sit down beside him and cut the loaf in half.

Corvane had watched this from the shadows of the cargo netting with an expression he hoped resembled mild boredom and suspected resembled hunger, because he hadn't eaten since morning and the bread smelled excellent.

*There*, he thought. *That's the lever.*

A man with a secret kindness is a man who can be blackmailed. A man with a secret kindness and a satchel full of diplomatic codes is a man who can be *courted*.

So that evening, when the deckhand came off his watch and found a stranger sitting on his sea chest—not his, technically; everything aboard belonged to the Meridian now—turning a worn letter over in his hands as though he didn't know its contents by heart, Corvane was ready.

"You're Winterbourne," he said pleasantly, and watched the man go very still in the particular way of soldiers and couriers and other professionals whose stillness is itself a confession.

"I think you have me confused with someone."

"I often do." Corvane smiled, apologetic, self-deprecating, the smile of a man who apologized to furniture when he bumped into it. "I confuse everyone. It's my one talent. Corvane." He did not offer the hand; offering hands invited refusal, and refusals set the temperature wrong. "Formerly of the Chastain Street prison. Formerly of several places. Currently of nowhere, which I find restful."

The deckhand—Dominic, because a name denied twice was a name confirmed—hadn't moved. His eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, to the letter in Corvane's fingers.

*Ah*, thought Corvane. *There it is.*

"You've been looking at this," he said, lifting the letter slightly, "for three days. You think I haven't noticed. Everyone believes they're unobserved; it's the great mercy of the human condition." He turned the letter so the direction of the fold caught the lamplight—the left-handed crease, the little ink blot shaped like a bird at the fourth line, the slant of the hand that leaned east like a wind-blown tree. "You recognize the writing."

"I don't know what you—"

"Her name," Corvane said gently, "is Lys."

* * *

What followed was, Corvane reflected afterward, one of the finer performances of his career, and he had once performed a dying man for nine days to escape a work detail, complete with authentic fever.

He gave Dominic the truth first, because truth was the only currency that bought trust, and he happened to be rich in it. Yes, he had known her. Yes, in Chastain Street, block C, where the republic had sent the people it was embarrassed by before it sent them anywhere worse. She had been in the cell across from his for fourteen months. She had taught him to play a game with knucklebones and a slate floor. She had laughed at his jokes, which put her in a category of perhaps four living persons. She had—

Here he paused, and let the pause do its work, because the next part was also true and therefore devastating.

"—she was taken out in March," he said. "Two years ago. They told us she was executed. There was even a list. Her name was on it, in proper order, alphabetized. The regime was very tidy about its murders." He turned the letter over. "Tidiness, it turns out, is not proof."

"And this?" Dominic's voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man walking across ice while insisting it isn't ice. "You expect me to believe a dead woman wrote this?"

"I expect you to *want* to believe it," Corvane said. "Which is more useful, and more honest." He held the letter out—not all the way, just far enough. "Read the postscript."

Dominic hesitated. Then, because he was his sister's brother and hesitation had never once saved either of them, he took it.

Corvane watched him read. Watched the professional mask hold for exactly four lines and then fail at the fifth, where the ink bird sat. Watched his thumb press against the paper hard enough to whiten, as though he could hold the hand that had written it through the page.

"She called me Dommick," Dominic said quietly. "Nobody else ever spelled it that way. She said the extra letters were for the parts of me she liked best."

"Sentimental child," said Corvane, with such precise affection that Dominic looked up sharply, and Corvane shrugged. "She called me that too. About herself. She knew exactly what she was."

"What do you want?"

And there it was—the whole conversation arriving at its destination like a train into a station, right on schedule. Corvane rose from the sea chest, slowly, joints cracking in the cold, and made himself look tired rather than dangerous, which required effort, because he was both.

"There's a fishing village," he said. "Two days south of the neutral harbor, past the shoals the charts stopped charting when the admiralty stopped existing. She's there. She has a berth on a coastal trader—the *Kestrel*, out of Maren, if names matter to you, and they should, because names are all any of us have left. She has a berth," he repeated, "but she does not have safe passage, because the waters between here and there are full of men who would very much like to know what the Meridian is carrying, and some of them shoot first and alphabetize later."

"The codes." Dominic said it the way a man names a disease he suspects he has.

"I never said—"

"You've been watching me for three days. You boarded at Vellermont with letters timed to arrive when I'd be too far out to turn back. You want the codes, and you're selling me my sister." Dominic folded the letter along its old creases, precisely, and held it back out. "I grew up with a sister who bargained professionally. I know the accent."

For a moment—briefly, dangerously—Corvane almost liked him.

"Let me be plainer, then, since flattery is wasted on you." He did not take the letter back. "Yes. I want what you're carrying. And yes, I am prepared to trade you the single most valuable object in your universe to get it. That's not a trick, Winterbourne; that's a *market*. Everything aboard this ship is for sale—the cabins, the berths, the loyalty of the loyalists, the conscience of the profiteers, and the memory of that dear old archivist who looks at everyone as though cataloguing them for posterity, which, knowing Merrick, he is. The only difference between me and everyone else aboard is that I'm honest about my price."

"And hers? What's her price?"

Corvane paused.

This was the question he had rehearsed answers to—three of them, graded by plausibility—and found, standing in the cold with the gulls screaming overhead, that he disliked all three.

"Hers?" he said at last. "Hers is that you live. She didn't ask for the codes, Winterbourne. She asked for *you*. The codes were my addition." He smiled, thin and rueful. "Consider which of us is the merchant and which is the hostage."

"That's a very convenient story."

"It's the only kind I tell. Convenient stories survive interrogation." He finally took the letter back, tucked it inside his coat, over the heart, where documents traveled when they mattered. "You needn't decide tonight. You needn't decide tomorrow. But decide before the weather turns—I've been on this ocean long enough to smell what's coming, and storms make men honest in ways they regret." He stepped past Dominic, then paused, close enough to speak low. "One more thing, gratis, because I'm feeling generous and you look like a man who keeps accounts. Your friend the quartermaster has begun keeping a second manifest. Names that should be dead. If I found yours on it, others will. Whatever you choose—choose *soon*."

He walked away down the dark deck, stooped and unremarkable, the man you wouldn't remember tomorrow, and permitted himself, once he was certain no one was watching, to exhale.

* * *

In his hammock that night—or rather in the corner of the hold where his hammock nominally existed but where he preferred to sit upright against the hull, an old prison habit, sleeping against stone—Corvane took out the letter and read it again by the light of a stub of candle he'd traded two fishhooks for.

*They told you I was dead because it was easier than telling you what I did to stay alive,* she had written, in that eastward-slanting hand. *When you learn what I did, you may hate me. Hate me alive, Dommick. It's better for both of us.*

Below that, the postscript, the ink bird, the words that had cracked the courier open on the aft deck like a walnut:

*P.S. — Tell him about the knucklebones game. He'll know it's really me. Nobody else cheats at it the way I taught him.*

Corvane smiled at the ceiling of the hold, at the shadowed ribs of the ship around him, at the whole creaking, leaking, desperate wooden world.

The game was real. The cheating was real. The cell across from his, the fourteen months, the laughter—all of it, true, every word, which was the great secret of his profession that no one ever believed: the best lies contained no lies at all, only truths arranged in useful orders.

He had not mentioned, for instance, that the Kestrel's berth was contingent on delivery. He had not mentioned that the men in the southern waters answered to his employers. He had not mentioned what his employers intended for a ship full of exiles carrying legitimacy in a satchel, or why a parley flag had already been folded into his luggage like a spare shirt.

None of that was lying. All of it was simply truth, waiting its turn.

Above him, faint through the deck planks, he heard the wind change—swing south, then west, gathering its skirts. He heard the quartermaster's boots cross the deck overhead, quick and decisive, a woman recalculating.

*Storm coming*, Corvane thought, clicking his slow watch shut. *Good. Storms loosen what calm weather seals.*

He blew out the candle and, in the dark, began composing the dispatch he would send the moment he knew which way the courier would break—because the man would break; they all broke, it was merely a question of toward whom—and whether it would be a report of success, or a request for soldiers.

By morning, the barometer had begun to fall, and far to the southwest, over water no one was watching, the sky had gone the color of a bruise deciding where to land.
