## 7. Storm Glass

The glass fell all night, and Odile watched it fall the way other people watch a slow leak in a boat they cannot beach.

She kept the barometer on the shelf above her charts, a brass thing with a face like a disapproving aunt, and every hour she tapped it twice with one knuckle—once for respect, once for honesty. By the third tap past midnight it had dropped low enough that she said aloud, to no one, "Well then," and went up to tell the helmsman to shorten sail before the wind made the decision for them.

The Meridian did not like winter. She was a merchant hull pressed into evacuation service, built to carry wine and wool, not four hundred frightened souls and their trunks of silver. She rolled like a drunkard remembering an old grievance. Odile had sailed her eleven years and loved her anyway, the way you love a relative who embarrasses you at gatherings.

"Glass is down to twenty-nine and falling," she told Halvard at the wheel. He was sixty if he was a day, with hands like knotted rope, and he nodded the way old sailors nod—which is to say he had already known.

"Passengers will be sick," he said.

"Passengers are already sick."

"Then they'll be sicker, and louder about it."

Odile almost smiled. That was the trouble with this voyage: everything true sounded like complaint. She climbed down into the waist of the ship to see to the deck cargo lashed under tarpaulins, and there she found Dominic Winterbourne hauling on a line in the rain, sleeves rolled, hair plastered to his skull, looking for all the world like exactly what he pretended to be—a deckhand who had signed on at the last port because a strong back was worth a berth.

He was not a deckhand. Odile had known that since the second day, when she'd caught him coiling line with a clerk's precision, each loop measured as though it might be inspected. Sailors coil fast and ugly; clerks coil beautifully. But she'd said nothing, because whatever else he was, he worked hard, asked no questions, and had once spent four hours bailing out the aft bilge without being asked, which put him above half the crew in her estimation.

"Storm coming," she said to him.

"Yes, ma'am." He didn't stop hauling.

"You'll want to get below when it hits. Passengers'll be screaming and throwing up and praying to three different gods, and none of them will be listening."

"I'm better useful up here."

Odile studied him a moment. Rain ran off his chin. There was something in his face she recognized from other men in other bad years—the look of a person who had decided that stillness was safety, that if he just kept moving and kept his head down, history would forget to include him.

"Suit yourself," she said. "But useful and alive go together better than useful and drowned."

* * *

The gale arrived two hours after dawn, the way gales do—not gradually but all at once, as though the sea had been holding its breath and finally let it out.

By noon the Meridian was running under storm canvas, and the passengers had discovered that the ocean was not a large flat thing between them and safety but an enormous hostile animal with opinions. Odile went below decks once, against her better judgment, to check the lashings in the hold, and came back up with her ears full of weeping and praying and the particular smell of four hundred people who have lost faith in their stomachs.

It was in the saloon—the grand saloon, which had once served first-class passengers little sandwiches and now served as the republic-in-miniature—that she saw the factions taking shape.

She hadn't meant to linger. But the ship rolled, a long sickening lean to port, and everything loose slid leftward, and among the things that slid were the pretenses. A man named Cassel—a fat, ringed, self-satisfied profiteer who had bought his passage triple-price and would not stop mentioning it—had gathered six or seven gentlemen around a lantern, and they were talking in the low urgent way of men dividing something.

"We should present a united position to the captain," Cassel was saying. "Those of us with assets at stake. The ship must make port quickly; every day drifting costs—"

"The ship makes port where ports will have us," said a woman Odile knew by sight only as Madame Ferreol, widow of a customs minister, her voice dry as old paper. "Unless you propose to row."

"I propose organization. Order. There are elements aboard—" here Cassel lowered his voice, though not enough, "—elements carrying things. Papers. Obligations. If the wrong cargo reaches a neutral harbor, we all pay for it."

Odile's spine went cold, and it wasn't the wet wool.

Across the saloon, huddled near the stove, sat the other faction—or rather, the faction that refused to be one. Loyalists, exiles, clerks, a schoolteacher, old Merrick the archivist wrapped in two coats with his hands around a tin cup he never drank from. They weren't organizing. They were enduring. And Odile thought, with a pang, that was the whole tragedy of the republic in one image: one side organizing to seize, the other side too decent or too broken to organize at all.

"Quartermaster." Merrick's voice stopped her at the door. The old man's eyes were bright behind his spectacles. "You've come to take our temperature?"

"Ship's temperature's the only one I keep, sir."

"A shame. Someone ought to be keeping the other kind." He nodded toward Cassel's lantern-circle. "They're forming a government, you know. Small as a lifeboat, but governments start smaller than that."

"And what are you forming?"

Merrick smiled faintly. "A memory. It doesn't need votes."

* * *

The worst of the storm hit at dusk, and Odile did not leave the deck for nine hours.

She remembered those hours afterward in fragments: Halvard at the wheel with salt frozen in his beard; a wave over the stern that carried away the chicken coop and very nearly a boy called Pieter; the mizzen sheet parting with a crack like a rifle shot; Dominic Winterbourne appearing beside her in the blackness, soaked through, saying nothing, simply putting his hands where she pointed them, again and again, until her arms burned and her voice gave out and the two of them fell into a rhythm that needed no words at all.

At some point in the smallest hours, wedged against the rail while the ship clawed through the dark, he shouted to her over the wind:

"How far to neutral water?"

"Two days! Three, if this keeps up!"

"And if the harbor closes?"

That question again. It had been circling the ship for days like a gull. Nobody answered it directly, because nobody wanted to look at it straight-on: the rumors that the neutral powers were nervous, that papers were being checked, that certain cargoes were suddenly contraband.

"That's tomorrow's weather!" Odile shouted back.

He laughed—actually laughed, a short startled bark—and for a moment in the middle of the storm, with death sloshing around their boots, they were simply two tired people sharing a joke, and Odile thought: *there it is, that's why I don't report him.* Not suspicion. Fellowship. The storm sorted people faster than any manifest could.

* * *

Morning came gray and bruised, the wind backing off to a hard moan, the sea still heaving like something recently wronged. Odile took stock: one boat smashed, the mizzen sheet spliced, forty chickens lost to the deep, three passengers injured—one badly, a man whose arm had been caught in a door slammed by a wave.

And the saloon politics had matured overnight, the way mold matures in a damp cellar.

Cassel had used the storm. That was the thing Odile understood by midmorning, watching from the doorway with a mug of tea she had no intention of drinking. While everyone else had been bailing and praying, Cassel and his circle had been talking. Now there was a petition circulating—elegant paper, elegant hand—requesting that the captain permit a "Passenger Committee for Order and Welfare" with authority to inspect baggage, regulate rations, and "advise" on navigation. Sixty signatures. Cassel claimed eighty.

"He wants to search for the codes himself," said Madame Ferreol, appearing at Odile's shoulder. She spoke quietly, eyes forward, like a woman commenting on the weather. "If he finds them, he sells them. If he can't find them, he'll sell the searching. Either way he profits. I knew his sort in the ministry. They were the ones who unlocked the doors."

"You could sign the other list instead," Odile said.

Ferreol's mouth twitched. "There is no other list. That's rather the problem, isn't it? The loyalists won't organize—they think organizing is what *they* did, the people who ruined everything. So they sit by the stove being virtuous, and virtue has never once stopped a thief." She sighed. "Someone ought to write a second list. Someone with standing. Not me—I'm a widow, widows are decorative. But a quartermaster. A quartermaster is practical."

"I count crates, madame. I don't count votes."

"Counting is counting." Ferreol drifted away, leaving Odile with her cold tea and an uncomfortable feeling, like a pebble in a boot.

* * *

In the afternoon the sea calmed enough for the injured man's arm to be splinted properly, and Odile went forward to check the forecastle, and there she found Tobias.

The boy was sitting on an upturned crate in the corner where the lamplight didn't reach, knees drawn up, watching the door with the fixed alertness of a small animal. Officially he was the nephew of a stoker and belonged nowhere near the passenger sections; unofficially he was everywhere, and everyone below decks knew him and fed him in the manner of people feeding a stray cat they pretend not to have adopted.

"You're wanted," Tobias said.

"Am I."

"Cassel's man came asking questions. About who signed on at the last port. He wrote names down." The boy's chin lifted. "I didn't like his face, so I listened."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Eleven years old, maybe twelve, with a smudge of coal on his cheek and the eyes of someone much older wearing a child's skin like an ill-fitting coat.

"What did you do?"

"Took his notebook." Tobias produced it from inside his shirt with the modest pride of a magician. "Dropped it in the soup. Well. In *a* soup. It was mostly water by then."

Odile bit the inside of her cheek to keep from laughing. "And Cassel's man?"

"Said the storm must have taken it. He was too embarrassed to argue."

"Tobias." She crouched to his level. "You understand that stealing from dangerous men has consequences."

"I didn't steal it. Soup stole it." His face went carefully blank. "Was that wrong? Should I give it back?"

The soup, mercifully, had rendered most of the names illegible, including—Odile noted with a small cold drop in her stomach—the entry that might have been *Winterbourne, D.*, now bloomed into grey pulp. She closed the ruined notebook.

"No," she said. "The sea provides. Go find Dominic Winterbourne and tell him Cassel's committee starts inspecting luggage tomorrow morning, and that a wise man checks his own bags tonight."

The boy slid off the crate. At the door he paused.

"Quartermaster? Why does everyone care what's in the bags?"

Because the republic is dying, Odile thought, and its last breath is written on paper, and paper weighs nothing and everything.

"Because some bags carry more than they weigh," she said. "Go on."

* * *

That evening, the glass began to rise. Odile tapped it twice—respect, honesty—and allowed herself one minute of relief before the ledger of worries reopened.

She stood at the stern rail in the cold blue dusk. Behind them: the wake, unspooling toward a country that no longer existed. Ahead: dark water, and somewhere beyond it, harbors with gates that might open or might not. Below: four hundred people, sorting themselves into the saved and the damned, the buyers and the sellers, the ones who organized and the ones who merely endured. Somewhere among them, a deckhand who coiled line like a clerk, carrying whatever it was that made fat Cassel lick his lips.

Halvard joined her at the rail, chewing the end of an unlit pipe.

"Committee inspects at dawn," he said.

"Mm."

"You going to allow it?"

"I'm going to stand next to it," Odile said, "with my ledger open, and count everything they touch, and remind them—with great politeness—that the charter says the master controls all spaces aboard, and the master has delegated that authority to me. Politely is the important word. Politely, in front of witnesses."

Halvard grunted approval. "And the deckhand?"

Odile watched the last light die on the water.

"Tell him the saloon's got a loose fitting aft of the stove," she said slowly. "Something a careful man might want to look at tonight, while everyone's at supper. Tell him the panel behind it comes off."

Halvard raised an eyebrow. "Is there a loose fitting?"

"There is now."

Below them, through the deck planks, came the sound of the saloon filling for supper—the clatter of tin plates, the murmur of factions, Cassel's laugh rolling out fat and satisfied over the heads of the hungry. And threading through it all, faint but unmistakable, the voice of old Merrick reading aloud from something, anything, because a room with a story in it was harder to divide.

Odile turned back to her charts. Two days to neutral water, if the harbors would have them. One night to hide whatever needed hiding.

The glass was rising. The storm, at least, kept honest appointments.

It was the men she worried about.
