## 8. Letters Never Burned

The letters had lived in Dominic's coat for eleven days, and he had treated them the way a man treats a wound he is afraid to look at: pressed flat, kept close, and never, under any circumstances, opened.

They rode against his ribs now as he climbed to the fo'c'sle after the midday ration, where the wind came off the water clean and cold enough to make a man feel briefly innocent. The *Meridian* rolled beneath him with the patient patience of an old woman carrying too many packages. Somewhere below his feet were six hundred souls, three factions pretending not to be factions, one stowaway boy who had recently been promoted from cargo to crew (a promotion that consisted entirely of being allowed to carry rope), and a satchel of codes that could crown or crucify whatever government rose from the ashes back on shore.

And against his ribs, in handwriting he would have known blindfolded, by touch alone, in the dark at the bottom of the sea: four letters from Lys.

He had read the first one. Only the first. He had read it the night Corvane handed it over at the last port, standing in the shadow of the customs house with his heart doing something undignified in his chest. He had gotten as far as *Dear Dom, I know what you'll think when you see this hand* — and then a whistle had blown, and Odile had shouted something about mooring lines, and he had folded the letter away like a man closing a door on a room where someone was crying.

Eleven days. He was, he reflected, a coward of considerable discipline.

"Staring at the horizon won't row us faster," said Odile, appearing beside him with two tin cups and the expression of a woman who had been waiting for him to be alone so she could corner him. She was sixty, built like a bollard, and had hands that could splice cable or break a jaw with equal efficiency. "Here. It's tea. Before you ask, no, it isn't good tea. The good tea went overboard in the storm, along with my dignity and half the starboard rail."

"Thank you," Dominic said, taking the cup. It tasted like hot salt and regret. He drank it anyway.

"You've been carrying those letters for near two weeks," Odile said, looking out at the water with the studied casualness of a woman who had raised four sons and knew exactly how guilt moved through a body. "Corvane's been watching you watch them. Man watches everything, that one. Like a cat at a mousehole."

"He can watch."

"Mm." She blew on her tea. "You know what my mother used to say? 'A letter unread is a debt unpaid.' She was talking about bills, mostly. But she'd have made an exception for you, I think. You've got the face of a man avoiding a bill."

"It's complicated."

"Most debts are." She drained her cup, set it on the capstan, and patted his shoulder once — a blow that would have felled a smaller man — before walking off toward the quarterdeck, leaving him alone with the wind and the letters and the slow, rolling accusation of the sea.

Dominic sat down on the hatch cover. He took out the first letter again — already opened, its creases soft from handling — and then, because apparently today was the day he intended to stop being a coward, he took out the other three as well.

He slit them open with his thumbnail, all at once, before his nerve could fail him, the way you pull a tooth.

* * *

*Dom.*

*I'm going to tell you the worst thing first, because I know you, and if I bury it at the end you'll only feel like I tricked you, and then you'll spend years wondering what else I buried. So here it is: I am alive because I talked. Because they put me in a room with a lamp and a man with a soft voice, and I told them names.*

*Not yours. Never yours. I want you to hold onto that, though I know how little comfort it will be. But other names. Names you also know.*

*I'll say them plainly, further down, because I owe them that much at least — plainness. But first I need you to understand how it happened, because I have rehearsed this letter four hundred times and every version begins with me explaining, and explanations are just shame wearing its good coat.*

*It started small. That's the truth nobody believes until it happens to them. They didn't ask me for treason. They asked me whether I'd seen a particular man at a particular café, and I had, and saying so seemed harmless, because surely everyone had seen him, he was always there. And then the man disappeared, and the soft-voiced one came back and thanked me, and asked about someone else, and I understood that the door behind me had closed, and that the only direction was forward, and that every step forward was made of somebody.*

*Do you remember Papa's shop? How the ledgers smelled? I think about that sometimes. I think: if I can still smell the ledgers, I am still myself. It's a stupid test. It has never once worked. I smell ledgers and I keep talking anyway, because the alternative is a cell with no window, and I discovered — God forgive me, Dom, I discovered it about me — that I would rather live badly than die well.*

*That's the confession. There it is. Your sister is a coward with a survivor's luck and a traitor's ledger, and she is alive, and she is writing to you from a fishing village north of Vell with a borrowed pen, and she loves you more than she deserves to love anyone.*

*Now the names, since I promised plainness.*

*Tomas Reiner. He ran the print shop off Cooper's Lane. He printed the pamphlets. They came for him in March.*

*The Aldana sisters. They sheltered couriers. I told them which house. I told them about the cellar.*

*And Merrick. Old Merrick, the archivist, who used to let us sit in the reading room when we were children and gave you those terrible honey biscuits you pretended not to like. They wanted to know where he kept the duplicates. The copies of the state papers, the ones hidden outside the capital. I told them there was a farm. I didn't know which farm. I swear to you I didn't know which farm, but I told them the region, and men with lanterns went knocking door to door through the whole valley, and he vanished, and I told myself he escaped, because that was easier than the arithmetic.*

*I heard he might be alive. On a ship going south. If he's aboard yours, Dom — and I suspect he is, because Corvane wouldn't have come to you otherwise, Corvane knows exactly what bait to bring — then you're going to look at me differently forever, and I deserve it, and I'm asking you for it anyway: look at me anyway.*

*I don't ask forgiveness. Forgiveness is a thing you earn and I haven't earned anything. I ask only this: don't decide about me while I'm still a ghost. Ghosts are easy to hate. They're weightless. Wait until I'm standing in front of you, heavy as anybody, and then decide.*

*Or don't wait. Don't write back. Burn this. You always burned things when you were angry — your compositions, my letters home from school, once a hat — and burning me would be the most sibling thing you've ever done, and I would even understand it.*

*But I hope you don't burn it. I hope you do the harder thing, which is to keep it, and hate it, and keep it anyway.*

*Your sister,*
*Lys*

*P.S. The pen is borrowed. The ink is bad. The handwriting is worse. Some things survive even a purge.*

*P.P.S. Tell Tobias — if the boy made it to you; Corvane said he might — that the blue scarf was mine, and I gave it to him on purpose, and he should keep it, and it means he's family now, and family is not a thing you get to unchoose, no matter what either of us does.*

Dominic read it twice. Then a third time, more slowly, the way you walk back through a burned house looking for anything that survived.

The wind had changed. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather while drowning. Below him, Tobias was hauling rope with a competence that two weeks earlier would have astonished everyone including Tobias, and the boy was wearing the blue scarf, knotted carelessly around his throat like a trophy, like a claim.

Family is not a thing you get to unchoose.

"Well," Dominic said aloud, to nobody. His voice cracked on the second word. "Damn it, Lys."

* * *

The second letter was shorter, written — judging by the shakier script — later, perhaps under time pressure, perhaps under watch. It was mostly practical. A fishing village called Sarnholt. A widow named Petra who owed Lys her life and could be trusted absolutely. A signal: a green cloth hung on the drying line meant *come ashore*, a red one meant *never come ashore, they are watching, go anywhere else in the world*.

But in the middle of the practicalities, one paragraph:

*I know what you're carrying. Don't deny it — Corvane described the satchel, and you've carried that same satchel since we were fifteen, you used to keep your poetry in it, yes, I knew about the poetry, I found the notebook when we were twelve and I have never once mentioned it because I am capable of mercy. If you still have the codes, then you are holding the republic in a bag, and I need you to hear this from someone who has seen the new regime up close, from inside its rooms, from beside its lamps: they are not strong, Dom. They are terrified. A frightened animal with a gun is still dangerous, but it is not a government, and it cannot last, and the only question is what gets built on the rubble and who holds the paper that says it may be built.*

*Bring me the codes and I can put them in the right hands. Hands I trust — yes, I know how that sounds coming from me. Test my hands. That's fair. Test everything about me. But don't let Corvane's hands near that satchel, whatever you do. Whatever he's told you, whatever he's promised — he is not working for me.*

She had underlined that last sentence twice, hard enough to tear the paper slightly.

Dominic stared at it. *He is not working for me.* Which raised the obvious and unpleasant question: who, precisely, was Corvane working for? And why did a man claiming to serve Lys carry letters she clearly hadn't written yesterday, in ink that had faded unevenly, describing events — the storm, the ration thefts, the knife fight in the hold — that had happened *after* the letters' dates?

He filed the thought away in the place where he kept all the thoughts he wasn't ready to have. It was getting crowded in there.

* * *

The third letter was the one that broke him a little.

It was dated eight months ago. It was not about codes or regimes or bargains. It was about their father.

*I went back to the shop. Or what's left of it. The front is a requisition office now, of all the banal things, men stamping forms where the counter used to be, and I stood outside for an hour like a madwoman staring at a window. But the back room — our back room, where you did your homework and I practiced forging your signature to sign your detention slips, which you never once noticed, which tells you everything about your powers of observation and everything about mine — the back room is untouched. They use it for storage. Nobody goes in. And on the shelf above the workbench, behind the jar of brushes, there is still a tin, and in the tin, Dom, I found what you'll already guess I found: the photograph. All three of us on the harbor wall, you making that face, me laughing, Papa squinting into the sun. And his last note to you. The one he wrote the night before they took him, the one you never received because the post died before the postman did.*

*I took both. I'm sorry. I told myself it was to keep them safe and that's even true, but mostly I took them because I needed to touch something of ours that wasn't ruined, and I have so little left that isn't.*

*I'm keeping the photograph. Consider it rent for what I'm about to enclose.*

*I'm sending Papa's note. Read it when you're ready. Not before. I mean that. It's short — he never wasted words on paper, he saved them all for shouting — but it will land on you like weather, and you should choose the day.*

*I chose today. Badly. I've been crying for an hour and the widow thinks I've lost my mind, which is fair, I lost it somewhere around March two years ago and it hasn't turned up.*

*Read it on a good day, Dom. Find yourself a good day. I know the sea doesn't offer many.*

*— L.*

Folded inside, thin as onion skin, a strip of paper in a cramped, slanting hand. Their father's hand. Dominic held it without unfolding it for a long moment, feeling its weight, which was nothing, which was everything.

Then he unfolded it, because he had never in his life done the sensible thing when a bad idea presented itself.

*Dom —*

*If you're reading this I'm either dead or detained, and either way, listen: take your sister and go. Don't argue with me, I can hear you arguing. Go south, past the mountains, and don't look back except once, because you're allowed one look back, everyone is, that's the rule I just invented.*

*Be useful. Be kind. Those two together are worth more than brave. Brave is common in collapsing countries. Useful and kind is rare.*

*And keep her fed. She forgets to eat when she's scheming, always has, since she was six. Check on her.*

*That's all. I love you both. Now go.*

*— Papa*

Dominic sat on the hatch cover with the paper shaking in his fingers and the tea gone cold in the tin cup beside him, and for a while he did nothing at all except breathe, and miss people, and listen to the ship creak around him like a large patient animal.

Useful and kind. Well. He was managing one of them, some days.

* * *

The fourth letter he read at dusk, up at the bow, away from the lamplight of the deck, with the coast of nowhere sliding past in the dark.

It was different from the others. Calmer. Colder, almost — the calm of someone who has stopped apologizing and started strategizing, and Dominic recognized the shift the way you recognize a change in wind direction: not by seeing it, but by feeling it against your face.

*Dom.*

*I've stopped writing you apologies. Not because I retract them, but because I've noticed that apology is a currency, and I've spent enough of mine that you should start asking what I'm buying with it.*

*Here's what I'm buying: a future with you in it. That's the whole account. Everything I did — every name, every cellar, every door I opened for men with lanterns — I did to stay alive long enough to stand in a room with you again, and I refuse to pretend that motive makes it noble. It doesn't. But it makes it *mine*. I own it. I'll carry it. Merrick, if he lives, can hate me to his grave and I will help him dig it.*

*Which brings me to him. If he's aboard — and I believe he is — then here is what I know that you don't: Merrick knew. About me. Before the end. One of the Aldana sisters got word out before they came for her, and it reached him, and he knew what I'd done, and do you know what he did with that knowledge? Nothing. He sent no warning to the others. He cut me off, quietly — stopped answering my notes, closed his accounts to me — but he never reported me, though reporting me would have bought him protection. He simply... let me go. Let me fall. And let me live with it.*

*I've thought about that for two years. Whether it was mercy or contempt. I've decided it was both, because it usually is, and because Merrick is the sort of man who understands that punishment and forgiveness can share a bed without scandal.*

*So when you find him — and you will, you're drawn to old men and libraries like a moth to a very dull flame — don't protect me from him. Don't soften it. Ask him. Let him say what I did in his own words, with his own silences. He earned that much. I stole it from him; give it back.*

*And then come to me, Dom. Come to Sarnholt. Bring whatever you're carrying, bring the boy, bring your anger and your shame and that ridiculous satchel full of poetry you think I don't know about. Bring all of it. I'll be on the dock with a red scarf or a green one, and whichever it is, I'll be your sister underneath it, and underneath that I'll be the woman who did what she did, and all three of us will just have to learn to live in the same body.*

*It's crowded in here already. What's one more.*

*— L.*

Dominic folded the letters together, slowly, and put them back inside his coat, against his ribs, where they had lived for eleven days and would live for eleven more, or eleven hundred, because she was right — he was not going to burn them. Burning required a decision, and he was not ready to decide anything except that his sister was alive, and monstrous, and beloved, and that all three facts occupied the same square inch of his chest without contradiction.

Love complicated by shame. Shame complicated by love. Neither one winning. Both of them staying.

"Ahoy, Winterbourne!"

Tobias scrambled up the fo'c'sle ladder, breathless, scarf flying, holding a lantern that swung wild shadows across the deck. "Mr. Merrick wants you. In the library. He says—" the boy paused, catching his breath, and delivered the message with the gravity of a court herald, "he says, quote, 'Tell him to come now, before his courage fails him, and tell him to bring the letters.'"

Dominic went very still.

"The letters," he repeated. "He said *the letters*."

"That's what he said." Tobias frowned, sensing the temperature drop. "Is something wrong?"

Below them, in the dark, the archivist's lamp was already lit in the little room of rescued papers, and it burned steady and yellow and patient, like a trap laid by a very kind man.

"No," Dominic said, standing, tucking the letters deep into his coat. "I don't think so. Stay here and mind the lantern, Toby. Keep it trimmed low."

"Why?"

"Because," Dominic said, starting down the ladder toward the one conversation he dreaded more than any storm or soldier, "I have a feeling we're about to learn what memory costs."

Behind him, the boy lifted the lantern high against the dark, and its light reached farther than either of them knew.
