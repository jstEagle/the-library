## 10. Mutiny in Miniature

Tobias had learned three things aboard the *Meridian*, and he counted them like coins.

First: the hold smelled of tar, salt pork, and other people's fear, and you got used to all three if you breathed through your mouth.

Second: nobody looked at a boy carrying a lantern. A boy with a lantern was furniture. He was a hinge, a bucket, a bit of the ship that had learned to walk. This was the most useful thing anyone had ever taught him, though Dominic hadn't meant it as a lesson — he'd just shoved the lamp into Tobias's hands one evening and said, "Go see what Odile wants," and Tobias had walked the whole length of the ship without a single adult asking him his name or his business or why his boots didn't fit.

Third: hunger made people stupid. Not weak — stupid. There was a difference, and the difference was that weak people knew they were being foolish and did it anyway, while hungry people genuinely believed that crawling into the ration locker at midnight was a clever idea no one had ever thought of before.

It was the third thing that got him into trouble on the night everything changed.

* * *

The hold of the *Meridian* at night was a country unto itself. Crates stacked like houses in a poor man's city, rope coiled in streets, barrels standing in for trees — though Tobias had seen real trees only twice in his life, so maybe they stood in for something else. The passengers who slept down here had hung blankets from the deck beams to make rooms, and the blanket-rooms had names, unofficially. The corner near the water casks was Loyalist Row, because the men there still wore their old government sashes folded up small, like handkerchiefs they were ashamed of. Amidships was where the Voss brothers held court, profiteers with soft hands and hard eyes who'd bought passage with gold and treated the ship as a delay between fortunes. And aft, nearest the warmest pipes, were the exiles — poets and clerks and one opera singer who sang only when she thought everyone was asleep, which she never quite managed, because her voice carried like weather.

Tobias belonged to none of them. He belonged to the space behind the spare canvas and the coil of signal hawsers, where Dominic had grudgingly permitted a bedroll after the incident with the salt beef, which was not a story Tobias intended to ever tell anyone, especially not Dominic, who had caught him mid-theft and instead of beating him had sighed like a disappointed schoolmaster and said, "If you're going to steal, boy, steal better," and then fed him anyway.

That was the thing about Dominic Winterbourne nobody would believe if Tobias ever told them: the man had a face like a locked ledger and hands that were endlessly, quietly kind. He mended things. He'd fixed Tobias's boot with a strip of leather and four nails, saying nothing, and Tobias had understood that this was how the man said *you may stay*.

So when Tobias woke past midnight to the sound of a padlock that should not have been turning, he did what any sensible stowaway-turned-deckhand would do.

He put out his little lamp, kept the stub lit under his shirt, and went to spy.

* * *

The ration locker sat beneath the mainmast housing, iron-banded, its padlock new — Odile had replaced it herself after the second week, muttering about "passengers with opinions about inventory." The lock now hung open on its hasp, and by the light of a shuttered lantern Tobias counted three figures crouched around it.

Two he recognized: a broad-shouldered man named Halloran from Loyalist Row, and a narrow youth with quick fingers whom everyone called Finch, though Tobias suspected it wasn't his name any more than Tobias was really just "boy." The third figure was large and unfamiliar, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin, face shadowed — and that was wrong, because Tobias had made a private study of every soul aboard, the way other boys studied birds or cards. He knew faces. He did not know this one.

They were loading flour sacks into a net. Not eating them. Loading them.

That distinction mattered. Tobias had watched enough hungry people to know what stealing-to-eat looked like: furtive, fast, crumbs in the beard, shame everywhere. This was slow and organized, sacks passed hand to hand and lowered toward... toward what? The hold's aft section, where the ballast ran and the hull curved close to the waterline. Where a determined man could knock loose a planking board and pass cargo out to a boat alongside, if a boat happened to be waiting in the dark of a winter sea.

Tobias's mouth went dry. He thought of the storm two nights gone, of Odile on the quarterdeck with spray frozen in her eyebrows, shouting that the ship was riding light in the stern, *riding light, somebody tell me why we ride light.* Everyone had blamed bad ballast. Nobody had blamed theft, because nobody but a boy who lived in the hold had been awake at the hours when the hold spoke its secrets.

Finch straightened, stretched, and said something too quiet to catch. The big man in the oilskin laughed — a low, wet sound — and patted a shape under his coat that was long and hard and absolutely not a flour sack.

A knife. Or worse.

Tobias did the arithmetic of fear quickly, the way he did all sums: if they found him, a boy alone in the dark, they might kill him for knowing. If he ran for the officers, they might finish loading before anyone came, and besides, half the crew liked Halloran, and the mate on watch tonight owed Halloran money — Tobias knew that too; he knew everything, that was his whole occupation.

But there was one person whose face went hard as ship's biscuit at the mere suggestion of short rations, one person who had already once chosen to feed a thief rather than report him, and who therefore could be trusted completely with the opposite problem: an honest boy reporting thieves.

Dominic.

* * *

Finding him took longer than the spying had. Dominic didn't sleep like other people; he slept in installments, like a miser counting out coins, and he moved through the ship at night the way ghosts moved through stories — present, then not, then present somewhere else entirely. Tobias checked the galley (empty except for the cook's cat, who regarded him with the contempt of management), the deck (rain, rail, dark), and finally the archivist's room, where lamplight showed under the door like a struck match.

He knocked the way Merrick had taught him — two soft, one soft, "like asking permission twice" — and the door opened on Dominic with a book in one hand and his boots off, which meant he'd been sitting with old Merrick past midnight again, listening to the old man read aloud from rescued state papers as though the republic were a patient that might yet sit up and drink some broth.

"Tobias." Dominic's voice dropped immediately, the way it always did, as if the very fact of the boy required discretion. "It's the middle of the night."

"Yes," Tobias agreed. "That's when things happen."

Dominic looked at him for a moment. Behind him, Merrick raised his head from his desk like a tortoise scenting rain. "What kind of things?" Dominic asked.

"Theft things. Hold things. A man with a knife under his coat and a hole in the hull-side and a boat waiting, I think, or going to be." Tobias delivered it flat and fast, the way he'd rehearsed it against the canvas wall twice before knocking. "Halloran and Finch and a third man I don't know. They're taking flour aft. They've been taking things for days — that's why we ride light. I heard Odile say it. I didn't know until tonight what it was."

The change in Dominic was remarkable mostly because it was so small. His face did nothing dramatic. But his hands set the book down with exaggerated care, the way you'd put down something fragile while picking up something dangerous, and he was already reaching for his boots.

"Merrick," he said quietly, "if anyone asks, I'm asleep."

"At your age," Merrick murmured, "that lie will serve you another thirty years. Take the boy?"

"He comes with me. He knows where they are, and he's quieter than I am."

"I'm quieter than everybody," Tobias said. It was true and he was proud of it.

"Go back to your bedroll after," Merrick added, catching Tobias's sleeve as he passed, and pressing something into his hand — a boiled sweet, ancient and slightly fuzzed at one edge. Tobias ate it anyway. You didn't refuse an archivist's rations; that was practically sacrilege.

* * *

The hold, when they reached it, had gone quiet in the specific way that meant activity had merely paused. Dominic pulled Tobias behind the great stack of crated glass — the opera singer's piano, disassembled and crated, which everyone agreed was either a treasure or an insanity — and put his lips close to the boy's ear.

"Show me the hole."

Tobias led him along the hull line, counting frames under his breath the way he counted everything, and stopped at a stretch of darkness where the air came different — colder, tasting of sea. Dominic pressed his palm flat against the planking, moved it slowly, and stopped at a board that gave, very slightly, under pressure. Loose. Recently loosened. Through the gap, faint and rhythmic: oar-thumps. Someone out there. Someone waiting.

"They've been selling our food to a boat," Tobias whispered. "While we starve politely above them."

"That's about the size of it." Dominic's voice was calm, but Tobias had learned to hear the second voice underneath it, the cold one, the one that did sums of its own. "How many nights do you think?"

"Four? Five? Finch started coming down late last week. I thought he was meeting someone sweet on him."

"And was someone sweet on him?"

"No. Someone was paying him."

Dominic exhaled through his nose — almost, almost a laugh. "You'd have made a fine customs officer in another life."

"In this life I make a fine stowaway. It's a growing profession."

Then the light came.

Not theirs. A shuttered lantern swinging wide, unguarded, and voices — Halloran's voice, low and irritated, and the wet laugh answering it, and the scrape of the flour sack net being hauled up for another run. They were coming back along the hull line, directly toward the loose board, directly toward the two figures crouched in the dark who had exactly one lantern-stub, one boy, and no weapons at all.

Dominic moved first. He pushed Tobias into the shadow between crates and stepped out into the aisle himself, empty-handed, unhurried, wearing the face of a man who had simply come down to check on his cargo and was mildly surprised to find company.

"Well," said Dominic, pleasantly. "Don't stop on my account."

The lantern swung up. Halloran's face went through several expressions in rapid succession — surprise, calculation, decision — and landed on a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Courier-man," he said. Tobias flinched at the word. He'd never heard anyone call Dominic that. "Late for a walk, aren't you?"

"Couldn't sleep. Ship rides light. Thought I'd count barrels." Dominic's tone stayed conversational, mild as milk. "Funny thing about arithmetic. It doesn't care who likes it."

The big man in the oilskin stepped forward, and his coat fell open just enough, and there it was: a knife, long and honest and ugly, with a handle worn smooth by other men's troubles. "Ship rides light," the man repeated. His accent was coastal, foreign, not from any part of the republic Tobias knew. "Maybe it rides lighter still, if the courier falls overboard."

Everything after that happened the way storms happen — slowly, and then all at once.

Halloran said, "Now, there's no need—" which in Tobias's experience meant there was every need. Finch bolted, because Finch was smart enough to know when arithmetic had turned against him. The big man lunged, not for Dominic's throat but for the loose board, the evidence, and Dominic — mild, milk-faced Dominic — kicked the flour sack directly into his path. The man went down onto it in a white explosion, came up coughing and swinging, and the knife opened a bright line across Dominic's forearm before Tobias, acting entirely on instinct and entirely without a plan, seized the coil of signal hawser he'd been sleeping behind all week and heaved it into the aisle.

Rope is heavier than it looks. Rope falling across a man's legs in the dark is heavier than anything. The big man stumbled, cursed in his coastal tongue, and Dominic hit him — not elegantly, not like the heroes in the opera singer's songs, but hard and low and with the full weary weight of a man who had clearly done this before and hated that he remembered how. The knife skittered away across the boards. Tobias dove on it, slid on spilled flour, and came up holding it at arm's length like a fish that had bitten him.

Above them, boots on the ladder. Voices. The mate off-watch, drawn by the noise, lantern high — and behind him, impossibly, Odile herself, hair bound up for sleep and eyes already fully, catastrophically awake.

She surveyed the scene: the scattered flour, the groaning man, Halloran frozen with guilt written across him in letters a child could read, Finch's abandoned cap, Dominic bleeding calmly onto the deck, and Tobias standing in the middle of it all brandishing a knife nearly as long as his arm.

"Well," said Odile, in the voice she used for hurricanes and other inconveniences. "Someone start talking, and someone else find me a tourniquet, and I'll decide which order."

* * *

They talked. Or rather, others talked while Tobias sat on a crate being bandaged — not because he was hurt, but because Odile had decided that anyone involved in a knife fight at thirteen years old was injured as a matter of principle, and her principle had won. The mate's hands were quick and gentle. Dominic sat beside him, his own forearm wrapped, watching the interrogations with the expression of a man doing long division.

Halloran broke first. He always would have; he was a loyalist the way some men are seasick, constantly and against his will, and he'd told himself the flour was owed to him, back wages for a government that had stopped existing. Finch, dragged back from the ladder by two sailors, confessed faster and more thoroughly, naming the big man as "a broker, he said, a salvage broker," and admitting he'd never asked whose boat waited outside the hull. The big man said nothing at all. He smiled with his wet laugh and let Odile lash his wrists to a stanchion, and when she demanded a name he gave one that made Dominic go very still beside Tobias — though he said nothing, then, about why.

The flour went back into the locker. The loose board was nailed down with a thoroughness that suggested the carpenter had opinions. And Odile, closing her little logbook, paused over the two of them — the courier and the stowaway, side by side, floured white as ghosts.

"You," she said to Tobias, pointing with the pencil. "I don't put boys on watch rosters. That's regulation."

"Yes, ma'am," Tobias said, heart dropping through three decks.

"But regulations were written for ships where the crew sleeps at night." She snapped the book shut. "From tomorrow, you're hold-walker, first watch. Lantern and ledger. Anything moves that shouldn't, you come to me or him"—the pencil swung to Dominic—"before you play hero. Am I understood?"

"Yes, ma'am," Tobias said, heart returning at double speed.

She stalked off. Dominic bumped his shoulder gently against Tobias's. "Hold-walker," he said. "Congratulations. You're crew."

"I was always crew. I just wasn't paid in anything but boiled sweets."

"Then we'll fix that. Odile pays in cheese."

They sat a moment in the quieting hold, listening to the sea work against the patched board, and Tobias felt something settle in his chest that had no name he knew — a feeling like being lashed to a mast in a good way, belonging to something that moved.

"Dominic," he said. "That name the big man wouldn't give. You knew it."

For a moment he thought Dominic wouldn't answer. Then, quietly, watching the dark: "I did. And tomorrow I'll want to know how a salvage broker from the coast comes aboard wearing an officer's coat from the new regime — and why he smiles like a man expecting a delivery."

"A delivery of what?"

Dominic's hand went, briefly, unconsciously, to the worn leather satchel that never left his side.

"I think," he said, "we're about to find out."
