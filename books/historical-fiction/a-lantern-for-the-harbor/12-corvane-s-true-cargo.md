## 12. Corvane's True Cargo

The trouble with secrets, Dominic had learned over two years of carrying other people's, was that they grew heavier the longer you held them. Not all at once. In ounces. A secret was like ballast that no one else could see, and every morning the Meridian rode a little lower in the water because of what Dominic kept stitched into his satchel of tar and rope.

He was thinking about this—thinking about nothing else he could afford to think about—when Corvane saved him from a falling block of ice.

They were working the forward deck in the grey hour after dawn, chipping frozen spray off the rigging before it could unbalance anything, and a fist-sized chunk let go from the forestay directly above where Dominic knelt with his mallet. He didn't see it. He heard Corvane shout—a short, sharp sound like a man who'd spent years being listened to—and then hands on his shoulders hauling him backward, and the ice came down and burst against the deck where his skull had been.

For a moment they just stood there, breathing white.

"Well," Corvane said at last. "That would have been an inconvenient way for a deckhand to die."

"Would it?" Dominic asked. His heart was doing something unhelpful. "Inconvenient for whom?"

"For me." Corvane smiled his careful smile, the one that never quite reached whatever was behind it. "You owe me now. I find debts are the most reliable form of acquaintance."

* * *

Dominic went below with the excuse of checking the bilge, which nobody questioned, because going below to check the bilge was what a man did when he wanted to be miserable in private. The hold smelled of tar and wet wool and the particular sourness of too many people breathing the same air for too many days. He crouched by his satchel and did not touch it. Touching it would mean deciding something, and he wasn't ready to decide anything except that his hands were shaking, and that the shaking had started when Corvane caught him, and had not stopped since.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he'd been circling for days like a gull over a dead fish.

Corvane had shouted *before* the ice fell.

Not as it came loose. Not as it dropped. Before. A half-second, maybe less—but Dominic had been a courier through the worst two years of the republic's life, and couriers survived by noticing exactly this kind of half-second. You noticed when a checkpoint guard's eyes went to your shoes instead of your face. You noticed when a friendly voice used a word slightly wrong. And you noticed when a stranger on a rolling deck looked up at the right spot on the rigging a full breath before there was any reason to.

It could have been luck. Sailors got lucky. But Corvane wasn't a sailor. Corvane moved along the gunwale like a man walking a corridor he owned. Corvane's sea-legs were perfect, which no landsman's ever were, and his hands were wrong for a passenger—calloused across the palm in the specific pattern of someone who practiced gripping things, not working them.

And there was the matter of the letters.

Dominic had read Lys's letters so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. Her hand slanted hard to the right; she crossed her t's with a single impatient stroke; she wrote her capital L's like a small man raising a lantern. Every letter Corvane carried was faithful to all of it. That was precisely what troubled him now, lying in the dark of the hold with his heart tapping out its small alarm. Faithful. Too faithful. A forgery that good required a model, and a model meant access, and access to Lys's handwriting meant—

No. He wouldn't finish the thought. He lay there and refused to finish it, the way you refuse to look over a ledge even while your feet carry you toward it.

Above him, boots crossed the deck. He knew the tread now, the way you come to know any rhythm repeated enough: Odile's quick decisive step; Merrick's shuffle, apologetic even through planking; Tobias's uneven boy's stride, still growing into itself. And Corvane's—measured, unhurried, the tread of a man who had nowhere to be because everywhere would wait for him.

The trap, when he finally saw its shape, assembled itself out of things he already knew, each piece innocent alone. It took him most of that day, turning pieces over while he coiled rope and pretended his mind was on the work.

Piece one: Corvane had boarded at the last port—the last port, after the gates elsewhere had closed, when boarding lists were checked with unusual care. Yet Corvane's papers had passed without a flicker of scrutiny. Either the port officials were incompetent, or Corvane's papers were very good, or someone had arranged their passage. Good papers cost money. Arranged passage cost influence.

Piece two: Corvane had sought out Dominic—or rather, had made it possible for Dominic to seek him out—dangling letters in Lys's hand like bait over deep water. He had never once pressed. He had offered the bargain almost lazily, passage for codes, and then let it sit. Patient men with valuable cargo did not press. They waited for the cargo to ripen.

Piece three—and this was the piece that turned his blood cold, that made him stop mid-coil with a length of hawser slack in his hands—piece three was the storm. During the gale, when half the ship had been retching and praying and Odile had been lashed to her own wheel, Corvane had gone through the passengers' belongings. Dominic hadn't seen it. But Tobias had mentioned it, sideways, in the way the boy mentioned everything sideways, a report wrapped in complaint: *that gentleman asks a lot of questions about whose bag is whose, doesn't he? Says he lost his own in the waves.* Nobody lost a bag in those waves and stayed that calm about it.

A man searching bags was a man looking for something specific. A man looking for diplomatic codes aboard an evacuation ship was not a fellow traveler. He was a retrieval agent.

And a regime that sent a retriever after codes did not, as a rule, intend for the ship carrying them to arrive anywhere useful.

* * *

He found Odile at dusk, in the narrow lane behind the galley where the steam from the cook's kettles fogged the porthole glass and everyone pretended not to hear anyone else's business. She was doing what she always did at day's end—reconciling her manifest against reality, pencil moving in small precise strokes.

"I need to ask you something," Dominic said quietly, "and I need you to answer without asking why."

Odile's pencil stopped. She looked up. Of everyone aboard, she had the best face for bad news: it absorbed it without spilling.

"Ask."

"When Corvane boarded—his papers. Who cleared them?"

"The port master's office." She said it evenly, but her eyes had sharpened. "Why?"

"And who countersigned?"

She hesitated. That hesitation told him more than the answer would have. "The office of the Provisional Liaison," she said finally. "Which is to say, the men the new government left behind to handle departures. It was irregular. I noted it at the time and was told my note was unnecessary."

"May I see the manifest entry?"

"You may not," she said, and then, watching his face, "but I can tell you what it says, because I've read it forty times trying to decide whether it bothered me. It lists him as a commercial attaché. Retired. There's a pension number." Her pencil tapped twice against the page. "Pension numbers are issued by the Ministry of the Interior. The same ministry that ran the tribunals."

The kettle steam hissed between them. Somewhere aft, a baby was crying with the exhausted persistence of a child who had cried for eleven straight days and intended to make it twelve.

"Odile," Dominic said. "If I told you there was something aboard this ship worth more than every passenger in it put together—worth enough that a certain government would prefer we all drowned rather than deliver it—what would you do?"

She set down her pencil with great care, the way Merrick handled the oldest documents in his archive, and looked at him for a long moment.

"I'd say," she answered, "that I've been keeping a list of things that don't add up about that man since the day he boarded. And that I'd want whoever knows the most about it to tell me the rest. Soon." A pause. "Tonight, preferably. Because tomorrow I log us into the approaches, and if there is a wolf following this ship, I'd rather know it before I'm standing on the doorstep."

"There's more than a wolf," Dominic said. "There's a shepherd. And he isn't hers."

* * *

He told her everything—or nearly everything—in the chartroom, with the lamp turned low and the door wedged shut. The codes. What they were: the sealed instruments, countersigned, that could constitute a lawful government-in-exile the moment they were presented at any neutral chancellery. What that meant: whichever faction held them held the republic's ghost, and a ghost with proper paperwork can inherit everything. He did not tell her about Lys. That wound was still his own, and besides, he wasn't yet sure what shape it was. A sister alive was either a miracle or an instrument, and until he knew which, he kept her folded away next to his heart, where all the dangerous documents traveled.

Odile listened the way she logged cargo: completely, without flinching, asking only the questions that mattered.

"So Corvane offers you passage to your sister in exchange for the codes. And you've begun to suspect the sister is real, but the offer is not."

"That's the shape of it."

"Mm." She stared at the chart pinned above the desk—the coast they would reach in three days, the neutral harbor marked with the neat printed confidence of maps that don't know they're obsolete. "Then consider what his actual orders must be. Retrieve the codes. And 'silence the ship.'" She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Those are two orders, courier. Not one. If retrieving the codes were sufficient, the second order would be redundant. Which means his principals expect the codes might be destroyed, or hidden, or refused—and they expect the people who know about them to survive otherwise. So they've given him permission to make sure we don't."

"He wouldn't need soldiers," Dominic said slowly. Working it through aloud made it worse, each conclusion arriving like cold water finding its level. "There are ways to sink a ship that look like weather. Ways to start a fire that looks like carelessness. Or—" and here his stomach turned, because he understood it all at once, elegantly, horribly—"or he doesn't need to do anything at all. He just needs the harbor to refuse us."

Odile went very still. "The refusal."

"The port cited the codes as contraband. How does a closed port know what contraband a ship carries unless someone tells them? Someone with official standing. Someone whose commercial-attaché papers let him send messages ahead." He laughed, and it came out bitter as bilge water. "We thought the refusal was misfortune. It's the first move. Cut us off from safe harbor, make us desperate, and then appear with the solution. *Give me the codes and I'll get you a berth anywhere.* He'll come back to us with an offer within a day—you watch. He'll have arranged it so that surrendering the codes looks like salvation. And once they're in his dispatch case, once he's confirmed where they're held and who holds them—"

"—the ship stops needing to arrive anywhere," Odile finished.

The lamp flame leaned in some draft neither of them could feel. Down the corridor, the sounds of the evening meal were assembling themselves: tin cups, the cook's bell, voices arguing cheerfully about whether the beans had improved or merely surrendered. Eleven hundred people, give or take, eating beans and believing in a harbor.

"We can't search his cabin," Odile said. "If he's what you think, he'll expect it, and whatever he lets us find will be the lie he wants found."

"No. We do something better. We let him think it's working." Dominic rubbed his eyes. He felt suddenly, enormously tired, the way he'd felt at twenty-two crossing a border with forged papers, understanding that from this night forward he would never again be entirely honest with another human being. "He wants me to come to him, frightened, ready to bargain. So that's what I'll do. I'll bring him something to trade. Something that looks like the codes."

"And the true ones?"

"Go somewhere Corvane would never think to look," Dominic said, and heard himself say it, and marveled at it even as he said it, because it was the last place he should trust and therefore the only place left. "Somewhere old. Somewhere that smells like dust and memory. Somewhere with a man inside it who has spent his whole life hiding important papers from governments."

Odile allowed herself, for the first time in Dominic's presence, a very small smile. "The archivist."

"The archivist."

"It's a terrible plan," she said. "He's the most conspicuous man aboard. Half the passengers go to him daily just to hear him read."

"Yes," Dominic agreed. "But Corvane is looking for a courier's satchel. He's counting tarred canvas and rope oil and false bottoms. He isn't looking for a folio bound as a sermon, filed under homiletics, in a collection no sane thief would steal because no sane thief thinks sermons are worth anything." He paused. "Also, Merrick reads everything. If there's a cipher key hidden in the binding, he'll have found it by Thursday and catalogued it."

That earned him a longer silence and, eventually, a nod. "I'll alter the log tonight. If we're searched at any port, the record will show Corvane embarked with two dispatch cases, not one." She met his eyes. "Small lies in official ink. My father would be ashamed of me."

"Your father," Dominic said gently, "was a harbormaster in a fallen republic. I imagine he'd understand."

* * *

He went to find Corvane.

This was the part he had not planned past, because planning past it meant admitting what he would have to feel, and he wanted to feel nothing at all for the length of this conversation. He found the man at the stern rail, watching the wake unspool behind them in the dark, that long pale seam of foam that was all the Meridian left of where she'd been.

"Dominic." Corvane didn't turn. Of course he knew his footsteps; he'd known them since the second day. "I wondered when you'd come. Debts want settling, and you've owed me since the ice."

"You saved my life."

"I improved an investment." Now he turned, and the lantern light caught his face and made it open, warm, reasonable—the face of a man you could tell things to. That was the gift, Dominic thought. Not the forged letters, not the papers, not the patience. The face. Somewhere in some ministry school they had taught him how to build that face, and somewhere in the building of it, he had perhaps forgotten there was anything underneath.

"I've been thinking about your offer," Dominic said.

"I hoped you might."

"My sister. Passage to her. In exchange for what I carry." He let his voice do the work: hesitant, cornered, a man stepping off a ledge. "I want to know she's real first. I want proof before I hand over anything."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not triumph, quite. Satisfaction, maybe, the quiet pleasure of a trap feeling its jaws take weight. "What sort of proof?"

"A letter in her hand that mentions something only she would know. Our mother's maiden name won't serve—it's in every file. Something from before. From home."

Corvane considered this, head tilted, and Dominic watched him perform consideration, and hated how good it was. "I can arrange that. Within a day, perhaps two, depending on the wind and whom I must speak with." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "You know, I'm glad you came to me. Whatever happens with these codes—and I suspect the coming days will be complicated—I'd like you to understand that the part about your sister was always true. Men like me deal in truths wrapped in lies. It's the wrapping people object to."

"And underneath?" Dominic asked. "When you unwrap it all the way? What's at the center of you, Corvane?"

The smile widened by one degree. "Employment," he said.

They stood a moment longer, two men at a rail in the dark, each convinced the other was the mark. Then Dominic wished him good night and walked away along the deck with an even stride, past the galley where Tobias was supposed to be washing cups and was certainly trading gossip instead, past the hold where his empty satchel lay in its nest of tar and rope like a snake's shed skin, past everything he was responsible for keeping alive.

At the ladder to the archive he stopped and gripped the rail and let himself shake, just briefly, just once—the whole betrayal of it passing through him like a cramp. Because the worst part wasn't the danger. He had lived with danger so long it felt like weather. The worst part was that he had *liked* Corvane. Some lonely, starving part of him had sat with that man at the rail and thought: here, finally, is someone who understands what it costs to carry things.

And the letters. God, the letters. If Lys was real—if somewhere in a fishing village she stood waiting with a berth and his name in her mouth—then Corvane had used her, used her survival, used two years of Dominic's grief, as bait in a trap meant to drown eleven hundred people. And if she wasn't real—

If she wasn't real, then the letters were the cruelest document ever drafted, and Dominic had read them aloud to himself in the dark like prayers.

Below him, lamplight glowed through the archive door seams, and he could hear Merrick's voice, low and unhurried, reading aloud to no one—to the papers, probably, or to the republic they remembered. Dominic took a breath. Then another. He smoothed his face into something presentable and went down the ladder, knocking softly, because some doors you still knocked on even at the end of the world.

"Merrick," he said. "I need to hide something in your sermons."

From inside, dry as dust and warm as bread: "My boy, I've been wondering when you'd ask. Come in. Mind the folios—they bite."

Behind them, far astern, riding the dark water they'd already crossed, a light blinked twice, paused, and blinked twice again—patient, professional, and keeping pace.
