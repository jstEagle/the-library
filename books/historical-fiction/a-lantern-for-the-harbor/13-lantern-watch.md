## 13. Lantern Watch

The old sailor's name was Brann, and he smelled permanently of pipe smoke and lamp oil, which Tobias decided was the smell of important things.

He had learned this on his fourth night aboard the *Meridian*, after the business with the rations and the knife. Word traveled fast in a ship that small, and it traveled fastest to the ears of people who had nothing better to do than listen, and Tobias had discovered — with a feeling he did not entirely trust — that he was now somebody. Not a stowaway. Not vermin. Somebody whose warning had mattered.

"You're the boy who watches," Brann said, appearing beside him at the rail one gray morning as though he'd grown there like barnacles.

"I watch everything," Tobias said carefully. "It's how you stay alive."

Brann laughed, a sound like gravel poured slowly into a bucket. "Aye. And do you know what you're watching *for*?"

Tobias considered this. He watched for soldiers, for quartermasters, for the particular way Dominic's shoulders moved when something was wrong. He watched for the cook's moods and the weather's lies and the passengers who organized themselves in corners like chess pieces. But what all of it was *for* — that was a question with no bottom.

"No," he admitted.

"Then come along, lad. I've got something to show you, and it wants showing before the dark comes early again."

* * *

What Brann showed him was a lantern.

It hung at the stern of the ship, above the steering oar's station — an ordinary thing, brass gone green with salt, its glass smoked at one side where someone long dead had let a candle burn too close. Tobias had looked at it a hundred times without once seeing it.

"That's the harbor-watch light," Brann said. "Every ship worth her salt carries one, and every port that means to be found keeps one burning. You know why?"

"So ships don't crash."

"So ships don't crash," Brann agreed, "and so ships that are lost can find their way back. It's not the same thing, lad. The first is just a light. The second is a promise." He tapped the glass with one cracked fingernail. "There's a language in it. My father taught me, and his mother taught him, back when we kept a watch-house on the cliffs at Vell. You want to learn it?"

Tobias wanted to say no. He wanted to say it very badly, because wanting things was how you got hurt, and he had a whole catalog of evidence on the subject. But his mouth said, "Yes," before the rest of him could vote.

* * *

The language of the lantern, it turned out, was patience itself.

"One flash, steady: all is well. Two quick: danger, stand off. Three slow, spaced like breaths: come home, the water is safe." Brann demonstrated with his hand cupped around an unlit stub of candle, opening and closing his fingers over the flame. "And then there's the hard ones. Four flashes, then dark, then two more — that means 'I see you, but I cannot help.' A watcher hates sending that one."

"Why would anyone send it? Why not just help?"

"Because sometimes helping means going to them," Brann said, "and a watcher's post is the light. You stay. That's the whole discipline of it. The hardest work aboard any ship is staying where you're needed when everything in you screams to run." He looked at Tobias sideways. "You'd know something about running, I expect."

Tobias's ears went hot. "I ran from a burning city. Everyone ran."

"Aye, and most everyone's still running, inside, even standing still." Brann struck a match and lit the candle properly, and the little flame threw their shadows huge against the stern rails. "But a man or boy who learns to hold a post — that one stops running, eventually. Not because the fear goes. Because the light needs keeping more than the fear needs feeding."

They practiced through the afternoon. Brann made him read signals off the far shore as they passed fishing boats, some real, some invented by Brann's mischievous old heart. ("That one there — three fishermen and a dog — what are they saying?" "One flash steady." "Wrong! Look at the second boat. Two quick. They're telling us there's a sandbar. See how they swing wide?" Tobias saw. Something in his chest unlocked slightly, like a door that had been rusted shut.)

By dusk he could manage the basic grammar: well, danger, come home, I-see-you-cannot-help. His arm ached from shielding and revealing the candle, and he had never been happier in his life.

* * *

Dominic found them there after supper, drawn by the light, and stood a moment at the edge of the circle it cast, the way he always did — as if unsure whether he was allowed inside it.

"He's got good hands," Brann said, apropos of nothing, nodding at Tobias. "Steady. You want steady hands in a signalman. Shaky hands tell lies."

"Does he," said Dominic, and there was something in his voice Tobias couldn't read — not quite surprise, not quite the guarded flatness he wore like a coat. "I didn't know you were studying navigation."

"It's not navigation," Tobias said. "It's talking. To people far away. With fire."

"Ah." Dominic crouched down to their level, which he did more often lately, another change Tobias had noticed and filed away among the things that might mean hope. "And what have you learned to say?"

Tobias held up his hand over the candle, suddenly shy. "One flash steady. All is well."

Something crossed Dominic's face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and gone, but Tobias had made a profession of watching, and he caught it. Grief and gladness in the same expression, tangled together like rope ends.

"All is well," Dominic repeated softly, as if testing whether the words would hold his weight. Then he stood. "Keep practicing. Both of you. It's a long winter yet, and—" he paused, choosing, Tobias could tell, between ten sentences and none, "—and it's good to have someone at the light."

He went below. Tobias stared after him.

"Your friend carries heavy cargo," Brann observed.

"Everyone does. There's a war on."

"There's always a war on somewhere. Most folk still travel light." Brann relit the candle where the wind had worried it. "Now. Four flashes, then dark, then two. Show me."

Tobias showed him. *I see you, but I cannot help.* The signal felt heavier tonight than it had at noon, though he could not have said why.

* * *

He woke in the night to footsteps overhead and climbed up quietly, out of habit, to see who moved.

It was Dominic at the rail, alone, holding a letter. Even in the dark Tobias knew it was a letter; he'd seen enough of them lately, seen the way paper could bend a person like wind bends wheat. Dominic stood a long time without reading it, just holding it, and then he folded it away inside his coat, next to his heart, which was where Tobias had noticed people put the letters that hurt them most.

Then Dominic looked up — straight at the stern lantern, unlit, swinging gently in its brackets — and stood there as if the empty lantern were asking him something.

Tobias stayed hidden. Some things you watched, and some things you kept.

But lying in his nest among the cargo crates afterward, he found himself counting flashes against the dark ceiling of the hold. One flash, steady: all is well. Except nothing was well; he'd known that since the city burned, and knowing it hadn't killed him yet. Two quick: danger. Three slow: come home, the water is safe.

*I see you,* he thought, toward the deck above, toward the man who fed him and taught him nothing aloud but everything by doing, *but I cannot help.*

Four flashes. Dark. Two more.

He fell asleep practicing the only signal Brann hadn't taught him yet, the one every watcher must invent alone: how to keep the light burning when your own hands want to shake.

Above him, the empty lantern swung in its brackets, waiting — the way, he would understand much later, it had always been waiting — for the night it would finally matter.
