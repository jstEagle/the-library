## 14. The Vote in the Saloon

Odile Marchand had kept the Meridian's books for nineteen years, and she had never once written down a lie. This was not because she was honest—honesty was a luxury, like fresh water, and she had learned to ration both—but because a ledger that lied would eventually betray its keeper. Numbers remembered everything. She trusted them the way other women trusted saints.

So when Captain Ferrant told her, on the morning of the ninth day out, that the ship would hold a vote, her first instinct was to ask for it in writing.

"A vote," she said. They stood in the passage outside the saloon, where the lamplight came through frosted glass in gray smears. "On what, precisely."

"On the codes." Ferrant rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. He had not slept properly since the harbor at Corvane's last port refused them, and it showed in the loose skin under his jaw, in the way his voice had gone quiet in the wrong places. "The neutral port sent terms. Surrender any diplomatic materials aboard, and they'll open the gates."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we keep sailing until we find a port that doesn't care, or until the coal runs out, whichever comes first." He smiled, which was worse than not smiling. "I've done the arithmetic, Odile. You'd do it better."

She had, of course, already done it. Eleven days of coal at best, nine hundred souls, water rationed to two liters a head. The arithmetic was a slow drowning written in tidy columns.

"Who votes?" she asked.

"Everyone. Passengers, crew, all of it." He said it flatly, daring her to object.

"It's madness," she said. "Nine hundred people cannot agree on soup. I've fed them for eleven days; I know."

"Perhaps. But they can agree on drowning, or not drowning. That's usually enough." He turned to go, then stopped. "Merrick asked for it, you know. The archivist. Said a ship that decides its own fate is worth more than a ship that arrives."

Odile said nothing, but she wrote the date in the margin of her ledger, because dates were how she prayed.

* * *

The saloon had been built for forty diners in happier times, when the Meridian ran mail and silk between ports that still existed. Now there were three hundred people crammed into it, standing on benches, hanging from the rail of the musicians' gallery, and the heat of them fogged the portholes until the winter sea outside was only a rumor of white.

Odile took her place at the captain's table with the ledger open before her, because someone had to count, and counting was its own kind of courage. Ferrant stood beside her. Merrick sat to one side, wrapped in two coats he could not warm himself in, his hands folded on a leather folder as though it might fly away without supervision.

The boy was there too—Tobias, the stowaway Dominic had adopted by the simple method of feeding him. Odile had logged him officially as "ship's apprentice, wages: one biscuit daily," which was the kindest fiction she'd ever entered into a book. He sat near the door with his knees pulled up, watching everyone with the fixed attention of a cat at a mousehole.

Ferrant rang the bell once. The noise did not so much die as collapse, the way tired crowds go quiet.

"You know why we're here," he said. "I'll not make speeches. There are men and women in this room who've lost more than a country, and I'll not insult them with oratory. Terms have come from the neutral harbor. Any diplomatic papers aboard are to be surrendered. In exchange—the gates open. All of us. Water, coal, safe anchorage." He paused. "Or we refuse, and we sail on, and we take our chances with the cold arithmetic. That's the whole of it. Who speaks first?"

A woman in a fishmonger's apron spoke first, because in Odile's experience it is always the practical ones who go first while the ideologues are still clearing their throats. "Surrender them," she said. "Papers don't breathe. My children do."

There was a rumble of assent—not unanimous, but broad, the sound of exhausted people grasping at the nearest solid thing.

Then a man near the gallery rail stood up, a merchant with gold still visible at his cuffs despite eleven days of trying to look poor. "You're fools. Those papers aren't paper, they're a government. Legitimize an exile council and every debt, every title, every claim we carry in our pockets becomes real again. Burn them and we're just refugees. Keep them and we're a republic."

"We're refugees either way," the fishwife shot back. "Yours is the only pocket that gets fatter."

It might have come to shoving right there if Merrick had not risen. He rose slowly, the way old trees fall slowly, and the room gave him the silence it gives to things that will not happen twice.

"I am Merrick," he said. "Some of you know what I was. For thirty years I kept the state archive—the treaties, the censuses, the court records, the births and deaths of a nation. When the palace burned I saved what I could carry, and what I could carry is now locked in a cabin on this ship, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing rather less than the fishwife's smallest child."

He looked around the room, and Odile saw that he was not looking at faces but counting them, the way she counted. Old habits of stewardship recognize each other.

"Now," he said, "a port offers us our lives for those papers. And I want to say plainly, so no one can claim later that the old man confused them: surrendering the codes may well save every soul aboard. It is the reasonable act. I have spent my life among reasonable acts. Archives are full of them—reasonable men surrendering unreasonable things for peace, and peace lasting a season, and the archives recording it all in beautiful hands." His voice caught, and he steadied it. "But hear me. A government-in-exile is not made of ships or soldiers or even codes. It is made of proof. Proof that we existed. Proof that the laws meant something. If we give that up to save our bodies, we will live—and something will have died that does not come back. Memory must survive even if men do not. Men are cheap, God forgive me. We are born by the thousand every day. But memory—" he touched the leather folder—"memory is born once."

The silence afterward was the kind that has weight. Odile wrote nothing, because there was nothing yet to record, and her pencil hovered over the page like a small bird deciding whether to land.

Then a young man near the door—a student, by the ink stains—stood and said, "Easy for memory to survive when it isn't your children drinking seawater," and the room broke into argument, and Odile began, grimly, to count raised hands.

* * *

They counted for an hour. Odile counted honestly, which meant she counted twice, because the first tally came out wrong in a way she distrusted. Three hundred and six souls in the saloon and passages beyond. For surrender: one hundred and seventy-one. Against: one hundred and twenty-two. Undecided, abstaining, or simply too frightened to raise a hand: thirteen.

"No majority," Ferrant said. "Not the two-thirds I wanted for anything this size."

"There's a majority for surrender," the merchant said. "One hundred and seventy-one. Counted by your own quartermaster."

"Against a third who say they'd sooner burn the ship than the archive," Ferrant replied. "That's not a majority, Master Quillon, that's a civil war with extra steps. We didn't flee a collapsing republic to found a smaller one in a saloon and lose it by dinnertime."

It was at this point, Odile noted in her ledger with a small private satisfaction, that Dominic Winterbourne stood up.

She had watched him for eleven days the way she watched everything—sideways, through numbers. He worked hard, tipped generously with labor rather than coin, and had appeared from nowhere with no history, which on the Meridian made him unremarkable, since nobody aboard had a history anymore except the bad kind. But she had seen him at the rails during the storm, calm in the specific way of men carrying something heavier than rope. And now she saw him stand, and saw Merrick watch him stand, with an expression she could not read and therefore did not like.

"I'm a deckhand," Dominic said. "Nobody here knows me, and I'd like to keep it that way, so I'll say my piece quickly. I've carried messages for a living. Small things—sealed, insignificant, heavy. Here's what I know about them: a message doesn't belong to the man who carries it. It belongs to whoever it's going to. And the question isn't whether these codes save us or doom us. The question is—who are they going to?" He looked around the room. "If we surrender them to a neutral port, they'll be locked in a vault, and in ten years some clerk will sell them back to whoever's in power, and everything Merrick saved them for dies politely in a filing cabinet. If we keep them, we're gambling nine hundred lives on a promise. I don't love either answer. But I notice nobody's asked the third question."

"Which is?" Ferrant said.

"What the person who entrusted them to this ship would want." Dominic sat down. The room murmured. Odile wrote: *deckhand speaks well; investigate later,* and underlined it twice, because she had a feeling about underlines.

* * *

The vote, taken again with the third question rattling in the room, came out nearly the same, which was somehow more honest: one hundred sixty-eight for surrender, one hundred thirty-one against, seven refusing to choose. Still no supermajority. Ferrant declared the matter unresolved and called a recess that everyone understood to mean *sleep on it, if you can.*

The crowd drained out into the cold corridors, arguing in low voices, and Odile stayed to close her ledger, and found Merrick had not moved. He sat with his folder in his lap, looking smaller than he had an hour ago, as though the debate had been conducted partly at his expense.

"You argued beautifully," she said, "and lost."

"I wasn't arguing to win." He smiled, and it transformed his face briefly into that of a much younger man, one who had perhaps once been reckless. "I was arguing so that losing would be recorded properly. That's most of an archivist's job, Madame Quartermaster—recording losses properly, so the winners can't pretend there weren't any." He gathered his coats around him and stood, swaying slightly. "Besides, I've won before. 1849. The whole archive, moved ahead of the artillery by four hours, because I convinced a wagon driver to vote with me. One man. One vote. It was enough."

"How much did you pay him?"

"Nothing." Merrick paused at the door. "I bought him soup. Democracy runs on soup, Madame. Remember that—it's the one political science you'll ever need."

Odile laughed—actually laughed, the first time in eleven days—and wrote in the margin: *soup, essential stores.* Then she closed the book, and felt the weight of it, and understood that whatever happened next, she would be the one who wrote it down.

* * *

She found the last thing waiting for her on the quarterdeck, where the cold had teeth in it. Tobias was there, sitting on a coil of line, swinging his legs, waiting with the transparent patience of the very young.

"Quartermaster," he said, formal as a little ambassador. "Dominic says to tell you he's sorry about the vote. And that he needs the ship's boat ready at second bell tonight. Quietly."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. The wind came off the water smelling of ice and diesel and the end of things. Somewhere below, three hundred people slept badly on their unresolved consciences, and somewhere beyond the dark horizon a neutral port waited with its gates half-open, pricing human lives against paperwork.

"Does he now," she said.

"He says you're the only one who counts straight."

"Well," said Odile, taking out her pencil, "he's not wrong about that."

She wrote the order in the log—*ship's boat, second bell, authorized*—and did not ask why, because she had been keeping books long enough to know that some entries explain themselves later, and some never explain themselves at all, and both kinds must be written in the same steady hand.

The pencil paused over the page. Then, in the margin, small enough that only she would ever see it:

*God help us, we're doing something.*
