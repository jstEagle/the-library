## 15. Sister, at a Distance

The message came wrapped in a herring.

Not literally—though Dominic would remember it that way for the rest of his life, because the truth was only slightly less absurd. It came inside a tin of smoked herring that a fisherman's boy had pressed into Odile's hands at the last water stop, along with a shrug and the claim that his father had "over-ordered." Odile, who trusted no gift smaller than a barrel and no kindness that arrived without a bill attached, had turned the tin over twice in her hands before passing it down the mess line like everything else.

Dominic had taken one because taking none would have been conspicuous, and he had learned in two years of hiding that the safest man on any ship is the man who behaves exactly as boringly as everyone around him.

The tin was heavier than herring.

He discovered this sitting on an overturned crate near the stern rail, the winter sea going gray to pewter below him, Tobias three feet away narrating an elaborate dispute between two gulls—"that one's the magistrate, he's crooked"—and Merrick dozing against a coil of line with a book open on his chest like a small tent. Dominic pried the lid with his knife, expecting brine.

Inside, beneath a respectable layer of actual fish, was oilcloth. Beneath the oilcloth, a letter. And beneath the letter, folded twice, a scrap of canvas with a single word stenciled in the old naval hand: *Meridian*.

He did not read the letter on deck. That was the first discipline of his old life asserting itself—the courier's reflex, the body remembering what the heart had tried to forget. He tucked it inside his coat, against his ribs, where the satchel's absence still felt like a missing tooth, and he smiled at Tobias and said something about the magistrate gull being bribed, and he walked below decks with his pulse hammering so loudly he was half afraid Merrick would wake and hear it.

* * *

In the dark of the rope locker, by the light of a stub of candle he allowed himself exactly four inches of, Dominic read his sister's handwriting for the third time in his life since her death.

The first time had been Corvane's letters, back at the last port—impossible, unbearable, written in a hand he knew better than his own because he'd taught her the loops himself when she was seven and furious that her r's looked like a fence fallen over. The second time had been alone, at night, the full letters, learning what she had done to survive: the names given, the friends signed away, Merrick among them, though Merrick lived, which was either mercy or arithmetic depending on how you held it up to the light.

This was the third time. And this time the letter began:

*Dom.*

Just that. Not "To the courier," not "To whoever holds this," not any of Corvane's careful circumlocutions. Just *Dom*, the way she'd scrawled it across a hundred schoolroom notes, across the margins of their father's ledgers, across the last postcard she'd sent from the capital before the purge swallowed everything north of the river.

*I won't waste your time or mine pretending I don't know what you carry. Corvane talks in his sleep, and I spent eleven months in a cell with him, which is long enough to learn a man's dreams and longer than anyone should have to.*

*Here is what matters. There is a fishing village called Sarnholt, forty leagues south of your course if Odile is still steering the way she used to steer, which is stubbornly. I am there. I have a berth—a real one, a boat that will take me beyond the reach of the new government, past the straits, gone. My own berth, Dom. I bought it with the only coin I had left, which is to say with the truth of where you were headed, sold to a man I did not entirely trust, so consider the source of everything else in this letter.*

*Bring the codes. The people who hold them can buy my passage twice over and yours besides, and then we can be finished with all of it—finished with governments and codes and other people's wars, and go somewhere the winters are soft and no one asks us to be anything but siblings who are late to dinner.*

*Or don't bring them. Burn this, keep your satchel, save your republic-in-exile, and know that I will understand. That's the worst part, Dom. I'll understand. I've become a person who understands everything.*

*There's a tide at Sarnholt that leaves in nine days. After that, even I can't promise anything.*

*Lys*

*P.S. — You always kept the lantern lit too long after I was home safe. Mother said you'd burn the harbor down out of love. She wasn't wrong about much.*

He read it four times. Then he sat in the dark of the rope locker with the candle guttering and did the thing he had been refusing to do since Corvane came aboard, which was to think about it plainly.

She was alive. That fact had stopped feeling real somewhere around the fourth day after Corvane's arrival; it had become a splinter he worked around, tender when pressed, ignored otherwise. Now here it was again, not a rumor carried by a stranger with a bargainer's smile but her own hand, her own loops, her own bitter joke at her own expense. *I've become a person who understands everything.* That was Lys. That was exactly, devastatingly Lys—the girl who'd once talked her way out of a whipping by convincing the harbormaster he'd already forgiven her, so thoroughly that he apologized to *her*.

Alive. Waiting. Nine days of tide.

And the price of her freedom was the weight in the false bottom of his sea chest: a cipher case that could turn a roomful of arguing exiles into a legitimate government, or turn the Meridian into a target, or—depending on whose hands received it—turn a collapsing nation's last hope into its final farce.

Dominic blew out the candle and sat in the blackness and let himself want things, just for a minute, the way a starving man is allowed to smell bread. He wanted to stand on a dock at Sarnholt and see her come down the shingle road with her collar up against the wind and her walk unchanged, that insolent rolling stride she'd learned from the fishermen and never surrendered to propriety. He wanted to say something cutting and have her say something worse back. He wanted, more than he had wanted anything since the purge, to be someone's brother instead of someone's courier.

Then the minute was over, because minutes were all he could afford, and he went back up into the cold and the noise and the ship full of people who trusted him without knowing his name.

* * *

"Your face has changed," Merrick said that evening.

They were in the archivist's room—that narrow compartment aft where Merrick kept the rescued papers of the republic in oiled boxes and catalogued them by lamplight with the devotion of a priest tending relics. Dominic had come ostensibly to help rebind a water-damaged register of trade licenses, which was the sort of errand Merrick accepted with grave courtesy and saw through completely every single time.

"My face has changed?"

"There is a particular expression," Merrick said, pressing paste along a spine with a brush finer than any brush ought to be aboard a refugee ship, "worn by men carrying news they cannot share. The jaw sets slightly left of center. You have it."

"You invented that expression just now to fish."

"I invent nothing. I archive." Merrick set the brush down and regarded him over the lamplight, and there it was again—that quality Dominic had never decided whether to call kindness or surveillance. "I will tell you something I have never told a living soul, since we're trading confidences. When I was a young clerk, I once misfiled a treaty. A border treaty. For six years, two provinces believed they shared a river that in fact belonged to neither of them, and the resulting friendship was the most durable peace in the region's history. When the error surfaced, the ministers asked me whether I regretted it." He smoothed the fresh spine with his thumb. "I told them the truth. I said: ask me whether the peace regrets it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind worth giving." Merrick picked the brush back up. "Whatever is setting your jaw left of center, Dominic—it will look different in nine days than it does tonight. Most things do. That is not an argument for waiting. It is only an observation that waiting is rarely the cowardice it feels like."

Dominic stared at him. "Nine days."

Merrick did not look up. "The tide tables are filed under Maritime, Coastal, Miscellaneous. I read everything that comes aboard this ship. It is a disease." A pause, delicate as the brushstroke. "I also read herring tins, when they pass through the manifest with the wrong weight. But I mention none of this, you'll notice. Discretion is simply filing that no one is permitted to open."

* * *

Tobias found him at dawn on the second day, which was becoming the boy's habit and Dominic's quiet catastrophe.

"You're doing the thinking face," Tobias announced, dropping onto the crate beside him and producing, from nowhere, half a biscuit, which he split with the solemnity of a treaty negotiation and handed over the larger portion.

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You do. It's like this." Tobias arranged his features into an uncannily accurate portrait of Dominic at his grimmest—brows knotted, mouth a flat line—and then ruined it by grinning. "Merrick says you get it when you're deciding something. He says deciding things is harder work than carrying them, and carriers don't train for it."

"Merrick says a great deal."

"He says you listen to it, though." Tobias swung his legs. Below them the gray water ran; above, the gulls resumed their corrupt judiciary. "Is it about the lady? The one from the letters?"

Dominic went very still. "What lady from what letters?"

"The letters Corvane carries. I help in the galley, and Corvane talks to the cook, and the cook reads everything upside down while she works, and she tells me things so I'll stop asking how the stove works." He shrugged, eight years old and already fluent in the economy of information aboard a crowded ship. "Everybody knows something, Mr. Winterbourne. Nobody knows everything. That's why ships float, Merrick says—all the little bits holding each other up."

For a moment Dominic couldn't speak. Then he took the larger piece of biscuit and ate it, because the boy was watching, and because some lessons were better taught by example than speech.

"The lady," Dominic said carefully, "is my sister."

Tobias considered this with the gravity it deserved. "The dead one?"

"She turned out to be less dead than advertised."

"Oh." Another silence, the working kind. "Are you glad?"

It was such a simple question and such an impossible one. Dominic looked out at the sea, where the light was coming up thin and silver, and found that the honest answer had teeth.

"Yes," he said at last. "And it's more complicated than glad. Grown-up things often are."

"I knew a woman in the capital," Tobias said, to the horizon rather than to him, in the way children deliver their heaviest cargo, "who came back after everybody thought the soldiers took her. Everybody was happy for a day. Then everybody found out what she'd done to come back, and they weren't happy anymore, and she had to leave anyway, except now nobody waved." He kicked his heels against the crate. "Was it like that?"

"It was exactly like that."

"Okay." Tobias nodded, satisfied, as though some ledger had balanced. "I'm still glad, though. Being alive is the part where you get another chance. Everything after that is just arguments about the chance." He stood, brushed off his trousers with tremendous dignity, and paused. "If she comes, does she like gulls? Because I've got the magistrate story nearly finished and it needs an audience that hasn't heard it."

"She hates gulls," Dominic said. "She was bitten by one at six years old and has held the grudge ever since. It's the longest relationship of her life."

"Good," said Tobias. "Grudges make the best endings."

* * *

That night he took out the satchel—not the codes themselves, but the satchel, its worn leather, the false bottom his father's hands had sewn—and laid it beside Lys's letter on his bunk, and made himself look at both at once.

Here was the arithmetic, and it refused to be gentle.

If he gave up the codes, he gave up every argument Merrick had ever made about memory, every vote the saloon passengers might cast, every fragile legitimacy the exiles aboard were building out of argument and hope. If he kept them, he abandoned his sister to a fishing village and a closing tide, and he did it knowingly, with her handwriting in his pocket, which was a wound no republic could justify.

Unless—

His mind, traitorous and tireless, kept building bridges. Send the codes with a courier he trusted—but whom? Every trustworthy soul aboard was needed elsewhere, and trust itself had become a currency inflated past recognition since Corvane came aboard. Copy the key and send only that—except the key without context was noise, and the context lived in his head. Split the difference, hedge, delay, wait for Sarnholt to come nearer, wait for the vote, wait for a sign—

Wait. Always the mind proposed waiting, as if waiting were free.

He thought of Merrick's misfiled treaty. Two provinces, one imaginary river, six accidental years of peace. Ask me whether the peace regrets it.

He thought of Tobias: *being alive is the part where you get another chance.*

He thought of Lys at seven, furious over her r's, and how he'd put his hand over hers on the page and moved it, loop by loop, until the fences stood up straight. You could not write a person's hand for her forever. At some point you lifted your own hand away, and whatever she wrote next was hers.

But oh, God, the wanting. That was the thing no one warned couriers about—that the hardest cargo is not the heavy kind but the kind that wants to be delivered *to you*, that sits in your own chest and weighs more every day, and no manifest ever lists it, and there is no rate of pay that covers it.

Dominic folded the letter along its old creases. He did not burn it. He suspected now that he never would; some documents earned their place in the archive of the heart regardless of what they cost.

Outside, the wind had begun to rise, a long low note in the rigging that Odile would read like scripture and the passengers would pretend not to hear. Somewhere south, forty leagues and closing, a tide was preparing to go out, indifferent as arithmetic.

He was still sitting there, jaw set slightly left of center, when a knock came at his door—two taps, soft, deliberate, in no rhythm any sailor aboard would use.

He opened it. Merrick stood in the passage with a lamp held high and his face the color of paper, and behind the old man's shoulder, at the end of the corridor, a figure detached itself from the shadows with unhurried familiarity, and smiled, and said:

"Well. This saves everyone a great deal of correspondence."

Corvane.

"I think," Merrick said quietly, without turning, "it is time the three of us discussed your sister."
