## 17. The Last Berth

The skiff sat in the shadow of the Meridian's stern like a secret nobody wanted to say aloud.

Dominic had come down to the davits in the grey hour before dawn, when even the gulls were too cold to complain, and he had been standing there long enough that his boots had gone stiff and the frost had written its small white signature along the gunwale. He counted the seats. He had counted them four times now, as though the number might improve with attention, the way a bad hand of cards sometimes does if you squint at it hopefully enough.

It did not improve. One seat.

Well—one seat, two oars, a water cask three-quarters full, a coil of line, and a sail that Odile had pronounced "adequate for a calm sea and an optimist." The storm had passed two nights ago and left behind that washed-clean silence that follows any great violence, the world scrubbed and stunned, as if it too were waiting to hear what would be decided.

He heard footsteps on the deck above and did not turn. There was only one person aboard who walked with that particular rhythm—half-sailor's roll, half-archivist's shuffle, as though her body could never quite agree on which profession it belonged to.

"You're up early," Odile said, arriving beside him at the rail. She handed him a mug of something hot that claimed to be coffee in the way that a drowned man claims to be swimming. "Or late. On this ship the distinction has stopped mattering."

"Couldn't sleep."

"Liar," she said comfortably. "You slept. I checked on you at the middle watch, the way I check on everyone, because I am a quartermaster and suspicion is my love language. You were asleep with your mouth open and your arm around that boy like a fence post around a lamb."

Dominic felt his ears go warm and was grateful for the dark. "I was keeping him warm."

"Mm. And the lamb was keeping you company." She blew on her coffee. Below them the skiff rocked against its lines, patient as a held breath. "You've been counting."

"It's obvious?"

"To me it is. To Tobias it will be, by tomorrow—he's sharp as a fishhook and twice as likely to catch you unawares. To Merrick it already is, but Merrick has the decency to pretend his eyes are worse than they are." She sipped. "One seat."

"One seat."

They stood together in the quiet. Somewhere below, in the hold, the last of the rescued state papers lay wrapped in oilcloth where he had hidden them, and somewhere inside his coat, folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth itself, Lys's letter waited with its terrible arithmetic: *a fishing village, a berth secured, bring what you carry and we begin again.* His sister. Alive. Writing to him in a hand he had known since she was six years old and had used it to forge their father's signature on a permission slip for the harbor fair, and gotten away with it, which should have been the first warning about Lys that anyone needed.

"I keep thinking," Dominic said, "that there must be a way to make the numbers kinder. Three people who ought to go. One seat. Surely if I stare at it long enough the mathematics will feel ashamed of itself and produce a second seat."

"Mathematics doesn't have shame," Odile said. "Believe me. I've tried to negotiate with ledgers my whole life." She set down her mug on the rail. "But I'll tell you what I told the saloon committee, before they voted themselves into a knot. A skiff isn't a verdict. It's a beginning. Whoever goes in it goes not to be saved but to *arrive*—and arriving is work, Dominic. It's the hardest kind. Somebody has to do it."

"And somebody has to stay and hold the lantern line," he said quietly. "You said that too."

"I say a lot of things. Most of them are true; it's one of my flaws."

* * *

The boy slept in the corner of the crew's berth that had become his without ceremony or negotiation, the way a cat claims a chair. Tobias had arranged his few possessions around him with the grave precision of a man fortifying a nation: a tin cup with a dent shaped like a crescent moon, half a biscuit saved against a hunger that had not yet arrived, and the little brass button from a soldier's coat that he had never explained and Dominic had never asked about, because some archives belong to their owners alone.

Dominic sat down on the deck beside him, carefully, the way one sits near a sleeping thing one is afraid of waking—or afraid of loving, which amounts to the same fear wearing different boots.

Tobias's face in sleep was a different country from his face awake. Awake, the boy was all edges and arithmetic, counting everything, trusting nothing, hoarding glances the way other children hoarded sweets. Asleep, he was simply nine years old. His breath came slow and whistled faintly through a chapped lip. His hand had curled around the edge of his blanket in a fist, as though even in dreams he expected the blanket to be taken away, and Dominic's chest did the thing it had been doing for weeks now whenever he looked at this child—a slow, painful loosening, like a knot giving way that he had tied himself and forgotten tying.

He had fed him instead of reporting him. That first night in the hold, with the lantern swinging and the boy pressed back among the cargo crates looking at him with the flat defiance of something small that has decided to die fighting—and Dominic had opened his own rations instead of raising the alarm, and everything after that had followed from it, the way a single dropped stitch unravels a whole row, except in reverse: a single act of kindness knitting up a whole garment he hadn't known he was making.

*Family,* Merrick had said once, in that mild voice of his that made every observation sound like a footnote to a very long and very wise book, *is less a matter of blood than of attendance. Who shows up. Who stays in the room.*

The skiff had one seat.

If Dominic put Tobias in it—and God, how easily his hands imagined doing it, lifting the sleeping boy over the rail, tucking him among the lines—the boy would arrive. The boy would live. Somewhere on a neutral shore, in a language he'd have to learn, Tobias would grow tall and learn a trade and perhaps, someday, tell someone about a ship called the Meridian and a man whose name he'd barely known. That was the whole of the argument, and it needed no elaboration; it stood complete as a cathedral.

And yet.

Merrick coughed in his sleep across the berth, a deep wet sound like a hull taking water, and turned painfully toward the bulkhead. The old archivist was failing—not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a candle fails, shorter each hour, the light more effortful. He had spent the voyage cataloguing a library that fit in four crates: the rescued papers of a dead republic, the deeds and decrees and dumb ordinary correspondence of a country that had existed for two hundred years and now existed in oilcloth and memory. When Dominic had asked him why he bothered, why not burn the weight and eat better, Merrick had looked genuinely puzzled, the way a mathematician looks when asked why two and two.

*"Because a people is a story it tells itself,"* he had said. *"Lose the story and you don't lose the past. You lose the future. Nothing to build from. They'll hand the survivors a flag and no reason to raise it."*

Merrick could not survive the open boat. Everyone knew it, including, with his terrible gentle clarity, Merrick himself. The cold and the damp would take him inside two days, and he would spend those two days apologizing for the inconvenience. But put him ashore—put him anywhere with walls and a table—and he might last a season, a year, long enough to teach. Long enough to put the story into someone else's keeping. Into a boy's keeping, perhaps. A sharp boy with good hands and a suspicious mind and nothing left to inherit but exactly this.

Dominic looked at Tobias sleeping and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the choice had never really been between the three of them at all.

It had been between saving one person and saving a thread.

* * *

He climbed to the aft deck while the sky was deciding whether to be grey or gold, and took out the letter.

He knew it by heart now—all of it, every line, the way you know a wound by heart. Lys wrote beautifully; she always had. Her sentences were little ships, rigged and graceful, running before the wind. She described the fishing village in terms that made it sound like Eden with better herring. She described her survival in terms that made it sound like luck. She did not describe the two years between the purge and this letter, except once, in a sentence so brief it might have been accidental:

*I did what I had to do to stay alive, and I am not going to apologize for being alive, and if you cannot love me with that included then do not come.*

There it was. She had always been able to do that—hide the largest thing in the room inside the smallest sentence, like a stone in a loaf of bread.

He thought about the names Merrick had finally given him. Names Lys had traded to stay breathing. Some of them Dominic had known. One of them had taught him to read tide tables. And Merrick had carried that knowledge in silence across two years and an ocean, silent not to protect Lys but to protect *him*, to spare her brother the arithmetic, and Dominic had been furious for one white-hot hour and then had understood that this was what family did—it absorbed the blows aimed at you and said nothing about the bruises until you were strong enough to look.

Love complicated by shame. Love *including* the shame, as Lys would say. Not despite it.

He folded the letter away and found his hands were shaking, and laughed at himself, quietly, because here he was, the most dangerous man aboard, courier of codes that could crown a government-in-exile, and what undid him was a piece of paper and a sleeping child and an old man's cough.

The codes. He almost smiled thinking of them. For weeks they had sat against his ribs like a second heart, and he had caught himself, more than once, enjoying the weight—the way power enjoys itself in us before we notice, whispering that we are not carrying it, it is carrying us, and upward. He had deciphered the first layer alone in the chartroom by shielded lamplight and had understood immediately why men had died for these pages and would die for them again: legitimacy. A signature. The difference between a mob in boats and a government. He had wanted, briefly and shamefully, to be the man who decided.

Now the decision had shrunk to human size, which was the only size decisions ever actually came in, whatever the history books pretended. One seat. One letter. One old man's memory and one boy's future and one sister waiting in a fishing village with her berth secured and her sins undeleted.

Send the codes to Lys. Send the boy in the skiff. Stay himself—with Odile at the lantern line, with Merrick and his crates, with the ship, whatever the ship was about to become. Three acts, one decision, none of them clean, all of them his.

"You look," said a voice behind him, "like a man doing sums he doesn't like."

Merrick lowered himself onto the hatch coaming with the elaborate care of a much older man, which he nearly was. His breath smoked in the cold air. Up close, in this light, Dominic could see how translucent his skin had gone, paper over flame.

"I'm deciding," Dominic said.

"No, you're not. You decided days ago. You're only now working up the courage to admit it to yourself, which is the expensive part of any decision." Merrick produced a boiled sweet from some pocket, examined it, and offered it to Dominic, who declined, and put it back with the satisfaction of a man who has completed a social obligation. "May I tell you what I think you've concluded? Not to influence you. Only because I'm an archivist and I can't bear uncatalogued material."

Dominic huffed. "Go on, then."

"The boy goes in the boat. He's young, he's clever, and he is—forgive the phrasing—the only investment aboard with compounding interest. The codes go to your sister, because codes in a dead man's pocket legitimize nothing, and because however much she has cost the world, she is yours, and family is not a reward for virtue." He said it without cruelty, gently, the way one reads a hard passage aloud. "And you stay. Because someone must hold what remains, and because—" the old man paused, and for once his footnoting voice faltered, "—because you are the sort of man who shows up, Dominic Winterbourne. I have watched you show up for thirty-one days. For a stowaway. For a library. For a sister who may not deserve it. Staying is just showing up with nowhere left to go."

Dominic found his throat had closed. He managed, eventually: "That's quite a catalog entry."

"I'm thorough." Merrick rose, wincing, and laid one dry hand briefly on Dominic's shoulder—the whole sermon, the entire archive, compressed into three seconds of touch. "Whatever you choose," he said, "choose it out loud. Secrets sink ships. Boats too."

* * *

He went back down and stood over the boy a long time, watching him sleep, memorizing him the way Merrick memorized documents—not the facts of the face but the fact of it, the living weight of him, so that whatever came, some part of Dominic would be carrying him always, the way he had carried the codes, the way Merrick carried a republic in four crates and a skull.

Then he woke him, gently, with a hand on his shoulder, and the boy came up out of sleep fast and silent as a fisher bird, eyes wide, fists ready, softening only when he saw who it was.

"Tobias," Dominic said. "Wake the others. Quietly. We're putting the skiff over at first light."

The boy blinked. "All of us?"

Dominic looked at him—at the dented cup and the brass button and the fist still half-curled around the blanket—and made himself smile.

"One seat," he said. "We're going to talk about who sits in it."

Above them, through the deck planks, came the sound he had been listening for without knowing it: oars, many oars, keeping time in the fog off the starboard bow—and under it, thin and wrong, a whistle that was not the Meridian's.
