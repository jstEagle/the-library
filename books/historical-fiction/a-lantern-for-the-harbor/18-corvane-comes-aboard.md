## 18. Corvane Comes Aboard

Tobias learned the ship's sounds the way other children learned their mothers' voices.

The *Meridian* talked in her sleep. She groaned along her seams when the swell came from the north, sighed through the ventilators when the wind dropped, and clicked and ticked in the hold as her timbers swelled and shrank like an old man's knees. Tobias knew all of it. He had spent enough nights curled in the dark between crates of salt pork to know the difference between the ship dreaming and the ship being afraid.

That was why, on the morning of the parley, he woke before anyone else.

The ship wasn't making her usual sounds. There was a stillness to her, a held breath, as if every plank had gone quiet at once to listen. And beneath the stillness — faint, far off across the water — came a sound that did not belong to any sea Tobias had ever known.

Oars. Many oars. Rowed in time.

He sat up so fast he cracked his head on a crate of lard, which under any other circumstances would have been the whole of his morning's misfortune. But the oars kept coming, steady as a clock, and above them — thin and clear over cold water — a trumpet. Not a naval call. A parade call. The kind soldiers played when they wanted you to know they were coming and wanted you to be afraid about it besides.

Tobias had been a stowaway long enough to have opinions about fear. His first opinion was that fear was a rat: it lived in the same places you lived, ate the same food, and could not be killed, only discouraged. His second opinion, learned aboard this particular ship from a courier with patient hands and a quartermaster who swore in three languages, was that fear was only useful if it made you move.

So he moved.

He went up through the hold the way Dominic had taught him — quick, quiet, one hand always touching something solid — and came out onto the main deck into a world gone strange with white.

A flag was flying at the foremast. Not the republic's broken star. Not the new regime's black chevron either. It was a plain white cloth, sewn hastily from what looked very much like a bedsheet, snapping in the gray wind.

Parley.

"They're asking for terms," said a voice near his elbow, and Tobias jumped a foot before realizing it was old Anders, the sailor who taught him lantern signals, leaning on the rail with his pipe unlit because there was nothing left aboard worth burning in it. "White cloth means they want to talk before they kill us. Considerate of them."

"Will they talk?"

Anders sucked his teeth. "Soldiers under a parley flag are like cats at a mousehole, lad. They'll sit polite as church until they decide they aren't."

Tobias looked out across the water and saw them coming. Two longboats, packed with men in the gray-green of the new regime, oars dipping in perfect unison, and standing in the bow of the first boat with his hands folded behind him like a man admiring the scenery —

Corvane.

Tobias's stomach turned over. He had liked Corvane, once, in a wary sort of way. Corvane had given him a honey biscuit in the second week, back when Tobias was still officially cargo and not yet officially a boy, and had asked intelligent questions about where Tobias slept and whether anyone would miss him. It had taken Tobias another week to understand that those questions hadn't been friendly. They'd been inventory.

Now here was Corvane, come back with friends, wearing a coat that was too good for the weather and a smile that was too good for his face.

Tobias ran to find Dominic.

* * *

He found him at the rail near the stern, talking low and fast with Odile, and for a moment Tobias simply watched them, because watching Dominic Winterbourne was a kind of education.

Dominic did not look afraid. That was the remarkable thing. He looked like a man doing arithmetic — eyes moving, jaw tight, everything behind his face adding up columns faster than any clerk alive. Odile stood beside him with her arms crossed, and she did look afraid, but she wore her fear the way she wore her coat: buttoned up, practical, with the sleeves rolled for work.

"Two boats," Odile was saying. "Thirty men, maybe thirty-five. We've got—" she counted something invisible, "—nine able seamen, a hundred-odd passengers, and a cook who fights dirty."

"The cook stays out of it," said Dominic. "We need breakfast."

"That's your contribution? *Breakfast?*"

"My contribution is that nobody panics before noon." Then his eyes found Tobias, and something in his face changed — softened and sharpened all at once, the way it always did. "Tobias. Good. I need you."

Those four words did more for Tobias's fear than any amount of bravery could have. *I need you.* Nobody had needed him in years. He stepped forward and stood straight, the way Merrick had shown him to stand when reciting dates.

"Corvane will ask to board under parley," Dominic said quietly. "By custom we can't refuse — and refusing would give them an excuse to stop pretending. So he comes aboard. He'll bring six men, no more; that's the custom too. While he's here, he'll want two things: to search for the codes, and to find me."

"You?" Tobias's voice cracked on it.

"I'm on their list. Probably near the top." Dominic said it the way he might have said *it looks like rain*. "So while Odile keeps Corvane busy upstairs, you and I are going downstairs, and we're going to do the most important work done on this ship today."

"Which is?"

Dominic almost smiled. "Filing."

* * *

The archivist's room was really a converted linen locker off the lower corridor, barely tall enough for Merrick to stand in, crammed floor to ceiling with rescued paper. Tobias had been inside exactly twice. It smelled of dust and lamp oil and, underneath it, faintly of the burned chancellery itself — as if the papers had carried the smoke of their burning across the water with them.

Merrick was already awake, of course. Merrick was always awake; Tobias suspected the old man slept standing up between thoughts, like a horse. He had laid out his reading glasses, his magnifying glass, and a single cup of chicory, and was regarding the door with an expression of profound scholarly annoyance.

"So," he said, before Dominic had finished explaining. "They've come for the codes."

"You knew?"

"I assumed it the moment I saw the sheet flying. Bed linen, by the look of it. Second-rate parley flags make for second-rate regimes." Merrick rose, joints cracking like small fireworks, and shuffled to the back wall of the locker. "Well then. You want my library."

Dominic hesitated — and Tobias, watching, saw the hesitation plainly. This was the room Merrick loved more than any place left in the world. Every paper in it was a person: a census, a court record, a child's school certificate stamped with a seal that no longer existed. Hiding the codes here meant hiding them among people, among names, among the republic itself.

"Merrick," Dominic began, "if it goes wrong—"

"If it goes wrong, they burn paper and an old man, and the world goes on being stupid." Merrick waved this off with the air of a man declining a second helping. "But it won't go wrong, because you're going to do it properly. Give them here."

What happened next took eleven minutes, and Tobias counted every one of them against the sound of boots overhead.

They did not hide the codes. That was the genius of it — or rather, it was Merrick's genius, delivered in the tone of a professor correcting an able student. Anyone searching for contraband looked for things that were hidden: locked boxes, false bottoms, oilcloth packets. So instead they gave the codes a job.

The diplomatic cipher, wrapped in its oilcloth, became a shipping manifest. Merrick wrote a header on it in his crabbed antique hand — *Cargo manifest, sloop *Meridian*, port of origin struck by censorship* — and slipped it into a folder with forty genuine manifests, each more boring than the last. The cipher key, a single folded page of numbers, became a page in a ledger of grain weights, its figures indistinguishable from forty thousand other figures unless you knew that every seventh number mattered. And the letter of accreditation — the paper that could crown a government-in-exile or hang its bearer — Merrick pressed flat and bound into the middle of a volume of harbor regulations, 1819 edition, a book so profoundly dull that even Tobias felt his eyelids drooping just holding it.

"No one," Merrick said, shelving it with a little pat, "has ever voluntarily opened the 1819 harbor regulations. I once watched a man weep rather than continue."

"And if they burn everything?" Tobias asked.

Merrick looked at him then, really looked, the way he did when a question deserved an honest answer instead of a kind one. "Then it lives in here." He tapped his own temple. "Forty years of service, boy. The key is in my head and half the archive is too. Paper burns. Memory has to be hunted down one skull at a time, and there aren't enough soldiers in the world for the job." He smiled thinly. "Though they may try."

Overhead, the boots stopped. A voice — smooth, carrying, horribly familiar — said something Tobias couldn't make out, and a ripple of nervous laughter went through the passengers crowded on deck.

"They're boarding," Dominic said. He put his hand briefly on Tobias's shoulder — a real hand, heavy and warm, not a pat but a weight, an anchor. "Your job now is the hardest one. Go up. Be a boy. Boys see everything and nobody watches them. If Corvane's men start searching, I need to know where they look first. Count them for me. Count everything."

"What do I do if—"

"Count," Dominic said. "That's all. Counting is how we win today."

Then he was folding himself into the shadows behind the archive shelf with a lamp and a book, a man becoming furniture, and Merrick was settling into his chair like a spider into a web, and Tobias was alone in the corridor with his heart going like a drum.

He counted to ten, the way Anders had taught him for steadying a lantern in wind. Then he went up to be a boy.

* * *

The deck had transformed. Corvane stood amidships in his fine coat, flanked by six soldiers in gray-green, radiating the particular courtesy of men who have guns and are enjoying not using them yet. Facing him stood Odile, and Tobias had never seen anything so beautiful in his life as the sight of Odile stalling.

She had chosen her ground well. She had met Corvane beside the main hatch, which meant the entire passenger population of the *Meridian* had an excellent view, and the passengers were pressing in around the edges like spectators at a trial — which meant Corvane couldn't simply drag anyone away without an audience.

"Monsieur Corvane," Odile was saying, in the voice she used for customs officials and other natural disasters, "you present a parley flag. Parley requires a grievance stated. State it."

"Grievance." Corvane spread his hands, and his smile widened by exactly one tooth. "Madame, there is no grievance. There is a misunderstanding. Your vessel carries material belonging to the Provisional Authority of the Republic. Documents. We wish only to retrieve them, extend the regime's protection to your passengers, and send everyone home warm."

"Home," someone muttered in the crowd, and it was not a kind word.

"Documents," Odile repeated. "Which documents? This ship carries eleven tons of paper. Half of it is unpaid bills from the chandlery in Vellport."

Corvane laughed — a real laugh, Tobias had to admit, a laugh that reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "You always did keep excellent accounts, Quartermaster. Very well. The documents in question concern state cryptography. Diplomatic seals. You will know them when you see them."

"And if we don't see them?"

"Then we search. With your permission, naturally." The word *naturally* did a great deal of work, standing there in front of thirty-five soldiers in boats.

Odile stroked her chin. She actually stroked her chin, slowly, drawing the moment out like taffy, and Tobias — wedged between a barrel and a coil of rope where he could see everything and be seen by no one — realized she was counting too. Counting the minutes. Every minute Corvane spent smiling on deck was a minute the codes spent safely buried in the 1819 harbor regulations.

"You'll want a manifest," Odile said. "I don't open my holds to officers without paperwork. That's regulation."

"It's a sinking republic, Madame Odile."

"It's *my* ship's hold," said Odile, "and regulation is the last thing we own that works. Give me a moment to fetch it."

She turned and walked aft at the pace of a woman who had never hurried in her life, and Corvane's smile thinned by a degree, and Tobias understood suddenly why Dominic trusted this woman with the ship. Stalling wasn't cowardice. Stalling was artillery.

But then Corvane stopped playing.

"Now, if you please," he said pleasantly, and snapped his fingers, and two soldiers stepped toward the hatchway, and a third — the big one with the scarred knuckles — swept his gaze across the crowd and stopped, just for a heartbeat, on a boy crouched between a barrel and some rope.

Tobias's blood went to ice water. *Be a boy,* Dominic had said. *Nobody watches boys.*

Except this man did. Because this man had been on the ship before. This man remembered the stowaway who got a honey biscuit and answered questions.

The soldier started toward him.

Tobias's mind did the thing it did best, the thing hunger had trained into him and the *Meridian* had refined: it moved fast and sideways. He didn't run — running was a confession. Instead he stood up, rubbed his stomach, and pointed urgently at the galley, then at the soldiers, then made the universal face of a boy sent to fetch food who had gotten distracted. Then he frowned, patted his pockets, and produced — miracle of miracles — the heel of yesterday's bread he'd stashed there at dawn, and took an enormous defiant bite of it, glaring at the scarred soldier as though *he* were the thief.

The soldier blinked. Somewhere behind his eyes, a category closed: *stowaway, irrelevant, fed.* He turned away toward the hatch.

And that was when Tobias saw the thing that made his mouth go dry anyway.

The fourth soldier had not gone below with the others. He had drifted aft, casual as fog, and stopped outside the linen locker. Outside the corridor to the archivist's room. He was lighting a cigarette with great patience, the way men stand watch.

Tobias chewed his bread and thought: *Count. Counting is how we win today.*

One guard at the archive. Six below, starting with the forward hold, farthest from the truth. Corvane on deck, occupied with Odile, who had returned with a manifest of genuinely impressive length and was now reading it aloud from the top.

*Item one: tar, pitch, forty kegs.*

Tobias slid along the rail, past the coils of rope, past Anders, who without looking at him shifted slightly to block the scarred soldier's line of sight — old sailors knew things — and ducked down the after ladder into the corridor.

The cigarette smoker heard him coming and turned, and Tobias walked straight up to him, chewing, and held up the bread.

"You want half? Cook sent me down with extras for the watch. There's biscuits too, but I ate those."

It was, objectively, a terrible lie. Tobias had never been sent anywhere by the cook in his life. But the soldier was cold, and bored, and bread was bread, and the war had been going on a long time. He took the half-loaf with a grunt, and Tobias stood there companionably while he ate it, chattering about biscuits, and all the while in his head he was counting — *one guard here, six below, four minutes since they went down, Dominic needs four more* — and when the soldier finished and flicked his crumbs away and finally, finally turned to go check on his fellows, Tobias waited until the boots faded, then slipped down the passage and scratched softly, twice, at the archive door.

It opened a crack. One eye, one lamp-lit sliver of Merrick's face.

"They found the manifests," Tobias whispered. "Not yet the regulations book. One guard was watching your door. I fed him."

Merrick regarded him over the spectacles for a long moment. "Fed him," he repeated.

"With bread. It was mostly a bribe."

For the first time in all the weeks Tobias had known him, the old archivist laughed — silently, shakingly, a laugh like leaves. "Boy," he said, "when they write the history of this ship — and they will, because I intend to write it myself — you are getting a chapter." He pressed something into Tobias's hand through the gap: a strip of dried apple, hoarded from who knew when. "Now go. Count some more. And Tobias — whatever happens next, whatever you hear — remember where the 1819 harbor regulations are shelved."

"Why?"

"Because books survive," said Merrick, closing the door, "when we give them good reasons to."

Up on deck, the reading of the manifest had reached *item nine*, and Corvane's patience had reached its end. Tobias felt it in the deck before he heard it — a change in the vibration under his feet, the sound of many boots below changing direction, all at once, toward the stern.

He started counting again. This time, he wasn't sure there would be enough numbers.
