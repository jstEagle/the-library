## 19. Fire on the Meridian

The parley flag went up at dawn, and it was the whitest thing Dominic had seen in weeks.

It hung from Corvane's launch like a bedsheet on a washline, absurd against the grey water, and Dominic stood at the rail of the *Meridian* watching it approach and thought: *that is a lie stitched large enough to be seen from shore.* Beside him, Odile said nothing. She had been saying nothing since midnight, which for Odile was a great deal of speech.

"Four soldiers," she said at last. "Count them."

"I count five."

"The fifth is carrying the lantern. He's not a soldier, he's furniture." She adjusted her collar against the wind. "Corvane will come up alone to the quarterdeck. The rest will wait at the gangway. He'll want tea, or brandy, or whatever passes for courtesy on this rusted ark, and he'll talk about terms until the sun is well clear of the water."

"And then?"

"And then he'll ask you where the codes are, Dominic, in that soft voice of his, and every man aboard will learn exactly what kind of flag that is."

Dominic had slept perhaps an hour. It had not been sleep so much as a long negotiation with his own heartbeat. Below, in Merrick's archive room, the diplomatic codes lay sewn into the spine of a shipping ledger between the manifests of a grain fleet and the minutes of a harbor commission nobody had ever read. Merrick had done the sewing himself, with thread and a sailmaker's needle, humming. *Power,* the old archivist liked to say, *should be filed under something dull.* Dominic had laughed when he first heard it. He did not laugh now.

Tobias was on deck before anyone summoned him, because Tobias appeared whenever something was happening, materializing out of rigging and shadow like a small grey cat with opinions. He was holding two hard biscuits and wearing the expression of a boy who has decided that if the world is ending, he will face it fed.

"They're coming," Tobias said, unnecessarily.

"They're coming," Dominic agreed.

"If they board, I know the way through the hold. All the ways. Even the one behind the tar barrels that Odile doesn't know about."

"She knows about it," Odile said without turning around.

Tobias considered this betrayal of his confidences and elected, with the resilience of youth, to ignore it. He handed one biscuit up to Dominic. It was stale and tasted faintly of the hold, and Dominic ate all of it, because his hands needed something honest to do.

* * *

Corvane came aboard at half past seven, and he came smiling.

That was the thing about the man — he smiled the way other men breathed, automatically, without meaning anything by it. He climbed the ladder with the easy grace of someone who had never once been seasick, brushed off his coat, and bowed to Odile as though she were hosting a salon rather than commanding a dying ship.

"Madam Quartermaster. Mr. Winterbourne." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Or shall we say *Courier* Winterbourne? I find I've grown fond of the full title. It has such weight to it."

"You're early," Dominic said. "I expected parleys after breakfast."

"Hunger makes men reasonable." Corvane accepted the chair Odile indicated with a flourish that mocked them both. Behind him, at the gangway, the four soldiers stood in the cold like posts driven into the deck. "So. Terms. You'll find them generous, I promise you. The regime remembers its friends."

"The regime hanged my friends," Dominic said. "From lampposts. I attended several of the ceremonies."

"The regime," Corvane corrected gently, "hanged its enemies, and did so publicly, which is more honesty than most governments manage. But let's not quarrel over history. History is settled. I'm here about the future — specifically, about a certain document currently enjoying an uneventful retirement somewhere aboard this vessel." His eyes moved across the deck, lazy as a fisherman's net. "Give it to me, and the *Meridian* keeps her papers, her passengers keep their heads, and every soul here steps onto dry ground by week's end. There's a port forty miles south that owes me favors. Neutral, hungry, and agreeable."

Odile's jaw tightened. Forty miles south was a fishing coast and a rumor. But it was also, possibly, true, and hope is a cruel currency because it spends so easily.

"And if we refuse?" Dominic asked.

Corvane spread his hands. The smile deepened by a degree. "Then I regret to inform you that this ship, already denied by two harbors, listed in three registries as unseaworthy, and carrying — I'm told — contraband of state, will be refused a fourth time, and a fifth, and a sixth, until she becomes what her paperwork says she is: a wreck awaiting an excuse." He rose, buttoning his coat. "You have until noon, Mr. Courier. Think of the boy."

He said it lightly. That was the obscenity of it. He said *think of the boy* the way a man might say *mind the step*, and then he descended the ladder with his soldiers falling in behind him, and the white flag fluttered down, and the launch pulled away into the mist.

Tobias had heard everything. Dominic knew it by the stillness of him, the way the boy stood by the rail not pretending to have been anywhere else.

"He knows about me?" Tobias asked.

"He knows about everyone," Dominic said. "It's how he stays employed."

* * *

Noon found them in the saloon, and the saloon found itself fuller than it had ever been.

The vote had been Merrick's idea, because Merrick believed — stubbornly, unfashionably, in the teeth of every evidence — that people should decide their own endings. So Odile called the assembly, and the loyalists sat on the port side and the profiteers sat on the starboard side and the exiles sat everywhere, and Merrick stood at the head of the table looking like a schoolmaster who has finally been given a classroom worthy of his disappointment.

The argument lasted two hours and was, in its way, the finest thing Dominic ever witnessed. A shipload of frightened people arguing procedure while the noose tightened — raising hands, counting voices, a woman named Seraf insisting on a secret ballot because her husband had been shot after a public one. Democracy in a drowning room. It should have been pitiful. Instead it was almost holy.

In the end the count was close: surrender the codes, buy the ship's safety. Ninety-one votes to eighty-eight.

Merrick looked at the tally for a long moment. Then he removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on.

"The vote is carried," he said. "And the quartermaster will note that I voted with the majority, because I believe in counting, even when the count is wrong." His voice was mild, but his eyes found Dominic's across the table and held there. "Memory must survive, gentlemen, even if men do not. That has always been my position. It remains so. Which is why the codes will not be surrendered — because they are not mine to surrender, nor yours. They belong to a republic that is dead but not finished, and dead things that are not finished have a way of settling their accounts."

A silence followed, in which ninety-one people understood, slowly, that the old man had just committed treason against their democracy in defense of a dead one.

It was Odile who broke it. She stood, and she said, "The vote instructed me to hand over the codes. I have searched this ship from keel to masthead and I can't find them. Perhaps they were lost in the storm. Perhaps they were never aboard. The sea keeps many secrets and I am only her bookkeeper." She closed her ledger with a snap. "Log it however you like."

Somebody laughed. Then somebody else. It was not a happy laugh, but it was the laugh of people deciding, together, to be braver than their own vote — and Dominic felt something loosen in his chest that he had been carrying since chapter one of this whole catastrophe, though of course he didn't think of it in chapters, he thought of it as the weight behind his breastbone that had finally, briefly, lifted.

* * *

They came at dusk instead of noon, which told Dominic everything: Corvane had never intended to honor any deadline. Deadlines were theater. He had spent the day moving his soldiers into the harbor watch positions and buying the pilot boat, and now the *Meridian*'s own longboat returned from shore flying no flag at all, and the first thing over the rail was not a man but fire.

The stern caught fast. Tar and dry rope are eager to burn; the sea wind fanned it like a bellows; within minutes the aft deck was an orange country with borders that moved. Shouting everywhere. Odile's voice cutting through it like a marlinspike — *life vests, women and children to the skiffs, form lines, FORM LINES* — and Dominic ran below into smoke.

The archive room was filling with it. Merrick was there already, hauling ledgers into a canvas sack with the methodical calm of a man packing for a holiday, coughing between armfuls.

"Merrick! Out. Now."

"When the sack is full."

"The sack is *full enough*—"

"It is never full," Merrick said, wheezing, laughing, terrible. "That is the whole tragedy of archives, boy."

Dominic got the sack. He got the old man too, one arm over his shoulder, and they came up into light that was wrong — too orange, too flickering — and there at the rail, in the chaos, stood Corvane.

Not boarding. Waiting. Holding a dispatch case under one arm the way a duelist holds a second pistol, and beside him the escape skiff, lowered, provisioned, one berth remaining because Odile had cut the others loose already filled. One berth. Dominic had known it would come to this arithmetic since the moment he'd read Lys's last letter, and knowing had not made the number smaller.

Corvane smiled his breathing-smile. "Mr. Courier. The ship is dying. The case, please, and I'll see your people ashore. My word on it."

"Your word," Dominic said, "is the whitest flag I ever saw."

But he was already moving, because he had spent the burning minutes doing the last piece of work of his old life. Inside his coat was a sealed packet — the codes' outer cipher, the routing layer, the part that proved authenticity. And in his hand, taken from the burning chartroom two minutes ago, was Corvane's own dispatch case, left behind in the officer's quarters when the agent came aboard to gloat: leather, government-stamped, bound for the new regime's coastal office by the morning courier run that Corvane himself had arranged.

Dominic walked to the rail. Below, in the skiff, Tobias looked up at him with a face trying very hard to be brave and failing beautifully.

"In you get," Dominic said.

"You're coming too." Not a question. A boy's whole theology in four words.

"No."

"You *promised*—" The word broke in half. Dominic crouched at the rail's edge, and the fire painted them both gold, and he took the boy's face in both hands the way he'd once taken a cracked teacup, gently, as if it might be mended by attention.

"Tobias. Listen to me, because I'll only say this once and you must carry it further than I can. Family isn't who you save. Anyone can save someone — it takes ten seconds and a skiff. Family is who you *stay* with. Who stands the watch. Who holds the light so the next ship finds the harbor." He pressed the sealed packet into the boy's jacket, over his heart, and folded the boy's arms across it. "This goes to Lys Winterbourne, the fishing village at Greywater Point. She'll know what it means. Tell her — tell her it isn't forgiveness, it's better. It's work. Tell her the lantern line runs north and I'm standing it."

"I don't want the berth," Tobias said, weeping now, furious, magnificent. "I want *you*."

"I know," Dominic said. "That's why you have to take it. Someone has to survive being loved by us, and you're the best candidate this ship ever produced."

Odile's hand fell on the boy's shoulder — firm, final — and the painter dropped, and the skiff swung away on the swell, and Tobias knelt in it facing backward, watching, until the smoke took him. Dominic watched longer than the smoke allowed. That is the privilege of the one who stays: he gets the last look.

Then he turned to Corvane, and he held out the government-stamped dispatch case, and Corvane took it with the eagerness of a man who had forgotten to check gifts for hooks.

Inside was not the codes. Inside was a single sheet of paper, and on it, in a courier's neat hand: *The republic files its memory under something dull. Ask your archivists where.* By morning it would be in regime hands, and by afternoon regime clerks would tear apart three ministries' worth of filing systems looking for documents that were, at that moment, in a canvas sack on a lifeboat, sewn into grain manifests, or — the true key of them, layer upon layer — walking north inside a twelve-year-old boy's coat toward a sister who had chosen, at last, to earn it.

"You'll hang for this," Corvane said. All the silk had gone out of his voice.

"Probably," Dominic agreed. "But not tonight, and not by you. Tonight I'm employed." He nodded aft, where the fire had reached the mainmast and the heat made the whole sky tremble. "Harbor watch. Somebody has to stand the lantern line so those boats don't founder in the dark. Odile's got the lamp. Merrick's got the list of who's aboard each skiff — names, all of them, so none of them get to vanish. And I've got the whistle." He produced the brass harbor-whistle from his pocket, the one from the first night, the small weight he'd carried through everything. "Three blasts for all safe. Two for missing. One for—" He stopped. Blew nothing. "We'll manage."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment, and something almost human crossed the man's face — puzzlement, maybe, the genuine bafflement of a creature that had never once stayed behind.

"You could have run," Corvane said. "You had the berth. Why?"

Dominic turned toward the fire, toward the black water beyond it where eleven boats bobbed and dipped and needed a light to steer by, and he answered honestly, because the moment deserved it:

"Because everyone I love is on that water. And I've finally learned the difference between saving them and keeping them."

* * *

They stood the line until nearly two in the morning.

Odile worked the lantern — trimming, refilling, swinging it in slow arcs, her arms steady as pilings. Merrick sat against the base of the mast with his ledger open on his knees, calling out counts as each skiff's lantern answered across the water: *four, five, six — all accounted — seven —* his voice getting smaller as the night went on, but never stopping. Dominic blew the whistle. Three blasts. Three blasts. Three blasts. The sweetest music the republic ever produced, and no one would ever write it down except a quartermaster in a logbook, in pencil, under weather conditions: *clear, cold, all souls delivered.*

The *Meridian* burned down to the waterline with a sound like applause.

Near the end, Merrick's hand found Dominic's wrist. "The key," the old man murmured, half-conscious, smiling at the flames. "The true cipher. Don't fret, boy. It's filed somewhere safe." He tapped his own temple, once. "Under something dull."

Dominic laughed — actually laughed, smoke in his lungs, salt on his face, laughing — and held the old man's hand, and kept the count.

When dawn came, grey and ordinary, eleven boats lay at anchor in the lee of the point. On the far shore, a fishing village had lit lamps along its jetty — welcome signals, hesitant, then steady. And high on the ridge above the harbor, where the old beacon tower stood empty, someone had lit a fire in the grate: a rough, improvised flame, visible for miles.

Odile squinted at it. "Who's up there?"

Dominic shaded his eyes. Small figure. Grey coat. Waving a lantern in the old pattern — the one Tobias had been taught by the old sailor, the one that meant *this way home.*

"Well," Dominic said, and found his voice had gone thick. "Well. It seems the watch has an apprentice."

He picked up the spare lantern. Merrick groaned to his feet. Odile shouldered the oil. And the three of them started up the hill toward the light, toward the village that had opened its gates, toward whatever remained — which was, Dominic understood at last, everything that mattered.

Behind them, the tide came in over the ashes of the *Meridian*, patient as a bookkeeper, closing the account.
