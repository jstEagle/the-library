## 20. Ash and Salt

The fire had been generous in one respect only: it had left the archive room a shell rather than a pyre. Merrick stood in the doorway at first light—the light that came not from the sun but from the smoldering bulkhead, a dull orange breathing like a sleeping animal—and tried to count what remained.

He was an old man with a cataloguer's habits. For forty years he had counted things: folios, crates, the number of steps between the Ministry reading room and his office (two hundred and eleven; he had once counted them during an earthquake and found the number comforting). Counting was how he kept panic at arm's length. Panic, like a bad borrower, always wanted to take more than it was entitled to.

So he counted.

One iron strongbox, sprung open, its contents charred to lace. Two shelving units collapsed against each other like tired dancers. Three drawers of the card catalogue had survived—drawers M through O, which meant that somewhere under the ash lay every document filed under *Maritime*, *Neutral Ports*, and *Ordinances*, and none of anything useful whatsoever. He permitted himself one small, dry laugh at this. The republic's memory, in its final hour, had chosen to save its shipping schedules.

"You'll want gloves," said Odile behind him.

She stood in the passage holding two pairs of sailmaker's palms, stiff with tar, and a bucket of seawater she set down with the care of a woman laying a wreath. Her face was gray with smoke and sleeplessness. The Meridian still floated—that was the miracle nobody aboard had yet found words for—but she floated the way a burned candle floats: technically, provisionally, on the sufferance of the sea.

"Thank you," Merrick said, and took the gloves. They were far too large. His hands had been described, by a librarian he had once loved unwisely, as "elegant," though Merrick suspected what she'd meant was *bony*. Now they disappeared into the palms entirely, and he felt briefly like a child playing at work.

They worked together through the morning, lifting wet ash in handfuls, passing buckets along the line of sailors who came when they could spare themselves from patching the hull. Nobody spoke much. There is a particular silence that attends the sorting of ruins, and Merrick had heard it before—in the Ministry cellar after the first purge, when they had smuggled out state papers in flour sacks—but it never became familiar. It only became bearable.

What he could not stop thinking about was the cipher key.

It had been written on three sheets of rice paper, folded into the false bottom of the strongbox, because Dominic Winterbourne had insisted—*insisted*, in that stubborn quiet way of his—on keeping a physical copy separate from the codes themselves. A sensible precaution. An archivist's precaution, in fact. Merrick had approved of it at the time.

And now those three sheets were ash, or very nearly ash, or something worse than ash: half-legible, teasing, a page of characters where every third glyph survived like teeth in an old jaw. He had knelt over them for an hour before dawn. He could read perhaps forty percent. Forty percent of a cipher key was worth precisely nothing, except as an instrument of torture for whoever possessed both the key's ghost and the codes' absence.

Except.

Merrick sat down on an overturned crate, which creaked a warning but held, and did the thing he had trained himself to do since boyhood, the thing that had made him valuable to ministries and dangerous to tyrants and tiresome at dinner parties.

He closed his eyes and read the sheets again—from memory.

* * *

His memory was not a gift. People called it that, and he had stopped correcting them decades ago because the correction sounded like boasting, but it was not a gift. It was a discipline, built the way a harbor wall is built: stone by stone, over years, mostly in weather nobody would choose. As a young clerk he had memorized the filing system so thoroughly that when the system was reorganized without notice, he had simply continued using the old one until the new one collapsed of its own weight. As a middle-aged archivist he had memorized the faces of every man who signed the execution orders—not out of hatred, though there was some, but because a record must be kept somewhere, and paper burns.

Paper burns. He looked at his hands, black to the wrist, and thought: *yes. Paper burns. That is why we were given heads.*

The trouble was that a cipher key was not like a filing system or a face. A filing system had *logic*, and logic forgives imperfect recall—one wrong drawer and the shape of the whole still shows. A cipher key was arbitrary. Every substitution table, every rotation schedule, every transposition order was pure caprice, invented by some committee of frightened men in a basement. To hold it in memory you had to hold it *exactly*, and exactness across five thousand glyphs was a thing even Merrick had never attempted.

But he had read those sheets eleven times. He had read them aloud, softly, in his cabin, while Dominic pretended to sleep in the bunk above. He had read them backward, because backward-reading exposed errors. He had tested himself on the third sheet's central column while walking the deck, reciting glyph-names under his breath like a madman, which Tobias had witnessed once and reported to Dominic as "Mr. Merrick talking to the fish again."

Eleven readings. Perhaps ninety percent retention. Possibly more—he genuinely did not know until he tried.

He tried.

He sat in the wrecked archive room with a slate Odile had salvaged and a stub of chalk, and he wrote out the first hundred glyphs of the primary substitution table from memory. Then he waited until the tide of memory settled, and wrote them again, and compared the two versions. Where they disagreed—and they disagreed in nine places—he wrote the disputed glyphs a third time, and where the third attempt sided with the first version twice and the second once, he marked all three with a dot and moved on, the way one marks a doubtful word in a damaged manuscript: honestly, and with grief.

By noon he had recovered perhaps six hundred glyphs with reasonable confidence, and had filled four slates, and had developed a headache of magnificent proportions.

"You look," said Tobias, appearing in the doorway with two bowls of barley soup, "like a man losing an argument with a wall."

"I am reconstructing a cipher key from memory," Merrick said, "and I appear to have misplaced the letter that comes after the water-glyph in the second rotation."

"The one that looks like a bird sitting down?"

Merrick stared at him.

Tobias shrugged, set down the bowls, and drew on the slate: a swift, confident little mark. "That one. I saw your papers that night. When I was hiding in here." He had the grace to look slightly guilty about it. "I wasn't reading. Mostly. But I see things. It's how I stay alive."

Merrick took the slate and examined the glyph. The boy had drawn it correctly. Not approximately—correctly, with the little tail-hook that distinguished it from the nearly identical glyph two positions earlier, the distinction that had cost Merrick twenty minutes and most of his remaining patience.

"Tobias," Merrick said gravely, "how would you feel about a career in archival science? The pay is terrible. The conditions are currently on fire. But the company is excellent."

The boy grinned, and for a moment the burnt room smelled less of ash and more of morning.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, Merrick reciting and writing, Tobias checking against the scorched originals where any fragment survived, catching him in errors with the mercilessness of youth. Twice the boy caught mistakes that mattered—a rotated pair of glyphs early in the second sheet that would have corrupted every date encoded thereafter. Twice Merrick corrected the record and felt the strange, humbling gratitude of a scholar whose student has saved him from publication in error.

It was during the second correction that Dominic found them.

Merrick heard his footsteps stop in the doorway and knew without looking what the man was seeing: the old archivist and the orphan boy, chalk-dusted, surrounded by slates covered in the secret heart of the republic, rebuilding from ruin the thing that had gotten the ship burned.

"You're recovering it," Dominic said quietly. Not a question.

"Some of it," Merrick said. "Perhaps enough. Perhaps not. I won't know until there are codes to try it against."

Dominic came into the room and stood over the slates a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the flat calm Merrick had learned, these last terrible days, to recognize as the sound of a man who has already made a decision and is merely waiting for the world to catch up.

"There's a fishing village on the coast. Lys is there. There's a boat going out tonight—I've arranged it with Corvane's own dispatch case, sealed, so it looks like regime correspondence if anyone stops it." He hesitated. "The codes go to her. She can carry them to the exile government. And the key—" he looked at the slates— "the key stays in your head, Merrick. Where no soldier can seize it, no fire can burn it, and no traitor can steal it. You're the vault now."

Merrick set down the chalk. His hands, he noticed, were steady, which surprised him; inside, something was swinging like a lamp in a gale.

"I'm seventy-one years old," he said. "I misplace my spectacles four times a day. Last week I called the bosun 'Minister Plenipotentiary' and apologized to a coil of rope. You are proposing to entrust the legitimacy of a government-in-exile to this head?"

"Yes," said Dominic.

"Why?"

"Because everything else it might be entrusted to has already burned," Dominic said. "Because you've spent your whole life carrying things that weren't yours so they'd survive. And because—" and here his voice roughened, just slightly— "you're family. Whatever the papers say."

Merrick looked at him for a long moment. Then he picked up the chalk again, because there was nothing adequate to say, and saying inadequate things aloud was a habit he had broken years ago in favor of work.

"I'll need the boy," he said instead. "His eyesight is better than mine and his memory is frankly alarming. Also he works for soup."

"Done," said Dominic, and Tobias, who had been listening with his whole body the way children do, sat up straighter than Merrick had seen him sit in all their weeks aboard.

There was, Merrick reflected, dipping his pen into grief and finding it wrote stewardship readily enough, worse legacies than this. Empires ended and were remembered only in their tax ledgers. Kings died and left statues that pigeons improved upon. But a mind that carried a free country's name inside it, glyph by glyph, faithfully, absurdly, against all sense—that mind could walk through any fire the century cared to set, and come out the other side still itself.

He worked until his eyes failed him at dusk. Then he slept two hours, woke, and worked again by lantern-light while the sea slapped patiently against the scorched hull, as it had against every hull since the beginning of the world, indifferent to what floated and what sank, waiting—as the sea always waits—for news of harbors.

Near midnight, Odile appeared at his elbow with tea and an expression he had learned to dread.

"Signal watch says lights on the headland," she said. "And there's a boat coming out. Small. Running dark." She paused. "Could be the fishing fleet. Could be our boat coming back. Could be neither."

Merrick looked at the slates stacked around him—the republic, rebuilt in chalk, fragile as a sandcastle and as real.

"Then," he said, "we had better finish before it arrives."
