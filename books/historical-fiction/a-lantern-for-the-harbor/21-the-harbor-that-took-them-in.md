## 21. The Harbor That Took Them In

The harbor that took them in was called Greywater, and it had never been famous for anything except herring and a stubborn refusal to flatter anyone.

Odile stood at the rail of what remained of the *Meridian* — the stern third of her, mostly, riding low and listing like a drunk uncle at a wedding — and watched the fishing boats come out to meet them. Not warships. Not a parley delegation under a white flag with soldiers hiding behind it. Fishing boats, eleven of them, their decks piled with blankets and bread loaves and jars of something that turned out, upon investigation, to be pickled onions.

"Are those onions?" Tobias asked beside her, his voice still hoarse from smoke.

"That's exactly what those are," Odile said.

"Is that... normal? Do harbors do that?"

"No," Odile said. "But this one does."

The first boat alongside carried a woman in oilskins who looked up at the scorched hull of the *Meridian* for a long moment, then at the survivors crowding her rails — maybe two hundred souls left of the six hundred who'd sailed, blackened, soaked, hollow-eyed — and said only, "Right. Come down in twos, mind the gap, and don't crush my nets." Her name was Marta Skellig, they learned later, and she owned half the herring fleet and all of the opinions in Greywater.

Odile went last. She always went last; it was the quartermaster's privilege and burden to be the final one off a dying ship. Before she climbed down she did something she'd been putting off since the fire: she walked the length of the surviving deck, touched the rail where the lantern line had been strung, and said, quietly, so no one could hear her being sentimental, "Thank you for holding."

Ships didn't answer. This one didn't either. But the tide moved beneath her, patient as ever, and Odile chose to take it as acknowledgment.

Then she climbed down into a fishing boat full of strangers and pickled onions, and the harbor took them in.

* * *

The work began before the sun set, because grief is no excuse for disorganization, and if Odile Vane had learned one thing across twenty-two years of manifests and cargo holds and passengers who lied about their luggage, it was that people survive better when there is a list.

She requisitioned the back room of the herring weigh-house from Marta Skellig by the simple method of walking in, setting her salvaged ledger on the table, and saying, "I need light, ink, and nobody talking to me for four hours."

Marta looked at the ledger, then at Odile's face — salt-scoured, smoke-rimmed, sixty years old and every one of them present — and said, "There's a stove. Mind you don't burn it like you burned your ship."

"We didn't burn our ship," Odile said. "Other people burned our ship. We were busy not dying."

"Even better," said Marta, and brought her a lamp.

And so Odile sat down with the greatest document of her career: the manifest of the departed, revised. The dead on the left page, the living on the right, entered with equal care, equal ink, equal weight of hand. She had promised herself this in the water, treading among debris while the *Meridian*'s fires lit the sky orange behind her: *every name, both columns, no shortcuts.* A quartermaster's job was to know what a vessel carried. This vessel had carried people. Therefore she would account for them.

It was slow work. Some names she knew only by face — the tall loyalist with the burned hands, the young mother who'd sung to her baby through the worst nights of the storm, the old cook whose name Odile realized, shamefully, she had never once recorded in any manifest because cooks never counted themselves. She sent Tobias out with a pencil stub and instructions: "Ask them their names. Ask them how they spell them. Do not guess. Guessing is how people get erased twice."

Tobias, who had lost everything twice already and come up grinning both times, took to the task like a duck to herring. He came back every hour smelling of other people's kitchens, having been fed continuously by grateful survivors who found a nine-year-old boy asking earnest spelling questions irresistible. By evening he could recite forty families' worth of names and had eaten approximately his own body weight in bread.

"Merrick says I should learn shorthand too," he reported, mouth full.

"Merrick should be lying down," Odile said.

"He says lying down is for people who've finished working."

"Merrick," Odile said, "is seventy-eight years old, survived a fire, and has the self-preservation instincts of a moth. Where is he now?"

"Sorting papers."

Of course he was.

* * *

She found him at dusk in the loft above the chandlery, which Marta had surrendered to the archive on the grounds that "old paper can't hurt anything." Merrick sat cross-legged on the floor amid crates of rescued state papers — the archive that Dominic had hidden inside, the archive that Corvane's soldiers had torn apart looking for codes and failed to find, the archive that had been carried out of the flames in fish barrels by volunteers who hadn't asked what it was worth, only that an old man was crying over it.

He looked terrible. His hands were bandaged where the embers had caught him. His cough had developed a wet, worrying note. He was, at that moment, carefully unfolding a water-stained proclamation from some ministry or another and smoothing it flat with a stone, humming.

"You're supposed to be resting," Odile said from the doorway.

"I rested in 1811," Merrick said placidly. "It didn't suit me."

"The doctor—"

"There is no doctor. There is Marta's cousin, who pulls teeth and believes in garlic. Garlic is fine for teeth but does nothing for lungs." He patted the floor beside him. "Sit. Look at this. It's the founding charter of the coastal fisheries board, 1809. Utterly unimportant. Four signatures, two of them misspelled." He smiled, and his eyes were wet again, though he pretended they weren't. "Do you know why I love the unimportant documents, Odile?"

"Because you're a strange man."

"Because the grand ones survive anyway. Charters and treaties — people fight over those, copy them, enshrine them. But who copies a fisheries board minute?" He smoothed the paper once more. "Nobody. So if it burns, it's gone, and with it goes the fact that on a Tuesday in March, four ordinary men argued for three hours about net weights, and then signed their names badly, and then presumably went to lunch. That's a whole world, gone, unless someone writes it down." He looked up at her. "Which is what you're doing downstairs, isn't it?"

Odile sat down on the floor after all, joints complaining, next to the strange man and his crates.

"Both columns," she said. "Dead and living. Equal care."

"Equal care," Merrick repeated softly. "You understand that most historians would call that a waste of ink."

"Historians can drown," Odile said.

They sat together in the lamplight for a while, sorting. It was, Odile thought, the closest thing to prayer she'd managed in years.

* * *

Dominic came to the weigh-house on the third day.

She heard him before she saw him — his knock, always the same, two soft raps, the knock of a man who'd spent two years trying not to be noticed by anyone. She called him in and found him looking worse than Merrick and pretending better, which was the family disease, apparently, and one she'd have to cure them both of eventually.

"How's the manifest?" he asked.

"Two hundred and six living. Ninety-one dead, recovered or witnessed. Fourteen missing, presumed drowned, listed as missing rather than dead because I don't presume anything anymore." She turned the ledger toward him. "Your name's at the top of the living column. Under 'occupation' I wrote 'harbor watch.' I hope that's accurate."

"It will be." He sat down heavily across from her. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the pen scratched, and outside a gull made its opinion known about something. Then Dominic said, "Tobias told me what you wrote for the boy. Under guardian."

Odile kept writing. "Did he."

"He says you put yourself. And me. And Merrick. All three."

"All three," she confirmed. "A boy needs redundancy. Any sailor knows that. One anchor is sentiment; three anchors is seamanship."

Dominic laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first she'd heard from him since the fire, and therefore worth more than the whole fisheries charter. "He asked me if it meant he had three fathers."

"What did you tell him?"

"That he'd have to take it up with the registrar." Dominic reached across and tapped the ledger. "That's you, I believe."

"Then it's official," Odile said. "Congratulations. Terrible news for all involved." And she went back to her lists before he could see her eyes, because she was a professional, and professionals finish the entry first.

* * *

On the fifth day, the town council of Greywater met in the chapel to decide whether the survivors could stay.

Odile attended, because she intended to speak, and because Tobias refused to stay away and someone responsible ought to sit next to him. The chapel held perhaps eighty people, half of them townsfolk, half of them refugees smelling of smoke and borrowed clothes. Marta Skellig presided with the air of a woman who had already decided the outcome but wished to enjoy watching everyone else catch up.

A grain merchant named Pell spoke against them first. "We're a town of four hundred," he said. "Two hundred mouths, no ships, no goods, winter coming on. Charity is a lovely thing until it starves your own children."

There was a rumble of agreement, honest and frightened, and Odile didn't hate him for it. Fear was a cargo too; she'd seen it loaded onto many ships.

Then Merrick rose. He had to lean on a crate to do it, and the chapel went quiet, partly out of respect and partly because he looked like death rehearsing. But his voice carried the way quiet voices sometimes do.

"Master Pell counts mouths," he said. "Let me count differently. In these fish barrels" — he gestured, and two volunteers tipped one open, spilling paper across the chapel floor, decades of rescued state records fanning out like strange pale fish — "is the memory of a republic. Its laws. Its courts. Its census of every soul it ever counted, including, I would wager, several of yours. When the new regime asks Greywater next spring whether it sheltered enemies of the state, and it will ask, you may say no and hope they believe you. Or—" his cough took him, and he waited it out, and went on, quieter, "or you may say yes, and mean it, and become part of something. Every government-in-exile in history started as a room full of hungry people arguing. Look around. You have the room."

Silence. Then, from the back, one of the refugee women — the young mother, the singer — stood up with her baby on her hip and said simply, "My husband was a herring fisherman. I can gut and salt faster than any man here, and I'll prove it tomorrow morning if anyone doubts me."

Someone laughed. Someone else said, "Can she really?" And Marta Skellig, who understood a tide when she saw one, said, "Motion's on the table. All in favor."

It wasn't unanimous. Odile noted that in her own private ledger of things — the vote had been thirty-one to nineteen, with abstentions, and nineteen frightened people were still frightened the next morning and the morning after. That was honest. That was real. Communities weren't rebuilt from unanimity; they were rebuilt from majorities that fed the minorities anyway, because everyone ate at the same tables eventually.

The harbor had taken them in. Now they would have to deserve it, daily, in small ways, forever.

* * *

On the eighth day, Odile finished the manifest.

She read it through twice, both columns, dead and living, ninety-one and two hundred and six, and then she did the thing she had been dreading and needing in equal measure: she walked down to the shore at low tide, where the wreckage of the *Meridian* lay strewn along the shingle like the bones of a whale, and she read the names aloud.

All of them. Both columns. It took two hours. Her voice gave out halfway through the living and she switched to whispering, and the sea hissed and the gulls complained, and a few survivors drifted down to stand at a respectful distance, hats off, listening to their own names and their neighbors'. Nobody spoke during it. At the end, Dominic — who had appeared without her noticing, carrying the harbor-watch lantern, unlit in the daylight — struck a match and lit it anyway, and held it up against the grey afternoon, burning uselessly and perfectly, the way rituals are supposed to burn.

"For the record," Odile said, closing the ledger, her voice ruined, "the vessel *Meridian* is hereby discharged. All souls accounted for."

"Aye," said Dominic softly. "Discharged."

Tobias, standing between them, took each of their hands without asking, and none of them mentioned it, and none of them let go for a while.

* * *

It was later that same evening — soup, bread, the chandlery loft warm with too many bodies — that the letter arrived.

A fishing boat had come in on the evening tide, and one of its crew handed a sealed packet up the line to Marta, who handed it to Odile, who looked at the address and felt the floor shift under her the way it hadn't even during the gale.

It was addressed to *the Keeper of the Light, Greywater Harbor*, in a hand Odile had seen exactly once before, two years ago, on a letter Dominic had shown her and then burned — a quick, slanted, defiant script that crossed its t's like slashes.

Lys Winterbourne's hand. On a letter addressed not to Dominic, but to the keeper of the light. To the office. To the man her brother had chosen to become.

Dominic was across the loft, reading to Tobias by lamplight. Merrick dozed against a crate of charters. Outside, the new lantern in the harbor tower — Greywater's own, ancient and underused, freshly cleaned by hands that needed the work — threw its steady circle across the water.

Odile stood holding the letter for a long moment, feeling its small weight, the weight of small things, which was after all the heaviest weight there was.

Then she crossed the loft, and put it in Dominic's hands, and said, "From the south coast. It's her writing. It's addressed to the keeper of the light — whoever that turns out to be."

The loft went very quiet. Even Tobias stopped breathing.

Dominic turned the letter over once, twice, and did not open it. He looked at Odile, and at Merrick waking, and at the boy leaning against his shoulder, at the whole improbable, improvised, pickled-onion architecture of the family the wreck had built — and Odile saw him make the decision she already knew he'd make, the one he'd been practicing all winter without knowing it.

"Not tonight," he said, and slid it unopened beneath the lamp base, where the light fell on it steadily, patiently, the way good lights do. "Tonight we're all accounted for. Tomorrow—" he exhaled, almost smiling, "tomorrow the keeper of the light will want to read his mail."

Outside, the harbor light turned, and turned, and turned.
