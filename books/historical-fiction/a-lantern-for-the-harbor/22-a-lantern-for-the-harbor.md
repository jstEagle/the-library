## 22. A Lantern for the Harbor

Spring came to the harbor the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and all at once.

One morning in late April, Dominic Winterbourne climbed the ninety-three steps to the lantern room with a bucket of lamp oil and found that the ice had left the moorings overnight. The water lay flat and silver, and along the far quay the fishing boats were already out, their sails catching a wind that smelled of thawed earth instead of frozen salt. He stood at the rail for a long moment with the bucket swinging from his hand, and he did not think about codes, or manifests, or men under parley flags. He thought: *the boats are out.* It was such a small thing. It was everything.

The town was called Greywater, though by spring it was less grey than its name promised. It sat on a shelf of coast two days' sail from where the *Meridian* had gone into the shallows burning, a working harbor with one church, three taverns, and an lighthouse the harbormaster had been begging the council to staff since before the war. They had gotten their keeper at last: a thin man with burn-scars on his forearms who asked for no wages beyond a room, oil, bread, and permission to keep a small library in the base of the tower.

"The library is irregular," the harbormaster had said, squinting at him across the table.

"So am I," Dominic had said, and that had apparently settled the matter.

That had been eight months ago, in the ash-colored autumn when the survivors of the *Meridian* came stumbling up Greywater's ramp like a procession of ghosts who had mislaid their haunting. Now the ghosts had taken up trades. Odile ran the harbormaster's office with a ledger so immaculate that merchants from three ports made the detour just to have their cargo recorded in her handwriting. Merrick—the archivist, the republic's memory, the old man who had once told Dominic over a drowning ship's saloon table that memory must survive even if men do not—had claimed a corner of the tower's ground floor and turned it into something between a schoolroom and a reliquary.

And Tobias—

Dominic grinned to himself as he reached the top of the stairs and set down his bucket. Tobias was currently, judging by the sounds drifting up from below, losing an argument about the Peloponnesian War.

"—but if Athens knew the fleet was doomed, why didn't they just *leave*, Master Merrick? I'd have left."

"Because pride, my boy, is the one cargo no captain can jettison." A pause, the creak of Merrick's chair. "Also because Syracuse controlled the harbor. Does that remind you of anything?"

"A lot of things," said Tobias darkly, and Dominic heard him flop into a chair with the dramatic abandon available only to twelve-year-olds and retired actors.

He lit the lamp—though the sun was high and the flame was more ceremony than necessity; Odile said he kept the light burning in daylight out of stubbornness, and he did not argue—and leaned on the rail of the gallery to look down at the harbor road. There was the bakery. There was the chandler's, whose sign had finally been repainted after Odile had written the owner a letter regarding the spelling of "rigging" that Dominic would have paid money to see. There was the post boat, just rounding the breakwater, white sail fat with wind.

His heart did the thing it always did when the post boat came. It had been doing it for six months, ever since the first letter arrived from the south—from a fishing village called Saltmere, from a woman who signed herself only *L.*, because some hands could not be mistaken even when they tried.

The first letter he had not opened for four days. It had sat on his table, squared to the edge of the wood, while he ate, slept, trimmed the wick, climbed the stairs, and thought about his sister at every hour except the hours he admitted to thinking about her. Lys, who had died twice—once in the official bulletins of the purge, and once again in her own letters, when she confessed what she had done to survive the first death: the names she had given, the friends she had sold, Merrick among them. Merrick, who had known, and stayed silent, and forgiven her before Dominic had managed to forgive himself for loving her anyway.

*Family is not who you save,* Merrick had told him on the last night of the *Meridian*, coughing smoke into his scarf while the deck glowed orange beneath them. *It is who you choose to remain with.* And then, because Merrick could never let a fine sentiment stand uncorrected: *"Remain" being the operative word. Do stop staring at the fire and help me carry these books.*

He had helped him carry the books.

The second letter, the one that had come in March, he had opened on the dock. It was short. It said: *I have a berth now. Not a grand one—a bunk above a smokehouse that smells of herring, but it is mine, and no one asks me questions here, or if they do, I have learned better answers. Corvane's dispatch case made me respectable. I hate that it worked. I read your codes' instructions and then I burned them, as you asked, and I will not pretend that didn't frighten me, holding in my hands the difference between one government and another and choosing to feed it to the stove. You always were braver than I was. You were also always worse at lying. Write to me, Dom. Or don't. But write.*

He had written. Three letters since, each one started seven times. In the last one he had finally told her about Tobias—not the facts, which she might have deduced, but the truth underneath them: *I looked at your letter and at the boy and at the last seat on the skiff, and I chose the boy, and I have never once regretted it, and I do not know what that makes me to you, or you to me, but I know it made me free.* Then, because honesty terrified him less than sentiment: *I keep a light now. It suits me. I was always good at staying in one place and terrible at pretending otherwise.*

She had not yet answered that one.

The post boat tied up. Dominic watched a figure disembark, watched Odile intercept it at the office door with her ledger under one arm like a shield, watched her take a single envelope, examine it front and back as though checking it for contraband—which, given what the last letters had cost them, was not entirely unreasonable—and then look up at the tower.

She raised one hand. Pointed up. Made a gesture that meant either *letter for you* or possibly *your chimney is smoking again,* which was Odile's second-favorite complaint after people who misspelled "rigging."

Dominic went down the ninety-three steps faster than any keeper ought to descend his own stairs.

* * *

The envelope lay on the schoolroom table between the inkwell and a half-eaten pear that Tobias had been saving against all evidence that anyone wanted it. Lys's hand on the address was steady. That steadiness frightened him more than shaking would have.

"You've gone pale," Merrick observed without looking up from his cataloguing. The old man's health had steadied through the winter—Odile's cooking and Tobias's bossing having accomplished what the ship's doctor had failed to—but his eyes were still the eyes of a man who measured everything against loss. "Sit down before you fall down. Tobias, fetch him water, and not from the rain barrel, we've discussed this."

"I *wasn't* going to use the rain barrel." Tobias fetched the water anyway, set it down with unnecessary force, and then planted himself directly in front of Dominic with his arms crossed, which was his way of saying *I'm staying and you can't make me leave and also I love you.* "Is it from her?"

"It's from her."

"Open it."

"He'll open it when he's ready," said Merrick mildly, turning a page. "Though I confess the suspense is aging me considerably, and I haven't the years to spare."

Dominic laughed—short, surprised out of him—and picked up the letter. His thumb slid under the flap. Stopped.

"Master Merrick," he said, "when you wrote to your wife, in the old days. Before you sealed the letter. Was there ever a moment when you almost didn't send it?"

Merrick set down his pen. For a moment he was quiet, and Dominic saw the republic pass behind his eyes—all of it, the halls and the fires and the forty years of papers carried out in wheelbarrows and held in one stubborn skull.

"Every time," Merrick said. "And every time I sent it anyway. Because a letter unsent is just a stone you carry, and a letter sent, however it lands—" he shrugged, a small motion that contained multitudes, "—is a bridge. Bridges are how families survive distances, Dominic. Believe me. I have been a family of one for too long to confuse solitude with peace."

Tobias, who understood more than he let on and let on more than he understood, reached over and tapped the envelope once, briskly, like a surgeon. "Bridge," he said.

So Dominic opened it.

*Dom—*

*I burned your last letter too. Not because I had to. Because it was true and true things burn hotter and I needed the warmth. This is what our family does now, apparently: we commit arson against correspondence and call it healing.*

*You asked what it makes us. Here is my answer, and I have rewritten it nine times, which you may verify by the state of this page:*

*It makes us people who failed each other in the ways that matter most, and loved each other anyway, which I have decided—for today, at least—is simply what people are. You gave my berth away and sent me a future instead of yourself, and I was angry for a month, and then I stood on the smokehouse steps watching the boats come home through fog, and I understood that you had done the only honest thing either of us has ever done. You stayed with what you chose. I am trying, slowly, badly, to do the same.*

*I am coming north in the summer. Not to stay—yet. To see whether the man who keeps the light can bear the woman who dimmed it. If he cannot, I will take the herring-smell bunk back and we will write each other true things and burn them until we are old. If he can—*

*Well. Tell the boy I remember him from Corvane's descriptions, and that I am bringing him something impractical. Tell the old man—I know he reads over your shoulder, he always did—that the debt is mine and I intend to pay it in visits. And tell yourself, from me, since you will never say it: well done, little brother. Well done.*

*Lys*

*P.S. The codes are safe. What remains of them is in one head in your tower and one head in mine, and neither of ours will talk, even under torture, because we Winterbournes are famously stubborn and only moderately famous.*

Dominic read it twice. On the second reading his vision blurred in a manner he attributed to lamp smoke, though the lamp was unlit and the window open to clean spring air.

"Well?" demanded Tobias.

"She's coming in the summer," Dominic said. His voice cracked on *summer* and he let it. "And she's bringing you something impractical."

Tobias's whole face ignited. "What kind of impractical?"

"The kind she gives. Last time it was a knife engraved with a proverb she'd misspelled."

"That's the *best kind* of impractical," said Tobias, with the authority of a boy who owned exactly one knife, engraved, misspelled, and treasured past all reason.

Merrick removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve longer than any spectacles required, and put them back on. "Summer," he said. "Good. That gives us until June to finish the Syracuse material. She'll want to test him on it; she always tested everyone. I should prepare a reading list." He paused. "And Dominic—tell her the archive survived. All of it. She'll want to know that the thing she nearly destroyed is the thing that saved the rest."

"She knows," Dominic said. "I told her about the cipher key. About how it's in your head."

"Ah." Merrick smiled, dry as old paper. "Then tell her the head in question is holding up admirably, thank you, despite the company it keeps."

* * *

That evening, Dominic climbed the tower at dusk with the ritual of it settling over him like a coat cut to his measure. Fill the reservoir. Trim the wick. Check the glass—clean it where gulls had left their opinions. Wind the mechanism that turned the great lens, so the beam swept the water in its slow, patient arc: *here, here, here, all is well, come home.*

From the gallery he could see the whole town going gold and blue with evening. Odile's office window, lamplit, her silhouette bent over the day's tallies. The bakery shuttering. Tobias on the breakwater with two other children, teaching them knots—or teaching them something; from this distance the curriculum was unclear, but there was a great deal of rope involved and occasional shouting, so probably knots. And at the base of the tower, warm light in the round window: Merrick's lamp, still burning over his books, because the old man claimed lamplight improved his memory, and because no one had the heart to point out that memory did not work that way, and because secretly they all suspected he was right.

The first night aboard the *Meridian*, hiding his satchel among tar and rope, Dominic had believed that survival meant disappearance—to be useful, unnoticed, anonymous as a deckhand's hands. He had spent a year learning how wrong he was. Survival, it turned out, meant the opposite: it meant standing somewhere visible, with a flame in your window and your name spoken aloud by people who expected an answer, and answering.

*Winterbourne,* Odile called it up the stairwell most mornings, in her ledger-keeper's voice, as though confirming receipt of him. *Present and accounted for?*

*Accounted for,* he'd answer. Every morning. Both of them knowing it was a joke and both of them knowing it was not a joke at all.

The sun went down. He lit the great lamp and watched the beam swing out over the water, touching the moored boats, the breakwater, the darkening hills, and then the open sea where—two hundred miles south, past the shoals and the closed ports and the country that had broken—a fishing village called Saltmere slept above its smokehouses, and a woman with their mother's hands was mending nets or writing letters or simply sitting on her steps, watching her own water.

*Come summer,* he thought at the horizon. *Come whenever you're ready. The light will be here.*

Below, faintly, Tobias's voice floated up the stairwell—he was supposed to be asleep and was audibly not—asking Merrick one last question about Athens and her doomed fleet. And Merrick's answer, unhurried, warm, worn smooth by telling:

"No, no—the lesson isn't that they lost, boy. The lesson is that they wrote it all down afterward, and so in every way that lasts forever, they won."

Dominic smiled, wound the mechanism, and settled into the keeper's chair with his coat around his shoulders, keeping the light.

Outside, the beam swept the water. *Here. Here. Here. All is well.*

Come home.
