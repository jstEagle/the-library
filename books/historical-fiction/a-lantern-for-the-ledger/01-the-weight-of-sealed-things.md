## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The rain had been falling on Callowmere for eleven days without conviction—too steady to be a storm, too indifferent to be a blessing—and by Thursday morning the river had climbed the quay steps and taken possession of Fishmonger's Row. Noor Eastwick waded through it to her ankles, boots in one hand, dispatch case strapped flat against her ribs beneath a coat cut deliberately dull: grey wool, mended twice at the elbow, the kind of coat that made eyes slide off it and continue toward something more interesting.

That was the whole art of her trade. Not speed, not cleverness, not the romance of hidden compartments that the penny dreadfuls sold in the capital. The art was being forgettable. A woman of no particular age carrying nothing worth stealing through streets where nobody looked twice. She had learned it from her mother in a dozen small lessons delivered over a kitchen table scoured white with use: *Wear the color of weather. Walk at the pace of errands. If someone asks what you're carrying, let them see your hands are empty before they finish the question.*

Carry nothing that can't be dropped. Remember nothing you can deny. Her mother had said it like a grace before meals, so often that Noor had stopped hearing it as wisdom and started hearing it as the sound of their household, the same way she heard the river and the gulls and the creak of the ferry rope. It was only now, with the water climbing and the war somewhere south doing whatever wars did to towns that weren't yours yet, that she understood the creed had been built for exactly this weather.

The dispatch sat against her ribs like a second heart. Oilskin-wrapped, wax-sealed, no larger than a prayer book, and inside it—she did not know. That was the discipline beneath the discipline. She carried codes between ministries, and a code was not a message; it was a message wearing armor, and you did not ask armor what it was protecting. She knew the cipher key by heart, every substitution table her mother had drilled into her with sugared almonds as rewards, but knowing how to open a letter was different from opening it, and Noor had spent nine years in the ministry's service never once reading what she carried. Curiosity was a weight too. She left it home with everything else.

The flooded row smelled of river mud and fish rot and the sweetish staleness of wet plaster. Old Marta Voss had dragged her tables onto the higher ground by the cooperage and was selling from them with the grim cheer of a woman who had survived three floods and intended to outlast this one out of spite. A boy punted a washtub down the middle of the street where the cobblestones should have been, poling along with a broom handle, and two old men watched him from a doorstep with the particular satisfaction of people finding entertainment free of charge.

"Morning, Eastwick," Marta called, because everyone in Callowmere knew everyone, which was precisely why Noor kept her face arranged into pleasant blankness. The courier's name opened no doors and closed none. It meant the daughter of Meret Eastwick, who had kept the census ledgers clean and the widow's pensions paid on time; it meant a quiet girl grown into a quieter woman who worked clerical hours at the provincial archive and brought her landlady salted herring at Lent. Nothing more. She had spent years sanding herself smooth, and the work had been good, and some mornings—most mornings—the smoothness felt like peace.

"Water's at the third step of the customs house," Marta said. "My late husband always said when the river takes the customs house steps, sell everything and pray."

"Did he ever follow his own advice?"

"He died rich in furniture and poor in sense." Marta waved her on. "Tell your registrar to move his papers up. Water doesn't respect archives."

Noor smiled the smile she kept for neighbors and went on. But the words snagged in her like a fishhook in wool. Aldous Fen and his cellar of paper, four hundred years of Callowmere's births and debts and quarrels shelved below the waterline of the town hall because Fen refused—"on principle," he said, though everyone suspected sentiment—to abandon the old registry room where his father had worked before him. She would remind him. She would remind him tomorrow, or the day after, when the dispatch was delivered and her hands were empty again.

*Tomorrow.* The word sat wrong in her mouth. For nine years the war had been a rumor with a southern accent, arriving as freight delays and price notices and, lately, young men from the ferry district leaving without saying goodbye properly. Tomorrow had always held. This morning, crossing the flooded row, Noor felt the first hairline crack in that certainty—not fear exactly, but a change in pressure, the way animals go quiet before weather turns. The army columns were moving up the river road. Everyone said so. Everyone had been saying so since spring, and spring had come and gone, and still the town had gone about its business with the invincible stupidity of places that had never burned.

She turned off the row at Tanner's Lane, wading now past her knees, and took the long way east. Long ways were part of the craft. A direct route was a habit, and habits could be studied; a courier who varied her path was a courier who arrived. So she went by the cooperage yard, past the chapel whose bell had been removed for munitions two winters ago—the empty arch above it looking oddly like a person with an opinion withheld—and came out behind the chandler's shop, where the floodwater ran clearer and the sound of the town changed. Here she could hear the river itself, working under the surface, patient as bookkeeping.

The dispatch case pressed warm against her side. She thought, not for the first time, about how strange it was that the most dangerous thing in Callowmere this morning was riding quietly against the ribs of the least remarkable woman in it. Somewhere north, ministries were waiting on these sealed lines the way feverish men waited on a physician. Grain requisitions, she guessed. Troop movements. The usual arithmetic of a republic counting its remaining days. She would deliver it to the relay office at Harrow Bend by nightfall, take her receipt, eat a bowl of barley soup, and walk back tomorrow with empty hands and an untroubled mind. Nine years of tomorrows just like it. The routine was the armor's other layer: no single delivery mattered enough to kill for, because there would always be another, and another, and the war could not stop a thing that moved like rainwater, everywhere a little, nowhere all at once.

At the corner by the rope walk she passed the proclamation board, freshly nailed up by order of the Provincial Assembly, its paper already curling at the edges from the damp. *CITIZENS ARE REMINDED THAT THE MOVEMENT OF UNREGISTERED CORRESPONDENCE IS AN OFFENSE AGAINST THE SECURITY OF THE STATE.* Below it, smaller, almost apologetic: *Registered couriers exempt upon presentation of credentials.*

Her credentials lived in a hollowed heel of her left boot, folded to the size of a communion wafer. They named her as a clerk of the Ministry of Ways and Means, third grade, authorized to convey administrative correspondence. The lie was official, laminated in wax and ribbon, and it was also true in the way that mattered: she really was a clerk, third grade, and she really did carry administrative correspondence, if you stretched the word *administrative* until it groaned. The ministry had understood, decades ago, that the safest cover story is one that costs nothing to maintain. Give the courier a real job. Let her do it sometimes. Truth was cheaper than invention.

She checked the board anyway—it was reflex, checking things—and noticed what she had half-expected and fully dreaded: a newer notice beneath the older one, pasted up within the last few days, its ink still sharp. *BY ORDER OF THE NINTH COLUMN, ALL RIVER TRAFFIC NORTH OF CALLOWMERE IS SUBJECT TO INSPECTION AND TOLL.* No signature beyond a stamped eagle-and-sword device she didn't recognize, and a date: three days hence.

Three days. Her dispatch was due at Harrow Bend tonight.

Noor stood in the flooded lane with rain running off her hood and let herself feel, for exactly four seconds, the cold weight of what she was carrying. Then she folded the feeling away the way she'd been taught—smallly, neatly, into a compartment that did not exist—and walked on. Four seconds was the allowance. Her mother had been specific about that too, though she'd never explained the number. *Feel it while you count to four,* she'd say, tapping the table. *Then it goes in the box. A courier who carries her fear alongside her cargo arrives heavy, and heavy women get searched.*

* * *

The relay office at Harrow Bend occupied the ground floor of a former granary, and the relay master, a widower named Corben with ink-stained cuffs and a hearing aid he pretended not to need, received Noor's dispatch with the ceremony of a man handing over a sleeping infant. Wax inspected, seal matched against his reference book, receipt written in triplicate. He squinted at her over the counter.

"You came the low road," he said. "Ferry district's flooded clear through."

"The low road was fine."

"Wasn't asking after your comfort." He lowered his voice, which for Corben meant speaking at ordinary volume. "There's soldiers at Wethering Ford already. Ninth Column advance party. Stopped the grain barges Tuesday. My runner came back saying they've got a list."

"A list of what?"

"Of things." Corben's mouth did something complicated. "Barges. Horses. Men of fighting age." He wrote the last receipt copy with unnecessary care, as if the pen itself might be conscripted. "They asked my runner whether Callowmere keeps a provincial ledger."

The words landed in Noor's chest like a stone dropped down a well—she heard them fall a long way before anything came back up. "Every province keeps a ledger," she said. "It's required by statute."

"I know what's required by statute. I've worked for this republic thirty-one years." He slid the receipts across. "I'm telling you what the soldiers asked. There's a difference, these days, between what's required and what's wanted, and I don't think the difference favors us."

Noor took the receipts and thanked him and said something mild about the weather, and walked back out into the rain with her hands empty and her mind anything but. The provincial ledger. Of course they wanted the provincial ledger; any competent quartermaster would want it—a census, a tax roll, a debt register, the complete inventory of a province rendered unto paper. It was sensible. It was administrative. It was exactly the kind of document Noor herself had spent her career treating as furniture.

So why did the well in her chest keep echoing?

Because she had helped copy it. Because twice a month for six years she had sat in the archive cellar with Aldous Fen, transcribing entries in her small even hand while Fen dictated and grumbled and drank tea that tasted of dust, and because three weeks ago, copying the autumn revisions, her eye had caught a column she'd never noticed before—a narrow margin column, coded, filled with the ministry shorthand she'd known since childhood. She had asked Fen what it denoted. He had looked at her for a long moment with his watery old eyes and said, *Resettlement,* in the voice men use for words they've decided not to think about, and had changed the subject to the state of the roof.

Resettlement. Beside forty names, give or take. She hadn't read further. That was the discipline; that was the box; she had put it away in four seconds and told herself it was agricultural relocation, labor levies, the ordinary shuffling of a republic rearranging its people the way a housekeeper rearranges furniture. Plenty of provinces had resettlement programs. It was administrative. It was furniture.

Walking home along the high embankment with the flooded fields spread silver to either side, Noor counted to four, and then to forty, and found the feeling would not go into the box. It stayed above her sternum like swallowed smoke. And beneath it, unwelcome as a stranger at a funeral, rose a memory she had not consulted in twenty years: a kitchen table, sugared almonds, her mother's hand moving over a slate. *Now the reverse table, little bird. If you can write the secret, you must be able to unsay it. A code you can't undo isn't a code, it's a grave.*

Meret Eastwick had designed marginal notation systems for three ministries. Everyone in the family had known it the way you know a parent has a job—vaguely, proudly, without examining it. *Your mother makes secrets legible to the right eyes,* her father used to joke, and Meret would say, *I make them illegible to the wrong ones, which is the better half of the work,* and the conversation would move on to supper. Noor had memorized the shorthand as a game, the way other children memorized songs. She had never once wondered, until this gray afternoon on the embankment, whose names her mother's elegant substitutions had been wrapped around.

She reached Callowmere as the light failed, the town lamps coming up one by one through the rain like cautious thoughts. From the embankment she could see the whole sweep of it: the quay, the bridge, the town hall with its slate roof dark against the sky, the archive cellar underneath holding four hundred years of Callowmere in paper and one coded column holding—something. Forty names, give or take. Her mother's name was not among them; she was dead six years and the ledger did not trouble itself with the dead.

But Sef Oduya's daughter was alive, exiled upriver these eight years. Old Marta Voss. The cooper's sons. Half the ferry district. Names, names—she caught herself cataloguing her neighbors against a column she couldn't read, and stopped, appalled, standing in the rain like a woman caught eavesdropping on her own town.

Four seconds. Into the box. She went home, dried her boots, ate bread and cheese standing at the window, and told herself the familiar litany: deliver, return, repeat. The system works. The system has always worked. You are a clerk of the Ministry of Ways and Means, third grade, and tomorrow you will remind Aldous Fen to move his papers above the waterline, and nothing in your life needs to change.

But that night she dreamed of her mother's kitchen, and the sugared almonds had turned to grains of rice, and each grain bore a name too small to read, and her mother was saying, patiently, the way she said everything: *A code you can't undo isn't a code, little bird. Go and look at what we wrote.*

She woke before dawn to a sound she had never heard in Callowmere before: boots. Many boots, in step, coming up the river road through the rain.
