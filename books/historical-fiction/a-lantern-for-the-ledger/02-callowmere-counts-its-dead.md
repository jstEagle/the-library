## 2. Callowmere Counts Its Dead

The archive cellar of Callowmere smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the particular sweetness of old paper—the smell, Noor Eastwick had long ago decided, of a town telling itself the truth. Above them, through three floors of the town hall and one of slate roof, the autumn rain went on with its small industrious sound, like a clerk adding columns that would never balance.

She had been in the cellar since before the lamps were lit, and her hands had gone stiff at the knuckles. She flexed them now, one at a time, over the copybook in front of her, where her figures marched down the page in her smallest, most forgettable hand. That was the whole art of her trade, if it could be called a trade: a hand so plain no one looked twice at it, a face arranged to be passed over, a memory trained to hold only what could be denied. *Carry nothing that can't be dropped,* her mother had told her, though not in those words, not exactly—her mother had taught by games, and the games had rules, and the rules had become the shape of Noor's life without her ever agreeing to it.

"Eastwick." Aldous Fen's voice came from the far end of the long table, dry as the dust he refused to have swept. "You've stopped writing."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're twenty-six years old. Rest your eyes when you're dead, as I intend to." He did not look up. He was bent over the provincial ledger itself, the great book that lay open on its lectern like something dissected, his magnifying glass in one hand, his copying pen in the other, working with the terrible patience of a man who knew that whatever was written twice could survive once. "Column nine. You've transposed the Vessin Street households again."

"No, I haven't."

He turned the copybook toward her with two fingers, the gesture of a magistrate presenting evidence. There it was: the Vessin Street entries, four houses out of order, harmless-looking, wrong.

Noor felt the small cold drop in her stomach that she always felt when she was caught, even in something this small, even by someone this harmless. She corrected the order without a word. Fen watched her do it, satisfied, then returned to his own labor.

That was the arrangement they had fallen into these past eleven days. Registrar Fen had decided, in the way of old men who read weather in their knees and history in their ledgers, that the province was about to come apart, and that Callowmere's book—the census, the tax rolls, the debts owed and forgiven, the births and deaths and marriages of some forty thousand souls from the river mouth to the marsh edge—ought to exist in more than one copy. The ministry in the capital wanted the ledger conveyed north for "central administration." Fen had been stalling the conveyance for weeks with letters full of respectful nonsense. In the meantime, he copied. And because a registrar past seventy could not copy forty thousand names alone, and because Noor Eastwick was the only person in town who could write in a hand small enough to fit six lines into an inch and remember what she'd written afterward, she came down into the cellar every evening after her courier rounds and wrote until the candles failed.

It was, she told herself, ordinary work. It was the kind of work that existed precisely so the world could go on pretending to be orderly while everything under it shifted. She was good at pretending. She had built her whole self out of pretending.

* * *

The cellar was long and low, its vaulted ceiling stained black with the damp of a hundred years, and along its walls stood the lesser books: the parish registers, the ferry tolls going back to the days when Callowmere had paid its taxes in eels, the debt rolls with their red annotations fading to rust. The provincial ledger sat alone at the center, on the iron lectern, chained at one corner—not locked, nothing so dramatic, merely chained, the way a cathedral dog is chained: as a statement of principle.

Fen worked from the front of the book forward; Noor worked from the back. This was practical—he needed the early census pages for his cross-references, and she needed only the current year's supplements, the marginalia, the recent annotations. It meant she spent her evenings in the last quarter of the book, among the fresh ink, where the republic had lately been writing its most private thoughts about the people of Callowmere.

She tried not to read what she copied. That was the discipline. A courier who reads becomes a witness, and a witness can be made to testify, and a person who can be made to testify is a person with weight—weight enough to drown with. Her creed ran through her head each evening as she uncapped the ink: *carry nothing that can't be dropped, remember nothing you can deny.* She carried sealed dispatches between ministries for wages too small to attract envy and too regular to attract attention. She was the daughter of a census clerk, a thin quiet woman with ink-stained cuffs, useful the way a stairwell is useful. Nobody remembered couriers. That was the point of couriers.

But the ledger's margins had begun to speak a language she couldn't entirely refuse to hear.

The annotations appeared beside certain household entries—a small circled figure in the left margin, sometimes a letter beneath it, sometimes a date rendered in the ministry's compressed notation. They had multiplied over the past two years, first sparsely, then in runs, then in whole streets. Fen had noticed them too. He had asked, once, at the start, in his dry way: "Do you know what the circled figures denote?" And Noor had said, "I copy them, I don't interpret them," which was true, and was also a lie, because she did know—not all of it, but enough. Ministry shorthand. Her mother's shorthand. The game-language of her childhood.

She copied a circled 3 beside the Odum family on Tanner's Row, all five of them, and did not think about what three might mean. She copied a circled 1 beside Widow Carrick, who sold eel pies on the quay and had once given Noor a free one out of pity for her mourning clothes, and did not think about what one might mean. She copied dates. She copied letters. She kept her hand moving, because a moving hand could not tremble, or could tremble without being seen.

The rain thickened overhead. Somewhere above them a door slammed, and footsteps crossed the floor, and faded. The town hall at night was mostly empty now; the clerks had stopped lingering since the news from Harrow Ford, since the Ninth Column had turned south off the high road. Everyone in Callowmere said *if they come here* in the tone of people who know perfectly well they are lying.

"Read me back page sixty-one," Fen said, around midnight, without raising his head.

Noor read him back page sixty-one: the fishing licenses of the lower weir, thirty-two names, thirty-two fees paid, two arrears noted. Her voice in the cellar sounded strange to her, flattened by stone. When she finished, silence took the room back, and in the silence she heard the thing she had been refusing to hear for eleven nights: the scratch of Fen's pen, the tick of the cooling stove, and underneath both, faint and steady, her own heart, keeping time like something counting down.

* * *

It happened on the twelfth night, and it happened the way these things happen—stupidly, sideways, in the middle of the most boring page in the book.

She had turned to the current-year supplement for the river districts, the roll of households assessed on the autumn survey, and she was copying the header—*District IV, Callowmere Quay and Marsh Approach, surveyed by*—and the surveyor's name, and the date, and then the first entry, and the second, and her pen was moving in its groove, her mind somewhere else entirely, up on the wet streets, planning tomorrow's delivery route, when the name arrived in front of her eyes.

**EASTWICK, MERET.** House on Rope Walk. Occupation: recording clerk (ret.). Household: one daughter. Assessment: standard.

And in the left margin, in fresh violet ink, a circled figure.

Noor did not move. She was aware, distantly, of Fen's pen scratching away at the front of the book, of the rain, of the candle burning straight and tall beside her elbow, of her own breath going shallow and then stopping altogether, and none of it seemed connected to her. The cellar might have emptied. The world might have folded up like a map. There was only the entry, and her mother's name sitting inside it the way a stone sits inside a shoe—small, wrong, impossible to stop feeling.

Her mother had been dead fourteen months. Buried in the churchyard above the tideline, under a stone Noor had chosen herself and paid for out of courier's wages, a modest stone with modest letters. Dead women did not appear on current-year surveys. Dead women did not get assessments marked *standard.* Somebody had entered Meret Eastwick into the autumn roll as though she still hung her coat behind the door on Rope Walk, and somebody else had come along after with violet ink and put a circled number beside her, and the number was—

She made herself look at it. She made herself, because the whole discipline of her life was that you looked at things; you did not look away, because looking away was how names got lost.

A circled 2.

Beside living households on the same street, the circles were 3s and 4s. Beside the Odums, a 3. Beside the Carrick woman, a 1. Beside most of Rope Walk, nothing at all. The numbers meant categories—that much she knew, had known since she was nine years old and thought the whole business was a game of codes and hidden treasure. Categories of what, the game had never said. Games didn't need to say. Grown governments did, and grown governments had stopped saying anything aloud two years ago, when the word *resettlement* had first appeared in a ministerial circular and everyone in every office had agreed, without anyone proposing it, to keep saying it in exactly that flat administrative voice, forever, and never once ask it a question.

*Resettlement.* Her mother's name, annotated for resettlement. Fourteen months in the churchyard above the tideline.

Noor found that her pen had stopped, and that a blot was spreading under its nib, a slow black bloom across the assessment column, drowning the word *standard.*

"Eastwick." Fen's voice, closer now. "Your ink."

"I know."

"You've blotted the—"

"I know." She lifted the pen. The blot had eaten the assessment line and part of the margin. Part of the circle. She should have been horrified—blotting a provincial record was the sort of sin Fen flayed apprentices for—but what she felt instead was a wild, ungrateful flicker of relief, as if the ink had done her mother a kindness, had covered the mark the way you'd throw a coat over a caged bird.

Fen came down the length of the table, moving slowly, one hand trailing the wood. He was a tall man gone stooped, with a face like a folded document and eyes that had spent seventy years learning to see what pages actually said rather than what they claimed. He looked at the blot. Then he looked at the entry above it, and Noor watched him read her mother's name, and watch himself decide whether to say anything about it.

"The blot," he said finally, "can be initialed and explained. Blots happen. Records survive blots." He paused. "Whose household is that?"

"You know whose household that is."

"I know whose house it is," Fen said. "I don't know whose household it is. Your mother has been dead—" he did the arithmetic without hesitation, because he was Fen, "—fourteen months and eleven days. She cannot be resettled. She cannot be categorized. Someone has entered her anyway." He drew off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a ritual Noor recognized as the man's way of buying time to feel things he considered inefficient. "Do you know what the circled figures denote?"

"I copy them," Noor said. "I don't interpret them."

"That was your answer in September."

"It's my answer now."

Fen put his spectacles back on. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence, patient, professional, like a third clerk in the room. Then he said, quietly, in a voice she had never heard him use—not dry now, not magisterial, just old and tired and honest:

"When I was a young man there was a purge in the western provinces. Before your birth. The rolls went north with violet marks in the margins, and the following spring the western parishes reported their populations reduced, and the ministry's summary used the phrase *administrative attrition.* Forty thousand people, give or take, filed under a phrase. I was a junior clerk in the capital then. I helped bind the summaries." He set one liver-spotted hand flat on the ledger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you suspected might wake angry. "I have spent fifty years telling myself the binding was innocent. Paper is innocent. Hands are innocent. It is a very comfortable doctrine, Miss Eastwick, and I recommend it to you, because I notice it isn't holding for either of us tonight."

Noor looked down at the page. At the blot, at the name, at the circle with its violet ring, neat as a wedding band.

"If they come here," she said, "they'll requisition this book."

"They will requisition this book."

"And the copies?"

"What copies?" said Aldous Fen.

She looked at him. He looked back with the flat gray patience of a man who had spent half a century committing small treasons against entropy and saw no reason to announce the latest one. Somewhere in the cellar's shadowed aisles, behind the lesser registers and the rolled maps and the boxes of eel-toll receipts, there was—she understood suddenly, with the click of a lock turning—a second copybook already underway, or a plan for one, hidden in the mind of the most law-abiding man in Callowmere.

"The conveyance order comes within the fortnight," Fen went on, resuming his seat, taking up his pen, becoming again the dry machine of accuracy. "Colonel somebody, Ninth Column, they'll want the original escorted north under guard. I will delay it with correspondence. Correspondence is my sword arm. But I am seventy-three years old, and my delays are mortal." The pen touched paper. "So we copy faster, Miss Eastwick. All of it. Even the margins."

"Especially the margins?" she said, and hated how carefully light her voice came out.

"Especially what the margins prove," Fen said. "Whatever it proves."

* * *

She walked home after two in the morning, through streets drowned to the kerbstones, carrying nothing that couldn't be dropped, remembering nothing she could deny—and failing, failing, the creed coming apart in her hands like wet paper. Rain needled the surface of the flooded lanes. A lantern burned in the ferry house where Sef Oduya lived; she saw his shadow pass the window, restless, and did not stop. On Rope Walk, her mother's house stood dark, its shutters tight, exactly as it had stood for fourteen months, exactly as if someone still expected to be counted there.

Noor let herself in, lit no lamp, sat down at the kitchen table in the dark, and put her hand flat on the wood where her mother's hand had worn the varnish pale.

*A circled 2.* She could hear the childhood lesson as clearly as if the chair opposite held a living teacher: *the margin speaks to the margin, little bird, and the body of the book never knows.* Nine years old, laughing, tracing violet circles on practice sheets, believing the circles were treasure maps.

They were treasure maps. The treasure was her mother's name, and the government had drawn a ring around it, and the ring was traveling north in a book that weighed eleven pounds and contained forty thousand souls.

Tomorrow she would deliver a sealed dispatch to the harbor office. Tomorrow evening she would descend into the cellar and copy, faster, all of it, especially the margins. And somewhere south, on the high road, with the mud of Harrow Ford not yet dried on its wheels, the Ninth Column was coming to collect the original—and Noor Eastwick understood, sitting in her mother's dark kitchen with the floodwater lapping at the doorsill of the world, that the moment it did, the only proof that any of these people had ever lived would be the copy in her own forgettable hand.

She heard the boots before she saw anything: not one pair, many, out on the quay road, moving at march cadence through the rain, and under them the groan of cartwheels heavy with something iron.

They had said a fortnight. They had said, at the very least, a week of correspondence first.

Noor stood up in the dark and blew out the candle she had never lit.
