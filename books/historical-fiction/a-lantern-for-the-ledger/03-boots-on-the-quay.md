## 3. Boots on the Quay

They came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were market days, ledger days, days of small arithmetic—the day Noor Eastwick had always trusted most, because nothing that happened on a Tuesday was meant to be remembered. Wars arrived on Fridays in the ballads. Invasions came at dawn with horns and burning granaries. Nobody had warned her that an army could simply appear between the fish stalls and the chandler's shop, quiet as weather.

She heard them before she saw them: not drums, not trumpets, but boots. Hundreds of pairs, striking the wet cobbles of the quay road in unison, a sound like a loom weaving something enormous and gray. The morning fog off the Vessel still hung in ropes between the warehouses, and out of it came the Ninth Column—first the scouts on lean horses, then the infantry in column four abreast, their packs creaking, their breath steaming, faces smudged and indifferent as men arriving to inspect a drainage works.

Noor stood beneath the awning of Verlaine's dried goods with a basket she no longer remembered carrying. Around her, the market performed its slow paralysis. A woman held an apple halfway to her mouth and forgot about it. Old Piette's scales swung empty on their chains, ticking against one another like nervous teeth. Someone's dog began to bark and was silenced by a look from a sergeant whose face suggested the dog had made an administrative error.

*Column,* she thought. *Ninth.* She ran through what the dispatches had taught her without meaning to teach her anything: Ninth Column, riverine deployment, attached quartermaster corps, a reputation for doing things by the book because the book was the only thing they believed in. Not butchers, then. Worse, in its way. Butchers wanted fear. These men wanted *records.*

At the head of the column rode a single officer on a chestnut mare too fine for the mud of Callowmere. He sat very straight, gloved hands folded on the pommel, and he did not look at the town so much as *over* it, the way a man looks over a table before laying out his papers. He wore the slate-gray of the Column with a colonel's bars dulled deliberately, so they caught no light. Later she would learn his name was Brase. That morning he was simply the shape at the front of the thing that had come to count them.

The column halted at the quay gate without being ordered to halt—it simply stopped, the way water stops at a sill—and the fog seemed to accept them, folding around their ranks until Callowmere looked like a town remembering itself wrong.

* * *

By noon there were soldiers on the ferry landings and chalk marks on every door of consequence. Noor watched from the archive steps, where Aldous Fen stood beside her with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man auditing heaven and finding discrepancies.

"They requisitioned the ferries," Fen said. "Both of them. Sef Oduya was told to report with his boat by evening bell or be charged with obstructing military transport." He said it flatly, but Noor saw how his thumb pressed white against his sleeve. "Obstructing. As if the river were theirs."

"The river is theirs now," Noor said. "That's rather the point."

Below them, the town hall square had become a surveyor's dream. Soldiers moved through it with the unhurried competence of men who had done this forty times: measuring, marking, nailing placards to the proclamation boards that had, for thirty years, carried nothing more alarming than tide tables and the price of lamp oil. A lieutenant with a clipboard walked the perimeter of the town hall itself, tapping walls, counting windows. Two clerks had already established themselves at the entrance with a folding table, a ledger of their own, and an inkwell weighted against the wind.

"Requisition," Fen read aloud from one of the fresh placards, his voice dry as old vellum. "*In accordance with Article Fourteen of the Provisional Governance Statute, the following properties of the township of Callowmere are hereby placed under the administration of the Ninth Column:*" He stopped reading and gestured instead, because the list was everywhere around them—the town hall, the granary annex, the weighbridge, the ferry rights, the archive.

"The archive," Noor said quietly.

"Every registry, census roll, debt schedule, and provincial record," Fen confirmed. "Which is to say, everything this town has ever admitted to being."

A soldier drove the first nail into the archive door frame and hung a board over the lintel where, for as long as Noor had been alive, a carved lantern had marked the entrance. The board was painted slate-gray, and the stencil on it read: **PROPERTY OF THE NINTH COLUMN — ACCESS BY WRITTEN AUTHORIZATION ONLY**. The hammer blows rang down the street like a bell rung for a funeral nobody had scheduled.

Noor felt it in her sternum first—a physical sensation, vertigo tilted sideways, the ground keeping its place while everything built upon it slid. It was not fear exactly. Fear had a direction; this had none. It was the feeling of watching your own house inventoried by strangers who handled your mother's teacups to assess their weight.

Because that was what they were doing. That was precisely, methodically, what they were doing. A corporal emerged from the town hall carrying the tax rolls bundled in their oilcloth, and he weighed the bundle in his hand—not reading it, just weighing it, the way you'd weigh a ham—and called out a number to the clerk at the table, who wrote the number down, and just like that, sixty years of Callowmere's taxes became a figure in someone else's arithmetic.

"They'll want the ledger next," Fen said. "The provincial ledger. It's the spine of the collection. Census, debts, annotations—" He stopped. His jaw worked once. "Everything is in it."

"I know what's in it," Noor said.

And she thought of the cellar, three nights past, lamplight and copying ink, her own hand cramping over duplicates while Fen dictated page numbers. She thought of a name in a margin column she had not let herself read again since. Her mother's name, printed in the registrar's patient hand, followed by a notation in cipher that she had understood instantly and wished she never had.

*Resettlement.* A word that traveled with soldiers now, the way lice traveled with blankets.

Down in the square, Colonel Brase dismounted. He did it gracefully, handing the reins to an orderly, and stood for a moment simply looking at the town hall—her town hall, where she'd been vaccinated, where the winter relief distributions happened, where the mayor's clerk kept a tin of sweets for children dragged in to register births. Brase looked at it the way a buyer looks at livestock. Then he said something to the lieutenant, who bowed and went inside, and Noor understood that the transaction was already complete; the looking was only ceremony.

* * *

They came for the ledger at dusk.

Noor was still in the archive—she had told herself she was cataloguing, which was even true, in the way that a drowning man is technically swimming—and she heard them coming down the cellar stairs before she saw them: boots on stone, four sets, and the voice of the clipboard lieutenant saying, "Mind the corners, mind the damp, the colonel wants it intact."

Fen stood in front of the shelf like a hedgehog presenting its spines. "The provincial ledger requires a removal authorization countersigned by the provincial registrar's office."

"The provincial registrar's office," the lieutenant said pleasantly, "is now a desk in my corridor." He held out a paper. Fen took it, read it with maddening thoroughness, and signed the receipt with a hand that shook only slightly, which Noor knew cost him more than any lie he had ever told.

Two soldiers lifted the ledger down. It was heavy—forty years of entries, bound in green hide gone black at the edges—and they carried it up the stairs between them with exaggerated care, as if it might bite, as if it were already evidence in a trial no one had announced. Noor pressed herself against the shadowed end of the shelving and watched the space on the shelf where it had lived. The wood there was lighter than the surrounding grain, a clean pale stripe, the way grass stays green under a stone. Forty years, and it left behind a stripe of light.

That, she thought, was the whole army in miniature. They removed a thing and left the shape of its absence and called the shape theirs now too.

Upstairs, through the window, she watched them load it onto a handcart with other bundles—the parish registers, the guild rolls, the harbor master's logs—and wheel it across the square toward the town hall, now flying the Column's gray pennant from a pole bolted that afternoon into stone that had held nothing but a flagpole rope for festivals. A soldier walked beside the cart with a rifle slung backward, guarding paper. Guarding names.

*Your names,* she thought. *Mine. Sef Oduya's. The boy who sweeps the fish market. Everyone who ever paid a toll or missed one.*

* * *

Night came down on Callowmere like a lid. From her room above the chandler's storeroom, Noor could see the town hall windows lit far later than any civic business required, and figures moving behind the glass with the steady rhythm of clerks. They were working. Of course they were working. An occupation, she understood now, was mostly overtime.

The proclamation boards went up through the evening. She watched two soldiers work their way along the quay, nailing gray boards over the archive door, over the weighbridge, over the ferry office, each hammer stroke carrying across the water with terrible clarity. When they reached the last board—the one for the archive door itself, covering the carved lantern—they worked quickly, professionally, and one of them steadied the nail with his thumb, and for a moment he was just a young man with cold hands doing a job in a town that hated him, and then the nail went home and he was the army again.

By full dark the town wore its new script. Gray boards everywhere, stenciled regulations, curfew hours, the schedule of required registrations. Callowmere had become a document that could only be read by standing very still in the street after dark, which was now illegal.

Noor sat with her back against the wall and did what she had been trained since childhood to do, the game her mother had taught her with kitchen tiles and flour-dusted margins: *remember the room, close your eyes, name everything.* The ledger's dimensions. The smell of its hide binding. The weight distribution when Fen lifted it—left-hand heavy, the early volumes' stitching swollen with damp. The cipher notation in the resettlement margins. Page counts. Margin codes. The particular way Fen said *annotations* when he meant something he wouldn't say aloud.

Carry nothing that can't be dropped. Remember nothing you can deny.

But they had taken the thing itself, and memory alone was suddenly not enough. Memory was testimony, and testimony needed a body, and bodies at checkpoints needed names.

Down in the square, the town hall windows burned on. Somewhere in that building, under lamplight, a clerk with clean hands was opening her town's ledger for the first time, running his fingers down columns of names, deciding—or merely preparing for others to decide—what the marks beside those names would come to mean.

Noor blew out her lamp and, in the dark, began to copy pages from memory, her finger tracing letters on the floorboards where no ink could find them.

She would need the pages themselves. And she would need to learn, before the learning killed her, exactly who was watching that building—and whether anyone inside it could be turned.
