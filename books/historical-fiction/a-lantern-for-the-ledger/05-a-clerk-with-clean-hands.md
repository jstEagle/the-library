## 5. A Clerk with Clean Hands

Elias Vant had been in the army long enough to know that wars were won by quartermasters and lost by clerks who fell behind on their entries.

This was a heresy he kept folded small inside himself, the way other men kept photographs of sweethearts. He had learned it at the supply depot at Harren's Ford, where an entire winter campaign had starved not for want of grain but because a requisition ledger had been misfiled under the wrong month, and no one—not the colonel, not the commissar, not the three lieutenants who shouted about it—could locate four hundred sacks of barley that were sitting, all the while, twenty paces from their own noses. Paper was the true terrain of any army. Whoever held the paper held the war. The rifles merely enforced its conclusions.

So when Colonel Brase summoned him to the requisitioned town hall of Callowmere and said, *Vant, you will index the provincial ledger*, Elias understood that he had been handed something more dangerous than ammunition.

The ledger lay on a trestle table in what had been the registrar's workroom—a long room above the archive cellar, lit by two tall windows that looked down on the quay. It was bound in oxblood leather gone soft as old bread at the corners, its spine cracked into segments like a river delta seen from above. Beside it, someone had left the previous clerk's half-finished inventory: a neat column of folio numbers, ending abruptly at 214, followed by nothing but a coffee ring and, inexplicably, a pressed sprig of some flowering weed flattened between two pages like a bookmark made by a child.

Elias hung his coat on the peg by the door—he was a man who hung his coat on the peg even when the building had been taken by force, especially then—and sat down at the table, and opened the book.

The first thing he noticed was the smell. Old paper had a smell; every clerk knew it, the vanilla-and-dust sweetness of lignin surrendering itself slowly to time. But this ledger smelled underneath of something else. River water. Tallow smoke. And faintly, persistently, of a flower he could not name, though it pulled at him the way a half-remembered melody does.

He began, as he always began, at the beginning.

*Province of Callowmere, Registry of Inhabitants, Being a True Account.* The hand changed every few years—one registrar retiring, another taking up the pen, each transition marked by a subtle shift in the slant of the letters, the way ink browned or blackened with fashion. Aldous Fen's hand appeared somewhere around page sixty: smaller than his predecessor's, precise without being fussy, the letters of a man who had decided early that beauty was a form of waste. Elias found he liked Fen's pages best. They read like good engineering.

He worked through the morning. Names, holdings, debts, births, deaths. The town assembled itself in his mind the way towns did when you read their ledgers whole: the ferryman Oduya with his three licenses and one outstanding fine; the chandler Wren whose debts to the grain factor mounted year over year like water rising in a lock; a widow Eastwick, Meret, deceased these six years, holding a lease on a dye-house that had passed—here the entry simply stopped, resumed three lines later in different ink—to her daughter.

It was while copying this entry into his index that Elias noticed the first wrongness.

His index was meant to mirror the ledger exactly. Folio for folio, line for line, so that any officer consulting the index would know precisely where in the original a given name lived. But the ledger's pagination skipped. Page 87 ran directly into page 90. Three leaves, gone—not torn out crudely, which would have left stubs and ragged ghosts of text, but removed cleanly, expertly, with a razor or a thin blade drawn close against the binding thread. The stitching itself showed no damage. Someone had known what they were doing.

Elias sat back. The window light moved across the table, touching the gap in the pages and passing over it as if the light too preferred not to see.

He checked further. Page 112 to 115: gone. Page 140 to 141. Page 158 through 163—a full quire, six leaves vanished in one clean surgical absence. He counted backward and forward, cross-checked the catchwords at the foot of each surviving page against the openings of the next, and built in his head a map of everything missing. Fourteen leaves. Not random. He turned to the register of resettlement annotations—the column Brase had ordered given priority—and felt the cold arrive in his stomach before his mind caught up.

Every single missing leaf had carried names in that column.

* * *

There was, Elias knew, a correct procedure for this discovery.

Article 31 of the Field Administration Code: *Any irregularity in seized records shall be reported within one day to the officer of the day, together with a full description of the affected material and such observations as may assist in the recovery of the same.*

He had written reports under Article 31 before. At Harren's Ford, a depot sergeant had skimmed boot leather off the manifests for two years, and it had been Elias's careful indexing—the kind of patient, unglamorous exactitude no one ever thanked anyone for—that had caught him. The sergeant had wept at his court-martial. Elias had felt nothing in particular, then or after. That was the thing people misunderstood about clerks: they assumed the work required coldness. It did not. It required only accuracy, and accuracy was a kind of cleanliness, and cleanliness asked nothing of your heart.

He drafted the report in his head even now, walking it sentence by sentence as he walked the corridor to fetch water for his pen. *Fourteen leaves removed prior to seizure, locations as follows.* Clean. Accurate. Airtight.

Then he came back up the stairs and stopped in the doorway, because there was a woman standing over his ledger.

She was not what he expected. Intruders, in his imagination—which was fed entirely by regulations and the cautionary examples circulated through the clerks' corps—were desperate men, or soldiers of fortune, or ideologues with burning eyes. This woman looked like nobody at all. Grey dress, grey shawl, hair pinned back with the economy of someone who owned exactly three pins and used them all. She stood at the table with her hands folded, not touching anything, in the posture of a person who had come to collect a debt rather than commit a crime. Her eyes went to the open ledger, then to his face, and Elias watched—with the peculiar slow clarity of a man watching his own professional life end—her expression settle into something calm and finished.

"You're counting," she said. Not a question.

"The pagination," Elias heard himself say, "is irregular."

"Is it."

"Fourteen leaves." He crossed the room. He did not know why he said the number aloud. Perhaps because saying it proved he had done his duty; perhaps because keeping it silent already felt like a second theft. "Removed cleanly, before the seizure. All of them bearing resettlement entries."

The woman did not flinch. That was the remarkable thing. Clerks saw men flinch all day—it was practically the substance of the job, the small muscular confessions faces made when confronted with their own accounts. This woman absorbed the number the way stone absorbs weather.

"You'll report it," she said.

"I'm required to."

"Yes." She nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd already entered in her own ledger. "Required. I remember what that word does to people."

"I don't know you," Elias said.

"No," she agreed. "That was the point of me."

And then—because the light through the window had shifted, and because she turned her head slightly toward it, and because Elias Vant had spent eleven years training his memory to hold faces the way his ledgers held names—he recognized her. Not her name. Her function. He had processed courier manifests for the Ninth Column since its arrival; he had seen this face on a transit pass, third category, bearer authorized for sealed dispatches between ministries, description: unremarkable. He remembered thinking, at the time, that the description was the most honest thing he'd ever filed.

"You're the courier," he said quietly. "Eastwick. Noor Eastwick."

Something moved behind her stillness then—not fear exactly, but the particular grief of a professional watching her one asset burn. "You're not supposed to know that either."

"No," Elias said. "I'm not."

* * *

They stood there a long moment, the two of them, with fourteen missing leaves hanging in the air between them like smoke that refused to disperse.

Outside the window, the quay went on being the quay. A soldier was re-nailing a proclamation board that the river wind kept loosening; the hammer strokes came up through the floorboards as a dull, patient pulse, like the heartbeat of something buried alive. Gulls argued over the fish market's leavings. A barge loaded with requisitioned timber pulled away downstream, and Elias found himself automatically noting its cargo class, its draft marks, its departure hour—eleven years of habit, the ledger-writing machine in his chest that never fully stopped.

"You took them," he said at last. "Before the Column arrived. You knew what was coming."

"I help the registrar copy documents. It's dull work. Dull work lets you see things." She said it flatly, offering him nothing, and Elias understood that this too was professionalism—hers. Two professionals, then, facing each other across a table, each holding the instrument of the other's ruin.

He should call the guard. There was one stationed at the stairhead, a boy named Petch with a carbine he cleaned too often and a crush on a girl in the bakery queue. It would take thirty seconds. Article 31, subsection two: *any person found tampering with seized records shall be detained pending inquiry.* He had the face memorized, the name confirmed, the crime established. His report would be flawless. His hands would be clean.

Instead Elias heard himself ask: "Why those pages?"

Noor Eastwick studied him for a moment, weighing—him, not her answer; he could feel himself being assessed, priced, entered somewhere in her internal accounts. Whatever she found apparently cleared her, because she said:

"Because a ledger is only paper until someone reads it aloud. Then it becomes a verdict. Those pages are verdicts on two hundred and forty-one people. Some of them are dead already. Most aren't. Yet." A pause, precise as a punctuation mark. "Your colonel hasn't read them aloud yet. I intend to make sure that by the time he wants to, the words will have left town."

"That's theft of state property."

"It's testimony," she said. "State property is just testimony that lost its nerve."

Elias looked down at the ledger—at Fen's beautiful, wasted precision; at the gaps where whole families had been excised from the record of themselves; at the pressed sprig of unnamed flowers some earlier hand had left between the pages like a small green prayer. He thought about Harren's Ford, about the sergeant who wept, about how cleanly his own reports had always cut. He thought about the word *required,* which Noor Eastwick had pronounced like a diagnosis.

Here is what Elias Vant understood in that moment, standing in the registrar's workroom with the river light going amber on the table: that he had spent his entire career believing that accuracy was neutrality. That a man who wrote down exactly what was, without addition or omission, had kept his hands clean no matter what the writing was *for*. And that this belief was the most elegant lie the army had ever taught him, because it meant a clerk could feed the firing squads forever and die believing he had never touched a gun.

The ledger didn't care who read it. But he cared what it said. Those were different things, and he had never once, in eleven years, let himself notice.

"Petch is at the stairhead," Elias said. His voice sounded strange to him, level and distant, as if someone else were speaking through his mouth in perfect, damning accuracy. "He changes position every quarter hour. There's a service stair behind the archive shelving; the soldiers don't use it because the treads groan. Third tread from the bottom is loose—you have to step wide."

Noor Eastwick went very still. "You're telling me how to steal from your army."

"I'm telling you," Elias said, "the state of my staircase. What you do with architectural information is your affair."

For the first time, something in her face unlocked—a flicker, quickly mastered, that might in another woman have been a smile. "You understand what happens to you if this goes wrong."

"I'm a clerk," Elias said. "I understand exactly what happens. I write the reports."

* * *

She left by the service stair, stepping wide on the third tread, and Elias returned to his chair and his index and his pen, and did the hardest piece of professional work of his life: he wrote the truth and hid it in plain sight.

*Folio review complete through page 200,* the entry read, in his most immaculate hand. *Pagination consistent throughout. No irregularities observed. Recommend continued systematic indexing at current rate; estimated completion, nine days.*

Nine days. Long enough, perhaps, for whatever she intended. Short enough to be plausible—he was, after all, a thorough man, and thoroughness took time. He blotted the page, sanded it, closed the ledger gently as one closes a door on a sleeping child, and sat for a while in the emptying light with his hands flat on the table, looking at them.

Clean hands. That was what everyone had always said about him, the officers who trusted him, the sergeants who feared his exactness. *Vant has clean hands.* He had worn the phrase like a medal.

Now he understood it differently. Hands stayed clean only so long as they held nothing. The moment you picked something up—fourteen stolen leaves, a stranger's secret, the knowledge that your beloved accuracy had been a weapon aimed at people whose names you could recite from memory—there was ink in the skin that no amount of sand would scour away. He flexed his fingers once, testing, as if he might see the stain already rising in them.

Below the window, on the quay, a funeral procession was forming—small, unofficial, forbidden, a knot of dark-clad mourners moving toward the churchyard with a coffin no wider than a child's. Elias watched it go and thought, with the terrible fluency of a man who had just discovered he could think such things: *someone should write down that boy's name. Properly. Before the army files him under 'incident.'*

On the morrow, he decided, he would begin indexing the resettlement column in earnest, as ordered. Every name, faithfully copied, exactly where the ledger said it was.

Which meant that every night thereafter, he would also know precisely which names were *not* there anymore—and who had taken them, and why, and how much longer the staircase's third tread could be counted on to groan.

He dipped his pen. Somewhere below, faint through the floorboards, the loose tread creaked, and went quiet, and Elias Vant began to write.
