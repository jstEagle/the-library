## 6. Three Checkpoints, One River

The map was not drawn on paper. Paper could be confiscated, burned, read by the wrong eyes at the wrong moment. The map was drawn in the only archive the Ninth Column could not requisition: the one behind Noor's eyes.

She walked the route north three times that first week of occupation, once for each checkpoint, and let her feet do what a pen would have gotten her arrested for.

* * *

The quay gate came first, because everything in Callowmere came to the quay eventually, the way all rivers come to the sea whether they mean to or not.

She went there on a morning of low grey cloud, carrying an empty market basket as her excuse and a half-loaf of bread as her alibi—bread bought from Marta Voss's stall with real coin, so that if a sentry asked, she had a vendor who would remember her face and vouch for its dullness. That was the old creed still working: carry nothing that can't be dropped, remember nothing you can deny. A woman buying bread is no one. A woman buying bread is everyone.

But the quay had changed since the boots came. The fishmongers were gone—not arrested, simply absent, their stalls shuttered, their scales hanging empty like little gallows. In their place stood two soldiers at a plank table set across the mouth of the quay road, with a third lounging against a bollard and a fourth walking the line of moored boats, tapping hulls with a baton as though counting them into some inventory only he could see. Behind them, nailed to the customs house wall, the proclamation board gleamed with fresh paint:

*BY ORDER OF THE NINTH COLUMN. ALL PERSONS LEAVING THE PROVINCE BY WATER OR ROAD WILL PRESENT PAPERS FOR INSPECTION. GOODS ARE SUBJECT TO REQUISITION. THE ARMY THANKS THE CITIZENS OF CALLOWMERE FOR THEIR COOPERATION.*

*Cooperation.* Noor read the word twice. It sat on the board the way a leech sits on skin—flat, patient, drawing something out of you while you pretend it isn't there.

She joined the small queue of townsfolk waiting to pass onto the quay proper—women with washing, a cooper with a handcart of staves, an old man leading a goat nobody had thought to confiscate yet. She watched how the inspection worked. Papers were not enough here; she could see that immediately. The sergeant at the table looked at faces longer than he looked at documents. He asked questions with the flat courtesy of a man reading from a list: *Where are you going. What for. How long.* And then—the part that made Noor's stomach tighten—he wrote something down after each answer. Every crossing, logged. Every name, entered.

A ledger of movement. They were building one even now, entry by entry, and every entry was a hook waiting for a fish.

She bought nothing, saw no one, sold no one anything. She walked home the long way, along the reed flats where the wind came off the water cold and honest, and in her head she drew the first line of the map: *quay gate—four men—one writes—faces checked before papers.*

It would not do. Not for pages. Not for anything that mattered.

* * *

The bridge toll was different, because the bridge was different. The old stone span across the Vessenne had carried grain carts and pilgrims and wedding parties for three hundred years without ever asking anyone their business, and now it had a toll house with a stovepipe chimney and a sentry box on either end, and the army had discovered that a bridge is the simplest machine in the world for turning people into a queue.

Noor rode north with a cousin's cart of late apples—her cousin Tilde owed the Eastwicks a favor going back to a flood year, and favors were the only currency the occupation hadn't figured out how to tax. She sat among the barrels in her plainest shawl and watched the toll through lowered lashes.

Two checkpoints' worth of difference here. At the quay they inspected people; at the bridge they inspected things. Carts were waved onto a gravel apron beside the toll house, and a corporal with a bayonet went through them with the bored thoroughness of a man who has done this four hundred times and found contraband twice. He probed flour sacks. He lifted tarpaulins. He rapped on barrel heads and listened—a good ear, Noor noted, filing it away—and when he found nothing he chalked a mark on the cart's side and waved it up to the toll clerk, another soldier, older, who recorded the load, the destination, and the driver's name in a book as thick as a Bible.

But the search was random. She saw it plainly: every fifth cart, more or less, or whenever the corporal's attention snagged on something. Four carts passed untouched between each inspection. It was arithmetic, and arithmetic had always been kind to Noor Eastwick. One chance in five. And convoys—she watched a grain convoy come down from the upland farms, eight wagons under a single requisition seal, searched once at the head of the column and waved through entire, because the army trusted its own paperwork more than it trusted any single farmer's sacks.

*A convoy,* she thought, standing hip-deep in apples, heart beating slow and deliberate. *Get inside a convoy and you are no longer cargo. You are paperwork.*

The second line of the map drew itself: *bridge toll—searches random—one in five—convoys pass as wholes.*

* * *

The marsh causeway she did not walk. She paid Sef Oduya to row her there instead, which was itself an education.

Sef's ferry was the last one still working the lower river, and only because the Ninth Column hadn't decided yet whether a ferryman was a civilian or a service. He was a broad, silent man gone grey at the temples, with forearms like mooring posts and a way of looking at you that made you feel audited. His boat smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular green rot of the marshes in autumn. Noor had known him her whole life the way you know a fixture—a doorframe, a stair rail—without ever knowing him at all. Her mother had known him better. That was the thing she was only beginning to understand about Meret Eastwick: the whole town had been full of these quiet debts to her, and Noor had never once seen her collect.

Sef took her up the eastern channel, past the drowned willows, to where the causeway began—a raised corduroy road of timber and packed earth running three miles through the reed beds toward the northern provinces, the only dry line across six miles of wet ground.

At its head stood the new post. Palisade stakes. A watchtower with a signal lamp, dark now but obviously built for dark nights. Sandbag emplacements facing both directions along the causeway, because the army feared what might come out of the marshes almost as much as what might try to leave through them. Smoke rose from cook fires. She counted figures against the grey sky: eight, perhaps ten, with a rotation she couldn't yet read.

And she heard the thing that made the blood move strangely in her ears: voices calling out. A cart creaking onto the causeway planks, and a sentry's voice carrying flat across the water—"*Name?*"—and a farmer's voice answering, "*Pellam, Jorin Pellam, out of Mereholt*"—and the scratch of a pencil, audible even at this distance, or perhaps only imagined.

They were naming people. Aloud, into a record, before letting them pass. The causeway post didn't just check papers; it demanded testimony. Every traveler became a name spoken into a book, and a name spoken into a book could be compared against any other book the army cared to open—including, someday soon, she was quite sure, a certain provincial ledger with its quiet annotations in the margins.

Her mother's name was in that ledger. If Noor's name went into the causeway's book and someone cross-referenced—she stopped the thought, folded it, put it away. Later. Grief was a luxury for after.

"Bad water this time of year," Sef said. It was the first thing he'd said since the willows. His eyes never left the channel ahead, but she understood he wasn't talking about the channel. "Reeds burn easy when it's dry. Army's been burning them at night. Says smugglers." A pause long enough to hold a whole argument. "Marsh folk don't much care for their reeds being burnt."

"I'd need a night crossing," Noor said. "Cargo. Small, but it can't get wet, and it can't be seen."

He rowed four strokes before answering. The oars knocked softly in their locks, a sound like a patient clock.

"Your mother," he said finally, "once told me a thing about cargo. Said the only cargo worth carrying is the kind you'd sink for."

"And would you?"

He looked at her then, fully, for the first time—those auditing eyes, taking her apart the way he'd take apart a seized engine, looking for what made her run. Whatever he found, he kept.

"Bring your cargo to the old eel sheds after the lamp patrol passes," he said. "Second watch, moonless nights only. I'll tell you which nights those are."

*Third line of the map:* *marsh causeway—ten men—a tower—a naming-book—and one boat, if the ferryman keeps his word.*

Three checkpoints. Three doors, each locked differently. The quay wanted faces; the bridge wanted luck; the causeway wanted names. And somewhere north of all three, past the last palisade stake, the world where the truth might still be worth something—if it arrived, if anyone read it, if the republic it belonged to hadn't already stopped existing.

* * *

She came back to the eel sheds on the third moonless night with the cargo sewn into oilcloth bundles no bigger than prayer books, and Sef was waiting with a lantern shuttered down to a coal-glow, and the river lay black and breathing around them.

He took one bundle, weighed it in his palm with a professional's delicacy, and set it down without opening it. He did not ask what was inside. That restraint told her he had already guessed, and had decided his guess changed nothing—or rather, that his guess was the reason he was here at all.

"There's a price," Sef said.

"Name it."

He reached into his coat—not quickly, letting her see every inch of the motion, a courtesy she recognized as the habit of a man who had lived among frightened people—and brought out a letter. Sealed with wax, addressed in careful, schoolroom-neat script. The paper was soft at the edges, handled often, carried for years.

"My daughter," he said. "Liss. She went north before the war, before the *last* war, when the purges came through the river towns. She writes me every season. I have never—" His jaw worked once. "I have never written back. Because letters travel through the same hands as everything else, and a letter from Sef Oduya to a girl who left under a false census entry is a rope they can hang us both with. So I write nothing. For eleven years I write nothing."

He held the letter out across the space between them, over the bundle of pages that contained, among other names, the name of the woman who had forged his daughter's safety—though Noor did not know that yet, and Sef watched her take the letter without knowing that she knew, and the weight of what each of them was hiding settled over the boat like fog.

"You're going north," he said. "Past the causeway, past whatever comes after. Someone who can do that can put a letter in a post office in a town where no one knows either of our names." His voice roughened, the river-smooth surface cracking for one syllable. "Eleven years, missy. Tell her her father isn't dead. Tell her the ferry still runs."

Noor turned the letter over in her hands. It was heavier than paper should be. Eleven years of unsaid things pressed into three pages, sealed under wax, and he was entrusting it to a stranger because strangers were the only couriers left who weren't already compromised—and perhaps, too, because her mother had once done something for him that he had never repaid, and debts like that don't age out, they only deepen, gathering interest in silence the way silt gathers on a hull.

"I'll carry it," she said. "I won't read it."

"I know you won't." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "You're Meret's girl. You carry things the way she did—like they weigh nothing and everything at once."

The lantern-coal guttered. Out on the causeway, faint as a heartbeat, the signal lamp blinked twice—patrol changing—and Sef's head came up like a heron's, all attention, all silence.

"Go home the reed path," he murmured. "Not the road. And missy—" he waited until she met his eyes, "—whatever's in those bundles. The army's started asking at the bridge about missing pages. Clerk from the column, going cart to cart, polite as Sunday. Asking about paper."

The fog closed in around the words. Noor stood very still in the swaying boat, the letter against her ribs, and felt the shape of her next nine days contract suddenly, sharply, like a fist closing.

Somewhere in Callowmere, someone in a uniform was already counting pages.
