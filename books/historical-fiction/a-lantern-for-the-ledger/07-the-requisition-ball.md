## 7. The Requisition Ball

The invitations went out on army stationery, which was the first joke of the evening, though no one in Callowmere laughed. *The Ninth Column requests the pleasure of your company at a dinner of civic harmony,* they read, in a hand so correct it looked machine-made. *Attendance is expected.* Noor had delivered three of them herself that morning, up the hill to the merchant houses on Vintner's Row, and had watched each recipient hold the envelope the way one holds a summons—by the corner, at arm's length, as if it might bite through the paper.

Now she stood in the requisitioned town hall, in a borrowed grey dress that belonged to the widow Halm, who had two daughters and only one good gown between them, and she waited to be useful. Translation, they had told her. The Colonel required a translator for the provincial dignitaries whose Vessanic was too rustic or too stubborn to serve. Noor spoke four languages and could pass in six more, but her true gift was not the languages. It was standing near a conversation without belonging to it, so thoroughly unnoticed that people said things in front of her as if she were a chair.

She intended tonight to be furniture.

The hall had been transformed with a violence of taste. The archive shelves along the east wall were draped in white linen like corpses. The registrar's long counting tables now bore candles in silver holders requisitioned from the chapel, and the floor where Aldous Fen had spent forty years on his knees among the ledgers was polished until it threw back the light in long amber ribbons. Someone had found bunting. Someone had hung it over the proclamation boards nailed across the archive door, so that the nails wore little festive triangles, red and cream, like wounds dressed for a party.

*They have dressed the cage,* Noor thought, and then corrected herself: *they have hung curtains in the window so the town can watch itself being eaten politely.*

Colonel Brase received his guests beneath the town's charter, which still hung above the dais because removing it would have required admitting it existed. He was smaller in person than his reputation, a compact man with a clerk's posture and a duelist's stillness, and he greeted each arrival by name—the name, the trade, the spouse's name, the number of children—so precisely that every citizen understood, without a word being said about it, exactly how well the ledger knew them. When he reached Noor he paused half a heartbeat longer than courtesy required.

"You are Eastwick," he said. "Courier class. Your file says discreet."

"Your file flatters me, Colonel."

"It is never wrong." He smiled with the lower half of his face only. "Tonight you are a vase. You hold flowers. You say nothing. Do you understand vases?"

"Perfectly, Colonel. I have been a vase all my life."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger; interest, filed away—and he moved on down the receiving line, and Noor felt the flicker follow her like a splinter working toward the bone.

* * *

Elias Vant arrived early, as clerks do, and left late, as clerks must, and in between he performed the most difficult work of his career: appearing to be nothing at all.

He had been assigned to the dinner the way one assigns a man to a flood wall—with sandbags of paperwork. His orders: log the guests against the ledger's attendance annotations. Note who came, who sent regrets, who lingered near whom. A map of the town's loyalties, drawn in wine rings and seating charts. He carried a small black book for the purpose and hated it with an efficiency that surprised him. Three weeks ago he would have done this without hating it. That was before the missing pages. Before the girl in the cellar with ink on her fingers and her mother's name folded inside her like a letter she could not open.

He watched her now from across the room. The courier, Eastwick. She stood by the tall windows holding a tray she did not need to hold, translating a fragment of conversation between the grain factor Ostermeyer and a captain of the Ninth, and she did it so smoothly that both men believed they were speaking their own thoughts. Her face was a well-run office: everything filed, nothing out of place. But Elias had learned to read offices. There was a drawer in her face that did not close all the way.

Brase saw it too. Elias had watched him see it, at the receiving line, and had felt something cold move down his spine. The Colonel collected discrepancies the way other men collected debts. He would come back for that drawer.

Dinner was served at eight. Quail from the confiscated estates, river fish poached from the river, bread made from flour the army had seized at the mill and then magnanimously returned baked. The town ate its own possessions and thanked its occupiers for the recipe. At the high table, Brase gave a speech in flawless Vessanic about harmony, about how the Column had not come as conquerors but as *custodians*—the word chosen, Elias was certain, the way a surgeon chooses a blade—and the hall applauded with the particular enthusiasm of people who have counted the exits.

Elias logged the applause. *Sustained. Unanimous. Two gentlemen ceased clapping when the word 'resettlement' passed.* He wrote it flatly, without adjectives, because adjectives were evidence of a soul and souls were contraband in the Ninth Column. Then he looked up and saw Noor moving through the service door toward the kitchens, and the cold thing moved down his spine again.

Because he had inventoried the dessert course himself that afternoon. And he knew what the pastry cook—a frightened man named Tuller, whose brother was on a list somewhere—had wrapped inside the napery of the Colonel's own place setting.

* * *

In the kitchen, the heat hit Noor like a held breath finally released. Steam, fat, the shout of orders in three languages, and beneath it all the low continuous murmur of fear, which has a sound like water under ice. She had asked to "inspect the service" in her capacity as translator for the kitchen staff—half of whom spoke marsh dialect and needed someone to interpret the army's instructions—and the steward had waved her through without looking up.

Tuller was plating the sweet: honeyed pears in a sauce the colour of old brass. His hands shook. Not badly. Enough.

"The napery," Noor said softly, in marsh dialect, taking a stack of folded cloths from the basket beside him as if to count them. "Which one?"

"Third fold from the end of the rack. The one with the burnt corner." His voice barely cleared the boil of a stockpot. "It's not mine. It came to me already folded, from outside. I was told—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I was told if I looked, my brother would stop waiting."

"I understand."

"Do you?" He looked at her then, really looked, and Noor saw that he had decided she was either his salvation or his executioner and had run out of time to determine which. "You're the one who carries things."

Noor lifted the napery stack and fanned it once, lightly, the way a woman checks linens for stains. Third from the end. Burnt corner. Through the cloth she felt the small stiff rectangle of paper, folded to the size of a communion wafer, sewn—no, pasted, wax-pasted—against the hem.

Her heart performed a single enormous beat and then, obediently, resumed its work. This was the creed. *Carry nothing that can't be dropped. Remember nothing you can deny.* She had recited it since she was nine years old, sitting cross-legged on her mother's bed while Meret Eastwick turned ministry shorthand into a game of animals and arrows. But her mother had never taught her what to do when the carrying happened in a room full of soldiers, under candlelight, with the Colonel's own dessert.

She took the whole stack. It was, after all, plausible—linens needed collecting; she was a servant tonight, invisible, a vase. She walked toward the pantry alcove where clean cloths were stored, and her mind was already composing the theft: turn into the alcove, strip the paper from the hem in four seconds, palm it, return the stack—

"Eastwick."

Brase's voice, from the kitchen doorway, was quiet enough to chill the soup.

* * *

Elias saw the Colonel enter the kitchens and felt the evening tilt.

There was no reason for Brase to be there. Commanders did not inspect dessert. Commanders inspected *people*, and Brase had come to inspect the translator, and Elias understood this with the clarity of a man watching a scale tip. In three weeks of indexing the ledger he had built, without meaning to, a private taxonomy of the Colonel's attention: how it rested on things slightly too long, how it circled back, how it never forgot. Brase had noticed something at the receiving line. Now he had come to open the drawer.

Elias moved. He did not decide to move; his body simply committed treason ahead of schedule, crossing the kitchen with his black book open and his face arranged into the expression he used for routine queries, which was no expression at all.

"Colonel. Forgive the interruption. The attendance reconciliation." He planted himself between Brase and Noor as naturally as a table leg. "There's a discrepancy in the guest list I need translated. The marsh dialect entries. The interpreter—Eastwick—is the only one available."

Brase regarded him. Up close, the Colonel's eyes were the pale grey of unlined ledger paper, and they did the same thing paper does: recorded everything, promised nothing.

"You manage the books, Vant."

"Yes, Colonel."

"Books balance because everything is written down. Everything." He let the word sit. "When something is missing, the absence itself is an entry. Did they teach you that in the ministry schools?"

"No, Colonel. I learned it here."

For a moment the kitchen noise seemed to arrange itself around the silence between the two men. Then Brase nodded once, the smallest possible concession, and turned—but as he turned his gaze crossed Noor, standing with her stack of napery, and rested on the stack itself for one full second, the way a jeweler rests on a stone.

"Lovely pears tonight," he said. "See that the cook is commended. By name." And he left.

Elias exhaled through his nose, slowly. Beside him, Noor had not moved. The steam rose around them both like weather.

"The pantry alcove," Elias said, very quietly, still facing forward, still wearing his nothing-face. "Ten seconds. The steward calls for wine service in eleven."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I wrote the service order." He opened his black book and pretended to make an entry. "Go."

She went. He kept his eyes on the page and wrote a line about quail consumption, and his hand did not shake, and he thought: *this is what complicity feels like from the inside. It feels like competence.*

* * *

The paper was warm from her palm when Noor finally read it, alone in the widow Halm's darkened parlour at midnight, by the light of a lamp shielded with a shawl. Wax-paste residue still clung to her thumbnail. She had re-folded the napery stack, returned it, translated the discrepancy Elias invented—there was none; he had simply handed her two names and let her talk—and smiled at the Colonel twice more before the evening released her. Her face ached from smiling. Faces always ache after you use them as armor.

She unfolded the wafer of paper.

Ministry shorthand. Her mother's shorthand—the same animals, the same arrows, the same little hook that meant *do not speak aloud*. Nine years old again, cross-legged on a quilted bed, Meret's finger tracing symbols in a dust-covered ledger: *This bird means troops. This fish means boats. This arrow through the fish means the boats carry them.* She had thought it a game. She had been trained the way one trains a child to swim, without ever saying aloud that someday there would be deep water.

She decoded it in the lamplight, line by line, and the deep water rose around her.

A deployment order. Two companies of the Ninth Column, redeploying north along the river road in four days' time. Reinforcements drawn *from the causeway post*—drawn away from it, thinning the third checkpoint, the last barrier between Callowmere and anywhere the ledger might survive. And below that, in the margin, a notation in plain cipher that her hands translated before her mind could stop them:

*Resettlement transport scheduled to accompany. Manifest to follow.*

The names would ride north with the soldiers. Or the soldiers would leave, and the lists would stay behind with whoever inherited the republic's corpse. Either way, the pages in her possession were suddenly not just testimony. They were a timetable.

She sat for a long moment in the dark, listening to the house settle, thinking of Tuller's shaking hands, of his brother waiting on some list, of Elias stepping into the space between her and the Colonel's gaze as casually as a man changing position at a card table. He had lied to his commander. Fluently. For her. He had not even hesitated long enough to become interesting to himself.

That was the most dangerous thing anyone in Callowmere had done all night, and he had done it with clean hands and a straight face, and Noor understood that she was no longer carrying this alone—which was, according to every rule her mother ever taught her, the beginning of the end.

She folded the order into the lining of her sleeve and pinched out the lamp.

Across town, in the barracks annex, Elias Vant sat at his desk with his black book open and entered the final line of the evening's record in his neat, deniable hand: *Dinner concluded without incident. Civic harmony achieved.*

Then he closed the book, and in the dark he allowed himself, for the first time since the army took his signature and made it official, to wonder what would happen when Colonel Brase reconciled the dessert napery against the laundry manifest—and found, in the third fold from the end, a burnt corner with nothing hidden in it at all.

Because Noor had taken the paper.

But Elias, ten seconds before the steward called for wine, had already unpicked the wax-paste seam, checked the contents, and sewn it shut again with thread from his kit—and now the only question in the world that mattered was whether the Colonel would ever learn that the clerk who balanced his books had held the secret in his own two hands, and let it walk out of the kitchen on a tray.
