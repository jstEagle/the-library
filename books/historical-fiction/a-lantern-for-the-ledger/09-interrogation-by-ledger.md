## 9. Interrogation by Ledger

The requisition order for the town hall's great chamber arrived at dawn, typed in triplicate, and by seven o'clock Elias Vant had carried three folding tables into the room where Callowmere had once held its harvest court. He arranged them in a shallow arc facing a fourth table, taller, set on the dais beneath the old stained-glass window of Saint Vessal holding her scales. The glass was cracked—war does that even to saints—and the morning light came through the fracture in a long slanted blade that fell across the dais like something waiting to be used.

Colonel Brase arrived at half past eight and adjusted nothing.

He stood for a moment in the center of the floor, hands clasped behind him, surveying the arrangement of tables and chairs and the single oil lamp he would not need until evening, and Elias understood that this too was inventory. The chamber. The light. The people who would soon sit in those chairs. All of it entered into some ledger inside the Colonel that no one else could read.

"Clerk," Brase said. "The transcription desk goes there." He indicated a spot to the left of the dais, close enough that every word spoken at the tall table would pass through Elias's pen before it reached any record. "You will write what is said. Not what is meant. Not what you infer. What is said."

"Yes, Colonel."

"And you will write it cleanly. I have read your hand. It is adequate." Brase paused, and his eyes did something Elias had learned to dread over six weeks of indexing confiscated property: they went soft, almost kind, the way a man looks at a tool he has decided not to replace yet. "Adequate hands are rare. Try to keep yours."

* * *

They began bringing the townsfolk in at nine.

Elias knew most of them by now—not as friends, clerks were not permitted friends among the requisitioned, but as entries. The widow Harsany, whose flour shop appeared in the ledger under *provisions, surplus*. The cooper Brammelt, listed twice, once for his workshop and once, in the resettlement column, for reasons the ledger declined to explain. Old Marta Ilves, who had taught half the town its letters and appeared only as a debt: four years of school fees, unpaid since the currency collapsed.

They came because the proclamation nailed to the archive door had ordered them to come. *All heads of household will present themselves for verification of census entries. Failure to present is itself an entry.* Brase had written that sentence himself, Elias had transcribed it, and he still remembered how the nib had caught on the paper at the word *itself*, as though even the ink flinched.

They sat in the chairs two at a time, hands folded, hats held against their chests like men in church, and Brase opened the provincial ledger before them with the tenderness of a priest opening scripture.

"Name."

"Widow Harsany. Odile Harsany."

"Residence."

"The flour shop, Quay Street. Number eleven."

Brase turned pages. The sound filled the chamber—the dry whisper of the ledger, the tick of his finger traveling down columns—and Elias wrote, and outside the high windows gulls argued over the fish market, and the ordinary morning of an ordinary town went on being ordinary just beyond the glass, which made everything inside the glass worse.

"Your entry records a son," Brase said. "Tam Harsany, age nineteen. Occupation: ferry laborer."

"He works the boats, yes."

"There is no annotation beside his name." Brase looked up, and his voice remained perfectly level, perfectly administrative, and that was the horror of it, Elias thought, that was the thing he would never be able to describe afterward to anyone who had not sat at this desk: the horror was not cruelty but its absence. "Do you understand what that means, Widow Harsany?"

She didn't. Elias could see she didn't—her face held the careful blankness of a woman trying to find the trap before stepping in it. "That he is... unmarked?"

"That he is unverified." Brase drew the ledger slightly toward himself, protective, proprietary. "An unverified resident is a discrepancy. Discrepancies require documentation. You will bring proof of your son's birth, residence, and employment to this office within three days. If the documentation is satisfactory, the discrepancy will be resolved."

"Resolved," she repeated.

"Resolved," Brase agreed pleasantly, and Elias wrote the word twice, once in the transcript and once, without meaning to, in the margin of his own mind, where it would stay.

* * *

By noon they had verified fourteen households, and Elias's wrist ached and his transcript ran to nine pages, and the pattern had become visible to everyone in the room including, he suspected, the Colonel himself, who wore the pattern like a tailored coat.

The ledger did not verify the town. The town verified the ledger. That was the inversion, and it was elegant, and Elias hated that he could see its elegance, hated the clerk's reflex in himself that admired a well-made instrument regardless of what it was built to do. Every answer the townsfolk gave was checked against the book; every hesitation was recorded; every recorded hesitation became grounds for further documentation, further summons, further fear. A woman's whole existence reduced to whether it matched a column of figures kept by strangers.

At half past noon the registrar was called.

Aldous Fen came up from the archive cellar slowly, one hand on the wall, and Elias felt something tighten in his chest at the sight of him. Fen had been down there for weeks—Brase had left him in charge of the confiscated collection, on the theory, Elias supposed, that a man would guard best the thing he loved, and love it Fen plainly did. He was seventy-one years old and moved like a man carrying furniture through narrow halls, carefully, always aware of his own bulk, and he climbed the three steps to the verification table and lowered himself into the chair with the gravity of a man sitting down to be judged by history rather than by a colonel.

"Aldous Fen. Registrar of the Provincial Archive, thirty-nine years' service."

"You know why I've summoned you," Brase said.

"I imagine it concerns the ledger."

"It concerns the ledger's completeness." Brase turned to a page near the front—a page Elias knew intimately, having indexed it, having counted its lines. "The census of Callowmere district, as bound. My predecessor's acquisition report describes the volume as complete. Your own accession stamp is on the flyleaf. Yet when my clerk"—a small nod toward Elias, who bent lower over his pen—"conducted a foliation check, he found irregularities. Tell me, Registrar. How many leaves are in the census section?"

The chamber went very quiet. Even the gulls seemed to pause.

Elias kept his eyes on his paper. But his pen stopped. Because he knew the true count—he had counted it three times, alone, at midnight, with a lamp turned low, because counting was his profession and he could not stop his hands from doing it even when the count mattered more than he wanted it to. Two hundred and forty-four. And he knew, too, that the number Brase's own acquisition report contained was wrong—Brase's staff had recorded two hundred forty-eight in their haste, and Brase had never recounted, because Brase trusted reports the way other men trusted scripture.

Fen knew it too. Elias watched the old man's face do nothing at all, absolutely nothing, a stillness so complete it was itself a kind of speech.

"Two hundred and fifty," Fen said.

The lie dropped into the room like a stone into still water.

Brase tilted his head. "Two hundred and fifty."

"Two hundred and fifty," Fen agreed. "I stamped the volume myself. I have stamped every volume in that cellar for thirty-nine years, Colonel, and I do not make errors of count."

"No," Brase said softly. "I don't suppose you do." He rose, walked to the verification table, and stood looking down at the registrar with his hands again clasped behind his back, and Elias realized with a lurch that the Colonel had known the correct number all along—that the question had been the trap, that the entire afternoon had been the trap, that every household verified since nine o'clock had been rehearsal for this moment, the demonstration, for the assembled town, of what happened to discrepancies.

Because Fen had given a number that matched neither the book nor the report. And both of those were now evidence of something.

"You have served this archive a long time, Registrar."

"Longer than the Republic has existed, Colonel."

"Yes. That is rather what I thought." Brase returned to the dais and sat, and when he spoke again his voice had resumed its administrative calm, which was somehow worse than anger. "Clerk. Take this down verbatim."

Elias took it down verbatim.

*At approximately twelve-forty hours, the registrar Aldous Fen, questioned regarding the foliation of the provincial census, stated a leaf count inconsistent with both the acquisition report of the Ninth Column and the physical volume itself. Asked to account for the discrepancy, the registrar declined. Asked whether leaves had been removed from the volume during or prior to military custody, the registrar declined. Asked to identify any person with access to the volume during the period in question, the registrar declined.*

Three declines. Elias wrote the word three times and each time his pen pressed harder, until the third *declined* tore slightly at the tail of the final letter, and he smoothed the paper with his palm as if he could smooth what it recorded.

"Aldous Fen," Brase said, "you are charged with administrative obstruction. You will be held pending review." He glanced at the townsfolk in the chairs—at widow Harsany, at Marta Ilves, at the cooper whose face had gone the color of tallow—"as a courtesy to the town's understanding of procedure. Verification resumes tomorrow. Nine o'clock. Discrepancies will continue to be resolved."

Two soldiers walked Fen out. At the door the old man stopped, and turned, and looked back across the chamber—not at Brase, not at the soldiers, but at the room itself, at the faces in the chairs, and something passed from his eyes to theirs that Elias could not transcribe because it was not language, though it was received, he could see, by every person present, received and folded away somewhere behind the ribs.

Then the door closed, and the boots faded down the corridor toward whatever cells the Ninth Column had established in the old customs house, and the chamber was quiet except for the scratch of Elias's pen finishing the last line of the record, which he wrote in full:

*End of session. Transcript certified true by E. Vant, Clerk, Ninth Column.*

He certified it true. Every word of it had happened. That was the obscenity of it—that the document was accurate, that accuracy itself had become the weapon, and that his own clean adequate hand was the hand that loaded it.

* * *

That night he stayed late in the indexing room, ostensibly completing the day's entries. The lamp burned low. Through the wall he could hear the archive cellar's silence, and beyond it, distantly, the river.

He found himself thinking about counts. Two hundred and forty-four leaves, and Fen had said two hundred and fifty, and the difference was six, and six leaves was twelve pages, and twelve pages was enough space for—well. For names. For columns of annotations in a marginal cipher that Elias had noticed weeks ago and catalogued, professionally, as *administrative shorthand, origin uncertain*. He had not tried to read them. Reading them would have been a step, and he had spent his whole career believing that a clerk's safety lay in never taking steps, only in recording the steps of others.

But Fen had lied. Fen, who had stamped thirty-nine years of truth into that cellar, had stood before a colonel and a room full of his neighbors and lied about a number, knowing what it would cost, and the lie had been for someone. Six missing leaves. Someone had taken them, or copied them, or hidden them, before the Ninth Column ever nailed its proclamations to the archive door.

Elias sat with his finished transcript before him and his pen in his hand and understood, with the slow cold clarity of ink drying, that tomorrow he would go down into the cellar and begin reading the margins.

And that whoever had taken the leaves—if they came back, if they were still coming back, if the cellar's silence had a visitor he had never once detected in six weeks of midnight indexing—would find the shelves disturbed, or the dust marked, or some other small discrepancy that a man who counted things could not help but leave behind.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the river, and far off, faint as a turned page, he heard footsteps on the quay that stopped, and started, and stopped again.
