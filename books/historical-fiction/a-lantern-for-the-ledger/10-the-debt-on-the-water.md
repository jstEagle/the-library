## 10. The Debt on the Water

A riverman learns early that water keeps no records. That's what I loved about it, before I understood that everything else does.

My father poled barges up the Vessan delta when the Republic still minted coins worth their weight. He taught me three things: how to read a current by the way reeds lean, how to keep my mouth shut when soldiers board a boat, and never — *never* — carry anything for a stranger you wouldn't be willing to drown with your own hands if the cargo turned out to be cursed. He said it as a joke. Rivers are where jokes go to die, and I have spent sixty-one years proving him right in ways he could not have imagined.

I am telling this wrong. I always tell it wrong. Let me start where it actually starts, which is not with me at all.

* * *

The Eastwick house stood on Tanner's Row, back when there were still tanners on Tanner's Row, before the smell drove them all downriver and the street filled up with clerks and widows and people who kept their shutters painted. Meret Eastwick kept hers green. Everyone else on the row went gray or brown or the color of old rope, but hers was green, a stubborn river-green, and you could find her house from the middle of the channel just by looking for the one shutter that refused to surrender to the season.

I knew her first the way everyone knew her — as the woman who walked fast and looked at nothing. Callowmere has always had its forgettable people, the ones the census-takers wave past because they've already decided what's written there. Meret Eastwick was the most forgettable woman in town, which is to say she was the most carefully seen woman in town, and only the careful ever noticed the difference.

Her husband fished the shallows. Her daughter — Noor, small then, all elbows and watching eyes — sat on the quay steps doing sums in chalk on the flat stones, washing them away with river water when the tide came in. I used to watch her do it. A whole ledger written and unwritten every day, six hours of arithmetic gone with one breath of tide. I thought it was a child's game. It took me twenty years to understand it was an education.

But none of that is why I owe them. Anyone can owe a debt of neighborliness. What I owe the Eastwicks is measured differently, and the coin of it is a girl named Tamar.

* * *

You have to understand what the year of the Grey Census was like, because the young ones now — Noor included, though she'd deny it — think the resettlement lists are something new. They are not new. The Republic has been counting its people into graves since before I could pole a barge; they only change the vocabulary. In the year of the Grey Census, the word was *hygienic review*. A nice word. A word that smelled of soap. They sent enumerators through every river town from Callowmere to the capital with blank ledgers and instructions nobody wrote down twice the same way.

The instruction, in practice, was this: mark the households that would not be missed.

Ferrymen are not missed easily — we're useful — but ferrymen's children are another matter. My daughter Tamar was born the winter the ice took the north channel, and her birth was registered late, sloppily, by me, in the margin of a harbor log because I couldn't afford the registrar's fee and didn't trust the office anyway. On paper, Tamar Oduya barely existed. And a person who barely exists is exactly the kind of person a hygienic review can make stop existing entirely, because who will ask after her? Who will count the missing among the uncounted?

The enumerator came to my boat on a Tuesday in the fog month. I remember his gloves — good leather, city leather, cracked at the knuckles from someone else's winters. He asked for names and ages and trades, and he wrote them down, and then he asked the question that wasn't a question: whether there were any persons resident aboard whose documentation was irregular.

I said no.

He smiled the way clerks smile when they already know better, and he wrote something in his ledger anyway, and I watched the nib move and felt the bottom drop out of my life the way it drops out under a boat when you've misjudged the sandbar. He wrote Tamar down. Not her name — a notation, a code, a little crosshatch of ministry shorthand in the margin beside my entry, and I did not know what the shorthand meant but I knew what men with gloves mean by writing things in margins.

Three days later the review lists went up on the archive door.

Tamar's description was on them. Not her name — they hadn't bothered to learn it. Just: *female child, undocumented resident, vessel moored south quay.* Eleven words. I have carried those eleven words in my chest for more than thirty years and they weigh more than any cargo I ever moved.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I am not proud of it, and I am not ashamed either, because pride and shame are both luxuries of people whose children are safely documented.

I went to the only person in Callowmere who might know what the margin-shorthand meant, and that was Meret Eastwick, because everyone in the river trade knew — the way you know things without knowing how you know them — that the Eastwick woman had once worked near the ministries, that she had come upriver years ago with a head full of official grammar and a face built for being overlooked, and that she now lived quietly on Tanner's Row behind a green shutter and asked no questions and answered fewer.

I knocked on her door at dusk. She opened it before I finished knocking, which should have told me something about how much she already knew.

"Sef Oduya," she said. "South quay. Your daughter's entry has a marginal annotation."

Not a question. She said it the way I'd say *the tide's turning.*

"I don't know what it means," I said, which was a lie, because I knew enough to be drowning in it.

"You know what it means." She stepped aside to let me in. "Come in off the step. If we're going to ruin ourselves, we'll do it indoors."

* * *

I will tell you what happened in that house, and then I will tell you what it cost, because the cost is the part nobody puts in ledgers, including — especially — the ones that matter most.

Meret Eastwick sat me at her table, lit one lamp, and turned her back to the window so the light wouldn't show my face to the street. Then she took a sheet of plain paper and wrote Tamar's name on it. Full name. Birth date corrected to the true one. Place of birth: not *vessel moored south quay* but *Callowmere parish, north district,* which was a lie of exactly the kind that turns a ghost into a citizen.

"An amended entry requires a countersignature from a licensed registrar and a filing fee paid within the fiscal quarter of record," she said, copying lines onto the paper in a hand so clean it looked printed. "Neither of which exists. So instead we do this."

She folded the paper small, smaller than seemed possible, and tucked it into the lining of her glove — the same kind of city-leather glove the enumerator wore, and I understood then that she had dressed herself in the costume of the people she was robbing.

"There is a clerk at the archive," she said, "who owes me a kindness I will not describe. Tomorrow morning I walk in as a verification agent from the district office — a role I have played before, and play convincingly, because I was trained for it by the same government I'm stealing from. I amend your daughter's entry in the provincial ledger. I burn the enumerator's marginal note. And Tamar Oduya becomes a girl who was born in Callowmere parish, north district, to a licensed ferrymen's household, documented since birth."

"And if they catch you?"

She looked at me with something I couldn't name at the time. I can name it now. It was the look of a woman pricing her own life against a stranger's child and finding the exchange rate acceptable.

"If they catch me," she said, "they catch a forger, and forgers hang. Which is why they won't catch me. Sit still. Drink that tea. You'll want your hands steady for what comes next."

"What comes next?"

"You're going to forget this conversation entirely," she said. "You're going to remember that you came here about a mooring fee, that we argued pleasantly about the price of eels, and that you left. Can you do that? Because I need you to be able to do it under questioning, and questioning is a thing riverside men get."

I drank the tea. We argued about eels. I left by the kitchen door into the dark of Tanner's Row, and by the time I reached my boat I genuinely could not have told you what we'd spoken of, because she had built the forgetting for me, brick by brick, and handed it over like a coat.

Three days later the review list was amended. Tamar's description vanished from the archive door between one posting and the next — quietly, administratively, the way the Republic erased people going the other direction. My daughter became real again on paper, and therefore real everywhere else, and I never thanked Meret Eastwick out loud because thanking her would have meant remembering, and remembering was the one payment she'd forbidden.

So instead I ferried her husband's fish free of charge for nine years, until the fever took him. Instead I watched the green shutter and made sure it stayed green — left paint on her step once, anonymously, a full pot of it, and she never mentioned it and neither did I. Instead, when her daughter grew and started carrying sealed things through flooded streets with that same look-at-nothing walk, I kept my mouth shut about it to every customs man and column officer who ever idled too long at the south quay.

That is how a debt ages. It doesn't shrink. It ferments. Every year you don't repay it, it grows heavier and sweeter and more dangerous, until one day a young woman with her mother's elbows sits across from you in your own boat and asks you to do the very thing that got her mother killed, and you discover the debt was never a weight at all.

It was a lantern. It was waiting all this time to be lit.

* * *

She came to me at slack water, two nights ago, in the hour when the marsh exhales and the reeds stand perfectly still and even the birds hold their opinions. She came down the bank the quiet way, the way her mother must have taught her without teaching her, and she sat in the stern of my boat like she'd been sitting there all her life.

"I need pages moved north," she said. "Past the toll. Past the causeway post."

I looked at her a long moment. The moon was thin and the water was black glass and I saw, in the set of her jaw, a woman I had buried fifteen years ago, risen and asking for the family boat.

"You know what they're burning in the reeds," I said. "Patrols fired the east flats last week. They'll fire the rest before the month's out. A night boat through burning marsh is a coffin with pretensions."

"Yes," she said.

"And you know what happens to a ferryman caught with ministry paper."

"Yes," she said again, and then, softer, with something that was almost her mother's voice: "I'm not asking you to forget this one, Sef. There isn't time to build the forgetting. I'm just asking."

I want to write down honestly what I felt, because this chapter of things deserves an honest scribe, even an unwilling one. What I felt was terror, yes — the old familiar cold that lives in a riverman's spine alongside the salt. But underneath the terror, older than it, was the memory of a lamp in a kitchen on Tanner's Row, and a woman pricing her own neck against my daughter's breath, and the sound of my own voice lying smoothly about eels because she had asked me to and because the asking itself was a gift I never earned.

Gratitude doesn't age the way wine does. Wine mellows. Gratitude hardens. It sets in a man like resin in wood, and you can plane the plank and paint the hull and sail her forty years, but the resin is in the grain, and when the plank finally breaks, it breaks along the resin line, along the debt, along the night a stranger ruined herself in a government ledger so that my Tamar could go on breathing.

"Noor," I said — her name, out loud, on open water, which is a thing I had not done in years because names on water travel — "do you know why I'm going to say yes?"

She shook her head.

"Because your mother never let me thank her," I said. "And a debt you can't repay gets passed down. Like a boat. Like a name. Like eleven words in a margin." I put my hand on the tiller, the old wood worn smooth by three generations of my family's grip. "We'll move them on the flood tide, the dark-of-moon runs, when the patrol boats draw too much water for the channels. Pages ride low and dry in the false bilge under the fish boxes. And there's a condition."

She stiffened. I almost laughed — she thought I was going to haggle, this girl who haggles with armies.

"A letter," I said. "One letter, carried north by whoever carries your pages. To my Tamar. She went upriver eight years ago, after the second census scare, and I haven't sent word because word travels both directions and I won't lead anything back to her. But a letter inside your cargo — inside the thing they'd hang us both for anyway — that costs nothing extra. Write it yourself, tonight, before you lose the nerve. Fathers are cowards about letters. It's the one honesty I have left."

For the first time since the army came, since the boards went over the archive door, since her mother's name surfaced in a column meant for graves — for the first time in all these drowned weeks, Noor Eastwick smiled. Small. Wry. All elbows and watching eyes, and underneath it, unmistakably, the green shutter standing stubborn against the season.

"One letter," she said. "And Sef — I know about Tamar. I found the amendment in the ledger. Her entry's in my mother's hand. I've known since the cellar."

Of course she had. Of course she'd read the margin and known the hand, because she was raised on that shorthand as a child's game, chalk sums washed away by the tide, and nothing in this town stays hidden from the people trained to see it. I looked at her and felt thirty years settle into place around the debt like a keel finding the channel.

* * *

This morning the smoke came early off the west flats. Not the patrol fires — those burn wet and slow, columns of white spite — but a hotter smoke, oily, deliberate. They're firing the reeds ahead of schedule. Brase's men, sweeping the marsh before the frost closes the channels, burning cover ahead of whatever they think is moving through it.

Sef Oduya has poled this river through two purges and one war and the year the ice took the north channel. He knows what burning reeds mean: they mean the water routes close soon, and whatever is going north must go *now*, on the next dark tide, or not at all.

The pages are sewn and ready in the fish boxes. The letter to Tamar sits wrapped in oilcloth beside them, my handwriting gone stiff and strange from disuse, saying things I should have said eight years ago. Tonight I'll take the boat up the narrow channel past the drowned willows, running dark, engine muffled, riding a flood tide that smells of ash.

And somewhere north of the marsh, at a toll booth or a causeway post, there are men with ledgers waiting to compare every name against every list. I have spent my whole life believing water keeps no records.

It's time to prove the ledgers wrong.
