## 12. Brase Learns Her Name

Colonel Brase did not sleep badly the night the capital's requisition manifests arrived. He slept exactly four hours, which was what he allotted himself in every occupied town, and he woke at the fourth bell to a lamp, a pot of black tea going cold at his elbow, and three crates of paper that smelled of river damp and other people's lives.

He worked the way he had always worked: left page to right, top entry to bottom, no skipping, no sentiment. The Ninth Column's quartermaster had once joked that Brase could find a deserter in a haystack because he would simply read the haystack until it confessed. The joke had made its way back to him and he had not laughed, but he had not corrected it either. It was accurate. Everything confessed eventually, if you read it long enough. Towns confessed. Ledgers confessed. Men who believed themselves invisible confessed in their own handwriting, dated and countersigned.

The courier manifests from the Ministry of External Correspondence were a masterpiece of the republic's particular folly: meticulous, standardized, and utterly blind to what they contained. Every registered courier in the Vessan service was issued a manifest book—carbon copies retained by the ministry, triplicate stubs carried on the body—and every dispatch logged a route, a seal number, a delivery window. The republic trusted paper so completely that it had built an entire intelligence apparatus out of the assumption that no one would ever be fool enough to steal from a system that recorded everything.

Brase poured his cold tea without drinking it and began.

* * *

The first hour was arithmetic. He laid the manifests for the Callowmere circuit across the long table in the requisitioned magistrate's office—the office still bore a child's chalk drawing of a heron on its slate board, which he had ordered left in place, though he could not have said why—and he ruled columns in pencil along the margins. Dispatches received at the northern relay. Dispatches logged as sent from Callowmere. Dispatches confirmed delivered. The difference between the columns was where truth lived; it always lived in the difference between columns.

For six weeks the numbers agreed with themselves. Then, eleven days after the Ninth Column crossed the river, they stopped agreeing.

Three dispatches sent from the provincial archive in Callowmere had never arrived at the northern relay. Not lost—he distrusted the word *lost*, which was what sloppy men said when they meant *stolen* or *intercepted* or *afraid*. The relay station's own receipt book showed the dispatches' scheduled arrival dates, each one initialed in advance by a clerk anticipating routine traffic, and then nothing. No arrival entry. No delay notation. No returned-to-sender. Three gaps, evenly spaced, like a man breathing through held teeth.

Brase ruled a second set of columns. Dates of the missing dispatches against dates of military movement. Against weather. Against market days. Against ferry schedules.

The ferry schedules were interesting.

Callowmere's ferries ran north twice weekly, weather permitting, and the requisition order he himself had signed—seventh day of occupation, all watercraft subject to Column inspection—had been enforced with the usual thoroughness. Yet the missing dispatches predated the requisition by only days, and their scheduled routes coincided precisely with the last unimpeded crossings before his soldiers stood on the quay. Someone had moved paper north on the final open water. Someone had known the window was closing and used it.

He wrote in the margin, in his small square hand: *Not panic. Planning.*

Panic was common and cheap. Panic dropped documents in wells and burned them in stoves and buried them in gardens where any corporal with a shovel could find them. But this—this was someone who understood the machinery well enough to ride it. Who knew the relay schedule. Who knew the ferry timetable. Who knew, possibly, that the manifests themselves would record everything, and had chosen to move contraband through the one channel too ordinary to search.

That was either arrogance or intimacy. In Brase's experience the two were frequently the same thing.

* * *

At the second bell he summoned Lieutenant Corvane, who arrived with rain still beaded on his collar and the particular stiffness of a man who had been enjoying his leave and resented the summons without daring to show it.

"The courier registers," Brase said. "All of them. Ministry-registered couriers assigned to the Callowmere circuit for the past two years. Names, physical descriptions, last known assignments."

Corvane hesitated. "Sir, the ministry's records were consolidated to the capital before we took the town. What remains here is—"

"Is the archive cellar under the town hall, which I have already had catalogued. I am asking you to fetch me the catalogue." Brase turned back to his columns. "And bring the duty roster of every clerk currently indexing confiscated materials."

"Yes, sir." Corvane paused at the door. "May I ask what we're looking for?"

"A person," Brase said, "who does not want to be looked for," and Corvane, understanding that this concluded the briefing, saluted and went out into the rain.

Alone again, Brase drew the archive catalogue toward him and read it the way he read everything—completely, without hurry. The cellar inventory was thorough in the way of a frightened registrar trying to demonstrate compliance: census volumes, tax rolls, deed registries, correspondence indexes. And there, item forty-one, the provincial ledger itself, noted as *removed to requisition storage, ninth day*, with an annotation in a different hand—clerk's hand, neat, professional—that the volume's pagination appeared irregular upon intake.

Irregular pagination. Brase touched the annotation with one finger, lightly, as if the ink might still be warm.

Pages missing from a ledger whose cipher annotations referenced a resettlement schedule. Pages missing before army intake—which meant removed between the army's seizure of the town and the ledger's transfer to storage, a window of less than two days—or removed before the seizure entirely, which meant someone in the archive had begun protecting those pages while the Ninth Column was still a rumor on the southern roads.

Either way: foreknowledge. Either way: hands inside the machine.

He sat back and regarded the ceiling, where water stains bloomed in continents that matched no real geography. Somewhere below him, under the nailed boards of the proclamation-covered archive door, the town's memory of itself sat in the dark, minus whatever pages a careful thief had already spirited away. And somewhere north of here—past the quay gate, over the bridge, along the marsh causeway—those pages were traveling toward whatever destination the republic's enemies, or the republic's ghosts, considered safe.

Brase did not think of it as theft. Theft implied loss, and nothing was ever truly lost; it was merely relocated, and relocation could be traced. He thought of it as a misfiled entry awaiting correction.

* * *

By evening the picture had assembled itself with the satisfying click of a lock accepting its key.

The ministry's duplicate register—a partial copy, mercifully, retained in the regional substation at Harrow Ford before the capital sealed itself—listed fourteen registered couriers on the Callowmere circuit. Eleven were men. Two were women old enough to be somebody's grandmother, their assignment histories short and rural. The fourteenth—

The fourteenth was a name with almost nothing attached to it.

Noor Eastwick. Registered eight years prior. Assignment history: intermittent dispatch runs, low-priority circuits, a commendation for reliability dated five years back, and then a long unremarkable stretch of small deliveries—sealed packets, cipher keys rotated quarterly, nothing flagged. Physical description entered at registration and never updated: height unremarkable, coloring unremarkable, age then twenty-two. A photograph had been required by regulation; the register noted *portrait declined, waiver granted, clerical oversight*.

Brase read the entry three times.

Eight years of perfect mediocrity. Eight years of being precisely forgettable enough to pass through checkpoints, garrisons, and border posts without a single officer's memory retaining her shape. He had interrogated smugglers who spent fortunes cultivating anonymity—burn scars purchased, accents studied, whole identities laundered through three jurisdictions. This woman had achieved it by doing nothing at all. By being, apparently, genuinely nothing: a face the world declined to file.

And she was local. Born Callowmere, the register said. Mother listed as next of kin: Meret Eastwick.

Meret Eastwick.

Brase reached for the provincial ledger's intake summary—the document his clerks had transcribed before the original was locked away—and found the name without difficulty, because he had read every name on that list twice already, the way a surgeon reads a map of vessels before cutting. Meret Eastwick, widow, weaver's row. Annotated in the ministry shorthand that his cryptographers had partially broken and fully feared: *resettlement priority, second tier*.

Second tier. His cryptographers believed second tier meant transport within the season rather than the month. The distinction mattered to no one except the people in it.

So. The courier whose mother stood marked in the ledger had been running dispatches through the very weeks the ledger changed hands. The courier whose mother stood marked had access, motive, and—given the family's residence in the town—every advantage of local knowledge. The courier whose mother stood marked had watched soldiers nail proclamation boards over the building where her mother's fate was filed in triplicate, and had done nothing visible at all.

Nothing visible. That was the tell. Brase had occupied four towns before Callowmere, and in each of them the guilty had done something visible—argued, wept, volunteered excessively, fled. The innocent performed grief loudly. The guilty performed ordinariness perfectly, and ordinariness performed perfectly had a texture to it, a smoothness, like a stone from a riverbed among stones that still had their rough edges.

"Corvane," he said, when the lieutenant reappeared with the duty rosters. "The clerks working the ledger index. Give me their names."

Corvane recited them. Five names. One of them, a Vant—Elias Vant, transferred in with the column's administrative detachment—had requested, informally, additional hours. Had asked to handle the intake personally. Had noted the irregular pagination in a report so carefully worded it might have been written by a lawyer or by a man choosing, with great precision, what not to say.

Brase circled the name once. Then, after a moment, he circled it again, because a single circle was a question and two circles was a decision, and he was not yet ready to decide about Vant. Clerks who noticed things were valuable. Clerks who reported things were trustworthy. Clerks who noticed things and worded their reports like lawyers were either the most honest men in the column or the most dangerous, and time would sort them, the way time sorted everything into its proper column.

* * *

He drafted the order that night by lamplight, in language stripped of everything but function.

*By authority of the Ninth Column and the Provisional Administration of the Callowmere District: effective immediately, all persons holding registration as couriers in the service of the former Ministry of External Correspondence are to present themselves for verification of credentials. Registered couriers failing to present within forty-eight hours will be considered absent without authorization and detained on appearance. All dispatches, seals, cipher materials, and manifest books in the possession of such persons are subject to immediate confiscation.*

He read it twice. It was clean. It did not name Noor Eastwick—naming her would warn her, and warning prey was the one cruelty a hunter must never permit himself—but the order's net was woven to her exact measurements. Forty-eight hours was enough time for a loyal courier to comply and not enough time for a guilty one to run far. Confiscation of manifest books would put her triplicate stubs in his hands, and her stubs would confirm or destroy his arithmetic. And if she presented herself—if she walked calmly into the town hall with her credentials and her forgettable face—then he would have her at a table, with her file open between them, and he would ask questions in the gentle voice he saved for people who believed themselves clever.

There was a version of this, he reflected, where he simply posted her description at every checkpoint and let the machinery grind her down. But descriptions failed against the deliberately unremarkable, and worse, they taught the quarry what was known. An order that named no names told her everything and nothing: that the net existed, but not whether it had closed around her specifically. She would have to decide, alone, in whatever room she was hiding in, whether the order was general hygiene or a hand reaching down the dark corridor toward her throat. And people alone in dark rooms, deciding, always moved. Movement was measurable.

He signed the order. He blotted it. He rang for the adjutant.

"Post this at the quay gate, the bridge toll, and the causeway post. Read aloud at morning muster. And—" he paused, pen still uncapped, "—have the duty clerks pull every dispatch stub, seal impression, and delivery receipt generated in this district since the day before we crossed the river. Everything. I want to know what paper moved and whose hands touched it."

"Yes, sir." The adjutant gathered the sheets. At the door he hesitated, the way men did when they wanted to ask something human. "The couriers, sir. Some of them will just be... tradespeople's daughters. Runners. Half of them won't know why they're being detained."

"They'll know," Brase said, "when they're asked," and the adjutant, having no answer for that, took the order out into the wet dark.

Brase remained at the table. Rain worked at the shutters. Down in the town, lamps were going out one by one behind curtained windows, and somewhere among those windows—above the weaver's row, perhaps, or in some room rented under a false name by a woman whose genius was having no name at all—a light was also burning, or wasn't, and a decision was also being made, or had already been made days ago by someone smarter than the people she was stealing from.

He did not feel hatred for her. Hatred was for things that defied you openly. What he felt was closer to professional appreciation, the way a chess player regards an opponent who has finally, after many quiet moves, become visible on the board. She had ridden his timetables, hidden in his routines, used the republic's own love of paperwork as her cloak. Very good. But the paperwork was his now. Every manifest she had ever filed was a thread leading out of the dark, and he had all night, and all the threads, and patience that the war had refined down to something almost mineral.

He took a fresh sheet and began, in his small square hand, a private memorandum: *Subject E., Noor. Known associations: Fen, Aldous (registrar; arrested, pending). Vant, Elias (clerk; status uncertain). Oduya—* and there he stopped, because the ferryman's name surfaced from the requisition rolls unbidden: Sef Oduya, boat license current, debt registry clear, a man with a daughter exiled north and a ferry that had run its last free crossing the week the dispatches went missing.

Everyone in this town owed someone something, Brase thought. Debts were the truest ledger a community kept, more honest than the census, more durable than the tax rolls. And debts could be collected.

He finished the memorandum. He folded it into his breast pocket, where it lay over his heart like a second, colder organ. Then he snuffed the lamp, and in the dark he listened to the rain, and thought about the heron on the slate board in the magistrate's office, drawn by some child years ago, standing on one leg in water that no longer fell anywhere on any map he trusted.

Forty-eight hours, he thought. Let us see what moves.

Two streets away and one floor up, a laundress's window stayed dark, and behind it Noor Eastwick—who did not yet know she was being hunted, only that hunting had a sound, a pressure, a change in the air like the hush before ice gives way—folded her last copied page into the lining of a coat, and waited for morning, and morning was already bringing the proclamation down the hill, past the shuttered shops, toward the quay where the sergeant would nail it to the post beside the ferry schedule, so that the two papers hung together in the rain: the times the boats would run, and the names of everyone who would no longer be permitted to disappear.
