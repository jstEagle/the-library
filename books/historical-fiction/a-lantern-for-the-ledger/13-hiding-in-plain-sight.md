## 13. Hiding in Plain Sight

The first thing I destroyed was my own face.

Not the face itself—Brase's men had no use for faces they'd never studied—but the face the town knew. The courier's face: eyes down, mouth closed, hands folded as if perpetually waiting to receive something sealed. I had worn it for eleven years, and it fit me like a second skin, and on the morning after the proclamation went up with my description inked beneath the eagle seal—a woman, mid-twenties, dark hair, carries documents; approach with caution—I stood at the basin in Marta Ilves's back room and taught my muscles a new habit.

*Look up,* I told them. *Look at people. Laugh badly. Talk too much.*

It was harder than learning to carry nothing. Invisibility had been my craft and my creed, and now the army had made a profession of noticing exactly what I had spent a lifetime perfecting.

Marta watched me from the doorway with her arms crossed over her apron, a broad woman with flour to the elbows and a voice that could curdle milk at forty paces. She had never once spoken to me before that morning beyond a nod at market. I knew why. My silence had read, in Callowmere, as judgment—as though a woman who carried secrets considered herself above the small gossip that stitched a town together. They had decided I thought myself better than them, and they had been wrong, but being wrong about someone is its own kind of intimacy. It means you've spent time thinking of them at all.

"Sit," she said now, and pointed at the stool by the stove. "And stop looking at the floor. Nobody in this town looks at their feet unless they're hiding something, and you're done hiding."

"I'm not done hiding," I said. "I'm just changing what."

"Same thing." She set a bowl down in front of me—lard, wood ash, a sliver of blue soap shaved off a bar—and pushed up my sleeves. "Wash your hair in this. It'll dull the color. And the way you walk—" she circled me once, squinting, the way she might appraise a hog at auction, "—you walk like you're carrying eggs you can't afford to break. Widows don't walk like that. Widows walk like the ground owes them money. Shoulders down. Let yourself sag."

I let myself sag. It felt obscene, like slouching through a cathedral.

* * *

I had come to Marta's door at dawn, soaked to the knee from the drainage ditches behind the tannery, carrying nothing—which was the whole point, which had always been the whole point, except that nothing was heavier than anything I'd ever carried before. My papers were pulp at the bottom of the reed beds east of the millrace. My courier's satchel was buried under a dead dog's grave behind the chandler's, because who digs up a dog? The copied ledger pages—the ones that survived the marshes, the ones Sef and I had wept over in the dark while the patrols burned the reeds orange—those were already north, sewn into Sef's oilskins, past the bridge toll and gone. What remained in Callowmere was only me, and Brase wanted me the way a collector wants the last stamp of a series.

I knocked at Marta Ilves's door because she was loud, and loud people are never suspected of harboring quiet ones, and because two winters ago I had watched her stand in the square during the grain requisition and tell a Ninth Column lieutenant, to his face and at volume, that his arithmetic was an insult to her grandmother. He had docked her ration. She had laughed at him.

She opened the door before I finished knocking, looked at me for three full seconds, and said, "Well. Took you long enough. Get inside before Hessel sees you; he still tells the soldiers everything, poor fool thinks honesty is armor."

"You knew?"

"I know everything that happens in this town, girl, I just don't say it. That's the difference between me and you." She pulled me in by the wrist. "You never said anything at all."

* * *

The laundress came first. Widow Ansa Kell, of the lower town, whose husband had drowned in the weir pond six years back—a real widow, a convenient one, gone to her sister's in Vellmark for the season, leaving behind a name and a washboard and a reputation for keeping to herself. Marta announced the arrangement over the fence to three neighbors before noon, loudly enough that by evening the story had the texture of old news: Ansa Kell was home again, poorer than ever, taking in washing from the garrison families because grief makes strange economics.

So I became Ansa Kell. I scrubbed the garrison's collars in water so cold my knuckles split, and the work was honest in a way my old life had never been—dirt that yielded, stains that confessed, every garment telling you plainly where it had been. A soldier's shirt told you he'd slept in it. A colonel's table linen told you he'd dined on pheasant while the lower town ate nettle soup. I learned more about the Ninth Column from its laundry than I had from any dispatch I'd ever carried, and there was a bitter joke in that which I kept folded away like the soap.

The strangeness was the talking. Ansa Kell talked. She complained about the price of lye, about her rheumatism, about the weather and the weevils and the insolence of subalterns who snapped their fingers for hot water. I built her voice out of pieces of other women—Marta's bluntness, the ferry-women's salt, the cadence of the fishwives on the quay—and I wore it until my jaw ached at night from all the words it hadn't said in eleven years. And here is the thing I did not expect: the town answered. They talked *back*. Old Piet at the pump asked after my sister in Vellmark (I invented one; her name was Lise and she had a limp and a bad husband). The baker's wife slipped me day-old rolls and called me *poor thing*. Children ran errands for me. A woman named Greta Voss, whose son was among the boy's funeral procession I had once walked behind with pages sewn into my mourning shawl—took my hands across the fence one grey afternoon and said, "You've got grief in you, Ansa. I can always tell. You carry it the same way I do."

I nearly wept into the wash water. Because she was right. She had looked directly at the truest thing in me and named it, and it hadn't cost me anything, and no one in eleven years of professional invisibility had ever *seen* me at all.

That was when I understood the shape of the trap Brase had sprung, and its unexpected mercy. He had taken my name and given me to the town. He had forced me out of the margins and into the common text. I had spent my whole adult life believing that belonging was a weakness—that to be known was to be held, and to be held was to be caught—and now, hunted, paperless, wearing another woman's shawl, I was more held than I had ever been. Not despite the danger. Inside it.

They hid me the way towns hide things: without ceremony, without discussion, in plain sight. When a patrol swept the lower lanes, Greta Voss handed me a basket of wet sheets and walked beside me chattering about her sister-in-law's gallstones until the soldiers passed. When the garrison posted a new likeness of me on the proclamation board—someone had described my jawline, damn Aldous Fen's meticulous memory for faces—old Piet stood in front of it for an hour complaining loudly that the army couldn't draw worth spit, that the woman pictured was too pretty by half, that no laundress in Callowmere had cheeks like that, and by nightfall half the town had agreed with him, including, apparently, some of the soldiers.

"They think we're stupid," Marta told me, kneading dough with the fury of a woman punishing an enemy in absentia. "Book people and soldier people both. They think because we talk about gallstones we don't count names." She punched the dough. "We count everything. We just do it in the kitchen instead of the ledger."

* * *

There were nights, though. Nights when Ansa Kell's borrowed voice went hoarse and the walls of the rented room felt like the inside of a seal, and I lay awake cataloguing everything the destruction of Noor Eastwick had cost. My mother's cipher lived nowhere now but my skull—if I died, the marginal codes of the provincial ledger became decoration again, harmless scratches, and the execution lists reverted to arithmetic. Fen sat somewhere in the army's custody because he had lied about a page count for me. Elias Vant had waved me through the toll against regulations and I didn't know if he'd paid for it yet. Sef was north or drowned; the marshes had taken half our cargo and very nearly me with it, and I could still feel the cold of the water in my chest on certain evenings, a little reservoir of the river that never warmed.

On the worst of those nights, Marta found me sitting on her kitchen floor at two in the morning, back against the cupboard, doing the thing I used to do as a child: tracing letters in spilled flour. *Meret Eastwick.* My mother's name. The name on the list.

Marta didn't ask what I was writing. She put a blanket around my shoulders, sat down on the floor beside me—an enormous woman folding herself onto cold stone without complaint—and said, "My mother's name was Sanna. Sanna Ilves. Died in the fever year. There's no record of her anywhere now; the parish book burned. You know what that's like? Walking around being a person whose mother doesn't exist on paper?"

"Yes," I said.

"So write it in the flour if it helps. Then sweep it up and bake bread. That's what we do. Paper burns. Bread keeps coming." She paused. "Your ma was Meret, wasn't she. Meret Eastwick. She mended sails for Sef Oduya's father, back when. Everybody remembers her. Quiet woman, but kind when it counted."

"She falsified a census entry," I said, before I could stop myself. "For Sef's daughter. Years ago. Saved her life."

Marta nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Kind when it counted. See?" She got up, groaning at her knees, and began measuring flour into the big bowl. "Now. Are you going to sit there all night being hunted, or are you going to learn to make bread? Because a widow who can't bake is a widow the neighbors gossip about, and gossip is the one thing that'll kill you faster than Brase."

I learned to make bread. Of course I did. I learned to make bread the way I had learned ciphers—obsessively, precisely, until my hands performed it without my mind—and somewhere between the third batch and the tenth, I noticed that my mind had stopped rehearsing escape routes and started planning meals. This frightened me more than the patrols did. Comfort, I had been trained to believe, was a solvent; it dissolved the edges off a courier until she was nobody at all.

But then—sitting in Greta Voss's kitchen, eating stew, laughing badly at a joke about the garrison chaplain—being nobody at all did not feel like dissolution. It felt like arrival.

* * *

The knock came on the ninth evening, three short raps and one long, which meant nothing to Ansa Kell and everything to whoever had taught it to the person knocking.

Marta opened the door a crack, then wide, and Elias Vant stepped into the lamplight looking like a man who had aged a season in a week. His clerk's neatness had come undone—stubble, a coat buttoned wrong, the tremor of a hand that has done a thing it cannot file away. Behind him the lane was empty and raining softly.

He saw me—at the table, in Ansa Kell's shawl, flour to the wrists—and stopped as if struck.

"You look different," he said.

"That's rather the idea."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You look *better*. That's what's alarming."

Marta, who missed nothing and forgave less, looked between us and said, "I'll be checking on the bread," and withdrew with a tact that would have astonished anyone who'd heard her at the market.

Elias sat down across from me without being asked, and up close I could see what the lamplight was kind enough to blur from farther off: he was afraid. Not of the patrol outside, not even of Brase. Afraid the way a man is afraid when he has finally read his own entry in a ledger and found himself annotated.

"They're certifying the lists next week," he said quietly. "Final signatures. Brase wants the whole resettlement schedule countersigned by column command and sent to the regional office, and then—" his throat worked, "—then it stops being a plan and becomes a date."

"How long?"

"Eleven days. Perhaps fewer." He turned his hat in his hands. "Noor. There's something else. Something I found while indexing. You need to hear it, and I need—" he looked up at me, and the crack in him widened, "—I need to give it to someone who'll know what to do, because I have discovered that I am a man who alters one line to save his sister and cannot stop seeing every other line, and I don't know anymore whether that makes me a criminal or the only honest clerk in the column."

Outside, the rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere toward the quay, a patrol's boots slapped through standing water, unhurried, certain of the town they owned.

"What did you find?" I asked.

Elias leaned across the table, and lowered his borrowed, careful voice, and told me.
