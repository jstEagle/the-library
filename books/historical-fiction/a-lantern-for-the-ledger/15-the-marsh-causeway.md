## 15. The Marsh Causeway

The reeds began to burn at dusk, and the smoke came down the river like a slow animal.

Noor stood in the stern of Sef's boat as it slipped out of the willow shade behind the tannery, and she watched the western marsh turn the color of a wound. The fires were small at first—a scatter of orange teeth along the far bank—but the wind fed them, and by the time Callowmere's last rooftops had sunk behind them, whole acres of reed were standing in flame, hissing, cracking, throwing up sparks that rose and died like startled birds.

"They burn them every few nights now," Sef said from the tiller. His voice was low, not for secrecy but for economy; he was a man who had learned to spend words the way poor men spend coin. "Says it flushes smugglers. Mostly it flushes herons."

"Mostly," Noor agreed.

She did not say what they both knew: that mostly was not always. That somewhere in that burning country there would be soldiers tonight with torches of their own, walking the causeway in pairs, watching the water for boats that rode too low. And riding low was precisely what this boat intended to do.

Between her feet, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with tarred twine, lay what remained of the ledger. Nine days ago there had been two hundred pages sewn into mourning shawls and hollowed ledger boards and the false bottom of a grain sack. Now there were sixty-one. She had counted them four times since noon, and each count had come out the same, which was its own kind of mercy. Sixty-one names' worth of pages—the resettlement lists for Callowmere proper and the river villages south of it, plus Aldous Fen's own index of annotations, without which the columns of numbers were only numbers.

Half the book had already gone north over the bridge toll, hidden in a convoy of barley, waved through by a clerk named Elias Vant who had looked at her with eyes that said *go* and lips that said nothing. The other half could not take that road again. Brase had tripled the toll garrison after the barley convoy; Noor had seen the new sentry huts going up from the window of the Widow Kessel's washhouse, where she had spent three days being nobody, scrubbing sheets until her knuckles split and no one asked her name.

So: the marshes. The long way. The way that drowned people.

"Sit," Sef said. "Low. If we're stopped, you're my sister's girl come to help cut peat."

"I don't know how to cut peat."

"Neither does anyone's sister's girl. That's why they send them." He almost smiled. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadows, an old boat carved by weather. "Sit low anyway."

* * *

They went with the pole and the paddle and finally, when the channel widened, with a rag of sail that smelled of fish oil and forty years. The marsh at night was a country of sounds: the plop of frogs falling silent ahead of them and resuming behind, the creak of sedge, the far-off crackle of the burning banks, and under everything the soft insistence of the water itself, talking to the hull. Noor sat low with the oilcloth bundle against her chest and learned the rhythm of Sef's poling the way she had once learned her mother's cipher—by repetition until it became a language.

Her mother. Meret Eastwick. Even now, even here, the name arrived in her mind wearing its ministry shorthand, the tight little figures Meret had taught her as a game on winter evenings: *this mark means debt, this one means moved away, this one means never coming back.* Noor had been seven years old and delighted. She had thought she was learning secrets. She had not understood, then, that some games are apprenticeships, and that her mother was training her the way other mothers trained daughters to bake bread—for survival, yes, but also because someone had to carry the truth past the men who burned things.

Sixty-one pages. Her mother's name was not among them anymore; those pages had crossed the bridge ten days ago, folded into a grain sack, and Noor had allowed herself one hour of grief and relief braided together before moving on. But Aunt Ilsa was in these pages. The miller's twin boys. Old Tamas who taught her to swim. Four hundred souls of the southern villages, written in Fen's careful hand, annotated in a cipher only three living people could read—and one of them was in the army's custody, and one of them was Colonel Brase, who did not need to read it, only to burn it.

Which left her.

"You're gripping it like it'll swim off," Sef observed.

"It might."

"Mm." He poled through a stand of drowned alders, their silver trunks rising out of black water. "My mother used to say fear is just your hands knowing what they're holding. You want to put it down. Don't. Hands learn."

"That's terrible advice."

"That's marsh advice." This time he did smile, briefly, and the firelight caught the scar along his jaw where a rope had bitten him thirty years ago, hauling his first ferry across the winter current. "Hold on. Causeway narrows ahead. We go dark from here."

He doused the little shielded lamp, and the world went from black-and-orange to pure black, relieved only by the glow of the burning reeds to starboard and, faintly, impossibly, by stars. Noor had forgotten the stars. In town there were always lanterns, always proclamation boards nailed over windows, always light meant to watch you. Out here the sky opened like a hand unclenching, and the marsh lay beneath it in silence, and for one full minute she felt something dangerously close to peace.

Then the wind shifted, and brought her the smell of the smoke properly for the first time—not woodsmoke, not peat-smoke, but something sweeter underneath, oily, chemical. Burning tar. Burning pitch. Burning proclamation boards, maybe, or barracks scrap, or—

"Oil," she said. "They've oiled the reeds."

Sef's pole stopped mid-stroke. He stood very still in the stern, head tilted, listening the way old ferrymen listen—with his spine.

"Aye," he said quietly. "That's not a clearing fire. That's a drive."

* * *

They heard the soldiers before they saw them—or rather, they heard the marsh hear them. A thousand birds went up from the eastern bank at once, a single clapping roar of wings, and Sef swung the boat hard toward a side channel so narrow the reeds closed over the bow like fingers closing over a coin.

"Down," he breathed. "All the way down."

Noor folded herself into the bilge, the oilcloth bundle pressed flat between her body and the wet planking, and pulled sedge-cuttings over herself because Sef shoved them at her, handfuls of rank green that smelled of rot and duck. Above her, through a lattice of stems, she watched the eastern sky pulse orange. Somewhere beyond it, boots were moving along the causeway in the old patrol rhythm she'd memorized at the quay gate: two quick, one drag, pause. Two quick, one drag, pause. Men who had marched so long their marching had become geology.

The boat drifted. Sef let it drift, pole shipped, letting the current do the lying for them. The channel bent. The reeds whispered against the hull with a sound exactly like paper being turned.

And then, close enough that she could hear individual voices, the patrol reached the causeway bridge thirty yards upstream.

"—burned clean to the third marker, Sergeant."

"Third marker's nothing. Oduya's channel's what matters. Any boat comes out of the marsh has to pass the causeway mouth, and any boat passes the causeway mouth—" A pause; the strike of flint; the smell of pipe smoke drifting down. "You see that heron?"

"Sergeant?"

"Heron flew up back there. Just now. Herons don't fly at night unless something walks under them."

Noor's heart did not speed up. It slowed, strangely, the way a clock slows when it is falling. She found her mother's voice in her memory, calm as arithmetic: *when they look for you, be smaller than their attention.* She made herself into a stone. She made the bundle into part of the boat. Sixty-one pages against her ribs rose and fell with her breathing, and she breathed the way Fen had taught the archive clerks to breathe during inventory—shallow, patient, invisible.

A long silence. Water lapped. Somewhere a frog resumed singing, cautiously, like a witness deciding whether to testify.

"Nothing walks out there tonight but eels," the younger voice said at last, with the specific weariness of a man who wanted his shift over. "It's the smoke making shapes."

Another silence. Then, unhurried, almost bored: "Fire another line of it anyway. From the second marker south. If anything's hiding in the channels, we'll make it choose."

Boots receded. Two quick, one drag, pause.

Sef didn't move for a hundred breaths. Then, without a word, he took up the pole and pushed them deeper into the labyrinth, away from the new line of fire, into channels so narrow Noor could have touched both banks with her elbows. The burning spread behind them. Ahead, the marsh was dark and open and full of the smell of scorched green, and the night had become a thing with two mouths, fire astern and soldiers abeam, and only one narrow throat between them.

"We can't go back," Noor whispered.

"No," Sef agreed.

"We can't stay."

"No."

"So we go through."

He looked at her across the bundle in her arms, and in the reflected firelight his old eyes held something that might have been respect or might have been apology. "Through the deep cut," he said. "Where the causeway pillars fell in, years back. Boat rides light enough. Barely."

*Barely.* Noor looked down at the oilcloth. At sixty-one pages, four hundred names, tarred twine, and the whole weight of a town's memory sitting in her lap like a sleeping child.

"How barely?" she asked.

* * *

The deep cut was a wound in the marsh where the old causeway had collapsed into the peat, and the water there ran blacker than anywhere else, and deeper, and cold in a way that summer water should not be. Sef talked her through it while he worked the boat into position: the current pulled east toward the flooded quarry, the safe line was a boat's length off the broken pillar, and whatever happened, whatever the hull did, the bundle stayed dry or nothing else mattered.

"The pages get wet, the ink runs, the book dies," he said. "You get wet, you shout, and I pull you out. Those are the two rules. In that order."

"I heard them."

"You didn't. Nobody hears them right the first time." He set the pole, gauged the current with a lifetime's eye, and pushed off.

For eleven strokes it was perfect. The boat slid along the dark seam between pillar and current, silent as a leaf, and Noor dared—she actually dared—to think the word *through.*

On the twelfth stroke, the submerged timber caught them.

It came out of the blackness without warning, a fallen crossbeam of the old causeway lying just under the surface, and it struck the hull amidships with a crack Noor felt in her teeth. The boat lurched, slewed, and the current—patient, enormous, indifferent—took hold of it like a hand taking a toy. Sef shouted something that was half command and half prayer. The gunwale dipped. Cold water poured across Noor's knees, her thighs, her waist, shocking the air out of her, and the bundle in her arms began to slide.

She did not think. Later, she would search her memory for the thought and find none, only action, the kind her mother's game had built into her bones across a hundred winter evenings: *carry nothing you can't drop—remember nothing you can't deny—* and underneath both creeds, older than either, the simple animal arithmetic of what must survive. She got the oilcloth above the waterline. She got it high. And the boat, tilting, treacherous, alive with floodwater, chose that exact moment to roll her over the side.

The marsh swallowed her whole.

Cold was the first country. It closed over her head and erased every border she had ever known—no town, no war, no name, just pressure and black and the roaring silence of water in the ears. Her boots dragged her down. Her coat filled and became armor made of river. She kicked, and the kicking accomplished nothing, and somewhere far above there was a gray smudge of firelight that might have been the surface or might have been a memory of it.

The bundle. Where was the bundle.

Her hands found it—still strapped across her chest by the tarred twine she'd wound twice around her shoulders at Sef's instruction, *in case,* he'd said, and she'd laughed inwardly at the caution. The oilcloth had held. It was buoyant, fighting upward, dragging her with it, and she clawed at the twine knot with fingers gone wooden, and could not tell if she was pulling herself up or drowning herself faster.

Then a fist closed on her collar.

The world broke open into air and firelight and the sound of her own coughing, vast and ugly, and Sef's arm around her chest, hauling her across the swamped gunwale while the boat lay half-sunk in the shallows past the cut, grounded on peat, listing like a drunk. He laid her on the thwart and put his face an inch from hers, old and furious and terrified.

"Breathe," he commanded, as though breath were a thing one could be ordered to do, and she obeyed, because some orders are simply true.

When she could speak, the first thing she said—the first thing, before her name, before thanks—was: "Count them."

* * *

They counted by the light of the burning reeds, on the grounded hull, with their clothes steaming. Noor's teeth chattered through her own name-tally, and Sef held the oilcloth open while she lifted out page after page, laying them on the dry thwart like cards dealt by a merciless hand.

Forty-two.

She counted again. Forty-two. Nineteen pages were gone—slipped from the loosened binding somewhere in the black water between the crossbeam and the shallows, dissolved already into the current, into the peat, into the country that kept what it took.

Noor sat with the number. Forty-two. Half of what had boarded at dusk, more than half of what had once been a book, a fraction of what had once been a town. The firelight moved on the wet pages, and the cipher marks in the margins gleamed like small dark insects, and she thought of Aunt Ilsa's entry, and the miller's twins, and old Tamas who taught her to swim in a river that had no idea what it would someday carry, and she tried to determine which nineteen were missing and found she could not—Fen's index pages had survived, but the mapping of index to list was in her head, not on the surviving sheets, and reconstructing it would take hours she did not have, kneeling in a sinking boat in a burning marsh with patrols on both banks.

"We go back," she said at last. "We find where it happened. Some of them will have snagged on the reeds—"

"Noor."

"Some of them. Paper floats. Fen used heavy stock, it floats for—"

"Noor." Sef's hand closed over hers, gentle as ballast. "The water's taken them. I've lived on this water fifty years. When it takes, it keeps."

She stared at the black channel where the deep cut lay, at the place where nineteen pages of four hundred lives had gone down into the dark, and she felt the loss physically, a cold below the cold of her soaked clothes, a hole in her midsection shaped like names. And then—slowly, terribly—another feeling rose through it, unwelcome and clear-headed, the courier's creed asserting itself over the mourner's: *what can be carried shrinks to what must be.* She had dropped what could be dropped. What remained was what remained. Forty-two pages. Two hundred-odd names. The southern villages, half-recorded. It was not the whole truth. It was more truth than the army possessed, and more than Brase could burn, and it was in her arms, and it was alive.

"We still go north," she said.

"Aye."

"Tonight. Before they extend the burn line to the causeway mouth."

Sef looked at her for a long moment—this dripping, shaking girl who had nearly drowned holding paper—and something in his weathered face settled, the way a rope settles when the load finally balances. "Tonight," he said. He began bailing with a wooden scoop, fast and wordless, and the boat rose by inches out of the marsh.

It was while they bailed, in the interval between one scoop and the next, that Noor saw it: snagged in the reeds at the waterline, six feet downstream, a pale rectangle lifting and dipping with the current. One page. Perhaps the only one the river was willing to return. She waded to it on numb legs, expecting a list, expecting names.

It was not a list. The water had blurred the cipher, but beneath it, in Fen's unmistakable upright hand, was a line she had never seen in any ledger—a note in plain script, hidden inside the margin code, legible only to someone who knew both:

*If these pages reach you, child, ask the laundress at the Kessel house what the soap papers say. Your mother designed the key. She will want you to know whose hands you carry.*

Noor read it twice. The firelight flickered. Downriver, faint but distinct, carried on the smoke-wind all the way from Callowmere, a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, but the washhouse bell that summoned the linen women at dawn.

Hours early. In the middle of the night. Which meant it was not calling them to work.

It was calling her.
