## 20. The Republic Ends Quietly

The news came the way most news came to Callowmere: late, secondhand, and carried by someone nobody trusted.

It was the baker's boy who brought it up from the ferry landing, breathless, flour still dusting his knuckles, shouting before he was even through the market square that the capital had fallen. That soldiers were weeping at the causeway post. That Colonel Brase's tent was being struck in the dark.

Noor heard it from the widow Verel's kitchen, where she had spent the night mending laundry that wasn't hers, under a name that wasn't hers, in a body she had only recently learned to inhabit without flinching. The word *fallen* went through the room like cold air through a cracked pane. Nobody cheered. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and the thing she would remember longest: nobody cheered. They set down their cups. They looked at one another. Old Verel crossed herself with a hand that shook, not from fear but from something older and harder to name—the reflex of a woman who had buried enough regimes to know that the death of one never announced what the next would be.

"The capital," someone said, as if tasting it. "Fallen."

As if the capital were a thing that could stand at all, after all these years of leaning on towns like Callowmere.

Noor walked out into the street because she could not bear the walls. The rain had begun an hour before dawn, a thin, persistent autumn rain that turned the cobbles the color of wet slate and made the town smell of stone and river and woodsmoke. She had no coat. She had stopped needing one, somehow; the weeks of being no one had worn her down to something weatherproof.

She stood in the middle of the square, in the rain, and listened.

That was how you knew, she thought. Not by the thunder everyone expected. You knew by the silence underneath it—the absence of boots on the quay, the absence of the morning requisition detail, the absence of the proclamation reader's flat voice droning from the town hall steps. For nine days and then nine more, Callowmere had lived inside a sound, the constant low hum of occupation, and now the hum had stopped, and the quiet it left behind was so total it rang.

* * *

By midmorning the truth of it assembled itself, piece by piece, the way everything in Callowmere assembled itself—through gossip, through glances, through Sef Oduya poling his ferry back across the grey water with news folded into his pockets like contraband.

The Ninth Column had withdrawn overnight. Not routed, not defeated—withdrawn, which was somehow worse. Brase had received his orders sometime after midnight, and by two o'clock the camp on the far meadow was struck, the wagons loaded, the horses saddled, and three hundred men marched south through the rain without a single lantern lit. They left behind their latrines, their ration tins, their dead—two graves behind the bridge tollhouse, marked with numbered stakes instead of names—and they left behind the paperwork.

That was the part Noor couldn't stop turning over. They had burned nothing. In all the histories she'd read, in all her mother's whispered lessons about how power ends, there was always fire—archives ablaze, records fed to stoves, the past going up in smoke while officers saved their own skins. But the Ninth Column had simply *left* the ledger. It lay where Elias had last indexed it, on the long table in the requisitioned archive, under a canvas sheet gone stiff with damp, its cipher columns and resettlement annotations intact, waiting for whoever came next to pick it up and begin again.

Empires don't burn their books when they fall, Noor thought, standing in the archive doorway that afternoon with rain dripping off her hair onto the threshold stones. They just stop reading them. And someone else starts.

The proclamations told the same story. Every wall in Callowmere had carried them for weeks—*BY ORDER OF THE NINTH COLUMN, CIVIC HARMONY SHALL BE OBSERVED*, the curfew notices, the resettlement announcements with their bland bureaucratic mercy, the reward notice for an unidentified female courier that had once had the power to stop her heart in the street. Now the rain worked at them steadily. The paste gave way first, at the corners. The paper curled, darkened, sagged. By evening the reward notice hung off the chandler's wall like shed skin, half its words dissolved into grey pulp, the description of her face running in long ink tears until it described no one at all.

She peeled it off herself. Just took hold of the soggy corner and pulled, slowly, feeling the glue resist and release, resist and release, until the whole sheet came away and lay in her hands, soft as cloth, illegible as a dream. Her invisibility, returned to her in pieces. She didn't know whether to laugh or to bury it.

She did neither. She dropped it in the gutter and let the rain take it apart the rest of the way.

* * *

They found Aldous Fen's chair still in the archive cellar.

Noor went down alone, carrying a lantern because the single high window let in almost nothing now, between the rain and the season. The cellar smelled of mildew and old paper and, faintly, of the man himself—tobacco and lamp oil—a smell already fading, already becoming memory rather than fact. His stool sat pushed back from the copying table at the angle of a man who intended to return. A stub of pencil lay where it had rolled from his fingers the day they took him. Someone—Elias, probably, in those last days before he surrendered himself—had laid a cloth over the ledger, tidy to the last, clerk's habit surviving even betrayal.

Noor sat down on Fen's stool and did not copy anything. She just sat, letting the lantern light pool around her, listening to the rain find its way through the ground above and drip somewhere in the dark.

He had lied about a page count for her. She kept coming back to that. An old man who had spent forty years making the province's numbers agree, who believed in the ledger the way priests believe in scripture, had looked at Colonel Brase across an interrogation table and given him a false count—and been taken away in irons for administrative obstruction, a phrase so bloodless it sounded like a clerical error rather than a sentence. He had known, Noor thought, exactly what he was doing. He had weighed his life against a page number and found the arithmetic acceptable. That was the kind of man he'd been, and she hadn't known it until it cost him everything, and now the stool sat empty and the pencil had rolled into the shadow under the table and no one knew where they had taken him, or if there was anywhere left to take him to, now that the taking-them-there had packed up and marched south in the rain.

She picked up the pencil stub and put it in her pocket. It seemed right that someone should carry it.

When she came up into the grey afternoon light, the town was in the streets. All of them, or near enough—out of doorways and workshops and the church that had been closed since the curfew began, standing in the rain without umbrellas, looking south down the river road where the Column had gone. Nobody spoke much. There was a strange etiquette to it, Noor observed, a courtesy the living extended to the moment: you did not cheer in front of your neighbor whose son had been shot at the bridge; you did not celebrate while Fen sat in some cell or grave; you stood in the rain and let your face do what it needed to do, and everyone pretended not to see.

But under the silence, Noor felt it moving through the crowd like current through water: they had seen her. All of them. The laundress who wasn't a laundress, the widow who appeared the week the courier vanished, the Eastwick girl whose mother's name sat in a confiscated book. They had watched her walk past checkpoints in borrowed grief, had hidden her in pantries and lofts and the space behind Verel's woodpile, had said nothing to the patrols, nothing to the informers, nothing even to one another—and now that the danger had marched away, they looked at her across the square, openly, one face after another, and nodded.

Not bowed. Nodded. As you nod to someone whose name you know.

Marta Verel pressed a warm roll into her hand without a word. The cooper's wife touched her elbow in passing. A boy she didn't know called out, "Miss Eastwick!" from a rooftop, testing the name aloud like a coin bitten for gold, and grinned when she didn't deny it.

For nine days she had traded on being forgettable, and the trade had nearly killed her, and now here was the terrible, disorienting discovery waiting on the other side of it: they remembered everything. Every page sewn into a mourning shawl. Every lie told at a checkpoint. The town had been keeping its own ledger the whole time, entries written in looks and silences, and Noor Eastwick was in it, in ink, and there was no dropping this cargo, no denying this memory, and she found—to her genuine astonishment—that she did not want to.

* * *

Sef Oduya was on the quay at dusk, coiling rope with the unhurried economy of a man who had decided the war was over and the rope agreed with him. The river ran high and brown with the rain. On the far bank, the meadow where the Column had camped was a churned brown scar already going soft at the edges, grass beginning its patient work of forgetting.

"They left at two," Sef said, without looking up. "I heard the wheels on the causeway. Counted them. Forty-one wagons." He tied off a coil. "Forty-one wagons to carry away a republic. Didn't need more."

"Is that what it was?" Noor asked. "A republic? I keep trying to remember."

"A republic is a ledger," Sef said. "Somebody writes in it, somebody gets erased. Only difference is who holds the pen this year." He finally looked at her, and his face held the weathered calm she had come to trust on the water. "Your mother would have said something sharper."

"My mother would have said it's raining and the proclamations are peeling and next spring someone will paste up new ones."

Sef laughed, short and real, then sobered. His eyes went south, toward the marshes, toward the causeway post where a sergeant had once refused an illegal order and a colonel had chosen not to test him. "There'll be new ones," he agreed. "Whoever comes next always brings paper. But—" he tapped his temple, "—they can't requisition what's in here. Your mother taught me that too, though she never knew she was teaching it."

Noor looked down the quay. The lamplighter had not come; the Column had taken the oil. So the lamps stayed dark, and in the gathering dusk the town showed itself by firelight instead, window by window, candle by candle, ordinary flames in ordinary rooms, and it occurred to Noor that she was watching Callowmere relight itself one small defiance at a time.

"Elias," she said. "Has anyone—"

"Nothing yet." Sef's voice gentled. "The prison wagons went south with the Column. Fen too, if he lived to be loaded. We'll know when the roads open. Or we won't, and we'll light candles anyway." He put a hand briefly, heavily, on her shoulder—the touch of a man paying a debt he insisted was still outstanding. "Go home, Noor Eastwick. Sleep under your own roof tonight. Tomorrow there's work."

"What work?"

Sef looked at her as though the answer were obvious, because it was.

"The ledger's still on the table," he said. "Half of it's ash and half of it's in our heads, and none of it's written down. Somebody's going to have to sit in that cellar and start counting Callowmere again—properly, this time. Name by name." He smiled, tired and crooked. "And everybody in this town knows exactly whose handwriting that should be."

* * *

That night, in her mother's house, which had become her house without her ever deciding it, Noor emptied her pocket onto the kitchen table by lantern light: the pencil stub, a scrap of the dissolved proclamation she'd fished back out of habit, and the single copied ledger page she had carried through three checkpoints and never managed to destroy.

She smoothed the page flat. The rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere south, a republic was finishing its dying in a language of surrender documents and transfer orders, and here in Callowmere the only sound of its passing was water finding the gutters, and paper rotting on the walls, and a woman sitting alone at a table with a dead pencil stub and a living list of names.

Her mother's name was on the page. Meret Eastwick. Listed for resettlement. Noor ran her thumb beneath the entry, over the cipher column her mother had invented as a child's game and a nation had turned into a weapon, and made herself a promise in the shorthand only two people had ever shared—one of them gone, one of them taken, both of them, somehow, still teaching her.

Tomorrow she would sharpen the pencil. Tomorrow she would sit in Aldous Fen's empty chair and open the ledger and call the first name aloud, and the town would answer.

Tonight, she lit the lantern and set it in the window—facing north, toward whatever roads would bring back the living, and the lists, and the debts owed to the dead.

The flame steadied. Outside, the rain went on quietly dismantling the republic, one proclamation at a time.
