## 21. Rebuilding the Record

The rain came the morning after the soldiers left, as if the sky had been waiting for permission. It fell on the proclamations nailed over the archive door until the paper softened and peeled, curling away from the boards like dead leaves from a branch, until by noon the words—ORDER OF THE NINTH COLUMN, BY DECREE OF THE PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY—hung in wet ribbons that anyone could tear down with one hand. Noor did not tear them down herself. She stood in the square with her collar up and watched a boy of perhaps nine do it, laughing, and thought: this is how it ends. Not with thunder. With rain on glue.

The town hall smelled of ash and abandonment when she pushed open its doors. The Ninth Column had withdrawn overnight, the way an animal leaves a den—leaving behind what it could not digest. Broken crates. A boot print in candle wax, enormous, going nowhere. The interrogation table where Aldous Fen had sat under lamplight while they read him his own town like a sentence. And in the cellar below, the archive room with its empty shelving, its scattered card files, its single window high on the wall letting in a gray aquarium light.

Noor descended the stairs with the satchel against her chest, the way she had carried it through the marshes, through the causeway post, through the terrible ordinary days after. The surviving pages were wrapped in oilcloth now, forty-one sheets out of two hundred and six, water-stained at their edges where Sef's boat had shipped the river over its gunwales on the worst night. She unwrapped them on Fen's old worktable and stood looking at them for a long time.

Forty-one names written in ink. One hundred and sixty-five names that existed now only in the heads of people who had mostly spent their lives trying not to be noticed.

She heard footsteps on the stairs—Sef's uneven tread, his left knee gone stiff since the cold water—and turned to find him filling the doorway, ducking under the lintel out of habit though he was not tall enough to need to. He carried two chairs, one under each arm.

"Fen's is still down here," he said. "Thought we'd want it."

"Set them all up," Noor said. "We'll be here a while."

He set the chairs down without comment, but he placed Fen's at the head of the table, facing the others, and neither of them remarked on that either. Some things were done simply by doing them.

* * *

They began the next day with a bell.

Sef had salvaged the ferry bell from his scuttled boat—it was all the river had left him—and hung it in the archive window frame, and at eight in the morning he rang it three times, the old signal for *the crossing runs*, and people came. Not many. Eleven the first day, standing in the cellar's dimness with their hats in their hands as if they were attending a funeral, which in a sense they were. Noor spread the surviving pages flat under glass panes lifted from picture frames, and above them she pinned a blank sheet, and beside the sheet she set the pen and ink.

"We write nothing," she told them, "until it has been said aloud."

An old woman near the back—the cooper's widow, Marta Ilves, whom Noor had never once seen speak to anyone voluntarily—cleared her throat. "Said aloud to whom?"

"To everyone." Noor looked around the room, at faces she had passed ten thousand times without meeting. "A name spoken once can be denied. A name spoken before witnesses becomes testimony. That was always their weapon—we let them do the counting, so only their counting existed. We count ourselves now."

It was Marta Ilves who spoke the first name. Her own husband's, given in full—given with the year of his birth and the year of his death and the fact that he had once won a ribbon at the autumn fair for a barrel with staves so tight it held wine without pitch—which no ledger had ever recorded and no ledger ever would have. Noor wrote it. The scratch of the nib was very loud in the quiet.

After that, the names came slowly, then not slowly at all.

* * *

They came every day for weeks. Farmers off the north road with mud to the knee. The ferry families. Children sent on errands who stayed, sitting on the stairs, absorbing the liturgy of it. A woman named Petra Voss arrived carrying a prayer book in which her grandmother had pressed four generations of birthdates between the psalms, dates that contradicted the official census by as much as eleven years, because the census-takers of the old republic had rounded people down the way merchants rounded coin. Noor took the prayer book in both hands and felt the weight of it—paper and board and pressed flowers—and understood that she was holding a rival archive, older and more honest than anything the ministries had produced.

Some days were harder than others. There was the day the tanner Brenner came to give the names of his household and got through his wife and daughters and then stopped, pen hovering over the line for his son, and could not say the boy's name aloud—could not make the sound—because the boy had been taken at the bridge checkpoint in the first week of the occupation, shot for running, and saying it would make it a thing that had happened to a person rather than a thing that had happened to everyone. The room waited. Sef rang the ferry bell once, softly, the signal for *hold fast*, and after a long minute Brenner's mouth worked and the name came out broken into syllables, and Noor wrote each syllable as it came, and when it was whole on the page Brenner sat down heavily in Fen's chair and wept with his face in his hands, and no one moved, and the ink dried.

There was the day a woman Noor did not recognize gave a name that matched one of the surviving pages—a resettlement annotation, column seven, the cipher shorthand her mother had taught her as a game rendered suddenly, horribly literal—and Noor had to stand, walk up the stairs, and breathe in the rain-smelling air of the square for a quarter hour before she could hold a pen again. Sef found her there. He did not ask if she was well. He leaned on the doorframe and looked out at the flooded quay with her.

"Your mother falsified my daughter's entry twenty years ago," he said. "One line of ink. I've owed that line my life since." He tapped the doorframe twice. "Ink is small. But it carries."

"I know what it carries," Noor said. "I've been smuggling it."

"Yes," said Sef. "And now you're building the barge."

* * *

Elias Vant came on the nineteenth day.

Noor saw him first from the window—standing across the square in a civilian coat that fit him badly, a soldier's posture ruined by a clerk's hesitation, looking at the archive door as a man looks at water he intends to enter knowing it will be cold. She went down and opened the door before he could decide.

"They released us in the general amnesty," he said, before any greeting. "When the capital fell. Brase was already gone—transferred west, or fled, depending on whose account you believe. There was no one left to sign our detention orders, so technically none of us were ever detained. The paperwork dissolved faster than we did." He almost smiled. It was the first time she had seen him attempt one.

"The paperwork always dissolves first," Noor said. "That's how you know the power's gone."

He looked past her into the cellar, at the tables, the glass-covered pages, the bell in the window. "May I—"

"You were posted to three checkpoints," Noor said. "You waved me through two of them and surrendered yourself at the third. You've served your apprenticeship. Come down and take a chair."

He worked differently than the rest of them, and better. Where the townsfolk remembered in impressions—he lived on the mill lane, he had a limp, he owed money to someone—Elias remembered in structure. He had indexed the confiscated ledger for the Ninth Column; he had transcribed Fen's interrogation; his mind held the skeleton of the original book the way a hand remembers a tool. When a witness gave a name, Elias knew which section it belonged to, which marginal codes had flanked it, which neighboring entries would have shared its page. He rebuilt the architecture around each name so that no name stood naked on the record but was clothed again in its context: debts, taxes, the small bureaucratic shadows that had made these people legible to the state, and therefore worth saving from it.

On his fourth day he paused over a reconstruction of the census roll and said, quietly, "Your mother's handwriting is in the original margins. I catalogued it. She annotated the cipher key itself—there are corrections in her hand, dated years back."

"She designed it," Noor said. "Fen told me. In the end, in his letter, folded into the laundry."

Elias nodded as though confirming a suspicion he'd carried since the bridge toll. "Then half of what we're rebuilding was hers to begin with."

Noor looked down at the page, at the fresh ink drying beside the ghost-marks of the old. *Survived in memory.* Her mother had written those words as dry administrative notation, a filing category, and her daughter had turned them into a battle cry without ever meaning to. Grief was a strange inheritance. It fit her hands perfectly.

* * *

Fen did not come.

Noor wrote to the prison hospital in Vareth twice and received one reply, dictated by a nurse: the registrar's health was poor, his mind wandered, he asked often about the weather in Callowmere. Whether he had refused to come or simply could not, the letter did not say, and Noor chose not to press, because some mercies are also doors best left unexamined. But his chair remained at the head of the table, and every morning she straightened it—aligned its legs with the floorboards, squared its back to the shelves—as if preparing a place at a table where the guest might yet arrive. Sef watched her do it for a week and then began doing it himself on the days she forgot.

"He taught me the page count lie," she said one evening, the two of them alone after hours, the lamps low. "When Brase interrogated him. He miscounted the folios on purpose—two hundred and six, he said, when it was two hundred and seven—and they arrested him for obstruction, and that arithmetic bought me the time to copy everything. He knew exactly what a wrong number was worth. And now I can't even bring him the finished book to check."

Sef coiled a length of rope he had no use for, his hands needing occupation the way old boatmen's hands always did. "Then leave the chair," he said. "Empty chairs keep better company than filled ones sometimes. They hold the shape of the person without arguing with them."

* * *

Winter deepened. The river froze at its edges and the bell rang anyway, muffled, faithful. The book grew.

By the thaw it ran to three hundred pages—not the ledger reborn but something larger, something the original had never dared to be. The old provincial ledger had counted people the way a tax counts them: as units, liabilities, columns of exposure. The new book recorded what could not be taxed or executed or resettled. It recorded that Yannik Ostrer's grandmother had sung at every wedding in Callowmere for forty years and that the tune was called *The Ferryman's Regret* and that three people still living could carry it. It recorded that the flood of the year the twins were born reached the third step of the chapel and that the date was disputed by two witnesses, both of whom were entered, both of whose versions were preserved. It recorded debts forgiven during the hard winter, quarrels mended, the name of the boy shot at the bridge—Tomas Brenner, fourteen, who was afraid of deep water and had run anyway—with the circumstances given plainly, without euphemism, because euphemism was the enemy's native language and this book would not speak it.

Every name spoken aloud before it was written. Every page read back to the room before it was sewn in. On Sundays Noor read the week's additions from the top of the cellar stairs, her voice carrying up through the town hall into the square, and people gathered outside to hear their neighbors' names pronounced in a government-free air, and the sound of it—she had no word for the sound of it except that it was the opposite of a proclamation.

One evening in late spring, Elias found her after closing, standing at Fen's chair with the completed first volume in her arms.

"It needs a title page," he said.

"It has one." She turned the book so he could see. Beneath the year, beneath the words *Provincial Register of Callowmere, Restored*, a single line in her steadiest hand:

*Compiled from memory, which does not surrender.*

Below that, a space had been left—an honest gap, ruled but unfilled—for the signature of the registrar of Callowmere, should a registrar ever sit in that chair again.

Elias studied the empty line for a long moment. Then he said, carefully, the way he said everything that mattered, "There's a letter. From Vareth. It came to the town hall yesterday and the interim council didn't know who—" He stopped, started again. "It's from the prison hospital, Noor. It's addressed to you."

The lamp flame stood very still. Outside, the thaw-water ran loud in the gutters, the river gathering itself for another season of carrying things north. Noor set the book down on the table, squared Fen's chair one last time, and took the letter in both hands, feeling the weight of it—paper and board and whatever mercy or grief the post had pressed between its folds.

She broke the seal.
