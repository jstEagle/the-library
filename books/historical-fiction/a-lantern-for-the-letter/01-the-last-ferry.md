## 1. The Last Ferry

The wind came off the water that morning with the smell of iron in it, and Marguerite Sandoval knew before she opened her eyes that the season was turning. She had lived on Pointe-Aubier long enough to read the air the way other women read letters — not the words of it, but what lay underneath. October had arrived early this year, arriving in the night like a guest who did not wait to be invited.

She rose in the dark, as she always did, and climbed the ninety-one steps to the lamp room, counting them out of habit rather than need. Henri had counted them once, joking that a keeper should know her stairs the way a priest knows his prayers. *Her* stairs, he'd said, even then. Even before.

The light had burned all night without trouble. She trimmed the wick anyway, wiped the glass clean of salt haze, and stood for a moment watching the beam sweep out across the gray water — once, twice, patient as breathing. Somewhere beyond that reach of light lay the mainland: the town, the railway, the ministry buildings where men signed papers that became wars or treaties or decrees. Marguerite had never seen any of it matter here. History, she had decided years ago, belonged to capitals and newspapers. It happened to other people, in other places, and by the time it reached an island it was already old news, already softened into talk over Madame Roux's counter. Her rock had its own calendar: tides, storms, fish, the ferry schedule. Nothing on it required a newspaper to explain.

She believed this the way one believes in a wall one has leaned against for years. It held her weight. It had always held her weight.

Down again, ninety-one steps, and the house smelled of coffee and woodsmoke, and Céline was at the table with her boots already on, which meant she was eager, which meant she was fourteen.

"You'll want to braid your hair," Marguerite said. "There's wind."

"There's always wind."

"There's always hair."

Céline laughed and let her mother work the braids without complaint, sitting still for the two minutes it took, which was two minutes more stillness than the girl ever granted anyone else. In the window behind her, the harbor was showing itself piece by piece as the dark gave way — the black hulls of the fishing boats, the low stone arm of the jetty, and beyond it the tidal bridge, its causeway just now emerging from the retreating water like something being slowly confessed to.

"The *Pélican* comes at nine," Céline said. "Madame Roux says it's the last crossing until spring, if the forecasts hold."

"Madame Roux says many things."

"This one Father Anselme repeated, so it's practically scripture."

Marguerite tied off the second braid. "Then pack your scripture carefully. And your Latin primer. And if Monsieur Girard at the school asks after me—"

"I'll tell him you're well and the light is lit and you haven't drowned." Céline grinned. "He asks every term. He thinks the sea is going to take you."

"The sea has no imagination," Marguerite said. "It only takes what stands still."

But she kissed the top of her daughter's head anyway, and let her hand rest there a moment longer than the joke allowed.

* * *

At half past eight they walked down to the jetty together, Céline pulling her trunk on a little cart whose wheel complained the whole way, and the island turned out as it always did for departures — not out of ceremony, but because on Pointe-Aubier anything that moved was worth watching. Tomas was there with his nets, mending by habit even though the *Pélican*'s approach was still a rumor of smoke on the horizon. Madame Roux stood outside her shop with her arms folded against the cold, dispensing last-minute parcels to mothers who would not see their children until Easter. Father Anselme had come down from the church in his good coat, his breath making small clouds that rose toward heaven whether he willed them or not.

"Mind the mainland," Tomas said to Céline, not looking up from his net. "It'll mind you back."

"That's very philosophical, Tomas."

"It's free, too."

The *Pélican* came in on the flood tide, a stubby steam ferry wearing a beard of gull-white droppings, and the deckhands took Céline's trunk with the indifference of men who had lifted ten thousand trunks and would lift ten thousand more. Marguerite embraced her daughter on the gangway and felt, as she always felt, the exact weight and warmth of her, filing it away the way a miser files a coin.

"Easter," Céline said into her shoulder.

"Easter. Write when the mail boat runs."

"Write when there's anything to write about," Céline corrected her, pulling back. "Which there never is."

"Good," Marguerite said. "Boring is safe."

The girl boarded. The gangway came up. The *Pélican* backed water, turned its blunt bow toward the open sound, and grew smaller and smaller until it was smoke, and then a suggestion of smoke, and then nothing, and the gulls settled back onto the jetty posts as though dismissing the whole affair.

Marguerite stood a while after the others had gone. The tide was beginning to turn; she could hear it change under the bridge, that deep slow intake of breath the channel took twice a day. By tonight the causeway stones would be awash. By tomorrow, if the weather held to its new temper, the water would stand across them altogether, and the island would be what it always became in winter: a world entire, population forty-one souls and one light.

She found she was smiling. She examined the smile and decided it was honest. There was loneliness in her — there had been since Henri died, four years past, a loneliness she kept in a drawer with his letters and opened only at night — but loneliness was a tax, not a wound. She paid it gladly for the tower, for the work, for the clean arithmetic of a life that could be measured in lamps trimmed and ships passed safely. Contentment, she told herself, walking back up the path. Contentment, with the door barred against the winter.

* * *

The storm signs came faster than the almanac promised.

By the third day of October the barometer at the foot of the stairs had begun to fall with an eagerness she didn't like, dropping through numbers the way a man descends stairs two at a time. By the fifth day the swell was coming in long and green from the southwest, waves that traveled too far to have been born of any local wind — waves sent ahead, messengers announcing some vast argument taking place out over the Atlantic where no one could see it.

On the evening of the sixth day, Marguerite climbed her ninety-one steps and lit the lamp, and while it warmed she stood at the gallery rail and looked south along the coast road toward the tidal bridge, three kilometers distant, a thin gray line stitched between island and mainland.

There were lights on it. Lanterns, moving. Men.

She watched for a long time, her shawl doing little against the wind. The bridge authority kept no men on the causeway after October; everyone knew that; the whole island knew the bridge closed itself and stayed closed, the sea performing the sealing so no clerk had to. But there they were — three points of light, moving with purpose, stopping, crouching, moving again. Working at something at the far span.

Hammering? No. Too far to hear over the wind. But their posture said hammering, or chaining, or posting.

Marguerite went inside and dug out the telescope, an old navy glass Henri had bought at auction, brass worn to brightness by hands long dead. She braced it on the gallery rail and brought the far lights close.

Men in uniform. Not bridge workers' canvas — coats, official coats, and beside them on the causeway a stack of planking and what looked like chain, great coils of it, being dragged across the roadway of the far span. As she watched, one of the figures raised a maul, and though the sound was eaten by the distance and the wind, she saw the blow land, and saw the plank it struck settle into place across the bridge like a bone set wrong.

They were closing it early. Sealing it by hand, ahead of the storms, ahead of the season, ahead of any announcement that might have reached the island by the ordinary mails. The *Pélican* had been the last ferry, then — not scheduled to be, but made so, retroactively, by whatever order those men were nailing into place.

Marguerite lowered the glass. Her heart was beating with a slowness she distrusted, the calm of the body before it understands what the mind has glimpsed.

Governments closed bridges for reasons. Weather, mostly. Sometimes money. Sometimes — and here the newspapers she disdained flickered at the edge of her thoughts, headlines about lists and decrees and names, the sort of news she skimmed past because it concerned capitals, not rocks —

Sometimes other reasons.

She looked south again, but the men and their lanterns had moved out of sight behind the span's stone footing, and the bridge lay dark except for the running water beginning to sheet across its lowest stretch, the tide coming home.

Behind her, in the lamp room, the light turned and turned, sweeping its patient arc over a sea that had nothing yet to show her.

Marguerite went in to log the weather, as the regulations required. Wind rising from the southwest. Swell abnormal. Visibility poor.

And under it, after a moment, because the glass had shown her what it showed her, she wrote: *Bridge sealed early. No notice given.*

She sat looking at those five words for a long time, in a house that suddenly felt less like contentment than like a held breath — and far below, at the edge of the lamplight, at the base of her own dark rocks, something knocked once against the stone, soft as a knuckle on a door.
