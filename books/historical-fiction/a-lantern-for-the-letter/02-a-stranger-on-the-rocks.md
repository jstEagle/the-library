## 2. A Stranger on the Rocks

The storm came in from the southwest the way storms always did, without asking. Marguerite had watched it build all afternoon — a bruise-colored wall stacking itself over the mainland, the gulls going quiet, the sea changing its mind about being a sea at all.

By dusk she had the shutters dogged and the lamp lit, though the light was less mercy than habit tonight. No boat would cross in this. The last ferry was two weeks gone; her daughter's letters would pile up in the post office at Saint-Aubin until the weather relented or didn't. She climbed the spiral stair one hundred and twelve steps to check the wick, trimmed it, came down again, and told herself the work was enough to fill a life.

It was not enough. It had not been enough for three years.

She was in the kitchen warming milk when she heard it — not a sound so much as a wrongness in the sound of the storm, a grinding beneath the wind like something enormous chewing stone. She stood with the pan in her hand and counted. The sea hit the point below the tower every eleven seconds; that rhythm had been her lullaby for nine years. The grinding broke it.

Then a second sound, sharper: wood splitting.

Marguerite set down the pan. She took the oilskin from its peg by the door and the lantern from its hook, and she went out into a night that tried immediately to take both from her.

* * *

The path down to the rocks was a goat track Henri had widened with pick and patience, and she knew it blind, which was fortunate, because seeing it would have stopped her. Wind came off the water flat and hard as a slammed door. Spray went over the point in sheets, and where the lantern above threw its revolving eye across the scene, she saw what the sea had done.

There was a boat on the ledges. Or there had been a boat. It lay broken-backed among the black rocks, planks scattered like spilled letters, and the water kept reaching up to take pieces back. A small craft — a skiff, maybe, or a fishing yawl from somewhere far enough away that no islander would be fool enough to sail it tonight.

"Hello!" she shouted, and the wind ate the word.

She got lower, hand over hand along the chain Henri had bolted into the rock for exactly this purpose, though in nine years she had used it only for salvage and once for a drowned sheep. The tide was coming in. Whatever the sea wanted tonight, it meant to keep.

She saw him at the third ledge, wedged in a cleft where the water couldn't quite finish its work. A man, face-down, one arm hooked through a loop of rope, the other arm wrapped around a leather satchel strapped tight to his chest with the stubbornness of a drowning man's arithmetic — the bag first, then the breath.

Marguerite knelt and got a fist into his collar. He was heavier than he looked, soaked through, and for one ugly moment the sea and she played tug-of-war with him and she thought the sea would win. Then his free hand found her wrist and gripped it, and he made a sound — not words, just a sound, the sound of a man deciding to live — and between them they got him up onto the shelf above the reach of the swell.

He coughed seawater onto the rock. He did not let go of the satchel.

"Can you walk?" she shouted.

His eyes opened. Even in the wild strobe of the lantern light she could see they were pale, gray-green, unfocused, then focusing — on her face, her oilskin, the light wheeling above them. His lips moved. The wind took whatever he said.

"Walk," she said again, and hauled him up.

* * *

It took the better part of an hour to get him up the path. Twice he fell; twice she caught him, and the second time his whole weight came against her and she felt how cold he was, cold as the rocks themselves, cold enough that her own bones ached in sympathy. At the door she stripped off his coat — he fought her on it, one-handed, still clutching the satchel, until exhaustion won — wrapped him in the blanket from Henri's chair, and put him before the stove with a hot brick against his back and brandy between his teeth.

He was forty perhaps, or fifty, hard to tell with the skin scrubbed raw and the hair plastered dark against his skull. A long face, a scholar's stoop even half-drowned. When the shivering eased enough for speech, he looked around the kitchen — at the copper pots, the chart of the coast pinned above the dresser, the photograph of a man in a keeper's uniform with a mustache and kind eyes — and something moved through his expression that Marguerite could not name and did not like.

"You are the keeper," he said. His French was good but accented, vowels shaped somewhere east of here.

"I am the keeper's widow."

"The keeper." His voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Sandoval."

She went very still. "How do you know that name?"

He closed his eyes. Water ran from his hair into the blanket. For a moment she thought he'd fainted, or chosen to. Then he said, "Henri Sandoval. Pointe-Aubier. I am looking—" a cough bent him double, "—I have been looking for this house for six weeks."

Nobody crossed to this island in winter by accident. Nobody sailed a small boat into the October gales toward a rock with one house on it unless the house was the destination. Marguerite stood with the brandy bottle in her hand and felt the evening tilt beneath her, the way the floor of a ship tilts.

"Who are you?" she said. "And how did you come to be in my sea?"

"Elias Brandt." He said it as if handing her something breakable. "A cartographer. And I came—" He looked down at the satchel, still strapped to him like a child refusing to be put down. "Madame. Before I say anything else. Your husband is dead?"

"Three years. The winter fever."

Something left his face then, some tension held so long it had become architecture, and what remained underneath was older and more tired. "Then I am sorry," he said, "for arriving too late and too early at once."

"I don't understand you."

"No." He managed something that was nearly a smile. "But you will ask me anyway, because you are your husband's wife, and in his letters he wrote—"

She set the bottle down hard. "You read Henri's letters?"

"He wrote them to me." Brandt's hands, wrapped around the tin cup she'd given him, were steady now, and she hated them for it. "Amsterdam, in 'eighty-eight. Your husband fell into the Herengracht canal. Drunk, he would want me to say — he was celebrating a chart corrected, a coastline forgiven. The water was freezing. Four men walked past him. I pulled him out."

Marguerite sat down slowly on the bench across the stove. She knew this story. She knew it the way you know a story someone tells at their own wedding — the canal, the stranger who fished him out, the friendship conducted thereafter entirely on paper because the stranger lived beyond maps' edges. Henri had spoken of Elias Brandt the way other men spoke of brothers lost young. There were letters in the cedar chest upstairs. Dozens of them. She had never thrown them out, though she had never been able to finish rereading them either.

"You're older than your letters," she said. "In his letters you're younger."

"We are all younger in letters, madame. It is why people write them."

Outside the storm leaned its shoulder against the house. Above them the lamp turned, patient, indifferent. Marguerite looked at this man dripping in her dead husband's blanket, in her dead husband's chair, holding a satchel he had carried into the sea rather than release, and she understood — with the particular clarity that arrives only when it is least welcome — that the calm season of her life had ended tonight along with everything else the storm took.

"Why here?" she asked. "Why now? What's in the bag?"

Brandt looked at the satchel. Then at her. And she saw him weigh something — not whether to lie to her, she thought, but which truth to spend.

"Henri promised me," he said, "that if I ever needed walls and silence, there was a house at the edge of France where the debt of a life would be honored." He lifted his chin toward the ceiling, toward the tower, toward the cedar chest full of paper. "I have come to collect a dead man's word. And in this bag, madame, are maps that certain men in Paris want burned — and certain men in Berlin want very much alive."

* * *

Later, when she had fed him soup he was too proud to admit he needed and settled him in the bunk room where visiting pilots used to sleep, Marguerite stood alone in the kitchen and listened to the storm work the point below.

She should have asked more questions. She should have asked what he'd done, whom he'd angered, what exactly traveled in that satchel that men in two capitals had opinions about. She should have remembered that promises are cheap to the dead and expensive to the living.

Instead she went upstairs, knelt at the cedar chest, and lifted out the topmost letter. Henri's handwriting, slanting and sure: *Elias, if you ever need it, the door at Pointe-Aubier opens from the inside, and my wife keeps the key.*

My wife keeps the key.

Below her, faint under the wind, she could hear him moving in the bunk room — the small sounds of a man checking that his satchel, his maps, his ruin, were all still there.

Marguerite sat on the floor with the letter in her lap until the candle burned low, and did not sleep at all.
