## 3. What Henri Promised

He woke to the smell of tar and woodsmoke and the sound of a woman arguing with a stove.

For a long time Elias Brandt lay still and let the argument wash over him, because it meant he was alive. The ceiling above him was low, whitewashed, cracked along one seam like a dried riverbed. A wool blanket scratched his chin. His hands — he looked at them with the detached curiosity of a man reading about himself in a newspaper — were wrapped in strips of linen gone gray at the knuckles.

The stove won the argument. He heard the door of the firebox clang shut, heard the woman mutter something in French that was not quite a curse and not quite a prayer, and then her footsteps crossed the floor toward him and stopped.

"You've been asleep two days," Marguerite Sandoval said. "I thought perhaps you intended to make a career of it."

He turned his head. She stood in the doorway with a basin in her hands and the gray light of morning behind her, and she was not what he had expected. Henri's letters had described a wife who laughed easily; the woman looking down at him now had the face of someone who had stopped laughing some years ago and did not intend to apologize for it.

"Madame Sandoval." His voice came out as gravel. "I would rise."

"Don't," she said. "You came ashore like a sack of wet rope. The doctor on the mainland is four hours away by good weather, and this is not good weather. Lie there and be a patient. It will annoy you less than being a corpse."

He almost smiled. It hurt his ribs to do even that much, so he filed the smile away for later use.

She set the basin on the table beside him, wrung out a cloth, and began unwrapping his hands without asking permission. Her fingers were quick and impersonal, a keeper's fingers, used to machinery and salt damage. When she reached the gash across his left palm she paused, just slightly — the pause of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like — and then cleaned it anyway, briskly, as though the wound belonged to a stranger.

"Rope burn," she said. "You held on."

"I held on."

"To what?"

"A boat," he said. "Briefly."

"And then?"

"Then to the rocks. Less briefly."

* * *

She fed him fish soup that afternoon, sitting across the table with her own bowl untouched, watching him eat with the patience of a customs officer. The keeper's quarters were small and fiercely tidy: charts rolled and tied, brass fittings polished until they shamed the daylight, a photograph on the shelf of a man with Henri's heavy shoulders and Henri's crooked smile, wearing a uniform that fit him badly.

Elias looked at the photograph too long. She noticed. Women like Marguerite Sandoval noticed everything; it was probably why they survived winters alone on rocks.

"You knew my husband," she said.

It was not a question. He set down the spoon.

"I knew him in Brest. Eleven years ago." He spoke French carefully, the way one walks on ice. "I was making coastal surveys. Your husband was — " he searched for the word, and found the true one, and decided to give it to her whole — "drunk. It was February. There was a canal behind the arsenal, and the ice had gone soft at the edges, and he walked home the short way because the long way required climbing stairs."

"He never told me that part."

"He never told anyone that part. I fished him out. He was very dignified about it afterward, in the way of drowning men. He bought me dinner and swore me to silence, and we became friends in the manner of men who have no reason to be friends — a German mapmaker and a French gunner's mate — which is to say, completely."

Marguerite was quiet. Outside the window the sea made its long gray complaint against the point.

"In his letters," she said at last, "he called you *the German who argues*. He wrote that you argued about everything. Borders. Tides. Whether Breton butter was better than Bavarian."

"It is better," Elias said. "That was never an argument, only a correction."

Something moved across her face — grief, or its cousin — and was put away again, folded small, like the maps in his satchel.

"Why are you here?" she asked.

And there it was. The question he had carried across four hundred miles of sea and one shipwreck, packed tighter than the charts, heavier than the water in his lungs. He had rehearsed a dozen versions of this answer on the deck of the fishing boat out of Saint-Malo, and every version had sounded like begging.

So he told her the truth instead.

* * *

"Three months ago," he said, "my name appeared on a list."

"What list?"

"The kind of list governments keep when they have run out of arguments and begun keeping enemies instead. My government's list, madame. Not yours. Though yours may follow — your republic has learned the habit from watching ours." He flexed his bandaged hand against the table. "I spent nine years surveying the coast from Dunkerque to the Spanish border. Naval charts. Depths, channels, sandbanks, the places where a harbor drowns twice a day and the places where it doesn't. Work any cartographer would call honorable. Then last spring the ministry asked me to revise certain charts — quietly, without filing the revisions. To make some waters shallower than they are, and others deeper. To draw errors into the record so that any fleet trusting our maps would trust them to its ruin."

"You refused."

"I copied everything first," Elias said, "and then I refused. There is a difference, and the difference is why men with pistols are looking for me. What I carry in that satchel is the truth about the northern approaches — every corrected depth, every real channel. Your navy wants it. My navy wants it burned. And I am a man caught between two navies with nothing to offer either except a dead friend's word."

Marguerite rose. She went to the window, stood with her back to him, arms folded — the posture of a lighthouse, he thought: something built to stand against weather.

"Henri promised you something," she said.

"Yes."

"Tell me exactly. Word for word. I'll know if you're lying — I knew his voice better than he did."

Elias closed his eyes. Eleven years fell away with disgraceful ease.

"We were drinking, the night before I sailed for Kiel. He was sober, which should have warned me something serious was coming. He said — " and here Brandt's careful French failed him for a moment, because the memory arrived not in words but in Henri's voice, rough and warm and ridiculous — "he said: *One day your beautiful maps will get you killed, German, and when that day comes you come to my light. Pointe-Aubier. Any season, any reason. My wife keeps the best soup on the coast and I keep a debt. Promise me you'll come.*"

Silence. When Marguerite turned around, her eyes were bright and furious, which he understood: fury was simply grief that had found somewhere to stand.

"He made you a promise," she said, "and then he went and died, and left me holding it."

"Yes."

"Do you know how many promises Henri made? He promised the priest he'd stop drinking. He promised me he'd take the mainland post. He promised Tomas a share of his boat. My husband was a generous man with things that cost him nothing, and now — " she stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Now a drowned man's word sits at my table eating my soup, and the whole republic would call him an enemy, and I am supposed to honor a promise I never heard him make."

"I didn't come to collect a debt," Elias said. "I came because there was nowhere else. That's different. I want you to understand it's different. A promise extracted from a drunk man — "

"Henri was not drunk that night. You said so yourself."

The trap of his own words closed neatly around him. He looked at her, and she looked back, and between them, unspoken, sat the photograph on the shelf: the man who could no longer keep his word, and the two people now required to keep it for him.

"I'll leave in the morning if you ask it," Elias said. "There's still a boat that runs the strait. I can be gone before anyone knows a German slept under your roof."

"No boat runs the strait," she said. "Not after this week. Haven't you seen the sky?"

He hadn't. He had been asleep for two days, and the world outside the little windows had narrowed to whitewash and lamplight. Now she opened the shutters fully, and he saw it: the clouds stacked black over the mainland like freight, the sea running green and heavy, the gulls blown backward off the point. The first of the great storms. The one that closed the bridge and sealed the island like a jar.

"When does it close?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. Perhaps tonight." She let the curtain fall. "So whatever you are to me, monsieur Brandt — enemy, guest, debt, burden — you are it until spring."

He should have felt trapped. Instead, shamefully, he felt the opposite: the loosening in the chest of a man setting down weight he had carried too far. For two months he had been running on open ground where any horizon might hold a policeman. Now the horizon itself had been taken away, and with it the exhausting fiction that escape was possible. There was only this room, this widow, this promise, this winter.

"There is one more thing you should know," he said. "Before you decide what I am."

She waited.

"The list my name appears on," Elias said slowly. "A copy of it travels. By wire, by post, by the mouths of officials who enjoy their work. If my government's consul learns where I landed — and consuls learn everything eventually — then this island stops being the edge of France and becomes a question. And questions arrive with inspectors. I have watched it happen in three ports already. They come politely. They bring papers."

Marguerite stood very still in the middle of her fiercely tidy room, among her husband's charts and her husband's photograph and her husband's promise, and Elias watched her do the arithmetic he had been doing since Saint-Malo: what a winter costs, what a word costs, what it costs to be the only person on a rock who knows the truth.

"My daughter is on the mainland," she said finally. "At school."

"Yes."

"If I'm caught harboring you, they don't punish only me."

"No," he said. "They never do."

She nodded once, briskly, the way she must have nodded at storms and broken lamps and all the other enormous indifferent things a keeper's life is made of. Then she took his empty bowl, and his bandages, and his future, and carried them toward the kitchen as if they were ordinary cargo.

"Then you'll need dry boots," she said. "The islanders count smoke, monsieur Brandt. Two chimneys on this rock, and one of them cold for six years. By Sunday someone will have noticed."

She paused at the doorway.

"So tomorrow, before the storm comes, you'll help me haul the winter stores up from the boathouse. In daylight. Where everyone can see you." Her mouth did something that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "If you're going to be my secret, you'd better learn to look like a hired man."

Outside, the first gust hit the tower and the glass hummed in its frame, and far across the darkening strait, invisible but already moving toward them, the winter began to close.
