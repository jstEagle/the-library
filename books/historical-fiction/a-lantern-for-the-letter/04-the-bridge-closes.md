## 4. The Bridge Closes

The storm came three days early.

Marguerite knew it first in her teeth — a low ache that arrived each autumn with the barometer's fall, the sea's way of announcing itself through whatever body happened to live beside it. She was trimming the lamp wicks when the ache began, and by the time she climbed to the gallery to look south, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise.

She had seen forty-one winters on this rock and could read a sky the way her daughter read novels: quickly, hungrily, with a growing dread of what came next. This sky said *days*. Not one storm but a procession of them, queued up beyond the horizon like omnibuses on a Paris boulevard, each one waiting its turn to strike the island and shake it.

"Early," she said aloud to no one, because saying things aloud had become a habit since the house held two instead of one.

Behind her, faint under the wind's first complaints, came the sound of Elias Brandt sweeping the oil room floor. He swept every morning now. He mended what he could reach, oiled hinges, stacked wood with an engineer's precision — a man making himself useful the way a lodger does when he cannot pay rent and knows it. She had not yet decided whether this industry moved her or unsettled her more.

By noon the first squall hit, horizontal rain that turned the world to gray glass. By evening the wind was singing in the tower's ironwork, that high keening note she knew better than any hymn. And down at the causeway, the tidal bridge — the thin stone spine that connected Pointe-Aubier to the mainland, walkable at low water, drowned twice a day even in calm seasons — went under and did not come back.

* * *

She went down to look at it anyway, two days into the blow, wrapped in Henri's old oilskin with the hood cinched so tight she could see only the path ahead of her boots.

The bridge was a rumor beneath white water. At low tide it should have shown itself, black stone glistening, gulls standing on the parapet like clerks waiting for a bank to open. Instead the sea stood over it by six feet and did not trouble to hide its pleasure about it. Spray came off the northern abutment in sheets. The little signal hut at the far end — where the ferryman's boy kept the tide board and the flag signals — had its door torn open, flapping like something wounded.

A man from the mainland works crew was there when she arrived, or rather she heard him shouting before she saw him: a small figure wedged in the lee of the abutment, bellowing at the water as though it might be shamed into receding.

"You'll not cross!" he shouted at her, when he saw her shape against the storm. "Official word! Sealed till spring!"

"I can see it's sealed," she shouted back. "The sea sealed it."

"The prefecture sealed it!" He seemed to feel this distinction mattered enormously. "No crossing till the equinox tides ease! Nobody on, nobody off! You've stores?"

"Enough."

"Then you've enough!" He retreated into his lee, conversation concluded, and Marguerite stood a moment longer looking at the drowned stones, feeling the meaning of it arrive in her chest like cold water arriving in a boot.

*Nobody on, nobody off.*

The island would hold perhaps sixty souls through winter, as it always had. Fish enough, potatoes enough, flour laid in by October, the priest's cellar full of wine that improved in direct proportion to how badly the winter went. They had done this forever. It was not the sealing that frightened anyone who lived here; it was a formality, ink applied to weather.

But she turned back up the path and looked once behind her, at the tower rising out of the murk with its lamp already lit against the early dark, and thought: *there is a man inside that tower whose name is on a list.*

And the bridge was under the sea until spring.

* * *

The wire office was a closet in Madame Roux's shop, and it became, over the following week, the most visited square meter of the island's territory.

Marguerite made herself go in on the fourth day, ostensibly for lamp oil and salt, actually to stand near the counter while Madame Roux read aloud — because Madame Roux always read aloud; privacy in telegrams being a concept she regarded as foreign and probably Catholic — the messages that crackled through from the mainland whenever the line held.

Mostly they were dull. Prices of cod. A birth in the Levasseur family. The bishop's Christmas schedule, received in November with the reverence due a relic.

Then, on a Tuesday between storms, a long one.

Madame Roux ran her finger down the paper. Her lips moved. Marguerite, arranging cans of oil on the counter with tremendous concentration, heard the pause and knew before the woman spoke that the weather had changed.

"'To the municipal officer,'" Madame Roux read slowly, "'Pointe-Aubier island. Be advised. Subject Brandt, Elias, cartographer, national of the German Empire, listed pursuant to decree of—'" she stumbled over the legal phrase "'—is believed to have landed on your coast following maritime distress. Report presence. Do not apprehend. Inspector Lefèvre will attend at first opportunity. Acknowledge.'"

The shop went quiet in the particular way shops go quiet when everyone is listening and pretending to examine merchandise.

Tomas Guénec was there, mending nothing, holding a coil of rope he had apparently brought as a prop. Old Père Anselme stood by the window with his hands folded. Two of the Roux daughters froze mid-argument over a ribbon.

"A German," Tomas said at last. Not loudly. That was the worst of it — he didn't say it loudly at all. He said it the way you'd note that frost was coming to the beans. "On the island. Since the wreck."

"There was a wreck," Madame Roux said. Her eyes had found Marguerite over the counter, mild and terrible as a lighthouse beam. "Boat smashed on the north rocks. We all heard it. Didn't we hear it, Marguerite?"

"You heard it."

"And anything washed ashore from a wreck belongs—" Madame Roux paused, savoring the proverb, "—well. To whomsoever pulls it from the water. That's the old law of the sea."

"That is the old law of the sea," Père Anselme agreed gently, which from him could mean anything.

Marguerite finished counting her coins onto the counter with fingers that behaved themselves, remarkably, as if they had been drilled. "I'll take the oil now, thank you, Camille," she said to the eldest Roux girl, and walked home through the spitting rain with the can swinging from her hand, and did not run, and was proud of not running the whole quarter mile, and arrived at her own door shaking.

Brandt looked up from the table where he was copying charts by lamplight — copying them, she had noticed, into smaller sheets, folding them flat, work that suddenly acquired a new and urgent significance.

"Well?" he said.

"The wire came." She set down the oil can. "Your name travels faster than you do. There's an inspector coming. Lefèvre. At the first chance the weather gives."

He sat very still. Outside, the wind threw a handful of gravel against the shutters, like a knock.

"Do not apprehend," she added. "Those were the words. *Do not apprehend.* Someone wants you handled quietly."

"Or someone wants me delivered quietly," Brandt said. "It amounts to the same courtesy." He looked at the folded papers, then at her. "You could send back one word on that wire. One word. *Confirmed.* And this ends here, in this kitchen, civilly."

"I know what one word would do."

"I'm telling you I would understand it."

"Don't be noble at me, Herr Brandt, I haven't the patience for it before supper." She untied her hood with hands that were finally, honestly trembling. "You pulled my husband out of a canal in Bremen eleven years ago. He wrote me about it four times. He wrote about you the way men write about brothers they're too embarrassed to say they love. And then he died in my arms on this island with the lamp turning above us, and his last letter unfinished, and I am the only person left alive who knows what he owed you — so don't tell me what one word would do. I've known for a week. I chose silence before the sea closed the road."

The words hung there. She had not known, until she said it, that it was true — that the choice had already been made, days ago, in the small lies and the unasked questions, in letting him sweep floors and mend nets as though he were simply what he pretended to be. Silence, she understood now, was not the absence of a decision. It was a decision, made hourly, renewed like a lamp's flame.

Brandt was quiet a long moment. Then he said, softly, in the tone of a man reading his own sentence:

"Then we are both on the list now. Yours just isn't printed yet."

* * *

That night the third storm of the procession came ashore, the worst yet, and Marguerite climbed the ninety-three steps to the lantern room and did the work her hands had done ten thousand times: trimmed, wound, polished, watched the great lens turn its patient wheel of light across the water.

From the gallery she could see the whole island laid out below — the cluster of houses with their shuttered windows, the church, the smoke standing off the chimneys sideways in the gale. Sixty souls. Every one of them now knowing what she knew. Every one of them deciding, hour by hour, what their silence meant.

Somewhere below, lamplight moved in her kitchen, where a German cartographer sat copying maps that navies wanted and others wanted burned, waiting for an inspector who could not come yet, on an island that could not let him go.

The light wheeled. The sea hammered the drowned bridge.

Marguerite realized, watching the beam swing over the black water toward a mainland she could not reach and that could not reach her, that she had spent her whole life believing history happened elsewhere — in capitals, in newspapers, in other people's languages.

History had crossed a flooded bridge and was sleeping in her guest bed.

And in the morning, she knew, the questions would begin in earnest.
