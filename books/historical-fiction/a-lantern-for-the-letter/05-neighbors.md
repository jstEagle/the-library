## 5. Neighbors

Tomas Gourvennec had fished the waters off Pointe-Aubier for thirty-one years, and he could read smoke the way other men read scripture. A wet fire spoke of hurry. A dry one, of comfort. And a fire that burned at odd hours — at dusk, at midnight, in the grey hour before dawn when no honest keeper needed warmth twice — spoke of someone who did not want to be seen.

He had been counting.

Three weeks since the storms closed the bridge. Three weeks since Marguerite Sandoval had come down to the harbor alone to buy flour and lamp oil and paraffin, buying, he noticed, enough bread flour for two households. He'd said nothing then. But on Thursday he'd rounded the headland with his creels heavy and seen it: a thread of woodsmoke rising from behind the tower, thin and blue and steady, at an hour when Marguerite should have been climbing her stairs to trim the wick.

Two chimneys' worth of living on a rock built for one.

He didn't go up. That was the thing about islanders — they never went straight at a thing. They went around it, the way water goes around a stone, patient, wearing it smooth. He went instead to Madame Roux's shop, where all things eventually went.

* * *

Marguerite knew they were talking about her before anyone said a word. She could feel it the way she felt weather coming — a pressure behind the ears, a change in the quality of the light. The island had gone quiet in a new way. Not the silence of winter, which was restful, but the silence of held breath.

She came down for supplies on the first clear Saturday in three weeks, and Madame Roux was waiting behind her counter like a spider who had already decided where the fly would land.

"Madame Sandoval." The shopkeeper's smile showed every tooth she had left. "You're looking well. Winter suits you."

"Winter suits no one, Madame Roux. I'll take two kilos of the brown flour, paraffin, and whatever tinned meat you have that isn't mackerel."

"Brown flour." Madame Roux weighed it slowly, letting the scoop click against the tin. "A lot of baking, is there? For a woman alone."

"I bake when I'm restless. It passes the time."

"Mm. My mother baked when she had something on her conscience." The scoop clicked. "Though I'm sure that isn't your case."

"Give me salted butter as well."

"Certainly." The butter came wrapped in paper, set down with exaggerated care. "You know, we had a strange thing reported. Père Anselme says old Yvonne Le Goff — you know how her eyes are, half-blind and yet she sees everything — Yvonne says she saw a man on your rocks last Tuesday. Tall fellow. Moving quick, like he didn't want to be caught at anything."

"The light needs tending from outside sometimes. I climb the gallery rail myself."

"A tall fellow, though." Madame Roux folded her arms atop the counter. "Yvonne was quite specific. She doesn't climb rails, that woman. She can barely climb her own stairs."

Marguerite counted coins out onto the counter, one by one, taking longer than the arithmetic required. "Yvonne sees gulls and calls them angels," she said finally. "If there were a man on my rocks, Madame Roux, he'd have drowned getting there. Nothing crosses that water in December but God's mistakes."

"And yet." The shopkeeper leaned in, voice dropping to a confessional register. "There's smoke. Tomas saw it. Two fires' worth, some nights. I've kept your account twenty years, Marguerite, and I'd hate to see it spoiled by a lie over flour."

The word *lie* sat between them like a fish left out too long.

"I don't lie," Marguerite said, and heard even as she said it how badly it landed — flat, too fast, a door shut with the latch not caught. She gathered her parcels. "Good day, Madame Roux."

Outside, the wind had turned. She smelled snow in it, and under the snow-smell, faintly, woodsmoke from the direction of the fishing sheds — though nothing was burning there. She understood, walking back along the frozen path with her parcels against her chest, that the island had begun to close around her the way the sea closed around the bridge. Quietly. Completely.

* * *

Brandt heard the church bell ring nine times and stopped sanding the skiff's gunwale.

Père Anselme was coming up the path. He could tell by the pace of the bell-tolling — no, that was foolishness, the bell rang for mass, it rang for whoever rang it — but his hands had learned in forty years of dangerous work to know when they were being watched, and right now every hair on them stood up.

He retreated through the lean-to door into the keeper's quarters, where Marguerite sat mending a lamp gasket. "The priest," he said.

"I know. I saw him leave the chapel." She didn't look up from her work. "Stay here. Say nothing unless he speaks to you directly, and if he does, speak French and speak little."

"He'll ask where I'm from."

"He'll ask nothing. He'll stand there being holy until I fill the silence for him. They train them for it." She set down the gasket. "Elias. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, you take the skiff and you go east along the coast and you don't look back at this tower. Henri's promise covers your life, not mine, and I've made my peace with what I'm spending."

Before he could answer, boots on gravel, and a knock with the rhythm of authority even when delivered gently. Marguerite opened the door to a small round man in a coat too heavy for him, snow in the wool of his collar.

"Père Anselme."

"Madame la gardienne. Forgive the intrusion. I was walking — well, I was walking toward you, if truth be told; I find honesty saves time in winter." His eyes went past her shoulder into the room, unhurried, cataloguing. "It's cold to be walking. May I?"

"You may stand in the doorway. The stove's small."

"So is my need." He smiled — a good smile, worn soft with use. Then, mildly, terribly: "Your chimney smokes at strange hours, madame. Tomas Gourvennec mentioned it at the harbor. And Madame Roux says you buy flour like a woman feeding a crew. And Yvonne Le Goff — God keep her — swears she saw a second man on your rocks, though we all know what Yvonne sees." He folded his hands. "I mention these things not because I believe them, but because the island believes them, and an island is a small parish with a long memory. So I have come to ask you plainly, as your priest: is there someone staying with you?"

The silence stretched. Marguerite felt Brandt's presence behind her like a hand on her back.

"There is work to be done here that I cannot do alone," she said. "I've taken on help."

"Help." The priest tasted the word. "Named?"

"A man who owed Henri a debt. That's all I'll say on a doorstep, Father, with the wind carrying."

Something moved in the old priest's face — not surprise; she suspected he'd known before he climbed the hill — but a kind of weary settling, like a man confirming a diagnosis he'd dreaded.

"A debt," he repeated. "Henri Sandoval has been dead two years, and still paying out." He looked past her again, deliberately this time, and raised his voice just enough. "Whoever you are, sir — this island feeds its own and buries its own, and it does both honestly. If you mean harm to this house or these people, no prayer of mine will cover you. If you don't—" He sighed, and the sigh was suddenly very old. "Then may God mend what your country broke. Good day, madame. I'll say a Mass for your husband. And, Marguerite—" He paused on the step, not turning. "The wire at the harbor office took a message yesterday. Someone on the mainland is asking questions about strangers. Be certain, before spring, that you know whose side you are standing on. Indecision reads as treason from a distance."

He went down the path, small and round and unhurried, and the snow began again behind him like a curtain drawn.

* * *

Tomas came at dusk three days later, and he did not come alone.

Marguerite saw them from the gallery — six men, maybe seven, trudging up from the harbor with lanterns unlit, which somehow was worse than if they'd lit them. Tomas led. Behind him came the Bihan brothers, old Guillou who repaired boats, and others whose faces she'd known for years and now couldn't read at all.

They didn't come to the tower door. They stopped at the boat shed — the shed where the repaired skiff lay under canvas — and stood around it in the falling snow like men at a graveside.

She went down. There was no dignity in waiting to be summoned on her own rock.

"Tomas."

"Madame." He touched his cap. The gesture was respectful. His eyes were not. "We found our nets mended. All winter, nets coming back from your rocks with knots no woman on this island ties — mainland knots, foreign ones. Guillou here found his punt's seam re-caulked, and a fine job of it, better than he'd have paid for. Somebody's been working this island in the dark like a ghost."

"Somebody," Guillou said, spitting amber into the snow, "with hands that know boats."

"We're not fools, madame." Tomas stepped closer, lowering his voice so the others strained to hear — kindness or cruelty, she couldn't tell. "We know what's in your house. We've known since the flour. And most of us would've let it bide till spring, because a man who mends nets without asking pay is a strange kind of enemy." He pulled something from inside his coat, unfolded it: a newspaper, weeks old, softened by damp. He held it out. On the front page, above the fold, a list of names in columns, and one name struck through in somebody's impatient pencil — struck through at the harbor office, she realized, by the telegraph clerk, long before any of them had seen it.

*ELIAS BRANDT. CARTOGRAPHER. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING.*

"The navy wants him," Tomas said quietly. "That's what the wire says. Not the police. The navy. And when the thaw comes, an inspector's coming, and every one of us will be asked what we knew and when we knew it, and whether we sheltered a spy on a rock with a naval light at its top." He folded the paper away. "So you tell me, madame, and tell them — is he ours now, or is he never? Because those are the only two choices an island gives a man. There's nothing in between."

Behind the men, at the edge of the lamplight from the tower, Brandt had appeared — drawn by the voices, unable to stay hidden, standing in the open snow with his hands empty at his sides. Six fishermen turned to look at him. Nobody spoke.

And it was Brandt who answered, in careful, accented French, pitched to carry: "I am yours," he said, "if you'll have me. If you won't — I'll still mend your nets. I owe this coast more than trouble." He paused, and the wind filled the pause. "Ask your boys on the water tonight whose caulking holds their hulls together. Then decide."

Old Guillou snorted. The Bihan brothers exchanged a look. Tomas studied the stranger for a long moment, then turned back to Marguerite with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life — pity and warning braided together.

"Decide fast, madame," he said. "The ice is cracking early this year. Word is the inspector won't wait for spring after all."
