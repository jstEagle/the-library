## 6. The Inspector's Wire

The telegram came on a Tuesday, carried up from the bridge by a boy whose cheeks were cracked red with cold. Brandt saw him first from the window of the keeper's quarters, where he had spent three weeks learning the geography of Marguerite Sandoval's small domestic world: where she kept the coffee, which floorboard groaned, how the wind rearranged itself around the tower at dusk. He watched the boy trudge the path, and something in his chest tightened before the boy even knocked. Telegrams did not come to islands in winter for good reasons.

He could have gone out. He should have gone out — taken the wire himself, read his own sentence first, spared her the reading of it. But he stood in the doorway like a coward and let her take it, and he watched her face do what faces do when they hold bad news behind the teeth: nothing at all.

She handed it to him without a word. The paper was damp at the corners.

TO THE GENDARMERIE POST, POINTE-AUBIER, RELAY VIA BRIDGE STATION. INSPECTOR LEFÈVRE, MINISTRY OF MARINE, WILL CROSS AT FIRST BREAK IN WEATHER TO CONDUCT INQUIRY RE: PRESENCE OF UNREGISTERED PERSON ON ISLAND. FULL COOPERATION REQUIRED. ACKNOWLEDGE.

Dated two days prior. Which meant that somewhere on the mainland, a man with a rubber stamp and a warm office had already decided how this would end, and was now simply waiting for the sea to agree with him.

"When?" Marguerite asked.

"The break," Brandt said. "They'll get a lull — there's always a lull after a blow like the last one. Two days. Three."

"Three weeks is more like it." But she said it the way people say things they don't believe, and they both looked at the horizon together, where the cloud bank sat low and grey and organized, like an army that had paused only to eat.

* * *

He worked on the skiff through the afternoon and told her at supper, because lying to her had become a thing he could no longer do cleanly, even small lies about mending fences. He set down his fork and said, "I'm going to take the boat."

Marguerite put down her own fork more slowly, as if the motion itself were an argument.

"No."

"There's a fishing boat comes out of Saint-Vaast some mornings when the swell eases—"

"You'll not make it to the shipping lanes. You'll not make it past the point." She said it flatly, without drama, and that was worse than shouting. "I've lived on this water twenty-two years, Elias. In January the sea doesn't kill you dramatically. It kills you administratively. Cold hands, a fouled sheet, four degrees of water, and then you're a report."

"I've crossed worse water."

"In summer. With charts. And look what the last crossing made of you." Her eyes went briefly to the scar along his hairline, the souvenir of the wreck that had delivered him here half-drowned, and he felt the familiar shame of being a burden wearing the costume of a guest.

"I can't be here when Lefèvre lands," he said. "You understand that. Whatever arrangement you imagine, whatever you think you can say to him — I won't have my safety purchased with your life. That wasn't the shape of Henri's promise. Refuge, he said. Not ruin."

"Henri promised—" She stopped. He watched her gather herself, and he realized with a strange clarity that she had been holding this sentence behind her teeth for weeks too, and that the telegram had knocked it loose.

* * *

"Henri promised you because Henri owed you," she said. They had moved to the stove; the lamp between them made a small yellow country neither wanted to leave. "But you should know what he owed you *for*. Not just the canal. Not just Berlin."

Brandt said nothing. He had learned, in these weeks, that silence was the currency she paid in.

"He wrote to me about you every year. Did you know that? Every Christmas, a letter, and every letter had a paragraph about Elias Brandt — *the German who reads maps the way priests read scripture*, he called you once. I knew your handwriting before I knew your face. When I pulled you off those rocks I thought, so this is the paragraph made flesh." She turned her cup in its saucer, once, twice. "What he never wrote was why he kept writing. Why a French naval officer spent twenty years carrying a debt to a German cartographer like a stone in his coat."

"It was the canal," Brandt said carefully. "Berlin, '79. He'd been drinking with the wrong delegation and walked into the Spree canal in the dark. I happened to be—"

"I know the story. It's a good story. It isn't true."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them the wind found a gap in the tower's stonework and played it like a flute.

"Tell me the true one, then," Brandt said quietly.

She looked at him for a long moment, deciding — he could see her deciding — whether he could be trusted with it. Whether anyone could. Then: "In '78 Henri surveyed a stretch of coast near Wilhelmshaven. Officially hydrographic work. Unofficially, he sold soundings to your navy. Depths, channels, the approaches. He told himself it was peacetime, everyone trades, no harm done. And then in '79 the war scare came, and he understood exactly what he'd drawn lines for, and he couldn't sleep, and he drank, and one night in Berlin he walked into a canal either by accident or by intention — he never told me which, and I never asked, because I was afraid of the answer." Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible discipline of her. "You pulled him out. And he spent the rest of his life paying you back for a rescue that maybe interrupted something else."

Brandt stared at her. Twenty years of letters, of gratitude, of a friendship conducted across a border in careful ink — and underneath all of it, a confession he had never been permitted to hear.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you're about to die politely in a boat to make things easier for me, and I won't have another man drowning to settle Henri's accounts." Something cracked in her voice on the last word, the first fracture he'd ever heard in it. "I've done my time watching men pay debts that way."

* * *

And because fairness demanded it — because she had given him the ugly architecture of her marriage — he gave her his.

"My government didn't make me a traitor," he said. "It made me useful, and then it made me a name on a list, and it never once asked whether I agreed to be either. Thirty years drawing coastlines. I thought I was making the world legible. Safer — ships finding harbors instead of rocks." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Legible. That's the word I used in my reports. And then the ministry started asking me to make certain waters *illegible*. To lose soundings. To draw a channel where none exists, so that if the day ever came, their ships would find harbor and yours would find rock." He met her eyes. "I refused. Quietly. I copied the true charts first, put them in a satchel, and started running. So yes — the navy wants what I carry. And my own country wants me dead for carrying it. I'm not fleeing your inspector, Marguerite. I'm fleeing everyone who ever taught me that a coastline is a weapon."

She was silent a long while. Outside, the gale tested the shutters like a landlord checking the locks.

"So we're both liars' widows," she said finally. "Widowed by a map and a letter."

"And bound," Brandt said, "by a dead man's word."

"Yes." She rose, took the telegram from the table, and held it over the stove until the paper curled and flamed. "Bound," she said. "Which means you don't sail into January to spare me. If Lefèvre crosses, we meet him together, or not at all."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue.

But the light behind her, in the tower room above, guttered once — and died.
