## 7. Fire on the Water

The smell woke her before the light did.

Marguerite had spent eleven years learning the island's vocabulary of scents — salt, kelp, tar, the iron tang of the lamp oil — and this one did not belong. It was burning paint and burning rope and something underneath that made her stomach clench before her mind could name it: burning wood that had been someone's hope.

She was out of bed with her boots unlaced, down the spiral stairs two steps at a time, and out into a night gone wrong. The wind had dropped to nothing — the first stillness in three weeks — and in that unnatural calm she could see it plainly: the skiff at its mooring in the lee of the breakwater, wrapped in flame from waterline to gunwale, burning like a votive candle set adrift on black glass.

"Brandt!" Her voice cracked across the yard. "Elias!"

He came out of the keeper's quarters already dressed, hair wild, face lit orange. He stood beside her at the seawall and said nothing for a long moment. That silence told her more than any words.

"You heard something," she said.

"No."

"Don't lie to me. Not tonight."

"I sleep badly," he said. "I hear everything. The sea. The house settling. If I had heard footsteps, or an oar in a rowlock—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "It was dry kindling, Marguerite. Someone brought pitch or paraffin. This was not an accident of a lantern left near canvas."

The skiff groaned. A plank split and the fire leaned sideways, throwing sparks out over the water where they died hissing, one by one, like small lives going out.

"There goes my argument," she said quietly.

"What?"

"The winter sea is death. You were to stay because the winter sea is death." She watched the mast catch, a thin black spear against the glow. "Now there is no boat to argue about."

Brandt's hands were fists at his sides. She saw him looking at the fire the way a man looks at a door closing — not with grief exactly, but with the arithmetic of a man recalculating everything. And beneath that, though he tried to hide it, something that looked almost like relief. She hated herself for seeing it.

*Or was it you?* The thought arrived uninvited and would not leave. *You spoke of destroying the maps. You knew the inspector was coming. A burned skiff burns evidence too.*

"I didn't do this," Brandt said, as if he'd heard her.

"I know," she said.

But she said it a half-second too late, and they both heard the half-second.

* * *

By dawn the whole island had seen it. There was no missing it; the smoke had climbed straight up in the dead air like a signal, and signals on Pointe-Aubier are read by everyone.

They came in ones and twos through the morning, the way people come when they want to look without seeming to look. Madame Roux appeared at the tower door with a jar of rendered fat she did not need to deliver, her sharp eyes cataloguing everything: the scorched mooring post, the black slick on the water, Elias Brandt standing in the doorway with his sleeves rolled and his hands stained with net tar.

"Tomas is coming up after mass," she said to Marguerite, low, setting the jar down harder than necessary. "There's talk."

"There's always talk."

"This isn't talk about weather." Madame Roux glanced once at Brandt, then away, and her mouth went thin. "A stranger winters on your rock. A stranger's smoke was seen in November when you told us your sister's boy was staying. A strange boat burns in the night. And now the *Gazette* has been passed from hand to hand on the mainland ferry run since December, and Tomas's cousin brought a copy back with the Christmas parcel." She smoothed her apron as if smoothing her conscience flat. "Marguerite. Girl. I've sold you flour for nine years. Whatever you're carrying, put it down before it burns your house too."

Then she was gone, down the path, not waiting for an answer, which was itself an answer.

Marguerite stood in the doorway holding a jar of fat she had no use for, and behind her, Brandt said, "You lied about a nephew?"

"My sister has no sons."

"Why?"

"Because 'a mending man who keeps to himself' invites questions, and 'my nephew' closes doors." She turned. "Because I am a bad liar and needed a simple one."

"It wasn't simple enough."

"No," she said. "It never is."

* * *

Tomas came at noon, and he did not come alone, which was the cruelty of it. He came with Yann Le Goff and old Perrin and two of the Bihan brothers, five men arranged on her seawall like a delegation, caps in their hands, which on this island meant a funeral or a reckoning.

Tomas was fifty and built like the breakwater he'd helped lay, and he had pulled Marguerite's dory off the rocks twice without ever mentioning it afterward. He looked at her now the way a man looks at a chart that has stopped matching the coast.

"We found this in the ash line on the beach this morning." He held out a scrap of charred canvas, no bigger than a prayer book cover. "And we've all been wondering what a skiff was doing at your mooring at all, Marguerite, because you keep no skiff. Your dory's on its blocks since October. So whose boat burns on our water in the night?"

"A friend's," she said.

"A friend." He let the word sit there. "The same friend who mended nets at Le Goff's landing in November and wouldn't give his name? The same friend whose smoke we smelled from the tower in the storms?" He reached inside his coat and brought out a newspaper, folded soft from handling, many hands having worried it. He unfolded it with terrible gentleness, like a man handling something dead, and laid it on the seawall stone between them.

She didn't need to look. She looked anyway.

The list ran down the column in dense print: names, descriptions, warrants pending. And there, fourth from the top, in ink that had begun to smudge from thumbing:

*BRANDT, ELIAS — cartographer. Wanted for questioning in matters touching naval charts. Last seen southbound, autumn.*

"Elias Brandt," Tomas read aloud, though every man present had already read it a dozen times. "German. The paper says the navy wants him. The paper says men like him sell what they draw." He raised his eyes to hers. "Henri Sandoval drowned eight years ago, Marguerite, and I rowed his body home, and I have kept your light company every winter since because a widow alone on a rock is a debt on all of us. So you tell me straight, and God sees I'm asking you kindly: is that man in your house the man on this paper?"

The gulls screamed somewhere over the point. The tide was turning; she could hear it in the channel, that long exhale under the world.

"Yes," she said.

The word fell among them like a stone into a well. Yann Le Goff stepped back as if it had splashed him. Old Perrin crossed himself.

"He was Henri's friend," Marguerite went on, and heard her own voice go high and strange, a voice she did not command. "Before the war, before any of this. Henri drowned once himself — in a canal in Bremen, years ago — and this man dove in after him and pulled him out. Henri wrote me about it. He wrote me that if ever that man asked anything of us, anything, we owed him a life." Her breath caught. "Henri couldn't keep that promise. He's dead. So I kept it."

"On our island?" Tomas said. "Without one word to any of us? With inspectors and newspapers and God knows what following him here?" His voice rose, and the kindness cracked clean through the middle of it, and what was underneath was worse than anger. "We're forty souls on this rock, Marguerite. Forty. One wire from the prefecture and they seal the bridge and search house to house, and every one of us answers for what you hid. My boys fish those waters. You put *us* in your satchel along with his maps."

"I put a debt in my house—"

"You put a stranger in ours!"

The silence after was total. Even the gulls seemed to withdraw. Down at the mooring the last black ribs of the skiff showed above the waterline, and Marguerite saw several of the men glance toward them, and saw the question move between their faces like wind through barley: *who burned it?* Because a burned boat could be mercy — a man stranded cannot flee — or it could be fear — neighbors making sure the problem stays on her rock and not theirs — or it could be the man himself, tidying up. Nobody said it aloud. That was how cruel this winter had made them: they thought it instead.

Tomas picked up his newspaper and folded it away, slow, exact, each crease deliberate.

"Mass is Sunday," he said. "Père Anselme will speak on it, I expect. And Marguerite—" He put his cap back on, and could not meet her eyes while he finished. "My wife sent bread over every week since your Henri went. Don't come to the shop for it anymore. It's nothing personal."

*It's nothing personal.* She stood at the seawall and watched the five of them go down the path single file, and understood that on an island of forty souls there is no such thing as impersonal, that every door closing anywhere is felt as a draft in every other house, and that she had spent three months believing silence was neutrality when silence was always, always a side.

Behind her, in the doorway of the keeper's quarters, Brandt waited until the men were gone before he spoke.

"They'll report it now. The name. To the priest, to the mainland, to whoever carries the wire."

"I know."

"The inspector will come faster."

"I know that too."

"And Marguerite—" He hesitated, and she turned and saw that he was not looking at her but at the wrecked skiff, at the black water where his escape had been, and his voice when it came was very quiet. "Whoever burned it wanted me to stay. Or wanted me gone. And I find I cannot tell which frightens me more."

That night the wind returned from the northeast, hard and rising, and by midnight it was screaming against the north glass, and Marguerite climbed the tower and trimmed the wick and looked out at a sea coming apart in the dark, and thought: *the worst is not here yet. The worst is still walking toward us across the water.*
