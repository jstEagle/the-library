## 8. The Long Night

The wind came up out of the southwest at noon like something that had been waiting all winter for permission.

Marguerite felt it before she heard it — a pressure in the tower's stones, a hum in the iron rail of the gallery — and by the time she reached the lamp room the barometer had fallen so far she tapped it twice to be sure. It was sure. The mercury lay in its well like a dead thing.

She had weathered thirty years of storms from this room. She knew their grammar: the long vowels of a sou'wester, the short brutal sentences of a north gale. But this one spoke a language she didn't know, a rising shriek that came in gusts and then did not stop coming, as if the sea itself had stood up and begun to walk.

By three o'clock the spray was going over the lantern. By four the light was burning behind glass she could not see out of, and she sat with her back against the pedestal listening to the world try to take the tower apart bolt by bolt.

She thought about Brandt below, in the keeper's quarters, mending his coat by lamplight. She thought about the burned skiff, the blackened mooring ring, Tomas's face when he'd held out the newspaper with its printed list of names. She thought: whatever happens tonight, we are all in the same boat now.

She did not know yet how literally that would prove true.

* * *

Brandt heard the light fail at half past nine.

He had spent enough nights in lighthouses — charting them, mostly, drawing their little stars on other men's maps — to know the sound of one working. There was a rhythm to it: the clockwork turn, the faint tick of the lens carriage, the great slow heartbeat of the beam passing overhead every eight seconds. He had fallen asleep to it for weeks without noticing, the way you fall asleep to your own pulse.

At half past nine the heartbeat stopped.

He was on his feet before he understood why, some older instinct dragging him upright. Then Marguerite was on the stairs, taking them two at a time, and he followed because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

The lamp room was chaos. She had the housing open already, wind screaming through it, spray coming in sideways like thrown gravel. The wick had drowned — he could see it, a black comma in a pool of oil that should have been burning. The feed line, frozen or fouled or simply beaten past endurance, had starved the flame mid-turn and then flooded it.

"The pump," she shouted, and he could barely make out the words. "It's jammed. I can't clear it alone."

"How long can the boat crews run dark?"

"Until the moon sets." She looked at the black ceiling of cloud. "There is no moon."

He understood. Somewhere out in that shrieking waste were fishing boats that had put out before the storm closed in — boats that would come home tonight because there was no choice, because nets cost money and sons were impatient — and they would come home steering on this light, the way their fathers had steered on it, the way everyone for forty years had steered on it. Take the light away and Pointe-Aubier became just another unmarked rock in an unmarked sea.

"Tell me what to do," he said.

She looked at him then, spray streaming down her face, and what passed between them needed no words, though later each would remember saying something. What she actually said was: "Hold the light."

* * *

Marguerite had cleared a frozen feed pump twice in her life, both times with Henri cursing cheerfully beside her, both times with hands that worked because fear made them stupid and obedient. Tonight her hands were her own and they shook.

The problem was simple and terrible. The pump valve was seized — ice, most likely, driven into the intake when the spray froze on everything above the waterline — and clearing it meant opening the oil line to the reservoir, which meant working bare-handed in a wind that could peel skin off bone, with oil everywhere and one stray spark between her and a fire that would end the island's history in ten seconds.

She stripped her right glove. The cold hit her hand like a hammer.

Behind her Brandt braced himself against the pedestal, arms locked around the lens carriage, holding the whole rotating assembly steady while she worked. Without him it would have been swinging loose, slamming, impossible to work under. He held it the way a man holds a door shut against a mob — not skill, just refusal.

"It's turning," he called. "Whatever you're doing, it's turning."

"Don't let it turn."

"I'm aware."

She got the valve nut free on the fourth attempt, fingers gone past pain into a white numbness that frightened her more than the wind. Ice came out of the line in a single hard pellet, spat onto the floor and skittering away into the dark. She thumbed the valve open, primed the line, felt oil move through it like blood returning to a limb.

"Wick," she shouted.

He was already there. Of course he was — he'd watched her trim that wick a hundred evenings from the corner of the room, pretending to read. His hands were steadier than hers now; hers were ruined with cold. He fed the wick, seated it, and looked at her.

"Now?"

"Now."

She struck the match inside the cup of her body, shielding it with everything she had. The flame bent nearly horizontal, guttered, caught the wick's edge —

— and died.

She struck another. Another. In the wind's voice she heard the sea saying *no, no, no*, and somewhere beneath it, impossibly far away, she thought she heard a bell — a boat's bell, small and patient, ringing on the water.

"They're out there," Brandt said. He'd heard it too. "More than one bell."

The third match caught. The wick took, blue then yellow then the steady gold she had known her whole married life, and the lens gathered it up and threw it out across the water like something being returned.

The beam swung. Eight seconds. Swung again.

Marguerite sagged against the pedestal and began, absurdly, to laugh — or to cry; in that wind there was no difference. Brandt put his good arm around her shoulders and held her against the shaking of the tower, and together they watched the light go round and round, out over the black water, out toward the bells.

* * *

From the gallery rail, twenty minutes later, they saw it happen.

A fishing boat — Tomas's boat, the *Marie-Josèphe*, though neither of them could have named her then — came out of the dark wall of spray a quarter mile east of where she should have been, running blind before the sea with three boys aboard and no idea how close the outer rocks stood. Marguerite saw the light touch her sails. Saw the boat's head come round, slow as a thought, correcting. Saw her slide past the white water where the rocks waited, close enough that the foam seemed to reach for her keel, and miss.

"She sees us," Brandt said.

"She sees us," Marguerite said.

They stayed on the gallery until the boat came around the point and into the lee, until they could hear, faint under the gale, voices shouting — young voices, terrified and then triumphant, the specific joy of the almost-drowned. A lantern waved from her deck. Marguerite lifted her dead-gloved hand and waved back, and beside her Elias Brandt, enemy of the republic, lifted his as well.

Neither of them moved to hide it. There was no one to see but the sea, and the sea kept its own counsel.

* * *

They came down at last near midnight, two figures crusted in salt ice, and found the tower door bursting open and half the island's men crowded into the base of the stairs with lamps and ropes and faces like ash. Tomas stood at the front, soaked through, having rowed out into the teeth of it looking for his boat when he couldn't reach her.

His eyes went from Marguerite to the German beside her and back again. The whole staircase understood, in one long silent moment, who had climbed.

"You relit it," Tomas said. Not a question. His voice was wrecked from shouting into wind.

"We relit it," Marguerite said.

Tomas looked at Brandt for a long time. Whatever moved behind his face was old and slow and heavy, a man hauling on lines that went down into deep water. At last he said, "My boy is on that boat. All three of them are somebody's boys."

"Yes," said Brandt.

Tomas nodded once, turned, and pushed out into the storm. Behind him the others filed past, and one of them — Madame Roux's nephew, sixteen, still wet — stopped on the stair and pressed something into Brandt's frozen hand without meeting his eyes: a flask, brandy, still warm from someone's stove.

Marguerite watched the boy go. Then she looked down at the newspaper folded in Tomas's abandoned lantern-light, lying on the step where he'd dropped it, the list of names showing, the name *Brandt* circled in somebody's pencil.

Outside, the wind began, very slowly, to fall.

And over the sound of it, from the direction of the village, she heard the wire office's shutter bang open — heard someone running through the streets calling that a telegram had come through, that the ice was breaking early, that a government inspector would cross on the morning tide.

Brandt picked up the flask. His hand, she noticed, was perfectly steady.

"So," he said. "Tomorrow, then."
