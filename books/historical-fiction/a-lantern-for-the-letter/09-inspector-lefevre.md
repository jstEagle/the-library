## 9. Inspector Lefèvre

The ice broke on a Tuesday, with a sound like a rifle shot.

I was mending the hinge of the lamp-room door when it came — a long cracking groan out of the channel, then a silence, then a hundred smaller reports as the floes loosened and began to grind against each other in the current. I stood at the rail and watched the gray water open itself for the first time in two months. Somewhere beyond the point, the mainland waited with its telegraph lines and its lists.

Marguerite came up the stairs behind me. She didn't speak. She had stopped speaking much at all since the night we climbed together into the gale; she carried that night the way one carries a heavy parcel, both hands occupied, no room left for anything else.

"He'll come now," I said.

"Yes."

"How many days?"

"Two. Three if the wind holds north." She looked out at the broken ice. "Elias. Before you say anything — I have already decided what I will do."

I turned to her. Her face was thinner than when I had washed ashore here, and there was a new stillness in it that frightened me more than any storm had.

"You should not decide anything for my sake," I said. "You've done enough. More than—"

"Henri did not promise you to me," she said. "He promised me to you. There is a difference, and I am only now understanding it." She folded her hands on the rail, the way she did when she was about to be immovable. "So listen. When Lefèvre comes, I will meet him myself. You will stay below, in the cellar, and you will not hear what is said."

"I can't let you—"

"You can't *let* me do anything," she said, and there was no cruelty in it, only fact. "That is the whole point of what I'm about to surrender."

* * *

He crossed on the third morning, in the mail boat's small launch, wearing a good coat gone bad at the seams and an expression I would learn, later, to recognize as the face of a man who had spent twenty years being sent to places where nothing he did would matter.

I watched from the cellar window — a slit of glass at ground level, crusted with salt — as Marguerite met him at the landing. She wore her keeper's uniform pressed and buttoned to the throat. Behind her, at a distance she had clearly calculated, stood Tomas Roux's brother and two of the fishermen, arms crossed, saying nothing. The whole island knew why the inspector had come. The whole island had decided, in the way islands decide, without votes or declarations, that whatever Marguerite Sandoval had done in the dark of the storm was theirs now too.

Three boys had come home alive because of that lantern. No wire from the capital could argue with that. But wires are not required to argue; they are only required to arrive.

Lefèvre shook her hand. He looked up at the tower — I saw him look, taking its measure, the fresh paint on the gallery rail, the glass cleaned until it burned — and then he said something I couldn't hear, and Marguerite led him up the path toward the house, and the fishermen let them pass.

I sat in the dark among the potatoes and the pickled herring and listened to the floorboards overhead.

* * *

They talked for two hours. I know because I counted the strikes of the kitchen clock through the ceiling, and because Marguerite told me afterward, every word, sitting across from me with her hands flat on the table as though giving testimony herself.

She did not offer him coffee. She put him in Henri's chair, which was either a courtesy or a warning, and she began before he could.

"Monsieur l'inspecteur," she said. "You have come about a name on a list. I will save us both the theater. Yes, Elias Brandt has been under my roof since October. Yes, I have known it from the first night. No, I will not tell you where he is now, because where he is now is nowhere you can reach before the next tide, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise."

She said Lefèvre had gone very still. Inspectors, she supposed, were rarely handed confessions like parcels at the door.

"You understand," he said at last, "that I am obliged to search the house."

"You are obliged to try. Search, then. But hear me first, because after you search, you will have to arrest me, and I would rather you made your decision as a man than as a form."

He heard her. She told him all of it — the wreck, the satchel, the promise written in Henri's last letter in his careful schoolboy hand: *If ever Brandt needs shelter, give it, and count it a debt owed to me.* She told him about the bridge closing, about the lies she'd told badly, about the nets Brandt had mended and the roof he'd fixed on Madame Roux's shed without being asked. And then she told him about the night of the great storm — how the lamp had failed, how three boys had been running dark toward the rocks, and how two hands had carried oil up those stairs in winds that should have killed them both.

"One of those hands," she said, "belongs to the man on your list. I don't ask you to call that patriotism. I ask you to tell me what your list is *for*. If it exists to protect this republic, then explain to me which part of the republic was protected by letting those boys drown."

* * *

In the cellar, I did not pray. I had lost the habit somewhere between Berlin and the canal where I pulled a drowning Frenchman out of the water eleven years ago, and God and I had not reconciled since. But I found myself doing something adjacent to prayer — rehearsing arguments, composing speeches I would never deliver, bargaining with a ceiling of floorboards.

It was Marguerite who bargained instead. She told me that too.

"I want something from you," she said to Lefèvre. "And I will pay for it."

"The ministry does not make trades, madame."

"The ministry doesn't. Men do. Here is the trade." She laid it out the way she trimmed wicks — precisely, without flourish. She would resign her post. Effective immediately, in writing, signed before witnesses. She would surrender the keys of Pointe-Aubier, thirty-one years of her family's keeping, into the hands of the republic. In exchange: no search entered into record, no testimony demanded of her or of any islander, and a report reading, in its entirety, that the inspector crossed to Pointe-Aubier, found no evidence of the presence of any foreign national, and noted the keeper's resignation on grounds of health and family circumstance.

"And Brandt?" Lefèvre asked.

"Gone. Before you sail back. Where, I cannot say, because I will not know, and neither will you. Your report writes itself, monsieur. A clean page. How often does the ministry hand you one?"

He was quiet a long time. Through the floor I heard nothing but the clock, striking eleven, twelve.

When he finally spoke, she said, his voice had changed — the official varnish worn through to something older and tireder underneath.

"Do you know what they send me, madame? Lists. Every year, longer lists. Half the names are clerks who wrote the wrong letter, professors who signed the wrong petition, men whose crime is a surname and a border. And every year I go where they point, and I write what is required, and nothing I write protects anyone. It only files people." A pause. "Last month my own son asked me what I do, for his school essay. What could I tell him? That I file people?"

"So don't file this one," Marguerite said. "Spend it. You have power, monsieur — a little, a mean little amount, the kind they give men like you and me so we can feel the shape of what we lack. Power corrupts, they say. Fine. Let it corrupt you once, in the direction of mercy. Nobody will ever know. That is the price of it, and the gift."

Another silence. Then, quietly:

"If this ever reaches Paris—"

"It won't. I am resigning. Resigned women are not believed; they are pitied. Pity is excellent insulation."

And Lefèvre — she said — laughed. One short, cracked syllable of a laugh, the first in what he implied had been years, and then he stood, and took out a plain notebook, and wrote while she dictated.

* * *

At dusk he knocked on the cellar door himself.

I will carry that knock to my grave. Not boots and authority — knuckles, gently, twice, like a neighbor asking to borrow a lamp. I opened the door and he looked at me for a long moment, this tired man in his fraying coat, and I braced for the arithmetic behind his eyes: career against conscience, list against lantern.

"You're the cartographer," he said.

"I was."

"My son likes maps." He almost smiled. "Draw him one someday. Of some place that doesn't exist. Those are the only safe countries now." He glanced back up the stairs, toward the sound of Marguerite moving in the kitchen. "She drove a hard bargain. Harder than most ministers I've dealt with. Sign nothing else she puts in front of you, Brandt. It's how she wins." He settled his coat. "Your boat leaves on the morning tide. The fisherman — Tomas — will take you past the point before anyone at the mainland office is awake. He volunteered. They all volunteered. I suggest you never learn their names properly; it's safer for everyone, and besides—" he stepped out into the blue evening, "—they seem determined to keep you whether you like it or not."

Then he paused at the foot of the stairs and did not turn around.

"For what it is worth," he said, "my report will say I found nothing. For once in my life, that will be true."

* * *

I went up and found Marguerite at the table, the resignation letter finished, the keys of Pointe-Aubier lying beside it in a small iron ring worn smooth by three generations of her family's hands.

"You gave up the tower," I said. "For me. For a debt you never owed."

"I owed it," she said. "Henri lent his life to you once, Elias. I'm only settling accounts." She pushed the keys aside, and her voice went quieter. "Besides. A light isn't the lamp. I learned that this winter. A light is whoever is willing to climb the stairs."

Tomorrow the ferry would come — the first of the new year, the first in months — and Tomas would take me out ahead of it, past the point, into open water, with Henri's last letter in my coat and a blank map in my head waiting for a country that didn't exist yet.

But tonight there was one thing left I hadn't asked her.

"Come with me," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Down the lane, a door opened — Madame Roux's cottage, lamplight spilling yellow onto the snow — and someone called Marguerite's name, warmly, the way you call someone who belongs to you.

"No," she said, and smiled, and reached for her coat. "But walk me down. They're expecting us both."

Downstairs, the whole island seemed to be waiting.
