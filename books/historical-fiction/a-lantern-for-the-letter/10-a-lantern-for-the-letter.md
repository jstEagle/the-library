## 10. A Lantern for the Letter

The ice broke on the twenty-eighth of December, and by the thirtieth the ferry was coming back.

Marguerite heard it before she saw it — the low cough of the engine carrying across the flat gray water, a sound she had not heard in two months. She stood at the gallery rail of Pointe-Aubier with her hands bare and burning in the cold, and watched it grow from a smudge into a shape, and thought: *so that is what hope sounds like. Like something ordinary.*

Below her, in the yard, Elias Brandt was stacking firewood he would never burn.

He had taken to doing that in the weeks since the long night — since the storm, since the dark boat, since the two of them had climbed the tower stairs together with their arms around each other's waists against the wind and put the light back into the sky with their own four hands. He worked now the way men work when they cannot say anything: steadily, uselessly, beautifully. He had mended the fence at Madame Roux's. He had re-shingled Tomas's roof. And every morning he split wood for a stove Marguerite would not be tending past tomorrow.

She went down to him.

"The ferry," she said.

He set down the axe. For a moment they only looked at each other, breath smoking between them, and all the things they did not say stood there too — Henri, the satchel of maps, the burned skiff, Lefèvre's gray face across the table when he said *I will look away once, madame, and never again.*

"It's time," Brandt said.

"Yes."

He wiped his hands on his coat, and she saw the shape in the inner pocket — the letter, soft-cornered now from handling, wrapped in oilcloth. Henri's last letter. Written the autumn before the fever took him, addressed in his looping hand to *E. Brandt, cartographer, my friend* — a letter Marguerite had found sealed in the drawer of the chart table after the funeral and had kept for three years without knowing why, until the night a half-drowned man clutched a satchel on her rocks and she understood at last that some letters are not meant to be read but delivered.

"You'll give it to him yourself," she said. "Not post it. Not leave it. You'll stand in front of him and hand it over."

Brandt almost smiled. "You sound like him."

"No one sounds like Henri." She turned toward the tower. "Come. Help me light her one last time."

* * *

They lit her together, though by rights it was no longer necessary — the sun still an hour from setting, the water calm, the season's work nearly done. But Marguerite had decided, in the way she decided everything, alone and without announcement, that the last lighting would be done properly. Wicks trimmed. Lens polished until the brass threw back her own face, warped and golden. The clockwork wound to its full turn so that whoever came after her would inherit a light already turning, already alive.

"Pointe-Aubier," Brandt said quietly, reading the plate above the lamp as if he were surveying it. "Do you know I have drawn this coast eleven times? Eleven surveys, and I never once drew the light working. It was always a notation. *Light, unattended. Keeper, one woman.*" He looked at her. "I am sorry for every map that made you a notation."

"I liked being a notation." She touched the lens housing, warm now under her palm. "It meant nobody was bothering me."

"And now?"

Now. She let the word sit. Now there was a cottage in the village — Tomas's sister's old house below the church, offered to her three nights ago by lamplight, by six islanders standing awkwardly in her kitchen as if they had rehearsed and then forgotten their lines. *It's empty,* Tomas had said. *It's got a good roof. I should know; I did the roof.* Madame Roux had said nothing at all, which from Madame Roux was a signed confession of affection. The priest had blessed the doorway before anyone had asked him to.

Now she was a keeper with no tower, or a neighbor with a house. She had not yet learned which.

"Now," she said, "I suppose I'll find out what people do who aren't keeping anything."

Brandt shook his head slowly. "You are keeping something. You've been keeping me since October. And before that—" he patted the letter through his coat "—you kept this."

The clockwork engaged. Above them the great lens began its slow rotation, and the first beam of the evening swung out across the water like a long arm reaching for the mainland, and Marguerite felt the old ache in her chest — the ache that was also the whole point of her, the thing Henri had loved her for and the thing she could not explain to inspectors or priests: that a light is not a building. A light is a promise you keep in the dark, over and over, for people you will never meet.

"Go down," she said. "Before I make this sentimental."

* * *

The ferry came in on the morning tide of the thirty-first, riding low with coal and flour and the year's first mail. Half the island stood on the quay to watch, because the first crossing after the ice was always an event, and because everyone knew — the way islands know things, without wires or newspapers — that the German was leaving on it.

No one said goodbye to him. That was not how it was done. Instead:

Tomas pressed a coil of new rope into his hands and said, "For your boat. Wherever yours ends up." Which meant: *I know we burned your skiff, and I will carry that.*

Madame Roux handed up a basket with bread and a jar of preserves and said, "These are not for you, they are for the ferryman, tell him." Which meant the opposite.

And the priest, Father Anselme, took both of Brandt's hands in his own chapped ones and said, in his terrible German, "Gute Reise," and then, in French, "God does not read government lists."

Marguerite stood apart, at the end of the quay, because she had chosen to be the keeper to the last and keepers do not crowd.

Brandt walked to her through the small crowd. Up close his eyes were the same eyes she had pulled out of the wreck foam in October — tired, precise, kind in a way he seemed embarrassed by.

"The letter," he said, and drew it out, oilcloth gleaming. "Are you certain? It's yours by marriage. His words."

"They're his promise," Marguerite said. "Promises travel with the person who owes them." She looked at the sea, then back. "Henri wrote it before either of us knew you'd need it. That's the whole trick of Henri. He was always early. Read it on the water. He'd have wanted an audience."

Brandt held the letter a moment longer, then tucked it away inside his coat, over his heart, where it had lived these three days. "Marguerite." He said her name the way he said place names — carefully, as if fixing it on a map. "When this is over — after the war that is coming, because it is coming — I will come back. As a friend, openly. Will there be a door?"

"There will be a door," she said. "It may be a cottage door. It will open."

The whistle blew. He gripped her hand once, hard, the grip of a man sealing a bargain with the only currency he had left, and walked up the gangway, and did not look back, because men like him never did, and she was glad of it, because if he had looked back she might have done something undignified on a public quay.

The ferry backed water, turned, and grew small. Marguerite stood with the wind pulling her shawl and watched it until it was a smudge again, and then not even that.

In his coat pocket, riding east over the winter sea, was Henri's handwriting. In her chest, riding nowhere, was the strange lightness of a debt paid by someone else's hand.

* * *

She lit the tower at dusk.

There was no technical need — the relief man, a young fellow from the mainland service named Pellier, had arrived on the same ferry, pink-faced and eager, and had spent the afternoon being shown the clockwork and the wicks and the trick of the draft door in an east wind. But the thirty-first of December, 1899, was hers, and the last lighting of the century at Pointe-Aubier would be done by the last keeper of Pointe-Aubier, and young Pellier, to his credit, understood without being told. He stood aside on the gallery and watched her work with the reverence of a man watching a ceremony he'd only read about.

When the lamp caught and the lens began to turn, Marguerite stepped back and looked at her hands. Forty years of lamp-oil in the creases, her mother's knuckles, Henri's ring gone thin at the band.

"Well," she said aloud, to the light, to the rock, to the century. "That's done."

Pellier cleared his throat behind her. "Madame Sandoval — the keys."

She took them off the hook by the door — the iron key, the brass key, the small key to the oil store that had hung beside her bed for nineteen years — and weighed them in her palm. They were heavier than they looked. Everything true was.

"Mind the draft door in an east wind," she said, and put them in his hand, and closed his fingers over them like a woman closing a dead man's eyes.

Then she went down the hundred and twelve steps, and out the door, and did not look back either.

* * *

The cottage smelled of other people's winters — cold ash, dried lavender, the ghost of somebody's soup. Her few boxes sat in the middle of the floor where Tomas's boys had dumped them, and the single window gave onto the lane and the church wall and nothing else. No sea. She had not thought about that until now, standing in the blue dusk of her first owned darkness, and the lack of it went through her like a splinter.

A knock. Then another knock, before she answered, because islanders did not wait.

Tomas filled the doorway with lantern in hand, and behind him, arranged along the lane like a delegation, stood Madame Roux, and Father Anselme, and the Roux boy, and old Yvonne from the nets, and others, faces she knew better than she had ever admitted.

"We thought," Tomas said, with the eloquence of a man who had rehearsed and forgotten his lines again, "the stove wants lighting. First night and all."

"It's bad luck," Madame Roux declared, pushing past him, "to spend New Year's Eve alone. Everyone knows this. It's practically doctrine."

"I don't recall it in scripture," said the priest, coming in anyway.

They lit her stove. They set food on her table that none of them would admit to having prepared. The Roux boy asked if the tower was haunted and was told firmly that it was not, by three people simultaneously. And at midnight, when the church bell stumbled through the year's turning, they stood in her kitchen — her kitchen — with glasses of rough cider, and Tomas raised his and said, gruffly, to the room rather than to her:

"To the light."

"To the light," they echoed.

Marguerite drank, and looked at their faces in the stove-glow — weathered, suspicious, loyal, hers — and understood at last what she had inherited. Not the tower. Not the keys. This. A debt paid forward in bread and bad jokes and a roof mended twice, belonging earned in the dark like light itself: kept, and kept, and kept, until one day you looked up and it was yours.

Later, when they had gone and the stove ticked and the lane was quiet, she stood at the little window and looked east, toward the water she could not see, and knew exactly where the beam was sweeping at this hour, faithful and blind, over a sea with one less enemy on it.

Somewhere out there, a letter was traveling. Somewhere out there, a door was promised.

She banked the fire, and slept, and in the morning — the first morning of the new century — woke to the smell of bread left warm on her sill, and went to the door, and opened it.
