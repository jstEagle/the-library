## 1. The Weight of Paper

The morning after the funeral, the workshop smelled of tallow and wet wool and something else beneath both — ink, old ink, the particular sour-sweetness of iron gall gone brown in its bottles. Ines Halloran stood at his master's desk with a blank inventory sheet before him and did not write on it.

Outside, Vellamont was doing what river towns do after a death: carrying on. Carts ground through slush on the quay road. A dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. Somewhere down the lane a woman was scolding children whose laughter kept escaping her like birds from a sack. The world had no interest in pausing for Aldous Vane, who had spent forty years measuring it.

Ines dipped his pen. Wrote: *One drafting table, oak, worn at left edge.* Stopped.

It was not that he couldn't catalogue. Cataloguing was half of what an apprentice mapmaker was — the other half being the drawing of lines so fine and true that a man could trust his life to them. He had been trained since he was eleven years old, an orphan out of the parish rolls with steady hands and no family to distract him, which Master Vane had once said aloud, without malice, as though it were a qualification listed in a contract. *Steady hands, no attachments. Ideal.*

He had thought, at fourteen, that this was a compliment.

Now he sat among the effects of the only person who had ever claimed him, even claim was too generous a word, and the inventory sheet felt like a shovel.

*One drafting table, oak, worn at left edge.* The wear was from Vane's elbow. Forty years of the same elbow, leaning into the same work, until the wood remembered him. Ines put down the pen and pressed his own thumb into the hollow and held it there until his eyes burned, and then he picked the pen back up, because grief that stopped you working was just another way of lying down, and Vane had never once lain down. Not when the fever took him last winter. Not three weeks ago, coughing blood into a rag behind his back while he corrected Ines's rendering of the weir at Tarn's Mill — *the curve is wrong, boy, water doesn't apologize* — not even on the last morning, when he'd made Ines bring him the good dividers so he could check them against each other one final time, as though accuracy were a prayer he intended to finish.

*One drafting table. One stool. One lamp, brass, dented base.*

*Assorted pens, nineteen. Nibs, copper, in tin box.*

*Dividers, brass, pair, master quality.*

He wrote these things because they were true and because truth in columns was the only language he had for what he could not say. But there were four drawers in the desk and he catalogued only three of them, and he knew it, and his hand did not shake, which surprised him.

The fourth drawer held the atlas.

* * *

It was not called an atlas. Officially, in the guild's ledger, it was *Vane — miscellaneous folios, unfinished commission*, and officially it had been commissioned by nobody, because Vane had been careful, and carelessness was the one sin he never committed. It lived in a binding of grey board wrapped in oilcloth, and it was heavy the way lead is heavy — flatly, densely, without any drama about it. Ines lifted it now and set it on the table beside the inventory sheet and looked at it the way you look at a sleeping animal you have decided, without quite admitting the decision, not to wake.

He had known of it for years. Apprentices know everything and are told nothing; that is the whole architecture of the arrangement. He had seen Vane working on it late, seen the grey board come out of the drawer on evenings when the guild steward's rounds were done, seen his master hunched over it with a magnifier and an expression Ines had never seen him wear over any paid commission — not concentration exactly. Something closer to grievance. As if the maps argued with him and he was losing, and intended to keep losing until he won.

Vane had never explained it. He had explained everything else, endlessly, mercilessly — the mathematics of contour intervals, the ethics of scale distortion (*a liar's first tool is a comfortable map*), why north was a convention and not a fact and why a good surveyor must nonetheless treat it as scripture. But the grey atlas he had worked on in silence, and in the last month, as the cough worsened, he had begun to say things instead.

*Boy. If anything happens to me, the folios go nowhere. Not to the guild. Nowhere. Do you hear me?*

*I hear you, master.*

*You don't. You will.* A long coughing fit, then, quieter, almost gentle, which was worse: *You're the steadiest hands I ever trained. That's all I'll say. Steady hands, and no one watching them.*

And then he had died on a Tuesday, between the second bell and the third, sitting upright in his chair with the magnifier still in his fist, and the town had given him a decent funeral because he'd paid the sexton in advance — of course he had, Vane planned everything except how to stay alive — and now it was Wednesday, and the guild steward would come at noon to inventory the workshop, because dead men's property flowed downhill to institutions the way water flows to the sea, and Ines had decided sometime in the night, staring at the ceiling above his pallet, that some of this water was not going to reach the sea.

He did not examine the decision. Examining it would mean finding reasons, and reasons could be argued with, and he had already noticed about himself that he could lose arguments with reasonable men. So he simply acted, the way Vane had taught him to act with a line that must be drawn true: commit, and do not lift the pen.

He fetched the floorboard pry-bar from under the stairs — used for lifting boards when the drains backed up, innocent, dull, unremarkable — and chose the spot in ten seconds, because he had helped repair that corner of the floor two winters ago and had never forgotten which joist ran where. Under the third board from the wall, beside the cold hearth, where the wood was darkened by years of ash-ghost and no steward's eye would linger. He wrapped the atlas again in its oilcloth, and added, after a moment, the tin box of copper nibs — Vane's nibs, cut and recut by his own hand, each one shaped to the pressure of his grip — because it seemed obscene to let them be weighed and valued like candlesticks.

The board went back down. The dust went back over the seam, rubbed in with his thumb, ash-dark, invisible. He stood, and found his heart was going hard and fast, and made himself stand there another full minute, doing nothing, letting the room return to being a room.

That was when someone knocked at the street door.

* * *

Steward Bram Halloway was a broad, unhurried man with the face of a man who has spent thirty years saying unpleasant things in pleasant tones. He came in stamping snow off his boots, bringing the cold in with him like a debt collector, and he clapped Ines on the shoulder with a hand like a ham.

"Master Halloran." He always used the honorific with apprentices, which was either kindness or mockery depending on the day. "Poor business, poor business. A great loss to the guild. Sit, sit — no, don't sit, we've work. Show me what you've begun."

Ines showed him the sheet. Halloway read it the way he read everything, quickly and generously, nodding, murmuring, and Ines watched him and understood that the generosity was the technique. A man who felt trusted did not count the drawers.

"Good start. Thorough. He trained you well." Halloway moved through the workshop with the ease of ownership, tapping the table, weighing the lamp, opening drawers. First. Second. Third. Ines stood very still by the hearth and discovered that a man can hear his own blood if the room is quiet enough.

"The instruments go to the guild stores," Halloway said. "Standard practice. Some of it you'll keep access to — the dividers, certainly, you'll need them. The paper stock, the inks. You're to have the workshop use of the space until the guild decides disposition." He paused at the desk, looking at the fourth drawer, and Ines felt the pause the way you feel a change in wind before any leaf moves.

"He kept papers here," Halloway said. "Folios. Commission work?"

"Some," Ines said. "Practice sheets. Old exercises."

"Mm." Halloway opened the drawer.

Inside lay exactly what Ines had placed there at dawn, having risen before the light to arrange it: a fat sheaf of student exercises, twenty years of them, Ines's own early attempts at lettering and coastline hachures, deliberately misfiled to look neglected. Beneath them, a few of Vane's genuine practice sheets — real ones, chosen because they were genuinely worthless. A man hiding a thing does best to hide it inside a smaller true thing.

Halloway flipped through them without interest. "Exercises."

"Yes, sir."

"He was teaching you the coast hatching? His coast hatching?" Something almost warm entered the steward's voice. "There wasn't a draftsman south of the capital could match it. The guild will want examples preserved. I'll take a selection." He tucked a handful of sheets under his arm, closed the drawer, and turned back to Ines with the pleasant face fully deployed. "The rest — the personal papers, any commissions in progress, unfinished work — those are guild property now. You understand. Standard practice. Anything of record value especially. The Crown's been particular lately about records."

*Particular about records.* Ines kept his face in the configuration he had learned across eleven years of apprenticeship: attentive, slightly dim, entirely safe. "I haven't come across anything like that, sir. Mostly the exercises. And letters."

"Letters?"

"Suppliers. Ink orders. Paper orders."

Halloway nodded slowly, and for a moment — Ines would remember this moment for years afterward — the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and what looked out of the steward's face was tiredness, plain human tiredness, the look of a man carrying something heavier than inventories. Then it was gone, folded away like a chart, and he was pleasant again.

"Well. If anything turns up — folios, surveys, anything in his hand that isn't exercise work — you'll bring it to me directly. Not to the chapter house, to me. There are people coming in the spring who'll want the guild's holdings in order, and I'd sooner they find order than find gaps. Gaps make questions, questions make trouble, and trouble lands on small shoulders first." He patted Ines's shoulder again, gently this time. "Yours are small shoulders, Master Halloran. No offense meant. Just the shape of the world."

"No offense taken, sir."

At the door he paused. Snow was coming harder now, fat flakes falling straight down, the kind that meant the river was freezing early and hard. "He spoke of you, near the end. Asked me to see you kept on. Said you'd a future if you kept your head down." Halloway smiled without much in it. "Head down. His words. Odd advice from a man who spent his life looking at horizons, eh?"

He left. The door thumped. The snow fell.

* * *

Ines ate bread and cheese standing up, tasting none of it, and then he went back to the third board from the wall and lifted it and took out the atlas and unwrapped it on the table, because he had realized, somewhere between the knocking and the leaving, that he had hidden a thing he had barely looked at. He had protected a mystery he hadn't earned. Vane had taught him better than that: *never render what you haven't surveyed, boy. Guesswork dressed as draftsmanship is forgery.*

So he would look. Properly. Before the guilt or the fear or the spring could take the decision out of his hands.

He cleared the table. He trimmed a fresh lamp. He opened the grey boards.

The first sheet was a map of the river valley, the whole of it, from Vellamont up past the orchard country — drawn at a scale far finer than any commission would pay for, every bend of the river rendered in Vane's unmistakable hand, the coast hatching so fine it looked like breath on glass. Ines had seen his master's commissioned work all his life. This was different. Commissioned maps answered questions. This map was asking one.

In the lower margin, in tiny letters, was written: *As it stands, in the year of our Lord 1853. Let it stand so in the record.*

The second sheet was the orchard villages. Corryn, Fenn Hollow, Marle Bridge, the scattered crofts between — places Ines knew as names on the Crown's tax rolls, places the canal survey had marked, two years ago, with a single dismissive notation: *low-value riparian land, sparsely improved.* On Vane's sheet they blazed with detail. Field boundaries. Tree-by-tree plantings, dated. Wells. Boundary stones with their inscriptions copied out. And along every boundary line, in that same tiny margin hand, measurements — cross-checked, re-cross-checked, each one annotated with dates going back decades, some marked *per deed,* some marked *per witness,* one marked, chillingly, *per the old parish book, before they burned the half of it.*

Before they burned the half of it.

Ines turned pages. His hands were steady — steady hands, ideal — but something behind them was not. The atlas went on and on. Boundaries that matched no survey he had ever seen filed. Holdings documented in such loving, furious detail that it could only be evidence, and evidence of what? Of the fact that these villages existed? That they had existed, some of them, for longer than the Crown's own claims ran?

He reached a sheet near the middle and stopped.

It was the canal route itself — the official line, copied faithfully from the Crown survey, the one every guild member had applauded at the autumn meeting. But Vane had overlaid it on his own measured valley, and where the two disagreed, he had marked the disagreement in red, and the red was everywhere. The Crown's river bends did not bend where the river bent. The Crown's elevations rose where Vane's levels fell. It was not a difference of opinion. It was as if the official survey had been drawn by a man describing a valley he had never visited — or by a man describing a valley he wished existed, one in which the orchard villages sat low and worthless, already halfway drowned in the imagination, ready for the water.

In the margin of that sheet, one line, larger than the rest, pressed deep enough into the paper to be read by fingertip:

*They have redrawn the ground to drown it. Find the true lines, boy. Keep them true.*

Not addressed to anyone. Addressed to whoever opened the book.

Ines sat for a long time in the cold workshop with the lamp burning and the snow ticking against the shutters, and he did not think *this is treason* or *this is my ruin,* although both thoughts arrived, punctual as bells, and waited to be admitted. What he thought was simpler and more terrible: *he chose me.* Out of all the guild's draftsmen, all its licensed men with names and standing and votes, Aldous Vane had spent his last months pressing his deepest secret into the hands of an orphan apprentice with no standing — because standing could be revoked, licenses could be withdrawn, but a boy whom nobody bothered to watch had been free to carry a drawer's worth of truth across a room.

*Steady hands, and no one watching them.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river ice groaned somewhere in the dark, a long sound like a door being forced. Ines closed the atlas, wrapped it, lowered it beneath the floor, and set the board home, and knelt there a moment with his palm flat on the cold wood, the way you'd rest a hand on a grave.

Then he got up, cleaned the lamp, squared the inventory sheet on the desk, and added, in fair copy, the day's remaining entries — *one lamp, brass; assorted pens; one stool* — a complete and honest account of everything except the thing that mattered, written in the best hand of his life.

Tomorrow the steward would want the inventory finished. Tomorrow the town would begin talking about spring, about the commission, about the bridge closing for the season. Tomorrow he would have to decide what a man owed to a dead man's instructions when following them made him a criminal.

Tonight he banked the fire, and lay on his pallet in the dark above the empty workshop, listening to the ice talk in the river, and understood with perfect clarity that he had already decided. The decision had been made sometime between the fourth drawer and the knock at the door — perhaps before that, perhaps years ago, in all the mornings an exacting old man had leaned over his shoulder and taught him that water doesn't apologize and neither should a true line.

The atlas was under the floor. The steward wanted everything handed over. And in the morning, Ines Halloran would open the atlas again, and study it properly this time — because a voice pressed deep enough into paper to be read by fingertip deserved at least that much.

*Find the true lines, boy.*

He slept, badly, like a man guarding a door in his sleep.
