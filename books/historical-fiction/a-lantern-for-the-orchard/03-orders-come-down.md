## 3. Orders Come Down

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which Ines would remember later because Tuesdays were the days the guild steward, Master Corben Hale, counted the candle stubs.

He was grinding pigment when the rider came — a government courier in oiled leather, boots crusted with road salt, who did not dismount but handed down a document sealed with the broad blue wax of the Crown Survey Office. Hale took it with both hands, the way a man accepts a summons or a diagnosis. The courier turned his horse and was gone before the seal was even broken, as though whatever the paper carried might be catching.

Ines kept grinding. His mortar made its small patient sound — ochre against stone — while Hale read at the doorway, lips moving slightly, an old habit he'd never cured. The workshop smelled of linseed and cold ash and, underneath everything, the ghost of Vane's pipe tobacco that no amount of sweeping had lifted from the rafters.

"Put down the ochre," Hale said.

Ines put it down.

Hale read the letter aloud. He had a steward's voice, flat and carrying, built for reading tariffs aloud to rooms of restless men, and he gave the Crown's prose exactly the intonation it deserved: none.

*By order of the Commissioners Appointed for the Survey of the Vellamont Canal Route, all papers, maps, plates, instruments, drafts, marginalia, correspondence, and effects of the late Aldous Vane, Mapmaker to the Guild, are hereby declared property of the Crown, having been produced in service thereof. Said materials are to be catalogued and surrendered intact to the Office of the Commission no later than the first week of March, upon arrival of the survey party. Any materials found withheld shall constitute theft from the Crown, punishable under statute.*

There was more — signatures, countersignatures, a clause about instruments — but the shape of it was done. *All papers. All maps. All marginalia.*

Ines looked at his hands. There was ochre under the nails, ground fine as thought.

"The commission comes in spring," Hale said. "March." He folded the letter along its original creases with unnecessary precision. "That gives us eleven weeks."

"Eleven weeks," Ines said, because something was required of him.

"You were closer to him than anyone."

"I mixed his ink."

"You slept in his house." Hale said it without accusation, which was worse. "You know where everything is. You'll help me catalogue it all. Every sheet, every instrument, every scrap in his own hand. We'll make our inventory first, so there can be no dispute about what was surrendered."

"Yes, Master Hale."

Hale studied him a moment longer. He was not a cruel man; Ines had learned that much in six years. He was a man who believed that institutions were the only raft on a deep river, and that anyone who let go of the raft drowned stupidly. "You'll be provided for," he said. "The guild doesn't discard apprentices. When the commission takes the papers, you'll be transferred to the drafting room in Corvane. It's a good post. Better prospects than this river town will ever offer you."

"I don't want Corvane."

"Want is a word for men with standing." Hale said it wearily, not unkindly, like a lesson he himself had paid full price for. "Go on. Get back to your pigments."

Ines went back to his pigments. His hands worked. His mind was already elsewhere — up the stairs, behind the loose board at the base of the linen press where the atlas lay wrapped in oilcloth, breathing its slow paper breath.

Eleven weeks.

* * *

That night he could not sleep, and around the second bell he lit a stub of candle and went down into the workshop, telling himself he only wanted to look at the atlas, only to confirm it was still there, only to run his thumb along the oilcloth the way other men touch a saint's medal.

It was there. Of course it was there. Nothing had moved it but him.

But once he had it unwrapped on the drafting table he could not stop reading. He turned to the sheets of the eastern orchard villages — Ashenmoor, Little Ferrenby, the hamlets along the old drover road — and looked again at what he had spent two days refusing to understand: that Vane had drawn the boundaries true. Not the boundaries of the Crown survey, which ran neat and convenient along the canal route, but the boundaries as the deeds recorded them, as the parish books recorded them, as the oldest trees themselves seemed to record them, planted in rows that followed lines no Crown surveyor had bothered to walk.

And in the margin, in Vane's small exact hand, beside a bend in the river that the official map straightened out of existence:

*The river has turned here twice since the 1809 flood. The survey of 1847 shows neither turn. A map that forgets a river's memory is not mistaken. It is obedient.*

Obedient. Ines read the word four times. It was such a quiet word for such a large accusation.

He sat down on Vane's stool — the stool still held the shape of the dead man, or Ines imagined it did — and tried to think like a mapmaker instead of a frightened boy. Facts first. What did he actually possess? An unfinished atlas, perhaps three-quarters complete, documenting the orchard country with a precision that contradicted the Crown's own published survey. Marginalia that grew more pointed the deeper one read, as if the man had been arguing with someone across years. And now a letter declaring all of it Crown property, to be surrendered intact, with *withheld* named as theft.

What did he actually have? No family. No money beyond his apprentice's allowance of eight marks a month. No name that opened doors — Halloran, a foundling's surname given by a workhouse clerk who'd liked the sound of it. No standing whatsoever. If Aldous Vane, a man with forty years' reputation, could be declared posthumously obedient to the Crown, then what was Ines Halloran? A line item. A name on a transfer order to Corvane. And if he made trouble — if a single sheet went missing between inventory and surrender —

He saw it with terrible clarity, sitting in the cold workshop: how easily he could be erased. Not killed. Nothing so dramatic. Simply corrected. A note in the guild ledger: *apprentice dismissed, character unsatisfactory.* A reference withheld. A door in Corvane quietly closed. Men like Ines did not fall; they were subtracted, and the sum never changed.

Vane had been subtracted too, in his way. Dead at sixty-one of his heart, and already the town spoke of him as a talented eccentric, a man who'd gone strange in his last years, who'd walked the orchards talking to farmers instead of gentlemen. That was how it began — not with the fire but with the adjective.

The candle guttered. Ines wrapped the atlas back in its oilcloth, replaced the board, pressed the floor dust into the seam with the heel of his hand until even he couldn't find it.

Then he stood in the middle of the dark workshop and admitted it to himself plainly, because a mapmaker deals in plain things: he had already decided. Sometime between the funeral and tonight — maybe the moment he'd heard Hale's boots on the stair and felt the atlas's weight through the floorboards — he had decided that not everything would go into the inventory.

He didn't yet know what he would do about it. He didn't know if he was capable of anything braver than hiding paper under a floorboard. But the decision was made, the way a river makes a decision about which way to run long before the water arrives.

* * *

The catalogue began Thursday. They worked through Vane's effects drawer by drawer: dividers, scale rules, the brass alidade with its worn boxwood case, bundles of field notes tied with string gone soft as cloth. Hale inventoried aloud and Ines wrote, and between them they performed the strange liturgy of reducing a life to columns.

"Instrument case, walnut, containing six pens. Field book, number fourteen, October entries, incomplete. Correspondence, Guild of Cartographers, 1831 to 1850, four hundred and eleven items." Hale paused over a bundle. "He kept everything. Forty years of receipts for ink."

"He said paper remembers what men won't," Ines said. Then wished he hadn't.

Hale looked at him. "Did he say that often?"

"Sometimes."

"Hm." Hale set the bundle down and moved on, and Ines understood that the steward was not a fool. He was counting candle stubs and counting other things too. He knew an apprentice could hide things. He was choosing, for now, to believe he wouldn't.

That was the worst part — being trusted by a man whose trust depended entirely on Ines betraying either the guild or the truth, with no third door marked *exit.*

They worked five days. By Friday evening the inventory stood at ninety-three pages of fair copy, and every item on it existed except one: the atlas itself, which appeared nowhere, because no one alive except Ines knew it existed, and Vane had left no mention of it in his will. The atlas was a ghost. Ghosts, Ines reasoned, could not be stolen.

On Saturday morning the second letter came.

This one was addressed not to the guild but to *the Executor of the Estate of A. Vane,* and it was signed personally — a flowing, confident signature — by **Corvus Lyle, Historian to the Commission.** Ines watched Hale open it, and watched something move behind the steward's face, some recalculation.

Lyle wrote beautifully. That was the first thing. The letter was courteous almost to the point of tenderness, condoling the guild on its loss, praising Vane's early work in terms that would have pleased the living man — *the coastal charts of 1826 remain, in my judgment, unsurpassed* — and then, with a sentence so smooth it passed before Ines could catch it, doing the second thing:

*Naturally the later materials will require editorial attention. A great mind in decline produces fragments that, unexamined, may misrepresent both the man and the Crown's works. The Commission intends to ensure Master Vane's legacy is presented accurately.*

Accurately. Ines heard Vane's voice say *obediently*, and had to keep his face still.

"There," Hale said, setting the letter down. "You see? They'll handle it properly. Lyle is a scholar, not a soldier. He wants Vane remembered well."

"And if what Vane drew isn't accurate?" Ines asked. He heard his own voice as if from outside — level, curious, a craftsman's question. "If his last work contradicts the survey?"

Hale gathered the letters together, squared their edges. "Then his last work was wrong," he said. "Ines. Listen to me." He came around the table and stood close, and lowered his voice, and for a moment he wasn't the steward at all but just a tired man of fifty who had served institutions his whole life and seen what happened to people who hadn't. "You're twenty-two years old. You have no family, no money, no name worth printing. I say this not to wound you but because nobody else will say it honestly: the world does not protect boys like you. It uses you up and writes you off its books. The guild is the only wall between you and that, and the guild survives by handing over what the Crown asks for. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I should comply."

"I'm telling you that in March, when the commission crosses that bridge, everything in this building must be on my inventory and nothing else. Whatever grief you feel, whatever loyalty — grief and loyalty are luxuries, boy. Compliance is food." He straightened. "Inventory resumes Monday. We'll do the loft and the cellar. Everything."

Everything. Ines nodded and thanked him and walked home through the falling dusk, past the chapel, past the cooper's yard where someone was singing a working song with words too rough for church, and up to the empty house where a dead man's chair still sat by a dead man's stove.

Loft and cellar. Everything.

Except, Ines thought, standing in the doorway with the cold at his back, the thing that mattered most was not in the loft or the cellar. It was under a floorboard in a room above, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread, and it could hang him, and he could not — his hands told him this more surely than his head — he could not give it up.

March, Hale had said. Eleven weeks.

But the bridge, someone had mentioned at supper that week, would close with the ice any day now. And after it closed, nothing crossed the river until thaw — not couriers, not commissions, not orders.

Not even, Ines thought, pulling off his boots in the dark, instructions.

He lay awake listening to the wind come down the valley, and somewhere past midnight he began, without quite deciding to, to plan a crossing.
