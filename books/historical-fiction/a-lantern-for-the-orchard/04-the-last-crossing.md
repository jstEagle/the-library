## 4. The Last Crossing

Ines Halloran had spent three days learning the weight of a secret, and he had learned this: it was heavier than paper had any right to be.

The atlas — or what existed of it, forty-one sheets and a sheaf of loose notes sewn into a oilcloth wrapper the size of a prayer book — weighed less than a loaf of bread. He knew because he had weighed them both on Vane's brass scales, alone in the workshop with the shutters drawn, feeling like a thief measuring his own conscience. The loaf came to two pounds, three ounces. The truth of nine villages came to one pound, eleven.

And yet when he tucked it under his coat and walked out into the December morning, his knees ached as though he carried a millstone.

Vellamont in the last week before the closing was a town holding its breath. The ferryman had pulled his boat already; the river ran thick with pancake ice that ground and groaned against the pilings like something feeding. The bridge — the old toll bridge, three arches of grey stone older than any record anyone could produce — was still open, but the guild criers had posted the notice: *upon first sustained frost-lock of the channel, the crossing closes until thaw.* Everyone in Vellamont knew what that meant. The road east would die for four months, maybe five. Whatever you meant to do on the other side of winter, you did it now.

Ines meant to cross.

He had told himself a hundred versions of it in the nights since the funeral, since the steward's inventory clerk had run his fingers along the shelves Ines had emptied an hour earlier. He was going to the provincial archive at Harrowgate, where records were kept honestly, or as honestly as anywhere was; he would deposit the atlas under a false name, a dead man's name, Vane's own name if he dared, and return before anyone missed him. A week's journey. He would be back before Twelfth Night. The snow would cover his tracks and his absence alike.

It was a good plan. It was the plan his master would have approved of, and that thought — that Aldous Vane, who had spent twenty years making himself invisible, who had written *let no single hand hold all of it* in cipher on the atlas margins — would have nodded at the scheme made Ines feel steady and sick at once. Because he was beginning to understand, in the small hours over the marginalia, that Vane had not merely documented the orchard country. He had built an argument. Every boundary line, every measured acre, every date of title transfer was laid down in the master's clean hand like testimony given under oath. And testimony was useless in a drawer. Testimony wanted a court.

Ines wasn't sure he wanted to be the one carrying it there.

He went down to the bridge on the morning of the fourth day to buy his passage — the toll collector took coin in advance during closing week, and the queue was already long: drovers with cattle lowing at the frozen mud, a peddler with a cart stacked so high it looked like a walking haystack, two nuns from the priory upstream, a wedding party from Millbrook come to have the banns read in Vellamont's cathedral before the roads shut them in for the season. The bride was laughing. Her veil snapped in the wind off the water like a banner.

Ines paid his penny. The toll collector, a woman named Grett with forearms like ship rope, counted him without looking up. "Cross by tomorrow noon," she said. "After that I make no promises. Ice is moving early this year."

"I'll cross tonight," Ines said.

"Night crossings are fools' work."

"Then I'll cross at dawn."

She looked up then, briefly, and her eyes did the thing eyes did in Vellamont lately — measured him, filed him away. "Dawn, then. God keep your feet."

He turned to go, and stopped, because across the queue, on the far landing, someone was watching him.

A woman stood at the eastern end of the bridge with a basket on her hip, half-turned into the wind. She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, dressed in the plain dark wool of the orchard country, with a red scarf knotted at her throat that was the only bright thing about her. She wasn't staring, exactly. She had the look of someone taking an inventory — the same flat, complete attention Ines had seen in his master's eyes when he looked at a stretch of coastline. As if she were drawing him.

Maren, he thought. The orchard keeper. He'd heard the name at the cooper's shop when he'd gone to price a crate for shipping his master's instruments. *Maren will take the windfalls off your hands,* the cooper had said. *She feeds everyone who's lost their place.* There had been something in the way he said it — not charity. Custody.

She held his gaze for three seconds longer than politeness allowed, then turned and went, unhurried, up the far bank toward the orchards, her basket swinging. Ines found he had been holding his breath.

That night he packed. Not much: the atlas wrapped in oilcloth and then in a spare shirt, sewn flat against his chest where it wouldn't show; a change of clothes; Vane's best pen and a stub of sealing wax; bread, cheese, the flask of brandy the apothecary had pressed on him after the funeral. He wrote no letter. Letters could be read, and everything he might say would damn him faster than silence.

He lay down in his clothes and did not sleep. Toward the coldest hour, he heard the river change its voice — a new note under the wind, deeper, slower, the sound of water being closed like a book.

He was on the bridge before light.

Frost had furred every stone white, and the river below had gone strange — the current still moved in the middle channel but the edges had locked, plates of ice shouldering against the piers, building, grinding upward. The sound was continuous now, a groaning like a large animal in pain somewhere under the world. Ines crossed himself without thinking about it and kept walking.

Grett was at the far gate, lantern raised, arguing with a carter whose wagon was loaded with barrels of salted herring. "No. No wagons. Foot traffic only after this morning — look at the span." She jerked her chin, and Ines followed the line of her arm and saw it: the middle arch, the great one over the deep channel, had grown a crack. Not dramatic. A hairline, running from the crown of the arch down toward the waterline, weeping a seam of ice. But hairlines in stone were how bridges died.

"The crack was there last year," the carter said.

"It was an inch long last year. It's a yard long today." Grett turned and saw Ines. "You. Dawn man. You want to cross, cross now, and don't stop in the middle for God or money."

Ines stepped past her onto the eastern span. His heart was hammering against the oilcloth packet like a bird against a window. Fifty yards. Thirty. He could see the far bank clearly in the pre-light, the orchards marching up the slope in their winter ranks, black trees in white fields, smoke rising from Maren's cottage chimney in a straight unbroken column —

The bridge screamed.

There was no other word. A sound like iron dragged over iron, rising through the stone under his boots, and Ines dropped to his knees on instinct as the whole deck shuddered. Behind him, Grett was shouting. Ahead, the crack in the middle arch — he couldn't see it, but he heard it, heard stone let go somewhere deep in the structure, a report muffled by ice and water like a gunshot fired in another room.

Then the near pier shifted.

It didn't fall. That was the horror of it. The old bridge was too well built to simply fall. Instead the entire eastern span settled — a hand's width, maybe two — with a groan that went on and on, and the deck tilted just enough that Ines felt the slope of it in his stomach, and the crack in the middle arch opened from a hairline to a finger's width, and through it he could see, absurdly, the sky.

"BACK!" Grett's voice, cracked raw. "Everyone back! OFF THE BRIDGE!"

Ines got up and ran. The packet beat against his chest like a second heart. He came off the western end stumbling into the crowd, into the smell of frightened cattle and woodsmoke, and turned around with everyone else to watch.

For a long moment nothing happened. The bridge stood there in the grey dawn, tilted, cracked, alive with a faint tremor like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. The ice below it pushed and pushed. And then, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the eastern pier gave up its footing in the frozen gravel and slid — slid, gracefully, terribly, downstream — and the eastern span folded into the channel with a roar that scattered the rooks from every tree on both banks.

Ice closed over it almost at once. That was the strangest part. Within minutes the wreckage was only a broken line of stones showing through white, like a sentence half-erased.

The crowd stood silent. Somewhere behind Ines, the bride from Millbrook began to cry, quietly, into her veil.

Grett stood at the edge of the ruin with her lantern, and Ines watched her do the arithmetic every face around him was doing: four months. Five, if the spring came late. No crossing until the water ran free. No post riders except what came down the north forest track, if it stayed open, which it never fully did. Vellamont and the orchard country sealed in a jar for the winter.

He put his hand flat against the packet over his heart.

*Well,* said a voice in his head that sounded exactly like Aldous Vane, dry and precise. *Now you know how the land feels.*

* * *

From the eastern bank, Maren watched the bridge die, and felt the sound of it go through her chest and settle somewhere beneath her ribs like a swallowed coal.

She had come down at first light to walk the bank, as she did most mornings — checking the ice, checking the osier beds, doing nothing that needed doing because the morning walk was how she thought. She'd seen the apprentice mapmaker start across. Thin young man, Vane's boy, the one who'd come sniffing round the cooper's shop asking quiet questions with a quiet face. She'd marked him weeks ago, the way she marked everything that moved through her country: a stranger, alone, carrying something he thought nobody could see. They always carried something. In her experience people carried things the way rivers carried silt — invisibly, and always toward the sea of somebody else's trouble.

She had been about to turn back up the hill when the bridge screamed.

Now she stood with the other orchard folk — a knot of them had gathered as they always gathered, word traveling faster than event — and watched the stones settle into the channel, and listened to the crowd on the far side become, in one long exhale, a town that could not leave.

"There goes the salt," said Piet beside her. He was a big man, slow-spoken, a field hand from the Vosse farms who'd come to her table eight years ago with empty pockets and never quite left. "There goes the lamp oil, the iron stock, the paper, the medicine. There goes the tax man, too, mind." He said the last part with a certain grim satisfaction.

"And there goes us getting anything out, ever," Maren said.

Piet shrugged. "We weren't getting much out anyway."

That was true, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them comfortably, the way old truths did. The canal survey had taken the valley's future two years ago, in an office none of them would ever see. The maps were drawn. The flood line was calculated. All that remained was for the water to be convinced, and water, unlike people, always did as it was told eventually. The bridge closing changed nothing about that. It only meant that whatever happened this winter would happen with no witnesses coming or going.

"Look at them," said Teodor at her hip.

The boy was twelve and had been hers since he was six, since the fever year, since his mother set him down in Maren's doorway and walked into the orchard and did not walk out. He was pointing at the far crowd, at the little figures milling at the broken end of the world. One figure stood apart from the others, motionless, hand pressed to his chest.

"That's the mapmaker's lad," Teodor said. "I've seen him at the chapelyard. He stands at Vane's stone a long time. He doesn't pray, though. He reads. Like he's checking the spelling."

Maren looked at the boy, then at the distant figure. Checking the spelling. Out of the mouths of children who named trees.

"You shouldn't watch people so closely," she said, without conviction, because she had taught him to watch closely herself — to know every tree in the orchard by name, every grafted limb, every boundary stone. You couldn't keep a thing alive without watching it closely. That was the whole of keeping.

"Neither should you," Teodor said placidly.

The crowd on the far bank was thinning, figures turning away into the town, and the lone figure turned last. Before he went, he looked across the water — across the quarter mile of grinding ice that was suddenly an ocean — and even at that distance Maren felt the look arrive, the way you felt weather change. A man looking at the place he could not reach and could not leave, and understanding both at once.

She knew that look. She'd worn it herself for years, standing at this same bank, in the autumn after the seizure courts took the Vosse farms, watching the dispossessed come over the bridge with their bundles and their silences. She had stood here and understood that her life had split into the part before and the part after, and that the bridge was the hinge.

Now the hinge was broken, and the mapmaker's boy was on the wrong side of it, and so was she, and so was everyone.

"Well," Piet said. He was already turning uphill; Piet wasted no grief on stone. "Bridge is broke. Porridge won't stir itself."

They went up through the orchards, Teodor running ahead the way he always did, touching each third tree as he passed — *Brigid, Old Brigid's daughter, the Widow Ash, the Twins* — his breath smoking, his mittened hand patting the frozen bark like greeting kin. Maren walked slower. From the ridge she could see the whole valley laid out under frost: the river, the ruined bridge stitching the two banks together with its broken thread, the town beyond with its cathedral spire and its guildhall roof, and beyond that, invisible, everywhere, the canal route the Crown had drawn straight through the hearts of nine villages like a blade laid on a map.

They think we're finished, she thought. They drew the lines and burned the deeds and now the bridge is gone and the winter will do their waiting for them. By spring there'll be nothing left to argue with but memories, and memories don't stand up in court.

But she was the keeper of a ledger as well as an orchard, and a ledger teaches you one lesson above all others: nothing is lost while it is written down. Names, harvests, debts, days — she had recorded thirty years of them in her own careful hand, because the parish books lied and the assize rolls lied and the only honest history of this valley lived in kitchens and cellars and the heads of people the government called redundant.

The bridge falling didn't end the argument. It only meant the argument would be held indoors, all winter, between neighbors.

Behind her, down at the water, the ice groaned and shoved and built itself higher against the broken stones, patient as a clerk.

Maren turned up the hill toward home, toward the porridge and the household and the winter, and did not yet know that before the week was out the mapmaker's boy would come down from the town with his secrets sewn against his chest, knock at her door, and lie to her face.

She only knew, watching the smoke rise straight from her own chimney, that staying had finally stopped being a choice — for him, and for all of them — and that everything which mattered would now be decided by whoever kept the truest accounts.
