## 5. A Room Above the Cooper's

The cooper's shop smelled of oak shavings and hot iron, and Ines stood in the doorway of it for a full minute before anyone noticed him — which was, he would later realize, the last time that would happen.

He had walked the working quarter twice before letting himself approach the wide-open doors. The quarter sat below the guild hill like a different country: lanes unpaved and frozen to rutted iron, smoke from a dozen low chimneys hanging in the windless cold, laundry lines strung between houses even in January because nobody here could afford to let a thaw go to waste. He had lived four years in Vellamont and had never once come down this hill except to buy nails.

That was the point of it. That was the whole design of his life, if he was honest — and Master Vane had trained him to be honest about measurements if nothing else. The guildhall sat on its hill with its limestone face and its leaded windows, and everything the guild did was designed so that a man could pass through it without being seen. Apprentices walked along marked corridors. They ate at marked hours. They spoke when spoken to, and their names appeared in no ledger but the guild's own. Ines Halloran, orphan of nowhere in particular, taken at eleven years old because he had steady hands and no family to ask questions about him — he had been made invisible the way a wall is made, brick by brick, and he had never once thought to resent it until now.

Now invisibility was the thing keeping him alive. And he had come down into a quarter where nobody respected it.

"You're standing in my doorway," said a voice inside the shop. "Either come in or stop blocking the light."

He came in. The cooper was a broad woman with forearms like bridge cables and sawdust in her eyebrows, and she looked at him the way he imagined surveyors looked at land they intended to take — completely, without hurry, missing nothing. Behind her, two apprentices staved hoops over an anvil, and both of them had also stopped work to look at him. Three faces. He felt each one like a hand on his shoulder.

"I'm looking for a room," he said. "I was told — I was told Maren Oduya keeps rooms."

"Told by who?"

"A man at the market. He said—" He realized he had not prepared this part. He had prepared how to carry the atlas bundle under his coat, how to answer questions from the steward, how to walk past the guildhouse without glancing up. He had not prepared a lie simple enough to survive being told to a cooper. "I'm an apprentice mapmaker," he said, falling back on truth, which was the only coin he had. "My master died in the autumn. The guild is — reorganizing. I need lodging out of the way until spring."

"Out of the way." She repeated it without mockery, tasting it. "You're the first person in this town who's ever asked for that."

* * *

Her name was Berta Koval, and the room she showed him was above the shop, reached by an outside stair whose boards rang under his boots like a struck barrel. One room. A bed, a stool, a window facing east over the frozen lane, and a stovepipe that carried warmth up from the coopering floor below, so that the room smelled faintly of char all night long.

"One crown a week," Berta said. "Paid ahead. You eat at Maren's table down the lane if you want eating — she'll feed you whether you pay or not, so pay her anyway or you'll shame us both. Rules are: no drink upstairs, no visitors after dark, and whatever business brought you down off that hill, keep it behind your teeth. Not because we're afraid of it." She looked at him steadily. "Because we've got enough of our own."

"I don't have any business," Ines said.

Berta smiled for the first time, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Course you don't," she said, and went back down the stairs.

He unpacked nothing, because there was almost nothing to unpack: his father's razor, two shirts, a pencil case, and the atlas wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of his second coat, which hung on the peg by the door looking like any poor apprentice's spare coat, which was exactly what it had to look like for as long as he lived. He sat on the bed and listened to the shop below — the ring of the driver, the hiss of the cresset, Berta's voice calling measurements — and he thought: this is what a hiding place sounds like. It sounds like other people.

At Master Vane's house there had been no sound at all, toward the end. Just the master's cough moving through the rooms like weather, and Ines cataloguing papers in the next room so the sound wouldn't have to be heard alone.

* * *

Maren Oduya kept her table in a house at the end of Cooper's Lane, a long low building that had been something else once — a granary, Teodor would tell him later; everything in the quarter had been something else once — with a door that was never fully shut and a room beyond it where twelve people ate at two tables pushed together.

He went down the first evening because the alternative was bread bought alone and eaten alone, and though loneliness had been his native climate for twenty-two years, the funeral had changed the temperature of it somehow. Grief alone was bearable. Grief alone in a room that smelled of someone else's death was a different animal.

Twelve people stopped talking when he came in.

He counted them the way Vane had taught him to count — quickly, by categories. Three old men, one blind. Four women ranging from gray-haired to a girl of maybe fifteen. A young man with a burn-scarred forearm. A boy of ten or eleven who was staring at him with frank, total interest, chewing with his mouth open. A broad quiet man at the end of the table who nodded at him and went back to his food. And at the head, ladling out barley soup with the economy of a woman who knew exactly how far twelve portions stretched, Maren.

She was perhaps fifty, square-built, with gray coming in at her temples and hands that had been orchard hands before they were anything else — he could see it in the way her fingers moved, the particular calluses of pruning and picking that no other labor builds. She looked at him the way Berta had, but longer, and with something else underneath the assessment. Something almost like recognition.

"The mapmaker," she said. Not a question.

"Berta told you?"

"Berta tells me everything that happens on this lane. Sit. There's soup. There's bread if Piet hasn't finished it — Piet, leave the man some bread."

The broad quiet man at the end pushed the loaf down the table toward Ines without a word, and Ines sat, and the talking resumed around him — but differently. He understood within minutes that it had resumed *for* him, that the conversation was a fabric being deliberately woven over his silence so that his silence wouldn't have to explain itself. They talked about ice on the millrace, about a cow calving at the Henderson place, about whether the river would freeze solid enough by Candlemas to walk a cart across. Ordinary talk. But he caught, beneath it, the other current: the words *the flats*, *before the seizure*, *her mother's trees*, dropped and picked up and set aside like tools these people were used to passing among themselves.

Nobody asked him his name. Somebody had already told them.

Afterward, while the bowls were being scraped — everyone scraped their own bowl, he noticed, including Maren, including the children; there was no hierarchy in scraping — the blind old man turned his white eyes toward Ines with terrible accuracy and said, "You sit like Aldous Vane used to sit."

The room went quiet again, and this time the quiet was not woven for him.

"You knew my master?" Ines said.

"Knew *of* him," the old man said. "Tam Oduya. Before my eyes went I'd see him up on the ridge some mornings, measuring. Never came down. Never spoke to a soul. Just stood up there with his instruments like a heron, and then gone." He turned his head slightly. "You've got his stillness. He waited like the ground waits. So do you."

"He was my master for four years," Ines said carefully. "He taught me drafting and survey."

"And never once came down and asked us what we knew about our own land," Tam said. No bitterness in it — or rather, bitterness so old it had become geology. "Well. He's dead, you're hungry, and the soup's gone. That's the whole of tonight's history lesson."

* * *

The boy's name was Teodor, and he attached himself to Ines the way burrs attach to wool — silently, completely, and with no intention whatsoever of letting go.

Ines found this out the second morning, when he climbed the outside stair to find Teodor sitting on his doorstep in the freezing dark, waiting.

"How did you know which room was mine?"

"Everyone knows which room is yours. You're the new one." Teodor said it as though this settled all epistemology forever. He was small and quick, with a gap in his front teeth and a chilblain on one ear, and he held a wooden slate covered in charcoal marks. "Can you read this?"

Ines looked. It was letters, badly formed but unmistakably letters — *T-E-O*, then a scrawl. "It says your name."

"It says my name," Teodor agreed. "Maren's teaching me but she gets tired at night. Can you teach me better? Berta says you're educated. Berta says educated people are useless but they can read."

"Berta says a great deal," Ines said, and heard himself say it warmly, and was startled by the warmth.

So it began. Each morning before the shop opened, Teodor sat on his step and Ines taught him letters on the wooden slate, and each morning the boy repaid him with information delivered with the indiscriminate generosity of a spring flood: that the blind man Tam had been the orchard's cider-press keeper before the first seizure took the eastern rows; that the burned young man was called Jozsef and had been hurt not in a fire but in a mill accident and lied about it because mills didn't pay compensation to the clumsy; that Piet, the broad quiet one, had carried Maren's dead husband three miles on his back when the fever came through; that the girl, Anka, sang songs in a language nobody else in the house spoke and cried sometimes at night and pretended in the morning that she hadn't.

"Why are you telling me all this?" Ines asked, on the fourth day.

Teodor shrugged. "You live here now."

Three words. Ines turned them over for days afterward, the way he might turn an unfamiliar coin. *You live here now.* At the guild, residence was a legal fact established by contract and witnessed signature. Here it was apparently established by sleeping somewhere twice and knowing where the step was icy.

* * *

The trouble — and it took him a week to name the trouble honestly, and he prided himself on naming things precisely — was that they noticed him.

Everything about his training ran against it. At the guild he had perfected the craft of going unobserved: entering halls during the change of bells, walking at the pace of servants, keeping his eyes on documents rather than faces, answering questions with exactly as many words as required. Invisible men survived. Invisible men got to keep their positions through changes of administration, through purges of favorites, through the slow political weather that ruined louder men. Vane had taught it without ever saying it aloud — *observe, record, be seen by no one* — and Ines had assumed it was simply good technique, the way you hold a pen.

Down in the working quarter it was not technique. It was rudeness.

They noticed when he skipped supper. Anka left bread on his step with a look of such open concern that he ate in the common room thereafter purely to prevent a recurrence. They noticed that he flinched at the sound of hooves on the lane — a habit from the week the steward's rider had come asking after Vane's effects — and Piet remarked on it once, mildly, in passing: "Expecting someone?" And Ines had lied, easily, saying he was startled by all sudden noise, and Piet had accepted it, and Ines lay awake that night feeling the lie like a stone in his boot.

They noticed his hands. This was the worst of it. He was sketching again — telling himself it was practice, telling himself a draftsman who stops drawing loses his line — quick studies in the evenings by lamplight: the stove, the stair, Teodor asleep against Tam's shoulder. And one evening Maren paused behind his chair on her way to the hearth and looked at the page, and said nothing at all, and the nothing lasted three full seconds, and he felt every one of them.

She was keeping accounts herself. He'd seen the ledger — couldn't help seeing it, it lived on a shelf by the door like a household saint — a fat book in a plain binding with a leather tie, and he had caught glimpses of its pages: columns, names, dates, entries in a firm upright hand. Debts owed to orchard families. Harvests taken. Names written beside names. He was a keeper of records himself; he knew what a living ledger looked like versus a dead one, and hers breathed.

Two kinds of people keep books like that, Vane used to say. Historians and creditors. Pray you never learn which one you are.

* * *

On the eighth night the wind came around to the north and drove the cold down the valley like water finding a crack, and the household pulled its tables close to the hearth, and Tam — who told stories the way other men breathed, continuously and without apparent effort — began the recitation.

Ines did not know yet that it was a ritual. He learned fast.

"Jozsef," Tam said, "born where?"

"Fellbruck, third terrace, under the blackheart cherries. My father pruned those trees thirty years. I lost my father to the fever year and my trees to the seizure, same winter." Jozsef said it flatly, the way a man recites a route he has walked too many times. Then, turning the ritual onward: "Anka."

Anka pulled her shawl tighter. "Born in the western flats. Our rows were quince." A pause. "I lost everyone, and I'm not saying more tonight."

"Nobody has to say more," Maren said, from the hearth, in a voice that closed the matter like a gate. "Piet."

"Born at the river bend, in the house the canal will take. Lost my wife, Marta, to the fever year. Kept her seeds. Still have them."

And so it went around the fire, each of them giving their coordinates in the geography of loss — born where, of what stock, lost whom — until the circle came around to Teodor, who gave his answers with the brisk pride of a boy who had finally been issued a birthplace: found at the orchard gate, raised by Maren, lost nothing he could remember, which everyone let stand because it was true and merciful.

Then the circle arrived at Ines.

The room did not turn to him dramatically. That was what unnerved him — there was no drama in it at all. They simply paused, the way a wagon pauses at a fork in the road, waiting to see which way the wheel would take.

"Ines," Maren said, gently. She did not say *born where*. She said only his name, and left the sentence open, and the open sentence sat in the warm room like a drawn blade lying on cloth.

He could feel the atlas against his ribs. He had worn the coat to dinner, as he wore it everywhere; the sewn lining pressed familiar and hard against his side, a second skeleton. Twelve faces. The stove ticking. Somewhere outside, the river grinding its ice.

"I was born in the capital," he said. "In a foundling house. I don't know my stock. I was taken as an apprentice at eleven and I've been in workshops ever since." He swallowed. "I haven't lost anyone. Except—" and the word was out before the caution caught it— "except my master. Last month."

The pause after it was not hostile. That was somehow worse. It was patient. It was the pause of people who had all said *lost someone* in this circle and knew exactly how much weight the word carried and were waiting to see whether he knew it too.

"Except your master," Maren repeated quietly. She stirred the fire. "Aldous Vane, who measured our ridge for years and never once came down to ask us a single thing about it."

"Maren," Tam said mildly.

"No, it's fair." She looked at Ines, and her eyes in the firelight were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "You've come down the hill now, mapmaker. Whatever brought you. So I'll say the thing I say to everyone who lands on this lane, and you can take it or leave it. Nothing here is free, and nothing here is owed. We feed you because feeding is what we do. But if you're going to live among people whose whole history is written down in no book but this one—" she touched the shelf near the ledger, not lifting it— "then someday somebody in this room is going to ask you a direct question. And when they do, I'd think hard about what kind of answer you want to be the one remembered."

"I understand," Ines said.

"Do you?" she said. Not cruelly. Curiously, almost — the genuine curiosity of a woman reading a document she suspected had a second page. "Half of you does. The half that understands is sitting right here eating our soup. The other half is still up that hill with your dead master's papers, deciding things."

The room made a small collective sound — not quite a breath, not quite agreement. Teodor looked at Ines with his frank, total gaze, and for once said nothing at all.

Ines walked back up the lane afterward through the blue-black cold, his breath smoking, the stars hard as nailheads. Below the hill the guildhall windows burned yellow and indifferent. Behind him the cooper's shop was dark, and above it his one small room, and against his ribs the oilcloth and the sewn secret and the four years of a dead man's handwriting, and he stood a moment in the middle of the lane, equidistant between the two houses of his life, unable to move in either direction.

The next morning, Teodor was not on his step. Instead, a note in a child's careful new letters, each word fought for:

*MAREN SAYS COME TO SUPPER EARLY. SHE WANTS TO ASK YOU SOMETHING PROPER.*

Ines read it three times. Down the lane, smoke was rising from the granary-house chimney, straight up in the still air, like a column being drawn on a chart — a single vertical line marking, precisely, where he was.
