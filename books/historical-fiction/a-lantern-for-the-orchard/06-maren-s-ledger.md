## 6. Maren's Ledger

The ledger lived in the third drawer of the dresser, beneath the good linens, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. Maren took it out every Sunday evening after the household had eaten and the younger ones had been sent up to the loft with Teodor to argue about whose turn it was at the window seat. She took it out now, this Sunday, because the week had been long and the snow had come early and there were three new mouths at her table who had arrived with nothing but their coats.

She set the lamp on the table, turned its wick low — oil was dearer than it had been in October — and opened the book.

It had been a farm ledger once. Her husband's father had kept it, and her husband before that, columns of bushels and shillings in a hand like fence posts. She had crossed all of that out the first winter she used it for her own purposes, drawn a single line through each page the way you'd fell a tree: cleanly, without apology, so the old grain of the record still showed underneath but nothing grew from it. Now the pages held what mattered.

*Jozef Brandt,* she read. *Owes the Halloran orchard two seasons' labor, unpaid, on account of his mother's coughing medicine. Two sons. Good with grafting knives. Will work it off when the courts are settled or I'll carry the debt myself.*

*Petra Voss. Owes the Aldous-row families a quarter-share of the '49 press. Paid in kind: mended every coat in the row through the frost month. Debt considered carried.*

*Teodor.* She stopped there, as she always stopped there, though there was nothing owed and nothing owing. *Teodor. Age eleven. Taken in the spring of '48 off the Vellamont road, half-starved, no name he'd answer to. No debt. No debt ever.*

She had learned bookkeeping the way most women of the orchards learned everything — by doing it while the men pretended they weren't watching. But her husband had been a patient man about figures, and before him her own mother had kept the parish accounts at Little Wethering until the seizure, and Maren had understood early what a record was for. A record was a weapon that only worked if you kept it dry. A record was the difference between *they say* and *I know*. The Crown could falsify a title deed; the Crown had done exactly that, twice now, to her certain knowledge. The Crown could not falsify forty years of harvest shares entered in a fair hand with dates and witnesses.

That was what the surveyors coming in the spring would never grasp, she thought. They would measure the land. They would not measure what the land had been made of. And when the water came up over the roots, the maps would show a flood line and the ledgers would show people, and only one of those documents would tell the truth.

She heard the floorboard above her — the one by the stair, the loose one, Piet kept saying he'd fix it and Piet fixed everything except that board — and she closed the ledger and slid it back into the drawer and put the linens over it, and by the time footsteps reached the bottom of the stairs she was darning a sock that needed no darning.

It was Ines Halloran.

He stood at the foot of the stairs in the lamplight from the kitchen doorway, holding his boots in one hand, which was something the others had taught him without meaning to — you came up the outside steps wet or muddy, you left your boots at the door, and the mapmaker had learned it in four days, faster than Petra's cousin had managed in a season. He looked, as always, like a man apologizing for the space he occupied.

"The cooper said you'd be down," he said. "There's a leak in the loft corner. Over my bed. I've put a pail under it but I wanted to ask where you keep the—"

"Pitch," she said. "Second shelf of the storeroom, behind the spare hoops. Sit down first."

He sat. That too had changed. The first night he'd stood in this kitchen like a bird deciding whether the windowsill was safe, and now he sat, though he perched on the edge of the bench as if the wood might object.

Maren studied him while she talked to him about pitch and pails and which corner of the loft roof the cooper swore was sound and wasn't. She was sixty-one years old and she had raised children who weren't hers and buried a husband who was and fed drifters through two hard winters, and she could read a person the way her mother read weather — not always right, but never wholly wrong.

What she read in Ines Halloran was this: a young man carrying something heavier than his coat suggested.

He carried himself carefully. Not the carefulness of pride — pride sits in the shoulders. This carefulness sat lower, in the way he moved his arms, the way he turned in a doorway, always presenting his left side to a room, always keeping whatever was at his breast pocket angled away from other bodies. She had seen smugglers do that with coins. She had seen priests do it with letters. She had done it herself, once, with a bundle of deeds wrapped in waxed cloth, walking them from the chapel to the cooper's cellar while soldiers drank at the inn and her heart went like a drum.

"You're staring," he said. Not accusing. Almost hopeful, as if being stared at were better than being invisible.

"I'm looking at your sock," she said, holding up her darning. "It's worse than the one I'm fixing. Give it here."

He hesitated, then unlaced the boot and pulled the sock off and handed it across, barefoot on her cold flags like a boy, and she felt something move in her chest that she didn't entirely welcome. Tenderness was expensive. Tenderness toward a man who wouldn't say what he carried was more expensive still.

"You've been here six days," she said. "You've mended the pail handle, you split wood slower than anyone I've ever seen but cleaner than anyone I've ever seen, and you eat like the food might be taken back. Where did you learn to eat like that?"

"The guildhall," he said. "Meals were timed."

"Timed." She threaded the needle. "And before the guildhall?"

"A foundling house. St. Brigid's, in the capital." He said it flatly, the way you recite a fact you've said so many times it has worn smooth. "Then an apprenticeship. Master Vane. You knew of him?"

"Everyone on this road knew of Aldous Vane." She tied off the thread and bit it. "Exact man. Cold hands, they said. Came through the orchards with his instruments every few years, measuring, asking questions nobody thought to lie about because he asked them so politely." She looked up. "You were his?"

"His apprentice. Four years." Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and she marked it. "He died last month."

"I know. Half the town walked to the burying." She folded the mended sock, set it on the table, and pushed it back toward him with two fingers. "What did he leave you?"

The question landed the way she'd meant it to land — lightly, almost idly, a landlady making conversation — and she watched it strike him anyway. His hand came up, small and quick, and pressed flat against his chest. Against the left side. Then down again to the table, and he smiled, and it was a good smile, well made, and it told her nothing except that he could make it.

"Some instruments," he said. "A few books. Nothing of value."

"Mm," said Maren.

* * *

She thought about it for two days, and on the second evening she decided.

Not decided to confront him — confrontation was a tool for people with standing, and she'd spent her whole life learning to work without it. Decided instead to show him. There was a difference. Confrontation asked a question; showing answered one he hadn't dared ask, which was *am I alone in this room?*

The household had gone up. Rain had come in the night and turned to sleet against the shutters, and the cooper's shop below was silent, and Maren sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned properly high because tonight was not a night for thrift, and the ledger open in front of her, and the door unlocked, which it never was.

Ines came down past midnight. She heard him hesitate at the bottom step — she heard everything in this house; thirty years will teach you the weight of every footfall — and then he saw the light and came into the kitchen, and stopped.

She watched him look at the open book. Watched his eyes go down the page the way a man reads anything written in columns — top to bottom, fast, hungry, the reading of someone trained to records. His lips parted slightly. His whole body leaned an inch toward the table before he caught himself.

"Sit," she said.

He sat.

"This is the household account," she said. "Food in, food out, who owes whom. That part any landlady keeps." She turned the page. "This is the other part. This is why the book lives under the linens."

The next pages were the orchard record. Names. Not debts of money — debts of history. *The Halloran rows, planted 1781 by Tomas Halloran, whose grandson is Jozef's neighbor and whose great-granddaughter married into the Voss family at Little Wethering before the seizure took the Wethering commons.* Harvest shares going back decades, witnesses named for each entry, weather noted, prices argued with. And interleaved among them, copied out in her smaller careful hand: fragments of deeds, boundary descriptions, the old words of the old grants, *from the stone bridge to the split oak, thence along the watercourse* — the language of a world the Crown survey had already begun to erase.

Ines had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man listening to music he knows.

"Where did you get these?" he asked quietly. His finger hovered above a boundary clause without touching it. "The Wethering common grant. That text was supposed to have burned."

"It nearly did." She kept her voice level. "Father Anselm hid what he could hide, the year they came for the parish papers. He paid for it. Lost his post, or most of its standing. He gave me copies of some things because I asked, and I asked because somebody had to hold them and he was already marked." She turned another page. "Forty years, Mr. Halloran. Forty years of shares and names and boundaries, entered fair, witnessed proper. When the commission comes in the spring with their tidy new map showing this whole valley as empty crown land waiting for water, I intend to put this book in front of whoever can still be reached. That's all it is. That's all it's ever been."

He was looking at the ledger the way she had once seen her mother look at the communion cup. Not greed. Recognition.

"You keep accounts," he said. It came out strange, half a question.

"I keep the truth in a form that can't be argued with," she said. "Dates, names, numbers. Anyone can weep. Not everyone can add." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a thing you love, and laid her hand flat on the cover, and looked at him straight across the lamp. "Now. Six days you've slept under this roof. You eat at my table. Teodor has taken you walking and come back talking about contour lines like they're scripture. I don't ask men their business — a woman who lodges strangers learns not to. But you carry something, Mr. Halloran. You carry it the way I carried those deeds from the chapel. Left side, turned away, hand flat when you're startled. I've done it myself. I know the weight."

The sleet ticked against the shutters. Down in the shop, a hoop shifted as the cold took the wood.

"So I'm asking you plainly, once, and you may refuse and nothing changes — your rent stays what it is, your place at the table stays what it is. What do you carry?"

And here is what Maren expected: she expected him to refuse. She had built the question to survive refusal. What she did not expect — what cracked something in her that she would spend the rest of the winter examining — was how badly she wanted him not to refuse. How much of her had already set a place for him.

Ines Halloran looked at the closed ledger under her hand for a long moment. Then he looked up, and she watched him decide, and she watched him decide against her, and it was done so smoothly, so gently, that she almost admired it.

"Instruments," he said. "Master Vane's instruments, and some of his papers. Guild property mostly. I'm meant to surrender them when the commission arrives, and I—" the pause was perfect, a hair short of confession— "I haven't decided what I owe to whom yet."

Every word true. Every word arranged like stones across a stream so you could cross without getting wet. She recognized the craft because she practiced it herself; she had just shown him the deepest thing she owned and he had paid her back in coin that rang honest and bought nothing.

"All right," she said.

"I'm sorry—"

"Don't apologize for lying carefully. It means you understand what's at stake." She rose, took the ledger to the dresser, wrapped it in its oilcloth, slid it under the linens, and closed the drawer. When she turned around he was still sitting where she'd left him, barefoot, mended sock forgotten on the table, looking at her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — not guilt exactly, and not fear. Closer to grief. The grief of a man who has just discovered he wanted to be caught.

"Go up to bed," she said. "The pail's under the leak. Pitch is behind the hoops, morning's soon enough." At the stairs he paused, one hand on the rail, and she added, because it was true and because he needed to hear it true from someone: "Whatever you're carrying — whatever you didn't say tonight — it's safer here than anywhere between here and the capital. That's not a trap. That's just arithmetic."

He nodded once and went up, and the loose board creaked under him, and Piet really would have to fix it.

* * *

Maren did not sleep. She sat with the banked lamp and thought about lying.

She had lied well in her life. To soldiers, to tax men, to a magistrate once, God forgive her, with a widow's black on and a dead man's name in her mouth. Lies told for the household were a form of bread; you baked them fresh and served them without shame. But Ines's lie had been different, and it took her half the night to name the difference: he hadn't lied to protect himself from her. He had lied to protect her from knowing. A man hiding stolen silver lies to keep it. A man hiding something that could hang everyone in the room lies to keep the room clean. He had looked at her ledger — forty years of her life in fair columns — and understood instantly what it would cost her if what he carried were found under this roof, and he had chosen, out of some crooked courtesy, to leave her deniable.

Which meant he was kinder than he let himself be, and more afraid than he let anyone see, and carrying something worth hanging for.

She got up in the dark and stood at the window. The sleet had stopped. Moonlight lay on the cooper's yard, on the woodpile Piet had built against winter, on the far hedges, and beyond them, invisible in the dark but present in her mind the way her own hands were present, the orchards ran down the valley — the Halloran rows, the Voss ground, the split oak, the watercourse. Under snow now. Measured, she had no doubt, in some young man's careful hand, drawn fair onto paper, folded, hidden, carried against the left side of his chest.

*Aldous Vane*, she thought. *Cold hands. Exact man. What did you put in the boy?*

Because that was the shape of it now, and she trusted shapes. The master had died and left his apprentice holding paper that the apprentice couldn't surrender and couldn't speak of, and the apprentice had run — no, not run, she corrected herself, he had *come*, there was a difference — he had come to the working quarter, to the cooper's shop, to her table, out of every lodging in Vellamont. Men don't choose by accident. Some part of him had known, before he knew, whose roof he needed.

She thought about waking him. She thought about telling him about Father Anselm, about the strongbox, about the year of the seizure, about all the ways this town had already bled for its papers — and she didn't, not yet. Trust given before it's earned becomes a debt the other man spends badly. He would come to her when the weight got too heavy. They always did. And when he did, she would be at this table with the lamp lit and the drawer unlocked.

Down in the shop, the cold took another hoop with a soft wooden knock, like a knuckle on a door.

In the morning she would put the mended sock outside his loft door, and say nothing about it. That was how it started, in households like hers. That was how a stranger became a fixture: one repaired thing at a time, left silently where he'd find it, until the day came — and it would come, the ice was already talking on the river — until the day came when the thing he carried could no longer be carried alone, and he brought it to the lamplight, and she was ready.

Above her, faintly, through the floor: the creak of the loose board, once, twice. Him, pacing. Awake too, then. Keeping his own accounts in the dark.

Maren smiled in the moonlight, went back to bed, and slept.
