## 7. What the Surveyors Burn

The rider came on a Tuesday, three weeks before the survey commission was expected, and he came with a warrant instead of a welcome.

Ines heard the horse before he saw it — the particular heavy tread of a government mount on frozen mud, a sound he'd learned to catalog in his years at Vane's window. By the time the rider drew up outside the cooper's shop, Ines was already at the glass with his collar pulled up, watching through the frost-ferns the way he had once watched for his master's return from the guildhall: half hoping, half bracing.

The man was young. That was the first thing. Younger than Ines, perhaps, though the Crown uniform aged its wearers by a decade. He dismounted stiffly, stamped feeling back into his boots, and unrolled a paper against the cooper's doorpost as though the doorpost were a table set for him. The cooper's daughter, Bets, stood in the yard with an armful of kindling and did not move to help him or hinder him. She simply watched, the way people in Vellamont had begun to watch anything wearing official colors — the stillness of a field animal deciding which way to run.

Ines did not need to read the paper. He knew what warrants looked like. He had spent six years copying them for the guild, transcribing the elegant, bloodless hand of the Chancery clerks onto maps that would outlive every name mentioned in them. *By order of His Majesty's Commission on Inland Navigation.* The words were always the same. The consequences were always elsewhere.

But this time the consequence was across the square, at the chapel.

He was down the stairs before he had decided to go, and out into air so cold it felt less like weather than like a verdict.

* * *

Father Anselm met them at the chapel door, and Ines arrived in time to see the old priest do something remarkable: nothing.

"Redundant records," the rider said. He had introduced himself as Corporal Vetch, and he said the word *redundant* with the careful pronunciation of someone who had been coached. "Duplicate registers. Superseded deeds. Anything not required under the new tithe schedule." He held up the warrant. "You're to assist."

"Assist," Anselm repeated.

"In sorting."

"I see." The priest folded his hands into his sleeves. He was a narrow man, grey as ash already, with a voice that seemed designed for whispering over sickbeds and now had to carry across a churchyard. "And who determines redundancy?"

"The commission's schedule does." Vetch tapped the paper. "It's all listed."

Behind him, two more men were hauling a brazier off the supply cart — a proper iron brazier, the kind used for heating pitch, not for warming hands. They set it in the snow beside the chapel steps, and one of them knelt and began feeding splints into it, shielding the flame with his body.

They had come prepared to burn things in February, in a town where firewood was rationed by the parish itself. Ines understood, standing there with his breath clouding, that this was not improvisation. This was logistics. Somewhere in a warm office in the capital, someone had drawn up a list, and someone else had signed it, and somewhere a third person had ordered braziers.

"You'll want to begin inside," Vetch said.

* * *

Inside, the chapel smelled of tallow and cold stone, and along the north wall ran the shelves that Ines had walked past a hundred times without truly seeing: the parish archive. Deeds going back past the founding of the bridge. Baptismal registers with orchard families' names inked in a dozen dead priests' hands. Tithe rolls, manor court records, the slow sedimentary layers of eighty years of ordinary life written down because writing it down was what parishes did.

Vane's atlas had been drawn from these very shelves. Ines knew that now. He had spent the winter nights tracing his master's marginalia — the little cross-references, *cf. parish roll 1741*, *deed of Alder Row, see chapel copy* — and understanding, sheet by sheet, that the unfinished atlas was not a map at all. It was an argument. Every boundary line Vane had drawn matched a document in this room, and every document contradicted the Crown's canal survey by a matter of acres, and those acres were orchards, and those orchards were people.

Now the documents were being sorted into two piles.

"Keep pile goes to the commission cart," Vetch explained, moving down the shelves with the schedule in his fist, reading titles aloud in a flat clerk's singsong. "Burn pile is everything else. You can help carry, if you like." He glanced at Ines without recognition — an apprentice meant nothing to him, a boy in a patched coat — and Ines made himself useful precisely so he could stay.

That was the horror of it, he thought later. How easy it was to stay. He simply picked up a box when a box needed carrying, and no one questioned him, and the morning proceeded with the orderly rhythm of any inventory he had ever conducted. The men were not cruel. One of them apologized when he knocked dust from a shelf onto Anselm's floor. They worked briskly, professionally, the way men work when the task has been defined narrowly enough that conscience has no purchase on it. Nothing here was being stolen. Nothing was even being destroyed, technically — only *consolidated*. The word appeared twice in the schedule. Redundant materials would be *disposed of locally to avoid transport costs.*

The burn pile grew. The keep pile stayed small.

"Deeds of Alder Row," Vetch read. A pause while he checked. "Superseded."

Anselm, sorting at the far end, said mildly, "Superseded by what?"

"The commission's title schedule."

"There is no commission yet. The ice hasn't broken."

"The schedule exists." Vetch tapped his chest, where the papers rode inside his coat, against his heart, like a second heartbeat. "That's what's been superseded."

Ines carried the box of Alder Row deeds toward the door himself. He told himself he was simply doing what anyone would do — following instructions, keeping his head down, staying close enough to see. But his hands knew better. His hands had spent six years learning the weight of paper, and these documents were heavier than their pages. Each one was a family's grip on its own ground. Each one bore signatures of men whose grandchildren were currently sleeping above the cooper's shop, displaced already in fact if not yet in law, waiting for the water to make it official.

Outside, the brazier roared. The wind had turned, and it carried the heat against the chapel wall like an open oven door.

He stopped on the top step with the box in his arms, and for a long moment he could not move.

* * *

What he remembered afterward, always, was not the flame but the sound. Paper does not scream. It sighs — a soft exhalation, almost contented, as the heat takes it. A deed of Alder Row, dated 1789, signed by a magistrate whose name Ines had copied into Vane's atlas margin just last month, went into the fire and gave up its ink in a breath. The wax seal bubbled first, a small red blister rising and collapsing, and then the vellum curled inward like a leaf in autumn frost, blackening from the edges, and the names went last because the names were written in iron gall ink and iron gall fights for its life.

Then they didn't.

The corporal's men worked steadily. They did not gloat. That was somehow worse. One of them warmed his hands at intervals, sensibly, because it was cold. Between loads he ate a heel of bread. The fire consumed the parish's memory at roughly the rate a man can eat breakfast.

A small crowd gathered across the square — Maren among them, arms crossed, Teodor pressed against her side though he was nearly too old for that and too proud to allow it and did it anyway. None of them spoke. What was there to say, and to whom? The rider represented a power that had not yet arrived in force and did not need to; its paper had arrived ahead of it, and paper, Ines understood with a clarity that felt like a fever breaking, was the true army. Armies took towns. Paper took *history*, quietly, in advance, so that when the soldiers finally came there would be nothing left to argue about.

Maren caught his eye across the square. She did not nod. She did not frown. She looked at him with the level attention she gave to everything — trees, debts, strangers — as if to ask what he intended to do about what he was seeing, and then looked away, having apparently concluded that the question was his to answer, not hers to press.

He looked down at the box in his arms.

There were four deeds left in it. Alder Row, parcels two through five. He could feel their edges through the wood.

* * *

He did not plan it. That was the thing he would never be able to explain to himself afterward, the thing that made the whole winter feel less like courage than like sleepwalking. There was no moment of decision. The corporal called for the next box, and Ines, descending the steps with Alder Row still in his arms, simply turned left instead of right — toward the chapel's side door instead of the fire — and stepped inside, and stood in the dark of the vestry with four deeds against his chest and his heart going like a bird in a closed room.

Through the wall he heard Vetch's flat voice: *"Apprentice! Next box."*

He waited. Counted to twenty. Heard footsteps cross the nave, pause, recede. Then Father Anselm was there, materializing out of the vestry gloom the way old priests do, as if stone and shadow had agreed to lend them shape.

"Give them here," the priest whispered.

Ines couldn't move. "I—"

"Under the altar cloth. Quickly." Anselm lifted the cloth himself, revealing not the vestment chest but a shallow gap behind it, between the chest and the wall, where a stack of something already lay hidden — Ines saw the corner of a register, the ribbon of a bound book — and then Anselm took the four deeds from his unresisting hands and slid them in and let the cloth fall, all in the space of ten seconds, with the practiced economy of a man who had done this before.

"How many?" Ines managed.

"Enough." Anselm's eyes were pale and steady. "Not enough. It depends on the week you ask me." He straightened the altar cloth, smoothed it once with both palms, a gesture so domestic it nearly undid Ines entirely. "You should go back out, boy. Before they count boxes."

"They're not counting."

"They will be, eventually. That's how it works. First they burn what's redundant, then they notice what's missing, then they burn what's inconvenient." Anselm opened the vestry door a hand's width, letting in the cold and the orange flicker of the fire. His voice dropped further. "Go."

Ines went. He picked up the nearest empty box, carried it back around to the steps, and handed it up to the corporal's men with a face he hoped was blank. His face had been trained for this, at least. Six years of being invisible in a great man's workshop. He had resented the training. Now it fit him like armor.

The burning continued until midday. When it was done, the brazier was doused with snow and loaded back onto the cart, and the keep pile — thin, obedient, harmless — went into a locked commission strongbox, and Corporal Vetch mounted his heavy horse and touched his cap to Father Anselm with genuine politeness, and rode south along the river road toward whatever other parish was waiting for him.

The square emptied slowly, the way crowds do after executions.

* * *

Ines did not go back to the cooper's shop. He walked instead up through the lanes to the ridge above the orchards, telling himself it was to clear his head, knowing it was because the orchards were where Vane's atlas lived in the world — the ink and the ground still corresponding, line for line, for another season at least.

From the ridge, the trees stood in their winter ranks, black and patient, snow in their crotches, each row exactly where the 1789 deed had said it would be. Where the burned deed had said it. Past tense. He kept catching himself doing that — shifting the tense, as if the fire had already reached backward through time and unwritten the signatures. The land was still here. The proof of the land was smoke over the river.

He thought of Vane's marginalia, the tiny disciplined script: *verify against parish original.* Half the atlas's authority rested on documents that no longer existed anywhere except in Vane's transcription — and in Ines's memory, now, and in whatever Anselm was hoarding behind the altar cloth. The master had drawn his map from these shelves assuming the shelves would endure. Archives endured. That was their whole purpose; that was the quiet covenant of every record ever kept. Nobody burns a parish archive. Except that somebody had, today, before lunch, and warmed his hands afterward, and eaten bread.

The urgency that rose in Ines then was not the dramatic kind. It was cold and specific and it tasted of iron. *The official history is already being written,* he thought. Not coming. Already here. It had arrived early, ahead of its own commission, ahead of the thaw, because the men who wrote history had learned that the cheapest time to destroy a counterargument is before anyone knows an argument is happening. Vane had understood it. That was why the atlas existed. That was why the cipher, why the margins, why the apprentice chosen and never quite told.

And Ines had spent three weeks hiding the atlas under floorboards, cataloguing it, admiring it, grieving over it — treating it as an inheritance to be kept rather than a weapon to be spent.

The wind moved through the orchard rows, and the bare branches made a sound like paper turning.

Down in the town, smoke still hung faintly over the chapel. He found he could not look at it directly anymore. Instead he looked at the trees — each one a line in a ledger that now existed in fewer copies than it had at dawn — and he made himself count them, row by row, the way Teodor counted them, the way Maren's ledger counted them, the way a man counts what he is responsible for.

When he got back down to the lanes, Father Anselm was waiting for him at the foot of the chapel steps, alone, breath steaming, hands folded into his sleeves.

"You knew the deeds by sight," the priest said. No greeting. "You carried that box like it weighed something. Most folk carry paper like it weighs nothing."

Ines said nothing.

"And this morning I put four deeds of Alder Row behind my altar cloth on the word *quickly* from a stranger's face." Anselm studied him with mild, terrible patience. "So you'll tell me what you're carrying, boy. Or you'll tell me what you're hiding. One or the other — but you'll tell me soon, because after today, *soon* is a currency we've stopped minting."

Far off across the fields, where the canal route would run, something glinted in the low sun — a line of stakes driven into the frozen ground, white-tipped, patient, already measuring the future flood.

Ines followed the priest's gaze to them, and did not answer, and knew he could not stay silent much longer.
