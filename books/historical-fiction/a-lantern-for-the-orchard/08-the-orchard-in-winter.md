## 8. The Orchard in Winter

The boy was waiting for me at the bottom of the lane before the sun had cleared the ridge, breath steaming, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had been cut for a bigger man and rolled at the cuffs twice.

"You're late," Teodor said.

"I'm early," I said. "You're earlier."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate and found it acceptable. "Maren says if you're going to draw her trees, you'll draw them right or not at all." He turned and started up the frost-hardened path without checking whether I followed, which was its own kind of instruction.

I had my satchel: two pencils, a stick of charcoal, a block of paper, the small folding board I used for field work. I had told myself, walking out of Vellamont in the grey cold, that this was practice. That a mapmaker who couldn't render terrain by hand was half a tradesman. That there was nothing strange about an apprentice walking into the orchards of families whose land the Crown intended to drown, sketching trees for no reason he could say aloud.

I was getting better at lying to myself. It seemed to be a skill this town taught quickly.

* * *

The orchards began where the town's last fence gave up, and they did not end so much as thin out toward the river, row after row of bare trees standing in the frozen grass like a congregation waiting through a long sermon. Frost had silvered every twig. Underfoot the ground rang faintly, hard as fired clay.

Teodor walked it the way I imagined a priest walks his parish — not looking, exactly, but attending. His head turned at intervals, cataloguing. Once he stopped, crouched, and pressed two fingers to the trunk of a young apple tree as if taking its pulse.

"This one's sick," he said. "Bark canker. Maren will want to cut it back before thaw or it'll spread to the Wexley."

"The Wexley?"

"The tree next door." He said it as though I were simple, and perhaps I was. "Trees have names. The ones worth keeping do. You don't name something you mean to lose."

I took out my pencil before he could see what that sentence did to my face.

"Which one is this?" I asked, gesturing at the whole slope.

He looked at me for a long moment — the look of someone deciding whether a stranger deserves information — and then, apparently deciding yes, began to walk and talk, and I began to follow and write, and for the next three hours I forgot entirely that I had come out here to practice.

* * *

His knowledge was not orderly. It came in bursts, sideways, attached to stories rather than quadrants. He'd point at a gnarled pear tree heavy with mistletoe and say, "Old Magdalene. Older than anyone. Grandfather Hallam grafted her himself when he was younger than me, and he died at eighty-one, so you can do the sums." Then, three rows later: "This whole block is the Widow Ferris's. Was. She sold to the Vellamont Fruit Company and they sold to nobody, because the company was a lie, and now the trees just belong to whoever keeps them. Maren says keeping is better than owning anyway. Owning you can lose on paper. Keeping you only lose by dying."

I wrote *Widow Ferris block* and underlined it twice and told myself the underline meant nothing.

"Do you know them all?" I asked. "Every tree?"

"Every one worth knowing." He shrugged. "Some are just trees. But the good ones — you learn their habits. Old Magdalene fruits every other year like she's sulking. The twins up on the high terrace ripen a week ahead of everything else and if you don't pick them fast the wasps take the lot. And there's one down by the water meadow—" he hesitated, and for the first time since we'd left the lane his voice lost its brisk certainty, "—that nobody picks anymore. We leave it."

"Why?"

"It belonged to the Kessler family. There's only the grandmother left and she's in the poorhouse across the river. Maren says the apples aren't ours to take." He kicked at a frozen clod of earth. "So every year they fall and rot and feed the ground, and Piet says that's still feeding somebody."

I stood there with my pencil poised over a blank square of paper and understood that I had never once, in six years of training, drawn a thing I loved. Vane would have called that a professional deficiency. Standing in the frost among named trees, watching a boy grieve fruit, I suspected it was a deficiency of another kind altogether.

"Draw Magdalene first," Teodor ordered. "She'll want to be done properly."

* * *

I worked while he supervised. This is harder than it sounds. A boy of eleven has no patience for artistic struggle; he wanted the tree to look like the tree, immediately, and informed me at length when it didn't.

"Her branches go wrong there. You've made her too neat. She leans. She's been leaning since the storm year."

I erased. Redrew. She leaned.

"And she's uglier than that. She's got the scar where the lightning took her south limb. You have to put the scar in or it isn't her."

I put the scar in. And somewhere between the scar and the lean, something shifted in my hands — some resistance I hadn't known I was carrying let go, and the pencil stopped recording and started listening. The tree came up off the page the way faces do when a portrait finally catches. Teodor studied it for a while without speaking, which from him was applause.

"That's her," he said finally. "That's actually her." He glanced up at me with sudden suspicion. "How'd you do that? You barely looked."

"I looked exactly enough," I said. "It's the trade. We're trained to see what's actually there instead of what we expect." I heard the words come out and winced inwardly, because they were Vane's words, worn smooth from repetition, and because they applied to far more than drawing.

"Your master taught you?"

"He tried." Six years of trying. A man who spoke in measurements and corrections, who never once praised a finished sheet but kept every one of them, which I had understood only lately was the same thing. "He was exacting."

"Maren's exacting too." Teodor said it comfortably, as if all the world's exacting people belonged to one family. "She says exacting means you think things matter. Lazy people aren't exacting because nothing matters enough to get right." He considered. "Was your master lazy? About people, I mean. Not maps."

I looked at him. He was examining a bud, unbothered, the question tossed off like a stone into a pond, and he waited with perfect patience for the ripples.

"He kept everyone at arm's length," I said at last. "I thought he didn't care. Now I'm not sure. Some men lock their doors so tightly they forget there was anything inside worth stealing."

"That's sad," Teodor decided, and moved on to the next tree, and the interview was over.

* * *

By midday the cold had worked through my boots and my fingers had gone stiff around the pencil, so we sat in the lee of the Widow Ferris block and ate bread and hard cheese from Teodor's satchel, sharing it without discussion, which is how things are shared in working households — automatically, proportionally, as a law of nature rather than a kindness.

From the rise we could see the river, flat and pewter-colored, and the closed bridge hanging above it with its broken span like a snapped bone. On the far bank, tiny with distance, smoke rose from the commission's advance camp. They were still stuck on their side, waiting for ice thick enough to carry men and materials across, or for orders bold enough to rebuild the span for themselves. Either way, spring was coming for us at the speed of weather.

"They're going to flood all this," Teodor said. Not a question. Children in this town said it the way children elsewhere said *it might rain.*

"So they claim."

"Maren says claims are cheap until someone makes them prove it." He chewed. "Are you going to make them prove it?"

The cheese went dry in my mouth. "I'm a mapmaker, Teo. I draw what's there."

"You drew Magdalene's scar," he said. "Nobody pays you for scars."

I had no answer for that, so I offered him the last of the bread, and he took it, and let me off. For now. I was beginning to understand that this household's mercy always came stamped *for now.*

* * *

We walked the lower terraces in the afternoon, and the light went long and amber across the frost, and I filled eleven sheets. I sketched the Kessler tree from a distance, bare and black against the water meadow, and did not write its name on the page, and felt the omission like a debt. I sketched the high terraces with the river beyond, and marked depths and contours out of pure habit, and then caught myself marking them — *this is the flood line, this is where the water will stand* — and turned the page over sharply, as if I'd written something obscene.

Because that was the truth underneath the whole morning, wasn't it. Every measurement I took was double-entry bookkeeping. On one side: practice, craft, a man improving his hand. On the other side: a survey of what was about to be destroyed, taken in secret, for purposes I refused to name even inside my own skull.

Vane had walked these same rows. I knew it now with certainty. His marginal notes described the Wexley grafts, the Ferris boundaries, the high-terrace drainage — details no guild surveyor would ever need, details that only mattered to someone recording a place the way you record a face of someone you love. He had come out here with his folding board and his terrible discipline and he had measured a world he already knew was condemned, and he had never once, in all those years, told me why.

Or had he? Hadn't he said, that last autumn, in the one conversation I kept returning to like a tongue to a cracked tooth: *"A map is a promise, boy. Ask yourself, every time you draw a line — who am I making this promise to, and will I keep it when it costs me?"* I had nodded and gone back to hatching riverbanks. I had thought it was rhetoric.

Teodor tugged my sleeve. "Come on. There's one more. The important one."

* * *

The important one stood alone on the highest terrace, older than everything around it, vast-limbed, its bark fissured deep as corduroy. Someone had nailed a small wooden lantern bracket to its trunk, long ago, the wood gone grey as driftwood.

"The Lantern Tree," Teodor said. "It's the oldest in the valley. Before the canal companies, before the Crown surveys, before anybody wrote anything down — the keepers hung a lantern in it every harvest eve. All the orchards lit theirs too, but this one first, and you could see it from the river for miles. The boatmen used to steer by it." He ran his hand along the old bracket. "They say as long as the lantern burns, the orchard remembers itself."

"Who says that?"

"Everyone." He looked at me as if the question were beneath us both. "Maren hangs it still. Every year. Even now." A pause, and then, with the terrible directness of children: "Even though there won't be many more years to hang it in."

I set up my board. My fingers had warmed enough to work. I drew the Lantern Tree the way I had drawn Magdalene — listening, not recording — and I drew the empty bracket too, and below the sketch, before I could stop myself, I wrote the date and one line: *Lantern Tree, highest terrace, planted by no one living.*

Then, smaller, in the margin, in the shorthand Vane had drilled into me: a boundary notation. The full extent of the upper orchards. The true extent. From the atlas.

I stared at what I'd written. It was a single line of ink. It committed me to nothing and everything. Anyone seeing it would read surveyor's habit. I knew what it was: the first entry in a record I had not agreed to keep.

"Ines." Teodor was watching the sky, not me. "Weather's turning. See the cloud off the ridge? We should go down before it snows."

"One moment." I finished the shading. Closed the book. And heard myself say, in a voice I didn't quite recognize: "Teodor. If someone asked you to keep something safe — something on paper — something that mattered more than being safe yourself — would you do it?"

He thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything. "Depends who's asking."

"And if it was me?"

Another pause. Snow began to fall, fine as sifted flour, catching in both our hair.

"Then I'd ask Maren first," he said. "But probably yes. She says you're one of us now whether you like it or not. She says that about everybody who eats our bread longer than a week." He grinned suddenly, all eleven years of him. "You've eaten a lot of our bread."

We walked down through the falling snow, past Magdalene leaning, past the Ferris block, past the black Kessler tree feeding its apples to the frozen ground, and behind us the Lantern Tree stood on its hill with its empty bracket, holding the shape of a flame that wasn't there yet.

At the gate to the cooper's yard, Teodor stopped.

"There was a man at the chapel yesterday asking questions," he said, casually, catastrophically. "Rode in from the far bank. Fancy coat. He was asking Father Anselm about old parish records, and about your master's papers, and whether anyone in town had 'inherited documents of interest.'" He pronounced the phrase carefully, having clearly rehearsed it. "Piet heard him. Piet hears everything." The boy looked up at me, snow melting on his eyelashes, and asked the question I'd been dreading since the bridge fell: "Ines, why would a stranger care what papers you inherited?"

The snow fell between us. Somewhere behind the walls of my chest, the countdown I had been refusing to hear ticked audibly forward.

"I don't know yet," I said.

It was the first lie I'd told him. I hoped it wouldn't be the last thing he remembered of me.
