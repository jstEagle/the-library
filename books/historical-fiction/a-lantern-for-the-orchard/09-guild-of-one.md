## 9. Guild of One

The knock came on the second morning of a thaw-that-wasn't, and Ines's heart stopped before his hand did.

He was at the table above the cooper's shop with a bowl of barley porridge going cold in front of him and Vane's cipher notes spread beneath a clean linen — laundry, if anyone asked, though no one had ever once asked him about his laundry. He swept the pages under the cloth in one motion he'd rehearsed without knowing it, the way you rehearse drowning.

The knock came again. Three raps, evenly spaced. Patient. Official.

"Ines Halloran." The voice belonged to the guild steward, Master Oren Fell, and it carried through the cheap door of the cooper's lodging the way water carries through cheap boots. "I can hear you moving. Open the door, boy, before the whole landing wonders why a disgraced apprentice has visitors."

Ines counted to four — long enough to seem unhurried, short enough not to seem defiant — and opened it.

Fell stood in the grey light looking like a man who had dressed for an argument and hoped to lose it. He was sixty, thick through the shoulders from thirty years of hauling ledgers instead of anything honest, and he had the particular pallor of someone who spent winter indoors counting other people's mistakes. Under one arm he carried a leather folio stamped with the guild's seal: a compass rose, quartered.

"Master Fell," Ines said. "I didn't think I still warranted visits."

"You warrant them less every day, which is precisely why I'm here." Fell stepped inside uninvited — stewards always did; doors were for people with standing — and looked around the room with the flat inventorying gaze Ines knew too well. The cot. The stove. The table. The linen bundle, which Ines realized a half-second too late he'd moved to sit between Fell and the window light.

"Laundry," Ines said.

"I didn't ask."

"No," Ines agreed. "You never do."

Fell lowered himself onto the cot's single stool, which groaned, and set the folio on his knees. For a moment he only looked at Ines, and Ines understood that this was the technique — that Fell had learned it from watching Vane, or perhaps Vane had learned it from watching him: silence as a chisel. Say nothing. Let the other man fill the space with something true.

It worked better on apprentices than on masters. Ines picked up his spoon and ate his cold porridge with deliberate slowness, and after a while Fell exhaled through his nose like a horse deciding against a fence.

"There was a letter at the guildhouse yesterday," Fell said. "Private courier, capital seal, addressed not to the guild but to me by name. Do you know how rarely the capital writes to me by name?"

"No."

"Never. In nineteen years, never." Fell opened the folio and drew out a sheet of good paper, heavy stock, the kind that cost more per page than Ines earned in a week back when he earned anything at all. He held it up but did not extend it. "The correspondent signs himself Corvus Lyle. Historian attached to the Canal Survey Commission. He writes — and I quote, because I've read it eleven times and I want to be sure I have it exact — *'I am given to understand that Master Aldous Vane kept certain folios beyond those catalogued at his death. A man of his habits would not have left his life's work in a state of disorder. If any materials passed informally to his students or household, I would consider their prompt return a service to history, and to the memory of your late colleague.'*"

Fell folded the letter along its existing crease, slowly, as if closing a small door.

"He calls Vane my colleague," Fell said. "Vane called me a filing clerk with delusions. And now the capital writes to ask after his missing papers, and signs itself *service to history*, and I am expected to answer."

"What will you answer?" Ines asked. His own voice sounded far away to him, level and thin, a line drawn across empty country.

"That is rather the question, isn't it." Fell stood, joints cracking, and went to the window — Ines's heart clenched as the man's shadow fell across the table, across the linen — and looked down into the yard where the cooper's apprentices were stacking staves. "You know what I expected to find here? Evidence. Something I could confiscate and carry back and be done with. That's what the letter wants me to do. It wants me to search your room like a bailiff. It gives me permission, in very polite language."

"And instead you're telling me about it."

"Instead I'm telling you about it," Fell said, "because twenty years ago I watched Aldous Vane ruin a young surveyor named Kettilby for nothing worse than correcting him in public, and because I have spent twenty years wondering what a man has to be, inside, to hold a grudge that long and that neat. And because last month I watched them bury him with full honors while half the mourners were already composing letters about what he'd left behind." He turned from the window. "I don't know what you have, Halloran. I'm not asking. But I'll tell you what I know, because unlike some people I believe information should move downhill as well as up."

Ines set down the spoon. His mouth had gone dry around the porridge.

"Lyle arrived at Marrow Ford two days ago," Fell said. "The bridge is closed, so he's interviewing by letter — everyone on the far bank, every parish clerk, every ferryman who ever rowed Vane across, anyone who ever sold him ink. He wrote to the chapel yesterday asking after Vane's confession record. He wrote to me asking after the guild's holdings. And this morning—" Fell tapped the folio— "a second letter came, same hand, addressed this time to *the executor of the Vane estate*. Which is a phrase with exactly one possible meaning, and we both know the guild never formally claimed the estate, because the estate was inventoried before I could arrive, by persons unknown, and found incomplete."

*Persons unknown.* Ines kept his face still. Beneath three floorboards in a workshop across town lay an atlas that could hang him, and the word *incomplete* was sitting in a capital letterhead traveling toward him at the speed of official paper.

"So," Fell said. "Here is what happens next, so you can't say an old man didn't warn you. Lyle cannot cross until the bridge opens. The ice says six weeks; the commission's engineers say four; engineers are always optimistic about rivers and pessimistic about everything else. When it opens, he comes with clerks and a warrant and the patience of a man paid by the year. Everything hidden between now and then is hidden well enough. Everything hidden after—" He shrugged, a whole philosophy in the gesture. "I answered his first letter this morning. I told him the guild's inventory was complete and correct. I signed it. If it turns out I lied to the Crown's historian, that is my neck, and I have calculated that it is a shorter neck than yours, so I can afford the risk better."

"Why?" It came out of Ines before he could dress it up. "Why risk anything for me?"

Fell gathered his folio and stood in the doorway a moment, and for once he looked his age and then some — a man tired of being the hinge.

"Because I inventoried Aldous Vane's life for nineteen years," he said, "and in nineteen years he never once wrote anything down that wasn't true. Whatever he hid, he hid it the way he did everything. Exactly. On purpose. And a man like that doesn't hide paper out of vanity." He pulled his collar up against the draft from the stairwell. "Lock your door. Answer no letters. And Halloran — stop eating cold porridge like a man with nowhere to be. A guilty man hurries. An innocent one complains. Learn to complain."

Then he was gone, boots on the stairs, and the door shut, and Ines stood alone in the room with the linen bundle and the sound of his own pulse.

* * *

That afternoon he walked to the workshop.

There was no reason to. There was every reason not to — Fell had said *answer no letters*, and the corollary was *go nowhere*, touch nothing, become a stone in a wall. But the atlas lay under the third-floor board in a building the guild still owned, in a room the guild could enter whenever it liked, and Ines had left it there because leaving it there had felt, in the first raw days, like leaving it in his master's keeping.

He saw now that it felt like that only because grief makes excellent architecture for bad decisions.

He let himself in with the key he was technically no longer entitled to hold, climbed past the drafting tables where dust had begun its slow annexation, and knelt at the boards with his heart going like a trip-hammer. The atlas came up wrapped in oilcloth, and he unwrapped just enough to see the first leaf — Vane's hand, that upright merciless script, the boundary lines of Hollis Orchard drawn to a tolerance no Crown surveyor would bother matching — and then wrapped it again, tighter this time, and sat back on his heels in the cold room.

Every knock at a door, from now on. Every boot on a stair. Every letter with a capital seal. He had thought hiding the atlas was a refuge, a place outside the story where a careful man could wait out the weather. But refuges were for people the story hadn't noticed yet. Lyle had noticed. Lyle had written the words *missing folios* and set them moving through the postal roads like a slow poison, and every day the poison traveled further and the ice grew thinner, and the arithmetic was simple and terrible: the truth could stay hidden only exactly as long as the river stayed frozen, and the river did not care what it froze over.

He carried the atlas out under his coat, across the yard, through lanes where he flinched from every footstep, feeling absurd and hunted and — beneath the fear, in a chamber of himself he did not open — awake. More awake than he had been in nine years of apprenticeship. Fear, it turned out, was a kind of attention.

At the cooper's, Maren was in the yard arguing with the cooper about the price of barrel hoops, her breath smoking, her ledger under her arm like a shield she'd forgotten to put down. She saw him come in hunched and white-faced and stopped mid-sentence.

"You look like a man who's read his own obituary," she said.

"Worse," Ines said. "Someone else's."

Her eyes narrowed — that ledger-keeper's narrowing, the look of a woman reconciling accounts — but the cooper demanded her attention and the moment closed over. Upstairs, Ines bolted the door, slid the atlas beneath the floorboards of the room that was now its third hiding place, and sat down to do what mapmakers do when the territory turns hostile.

He drew a map of his own danger.

On the back of a scrap of practice paper he made a timeline: today, and then the weeks running down to the thaw. At each week he marked what he knew — Lyle's letters arriving, Fell's lie on record, Father Anselm's warnings, Maren's questions — and what he didn't, and the columns of *known* and *unknown* refused to balance, and that was the finding, really. He had believed knowledge was safety. He had been trained his whole life to believe it: measure a thing truly and it cannot surprise you. But the atlas proved the land could be measured perfectly and stolen anyway, and now the same held for him. Perfectly known, thoroughly documented, and safe as orchard soil under a surveyor's stake.

A knock at the outer door of the cooperage, below, sent him straight off the stool.

Footsteps on the stairs — the landing — a pause — and then Teodor's voice, muffled, calling up that supper was ready and Piet had caught eels and if Ines didn't come down the eels would be eaten and also Teodor wanted to know if rivers froze unevenly, because the mill race had open water in the middle and he couldn't work out why, and did Ines know, and supper.

Ines put his forehead against the cool plaster and breathed.

Eels. A boy and a question about ice. The world insisting on continuing.

"Coming," he called, and his voice almost didn't shake.

But as he reached for the latch he stopped, because he had heard something in his own voice that frightened him more than Lyle's letter had. Not fear. Readiness. Somewhere in the last ten minutes — in the kneeling, the wrapping, the walking through hostile lanes with his master's true work against his ribs — the hiding had stopped being a refuge and become a countdown, and countdowns end, and endings require decisions, and somewhere under the fear a part of him had already begun, quietly, treacherously, to look forward to making one.

He went down to the eels. Above the table, on the nail where the household hung things waiting to be remembered, someone — Teodor, probably — had hung a lantern, unlit, saving it for dark.

Six weeks, said the ice.

Four, said the engineers.
