## 10. The Marginal Hand

The cipher had been there all along, and Ines had walked past it for nine days.

He saw it first as an irritation — a smudge of brown ink along the outer margins of the western sheets, a stain that followed the page edges like water damage. He had almost treated it. There was a preparation, lemon juice and alum, that lifted foxing from old paper without lifting the ink beneath, and he'd mixed a small dish of it on the third night and set it aside, meaning to work sheet by sheet through the atlas the way Vane would have wanted: methodically, in order, with clean hands.

Then he noticed the stain was *regular*.

Not blotting. Not bloom. It repeated. A short stroke at the top corner of every folio, then a longer one, then three dots descending like birds going down to roost. The same sequence, page after page, running down the right-hand margin of the whole western section, so small that anyone reading the maps themselves — the rivers, the boundaries, the careful hatching of orchard rows — would take it for dirt.

Vane had taught him better than that. *Nothing on a map is accidental, boy. A map is a lie you've agreed to tell carefully.* The old man used to say it while tapping the margin of whatever chart he was correcting, tapping exactly where Ines now understood the cipher lived.

Ines set down the dish of lemon juice untouched.

* * *

He worked by the good lamp, the one with the whale-oil that burned steady, because his hands were not steady and he needed something in the room that was. Below him, through the floorboards of the cooper's shop, came the ordinary sounds of the evening shift: hoops driven home, the tap-tap-tap of the trussing hammer, Piet's voice singing something tuneless about a girl and a barrel. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that would go on whether or not what he found tonight was worth finding.

The marginal hand was not Vane's map hand. That was the first thing. Vane drew boundaries in a fine, compressed italic, every letter leaning into its neighbor like fence posts in wind. The margin marks were different — looser, rounder, made fast. Ines laid a sheet under glass and looked until his eyes watered, and slowly the marks resolved into letters after all. Not Latin. Not even the guild's shorthand, which Vane had drilled into him across four winters of dictation.

It was numbers. Old-fashioned numerals, merchant-style, the kind clerks used for tallying casks. Each mark was a number; each cluster of marks was a word, keyed to something.

Keyed to what? A cipher needs a key, and Vane had trusted keys to no one — that much Ines knew from hard experience. Four years of apprenticeship and the man had never once told him where the deed-box key hung, though Ines had dusted around it daily.

So the key had to be in the atlas itself. Vane never hid anything where it couldn't be found by the person who already knew to look.

Ines went back to the beginning — the title folio, the one bearing Vane's name and the words *A General Survey of the Vellamont Waterlands*, which was itself a kind of joke, since the survey was anything but general. And there, in the cartouche, in the decorative border of leaves and fruit that Vane had drawn with more patience than taste, Ines counted.

Leaves. He counted leaves. Thirty-one on the left branch. He counted fruit. Seventeen apples, eight pears, six cherries — no. He started over. Sixty-one fruits total, arranged — he copied them onto scrap paper, apple pear cherry apple apple — and sat back.

A sequence. A keyed sequence, sixty-one long, matching roughly the sixty-one folios of the atlas. Each fruit stood for a letter or a numeral class, each leaf position for an offset. It was elegant and it was mad and it was entirely Vane: hiding the key in plain sight, in ornament, in the part of the map everyone's eye slides past.

His heart was going like the cooper's hammer.

He didn't decode it that night. He made himself copy the cartouche twice, check the copies against each other, and lock them in the floorboard cache before he allowed himself to begin. Discipline. Vane's voice in his head, dry as August: *An excited mapmaker makes excited lines, and excited lines are wrong lines.*

But when he finally lay down on the narrow bed, sleep wouldn't come. He kept seeing the margin marks. Kept thinking: *he wrote this knowing*. Not fearing — knowing. Knowing the Crown would come, knowing the guild would surrender everything, knowing some apprentice would be left holding paper that could hang him. And instead of destroying the atlas, Vane had built it a second life inside itself, folded small, ready to be carried away piece by piece.

By whom?

That question followed him down into sleep and was still there when he woke.

* * *

Decoding took three days.

It should have taken three hours, but Vane had built traps into it — deliberately, Ines realized with something between admiration and fury. Every tenth folio the key shifted, and if you didn't notice the shift you produced gibberish, and if you produced gibberish you might conclude the cipher was meaningless, a dead man's doodling, and give up. Vane had filtered his readers. Only someone patient enough to doubt their own work rather than the master's would get through.

On the fourth evening, working on the floor with the sheets spread in ordered rows, Ines transcribed the last cluster and read the whole thing straight through.

It began:

*If you are reading this in sequence, then you have the patience I lacked at your age, and probably the fear I had at mine. Both are useful. Do not waste either.*

Ines put down the pencil. For a moment he couldn't breathe properly. It was Vane's voice — not the classroom voice, not the correcting voice, but the voice Ines had heard perhaps five times in four years, late, tired, almost kind. The voice of the man underneath the exacting machine.

*You will want to know why I did not simply tell you. Because telling makes one keeper, and one keeper is one fire, one theft, one frightened confession away from nothing. I learned this the expensive way, and I will not write here how. What is held by many hands cannot be burned by one match.*

*The atlas divides as follows.*

And then the instructions, precise as a boundary line:

The western sheets — the true boundaries of the orchard villages, Kettle Row, Ashford Bend, the Maren holdings — were to be copied entire and carried out separately, no two parts traveling together, by persons unconnected to the guild. *Choose hands that owe the land something,* Vane wrote. *A debt remembers longer than loyalty does.*

The title proofs — the extracts from parish deeds that established who had held what, since when — were to go to whoever kept records of their own. *There will be such a person. There is always such a person in a threatened place. Find her.* Ines read *her* and felt the hair rise on his arms. Find her. As though Vane had known. As though somewhere in these pages, in some marginal note beside the Maren holdings he'd never examined closely enough, the old man had written her name.

The cipher keys themselves — the fruit-and-leaf cartouche, the offset tables — were to be memorized, never written, and lodged in the memory of a person of standing whom no one would think to search. Ines read that instruction twice and thought of Father Anselm in his cold chapel, saying Mass to half-empty benches, keeping everyone's accounts of sin. A man of standing no one would think to search. It fit like a key in a lock.

And the master index — the single sheet listing where every part went — was to remain with the reader. With Ines. Sewn into clothing, carried on the body, surrendered never.

*You will be tempted to keep it all together,* the final passage said. *Resist this. You are brave — I have watched you be brave in small ways for four years and pretended not to see, because praise is a debt and I could not afford yours. But bravery is not the same as survival, and the record matters more than either of us. If they take everything from you, let them take only your part. The rest must already be gone.*

*One thing more. When the waters come — and they will come, the canal was decided before I ever drew my first boundary — the orchard lanterns will be lit for the last time, as they have been lit for two hundred years at the end of harvest. Be there for it if you can. Someone should see. Seeing is also a kind of keeping.*

*I chose you because you were nobody. Forgive me. Nobody survives; somebody gets remembered. Be both.*

*— A.V.*

Ines sat on the cold floorboards for a long time. Down below, the cooperage had gone quiet; someone banked the stove; a door closed. The lamp burned its steady whale-oil steadiness and he did not move.

*A talented eccentric*, the guild steward had called Vane at the funeral. A difficult old man. A relic. And here was the truth instead: not eccentric — architect. Every dry correction, every withheld kindness, every year of teaching Ines to doubt his own hand had been load-bearing. The apprenticeship itself was part of the design. Ines hadn't been trained to draw maps. He'd been trained to be the hinge this atlas would swing on.

And Vane had never trusted him alone. Never trusted *anyone* alone. That was the awe of it and the dread of it, arriving together, inseparable. The plan required many hands — hands that owed the land something. Hands Ines would have to find, and choose, and ask. Asking meant confessing. Confessing meant that other people's safety became his doing.

He thought of the household above the cooper's shop. Maren with her ledger and her level gaze. Teodor naming trees. Piet singing badly. People who owed the orchards everything, whose names were already in Vane's pages whether they knew it or not.

*Choose hands that owe the land something.*

He had spent nine days keeping the atlas secret to protect himself. Now he understood the secret was never meant to be kept — only divided. Division was its protection. Hoarded, it was a death sentence; scattered, it was seed.

Ines rolled up the transcription, sewed the master index — he cut and trimmed it that night, small as a prayer card — into the lining of his coat, working the needle by lamplight with fingers that shook only a little. Then he put out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the river ice groan downstream, the sound of the trap still closed around the town.

Tomorrow he would have to speak to Maren. Tomorrow the lying would have to stop, or change shape at least.

Tonight, one thing remained. He took up the atlas one last time and turned to the folio covering the Maren holdings — the orchard above the town, the slopes, the spring house — and looked, for the first time, at the margins of *that* page with the decoded alphabet in his head.

Three clusters of marks. Short ones. A name.

He translated them slowly, not because it was difficult but because he wanted to make sure.

*Maren. Keeper. Trust.*

She had been in the atlas all along. His master had chosen her before Ines ever crossed the bridge — had chosen her, and chosen Ines, and left them to find each other.

Below the cooper's shop, faintly, a lantern moved along the yard, and someone was coming up the outside stair.
