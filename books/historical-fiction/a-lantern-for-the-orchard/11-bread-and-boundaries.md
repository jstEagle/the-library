## 11. Bread and Boundaries

The invitation came the way Maren did everything: without asking permission.

"You'll eat with us tonight," she said on Thursday morning, not looking up from her ledger. She was at the long table in the cooper's yard, tallying winter stores against winter mouths, and she said it in the tone of a woman announcing weather. "The baker's wife is bringing loaves. There'll be too much otherwise."

"I have work," Ines said.

"Your work will keep." She made a small mark with her pencil. "It always keeps. That's what work does. People don't."

He should have refused. He knew that even then — some part of him, the part Vane had trained like a muscle, understood that a mapmaker's safety lay in remaining unmapped, in being a man no household thought of when it set its table. But the smell of bread had already reached him from the bakery two doors down, and he had eaten alone for eleven days, and his refusal came out as something weaker than refusal.

"What time?"

"When the bell rings for vespers," Maren said. "Bring nothing. You've brought nothing worth bringing yet anyway."

She said it without malice — he was learning that about her, that she dealt in truths the way other people dealt in coins, quickly and without ceremony — but it landed harder than an insult would have. He went up to his room above the cooper's shop and sat with Vane's atlas for an hour without opening it, listening to the sounds of the working quarter assemble itself below: Piet arguing with the cooper's eldest daughter about the price of staves, Teodor whittling something on the steps, a woman named Ilse laughing at something Ines couldn't hear.

They were loud, these people. They were loud in a way the guildhall had never been. The guild spoke in whispers and margins; this place spoke at full voice, and every word seemed to land on him whether it was meant to or not.

* * *

The long table filled the cooper's front room the way water fills a channel — completely, finding every gap. Eleven people sat down to eat, and Ines counted them the way he counted everything, out of habit: Maren at the head; Teodor at her right hand; Piet the cooper, broad as a door; his wife Greta; the eldest daughter, Sabine, and the two younger children whose names Ines still confused; old Ilse, who had been a picker in the Halloran orchards before her hands failed; a drover named Bram home early from a bad season; two brothers, Ferenc and Dániel, displaced from the eastern villages the year before, who still ate as if the food might be taken back; and Ines himself, at the foot, where the table narrowed.

Bread was passed hand to hand. Not served — passed, each person breaking from the loaf nearest them, so that by the end everyone had eaten from everyone's loaf. Ines watched this happen with the attention he usually gave to contour lines, and understood only later that he was watching a ritual, older than any of them, older probably than the guild itself.

"Teodor," Maren said, "say the trees."

"It's a dinner, Mother, not a lesson—"

"Say the trees."

Teodor put down his knife. He was fourteen and wore the expression of a boy permanently embarrassed by his own knowledge, which was considerable. He closed his eyes.

"From the north wall," he said. "Old Katalin first — the pear, the one that split in the storm of '38 but kept bearing. Then the three sisters, apples — Veronika, Sarolta, and the little one we call Csilla because she blooms last and shines longest. Then down the row—" and he went on, tree by tree, forty-one names, his voice steady and unhurried, while the table went quiet around him. Some of the names were women's names. Some were men's. One was called Pista, after a dead man; one was called Little Bird, and nobody explained why, and nobody needed to.

Ines sat with bread going cold in his hand and felt something shift in his chest that he could not name and did not try to.

"The far one?" Maren asked, when Teodor finished.

"Aldous," Teodor said. "Still standing. Still bitter. Best fruit on the tree, if you wait long enough."

A few of them laughed. Ines did not. He was looking at his own hands.

* * *

The talk moved the way river talk moves — crops, prices, the ice on the bridge, a rumor that the Crown surveyors' advance camp had been seen at Tarnfeld, two days east. Ines listened the way he had been trained to listen, taking everything in and offering nothing, and for a while that worked, the way invisibility always works until someone decides to see you.

It was Bram the drover who turned to him first.

"Halloran. That's a guild name, isn't it? You were Vane's man."

"I was his apprentice."

"Vane." Bram chewed for a moment. "He came through the orchards every year. Never talked. Drew everything. My father used to say he drew like a man paying a debt."

"And you?" It was Piet, from further down the table, genial and direct. "What do you draw?"

"Maps."

"For whom?"

"The guild. The Crown, ultimately. Everything goes up the chain eventually."

"And what do they do with it all, the maps?" This was Ilse, the old woman, her ruined hands folded on the table. She asked it genuinely, without edge, and somehow that made it worse.

"They record things," Ines said. "Boundaries. Elevations. Water lines."

"Water lines." Ferenc, the younger of the eastern brothers, said it flatly. He had a scar running from his ear to his jaw that Ines had never asked about. "That's what my village is now. A water line. Somebody drew it, and then somebody read it, and then there was water."

The table had gone quiet in a different way than before. Not the hush of ritual — the hush of a question assembling itself.

Maren set down her cup. She did it carefully, the way she did everything, and looked at Ines across the length of the table with those gray eyes that took inventory of everything and gave back very little.

"Ines," she said. "I'm going to ask you something plainly, because I've found plainness saves time, and I haven't got much left. Whose side are you on?"

"Maren," Greta began, gently reproving—

"No. He sleeps under our roof. He eats our bread. My son has shown him every tree we have. I think I'm owed the question, and I think he's man enough to hear it." She didn't raise her voice. "Whose side are you on? The Crown's, that means to drown forty villages and call it improvement? The guild's, that draws their maps for them and calls it honest work? Or ours — the ones being measured?"

Every face at the table turned to him. Ines felt the familiar reflex rise, the apprentice's reflex, the orphan's reflex: be small, be useful, be unobjectionable. Say the thing that costs least.

"I'm a mapmaker," he said. "I don't take sides. I record what's true."

The words were out before he understood how bad they were. He saw it land — saw Ferenc's jaw tighten, saw Ilse look down at her hands, saw Piet exchange a glance with his wife. And he saw Teodor.

The boy wasn't angry. That was the terrible part. He looked at Ines with something close to compassion, the way you'd look at a lame bird, and said, quietly:

"That's a coward's answer, Mr. Halloran."

"Teodor." Maren's voice had an edge now, but not the one Ines expected. It wasn't protecting him from the boy. It was warning the boy to be gentle, and the gentleness was already there.

Teodor shrugged, fourteen years old and unarmored. "It's true, though. Saying you don't take sides just means you're waiting to see which side wins before you pick. The water doesn't care about recording things. When it comes up the valley, it won't ask what's true. Somebody has to decide to stand somewhere, or everybody drowns polite."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the cooper's dog barked once at the dark and stopped.

Ines looked down at the bread in his hand — bread he had broken from Piet's loaf, from Greta's loaf, from Maren's loaf — and felt shame arrive in him whole, like a tide. Not because the boy was cruel. Because the boy was kind, and right, and young enough to say it without flinching.

"You're correct," Ines heard himself say. His voice came out level, which cost more than shaking would have. "It was a coward's answer. I don't have a better one tonight. But I'll tell you what I can honestly say." He set the bread down. "I am not yet sure what I owe. But I know I owe something. And I know I'm not comfortable anymore, which I suspect is the beginning of it."

Nobody applauded. Nobody smiled. But Maren held his gaze for a long moment, and then she nodded once, small, and picked up her cup, and the table breathed again and the talk resumed around him like water resuming its course.

Ilse leaned over, near the end, when the others had drifted into arguments about spring prices. Her whisper smelled of dried apples.

"Don't mind the boy. He watched his father answer that question wrong. Took the safe side, the quiet side, the side that said *it won't come to us*. The water came anyway." She patted his arm with a hand like a bundle of twigs. "You'll decide. Men like you always decide late. But late is better than never, and sweeter than almost."

* * *

He climbed the stairs to his room well after midnight, past the sleeping sounds of the household, and lit his lamp, and took out the atlas.

He hadn't intended to open it. He told himself he would simply check the clasps, confirm the hiding place beneath the floorboard was undisturbed, perform the small nightly liturgy of secrecy that had become his religion. But the book was in his hands, and his hands had a will of their own lately, and he opened it to the sheets covering the western villages — Maren's country, the Halloran orchards, Teodor's forty-one trees.

And there, along the margin of the boundary sheet, in Vane's cramped, precise hand, he noticed for the first time a notation he had read a hundred times without seeing:

*Bearers wanted. Not one. The hand that carries alone is the hand they cut off.*

He stared at it for a long time.

Then he heard footsteps on the stair — light ones, deliberate ones, stopping outside his door — and a knock, soft and unmistakable, in the smallest hours of a night when no honest person knocks.
