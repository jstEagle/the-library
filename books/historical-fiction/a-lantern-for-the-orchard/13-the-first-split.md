## 13. The First Split

The copying began at midnight and by lamplight, because that was how Vane would have done it.

Ines had reasoned it out over three sleepless nights. The atlas was too large to move whole — a folio of forty-one sheets, each one the size of an open hymnal, bound in calfskin that creaked when you lifted it like something alive. Anyone seen carrying it anywhere would be remembered. But sheets could be folded. Sheets could be sewn into coat linings, rolled inside candle stubs, pressed flat beneath the false bottoms of bread tins. A map was only dangerous all together. Divided, it was just paper.

So he worked at the table in his room above the cooper's shop, with the shutter latched and a rag stuffed under the door to kill the spill of light, and he copied.

He had not known he could do this. That was the strangest part. Four years of apprenticeship had taught him lettering, gridding, the patient arithmetic of scale — but he had always assumed the hand itself was Vane's gift, untransferable, the way a singer's voice is hers alone. Yet somewhere between the second sheet and the sixth, his own line stopped trembling. His orchard boundaries came out clean. His hachures fell where hachures should fall. At one point he caught himself holding the pen exactly as Vane held it, thumb crooked, wrist floating above the page like a bird deciding whether to land, and he put the pen down for a full minute because his eyes were stinging and wet pens ruin work.

The western sheets were the ones that mattered most. Seven of them: Coldharbour Rise, the Fen Orchards, Maren's own ground at Tarnbeck, the mill villages along the old drover road. On these pages Vane had drawn what the deeds said and what the surveyors' plates said in two colors of ink, and where they disagreed — they disagreed everywhere — he had written the deed boundaries in iron-gall black and the Crown's claimed lines in a thin, apologetic red, so that the forgery looked like what it was: a wound laid over healthy flesh.

Ines copied both colors. He copied the marginalia too — the ciphered notes in Vane's small slanted hand, which he still could not fully read, though he understood now that they were instructions, that his master had written to him across four years of silence, *you will know when*.

He finished the seventh sheet near dawn on the fourth night. His fingers were gray with ink to the second knuckle. He blotted the last line, blew it dry, and sat looking at seven folded pieces of paper stacked beside the lamp, weighing perhaps as much as a loaf of bread.

They contained the legal death of a canal.

He wrapped them in oilcloth and waited for morning, because some things you do not deliver in the dark.

* * *

Maren was in the press house when he found her, checking the winter stores — barrels of cider apples packed in straw, the last of the perry racked and sealed. The building smelled of ferment and cold wood. She did not look up when he came in.

"You've got ink on your face," she said. "Left cheek. Like a smudge of soot."

He wiped at the wrong cheek. She watched him do it without correcting him, which was her way — let a man find his own errors, that was how you learned anything worth learning.

"I need to talk to you," Ines said. "Not here."

"There's nobody here."

"There's nobody here yet." He heard how it sounded and winced. "I'm sorry. I'm not— I don't think anyone's watching me. I don't actually know. That's rather the point."

That made her look up. She set down the apple scoop she'd been using, dusted her hands on her apron, and studied him the way she studied trees in late winter — looking for the bud that hadn't broken yet, judging whether it would.

"The chapel," she said. "Father Anselm keeps the vestry stove lit this hour. If we're going to be fools, we may as well be warm ones."

* * *

They walked separately, ten minutes apart, which felt theatrical and necessary at once. The town lay under its fourth hard frost; smoke stood straight up from every chimney like drawn threads. Ines passed the guildhouse without looking at it, which required an effort of discipline he was getting better at. The steward had sent no more letters since the warning about Corvus Lyle's inquiries. The silence was worse than the letters.

Father Anselm took one look at them both arriving within the quarter hour and said nothing except to bank the stove and go stand in the churchyard, ostentatiously inspecting a wall that needed no inspection. He had done this before, clearly. He had a whole vocabulary of leaving rooms.

Maren sat on the vestry bench with her forearms on her knees. "Well," she said. "You've been carrying something since the night you landed here soaked through. Piet says you sleep like a man guarding a door. Teodor says you talk in your sleep about folios. And you flinch whenever a rider comes down the high street. So. Say it or don't, but stop spending yourself saying nothing."

So Ines told her.

He told it badly at first and then better, the way you learn any difficult piece of work — by doing it. He told her about cataloguing Vane's effects the morning after the funeral, and the steward's inventory, and the floorboard. He told her about the marginalia, the true boundaries, the red lines. He told her about the Crown notice claiming everything Vane had ever touched, and about Lyle's letters, charming and meticulous, fishing for missing folios. He told her that the atlas documented her village — Tarnbeck, her grandfather's plantings, the boundary stones her family had maintained for three generations — and that the government meant to drown all of it and call the drowning lawful, and that the lawfulness was manufactured, drawn into existence by men who knew precisely what they were drawing.

"And I have it," he said. "All of it. Under my bed in a box that used to hold chisels. And I can't keep it there anymore, because if they come for me they take it in one armful and burn it in one afternoon, and then there's nothing. There's just their version, forever."

Maren listened the way she did everything: completely, without interruption, giving no sign of judgment until judgment was wanted.

"You're giving it to me," she said.

"I'm asking you to keep part of it. Seven sheets. The western grounds — yours, your neighbors'. If it's all in one place it dies in one place. If it's split—" He faltered. "Vane wrote something in the margins. Coded. I've been working through it. I think he planned this. I think he never meant one person to hold it. I think—" He stopped, because the next thing was the thing he'd been circling for days. "I think he chose me because I had nothing to lose, and I resented him for that my entire apprenticeship, and now I understand it was the qualification. An orphan carries things better than a man with a family. Except now—" He gestured helplessly at the vestry, the stove, the smell of apples drifting from the press house, the whole accumulating weight of the last two months. "Except now I appear to have acquired one. Which ruins the design entirely."

Maren didn't smile, quite. Something moved near her mouth and decided against it.

"Show me," she said.

He unwrapped the oilcloth on the bench between them and unfolded the first sheet — Tarnbeck, her own ground — and watched her read it.

She read maps the way he'd learned people read letters here: slowly, with the lips moving slightly, tracing the lines with one blunt finger. Her finger followed the black boundary of her orchards east along the watercourse, and stopped where the red line cut across it, amputating a third of the holding, and rested there.

"That's my grandmother's planting," she said. "The Russet row. Planted the year of the big frost, they tell it. Sixty-odd trees." Her finger stayed on the red line. "This line puts them in the canal."

"Yes."

"And this black line is what the deeds say."

"What your deeds say. What every deed back to the disafforestation grants says. It isn't ambiguous, Maren. It isn't even cleverly forged — well. It is cleverly forged, geometrically it's a masterpiece of nonsense, but legally it's transparent. They didn't bother hiding it because no one was ever meant to look. Records burn. Maps get corrected. Who's left to object?"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, Father Anselm had apparently concluded his inspection of the wall and moved on to a vigorous examination of the sky.

"My mother kept accounts," Maren said finally. "Not letters — she couldn't write much. But accounts. What we owed the widow Harkness, what the Harknesses owed us, how many bushels off which rows going back years, so when the tax man came and told us what we'd produced, we knew what our own trees had done. She used to say a number written down belongs to whoever wrote it until somebody else writes it truer." She looked up at him. "You're telling me you've got the truer number."

"I'm telling you I've got Vane's numbers, and Vane spent thirty years making sure they were true, and yes."

"Then why give them to me? Why not run? Ice'll bear a man walking north inside a month if this cold holds. You could be in the capital by spring with the whole atlas and a solicitor and your pick of newspapers."

Because, he almost said, because you'd all be flooded either way and I'd be safe and I couldn't live in the safe. But that wasn't honest, or not all of it.

"Because a stranger with papers is a courier," he said. "A stranger with papers and a name in this town — that's a witness. And because—" His voice did something embarrassing. He pushed past it. "Because you asked me once what I carried, and I lied to you, and I've been lying by increments ever since, and I'm tired. And because if they take me tomorrow, someone has to already have this. Not after. Already."

Maren looked at the seven folded sheets for a long time.

Then she picked them up, refolded the top one along its existing crease with a care that made his chest hurt, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and tucked the whole parcel inside her jacket against her body, the way women here carried coins and letters and, he supposed now, revolutions.

"No ceremony?" he asked. He'd rehearsed oaths. He'd expected, at minimum, a vow.

"What'd you want, a signature?" She stood. "You've known me since the bridge went down. You've eaten at my table twenty times. My boy draws your maps for you and calls them practice, and we both know they're not practice. Ceremony's for strangers." She buttoned her jacket. "One condition."

"Name it."

"You finish the rest, and you split it the same way, and you don't ask my permission for who — but you tell me who, after. I keep the ledger of this. All of it. Names and routes and what they carry, same as my mother's book, same as the harvest counts. If a page goes missing someday, somebody has to know what's missing." She tapped her own temple. "It doesn't all live in paper. Paper burns. Ask the chapel."

"Ask me," said Father Anselm mildly, from the doorway, having materialized with the soundlessness of a man long practiced at returning at the right moment. "Everything I've hidden has burned or been confiscated. Everything I remember remains." He looked between them with tired, shrewd kindness. "Though I confess I'm glad to see the paper moving again. It was starting to gather in one place, and gathered things are grave goods waiting to happen."

"You knew," Ines said.

"I knew a young man started copying maps at midnight behind a rag-stuffed door in a room with paper-thin walls above a cooperage." Anselm smiled faintly. "The cooper snores, incidentally, but he doesn't miss much."

* * *

Walking back, they took the long way along the frozen towpath of the future canal — the dead-straight trench the Crown had grubbed out two summers ago before the money arguments froze the project, now a scar of pale ice running arrow-straight through country that had never asked to be straight. Maren walked it without looking at it, the way you avoid looking at a scar. Ines looked. He couldn't help it; it was his training. Straight lines in landscape are always human decisions, and human decisions are always answerable.

At the bend where the towpath met the orchard track, she stopped.

"Do you know what frightens me?" she said, not turning around. "Not Lyle. Not the raiding. Not even the water." She turned. In the flat white light her face looked carved. "What frightens me is that yesterday I was a woman keeping apples and sheltering strays, and today I'm a conspirator, and it feels exactly the same. No drum. No change in the wind. You just wake up one morning and the line's been crossed and you're on the other side of it, and you check, and you wouldn't go back if they paid you." She shook her head slowly. "My grandmother crossed a line like that in 'twenty-nine, over the tithe. Family talked about it for a generation like it was a great thunderclap. I'd wager it felt like this. Like Tuesday."

"I'm sorry," Ines said. "I could have gone on lying. You'd have slept better."

"I'd have slept fine," Maren said. "But I'd have woken up drowned and ignorant, and ignorant is the part they're really after. The water's just the tool." She started walking again, then paused. "One more thing. The boy."

"Teodor?"

"He'll figure it out. He figures out everything, it's his affliction. When he does, you tell him yourself, before he works it out alone — because a secret a child discovers is a burden, and a secret a child is trusted with is a duty, and I know which one I'd rather he carried." She glanced sideways at him. "And Ines. If they catch you, and it comes to names — you give them mine first. Understand? Mine, loud and early. I'm a widow of no property with a reputation for taking in vagrants; they can't do anything to me that the flood isn't already doing. You're the guild's man. They'll want to make an example of you. Don't let them build the example out of the household."

"They won't catch me."

"Everyone says that," Maren said. "The ones who were wrong are dead, and the ones who were right are dead too, later, of something else. Say it anyway. It keeps the spine straight." 

They parted at the cooper's gate. She went in without looking back, the parcel flat against her ribs, and Ines stood in the lane a moment with his empty hands and his hammering heart, feeling the specific vertigo of a man who has set down a weight and discovered that setting it down makes it heavier, not lighter — because now it was shared, and shared things can be betrayed, and there was nothing between the truth and the fire now but Maren's jacket and Maren's word and the ordinary, breakable loyalty of one woman's Tuesday-morning decision.

Then, from upstairs, the scrape of the shutter. Teodor's face in the gap, sharp-eyed, reading Ines's face the way he read everything.

"Ines," the boy called down, softly, in the voice of someone who has already worked it out. "Where did she go just now? And why are you smiling like that?"

Ines looked up at him, and understood that the next conversation had already begun.
