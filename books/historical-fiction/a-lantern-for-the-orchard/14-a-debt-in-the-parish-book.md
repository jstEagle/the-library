## 14. A Debt in the Parish Book

Father Anselm had kept the key in his mouth for eleven years.

Not always, of course — that was the exaggeration of an old man telling himself stories — but he had carried it somewhere against his skin since the night in 1842 when two men from the Crown survey had come to the chapel with a warrant and a wheelbarrow and taken every deed, every tithe record, every baptismal register entry that named an orchard family's holding. They had been polite about it. That was the thing people forgot. Erasure, done properly, was polite.

He had hidden the strongbox that same night, before they came, because Aldous Vane had sent him a note that said only: *They are coming Thursday. Trust the cold.*

Trust the cold. Anselm had spent a day puzzling it, then understood: the crypt beneath the chapel floor was never warm. The old builders had set the foundation stones over a spring, and in winter the stones sweated frost. Anything stored there would be damp-ruined within a year — unless it was wrapped in waxed linen and packed with ash, as Vane himself instructed in a second note that arrived by the hand of a boy paid to forget who sent him.

So on Wednesday night, while his housekeeper slept, Anselm had knelt on the chapel floor and pried up three flagstones near the altar rail, and put into the earth everything the Crown would come for Thursday and not find.

They had found nothing. They had also found *him*, eventually. Not through evidence — there was none — but through arithmetic. The parish records were incomplete in ways too neat to be accident, and a bishop under pressure from the survey commission needed a priest to blame more than he needed a priest to be innocent. Anselm had been removed from his living in the spring of 1843. Not defrocked; that would have required proof. Simply *retired*, at forty-one years of age, with a pension small enough to keep him humble and a reputation quietly damaged enough to keep him silent.

He had stayed anyway. He had no other parish and no other town, and Vellamont was stitched into him like thread into cloth. He said Mass in the morning for whoever came, which was fewer every year, and in the afternoons he walked the riverbank, and in the evenings he sat in the cold rectory and thought about the weight of paper.

That was the phrase Vane had used once, in the last conversation they were ever seen having, at the market in the autumn of 1852. *Paper is heavier than stone, Anselm. Stone only falls on you once.*

Anselm had thought about that sentence for a year now, and he was thinking about it again this February evening, because Ines Halloran was standing at his door with snow in his hair and a look on his face that Anselm recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting.

* * *

The boy — he was hardly more, twenty-two perhaps, though grief had aged him past his count — had been coming to evening prayer off and on since the funeral. He did not pray, exactly. He sat. He sat in the third pew from the back with his hat on his knees and his eyes fixed on the altar as if the altar were a page he was trying to read. Anselm had watched him do it six times and had said nothing, because there is a kind of visitor you must let arrive at his own pace or he will bolt like a deer.

Tonight, though, the boy had lingered after the last candle was snuffed, and when Anselm came down the aisle with the keys, Ines stood up too fast, the way young men do when they have rehearsed a speech and lost their nerve.

"Father. I wanted — I've been meaning to ask."

"Ask," Anselm said, and unlocked the door, and held it open, because some conversations go better walking.

Ines fell into step beside him. "You knew my master. Before. Everyone says you knew him better than anyone."

"I knew him," Anselm said carefully, "for a long time."

"Did he ever—" The boy stopped at the edge of the churchyard where the path met the road, and the wind coming off the frozen river took his words and he had to start again. "Did he ever tell you what he was working on? At the end?"

Ah, Anselm thought. There it is.

He looked at Ines Halloran properly then, in the blue dusk, and saw what Maren Coldharbour's household must already have seen: a young man carrying something heavy and pretending it was nothing, walking with the particular straightness of someone who has decided not to limp. Anselm had spent eleven years perfecting that walk himself.

"He told me," Anselm said, "that if I ever met a young man of mine who asked me that question, I should invite him to supper."

Ines blinked. "I don't—"

"It's ready. It's been ready since Tuesday. Come along before the soup dies of old age."

* * *

The rectory kitchen was the only warm room in the building and Anselm made no apology for it. He fed the boy bread and bean soup and pickled onions from a jar older than their acquaintance, and he talked about nothing — the price of lamp oil, the state of the ice, the scandal of the bishop's new carriage — until Ines's shoulders came down out of his ears and he began, tentatively, to eat like a person rather than a guest.

Then Anselm set down his spoon and said, "Tell me about the atlas."

The silence lasted long enough that the fire popped twice.

"I don't know what you mean," Ines said.

"Ines." Anselm folded his hands on the table, the way he did when he meant a thing. "I am going to say several things now, and you may interrupt any of them, but I'd take it as a courtesy if you didn't lie to a man who went to prison-adjacent lengths for your master's sake. You inherited Aldous Vane's papers. You did not surrender them all to the guild steward. You are living above the cooper's shop instead of the guildhouse, which tells me you are keeping yourself out of certain reach. And you have spent six evenings in my church looking at my altar like a man doing sums. I have seen that face before. I saw it in a mirror, in the winter of 1842. So. Tell me about the atlas."

The boy's jaw worked. Anselm watched him weigh the lies available to him — and there were good ones, Anselm could see, well-made lies, apprentice work of a high order — and watched him set them down one by one.

"The boundaries," Ines said finally. "Vane surveyed the orchard villages' true boundaries. Every field line, every title, going back sixty years. And it doesn't match the Crown's canal survey. Not by a little. By whole farms." His voice dropped. "They've falsified the deeds, Father. The government has forged the land titles so the flooding will be legal. And Vane documented it, and now the commission wants his papers burned, and I—" He stopped. "I have them."

"Do you still have them," Anselm said, "or do you have them *somewhere*?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If they're in your coat, we are both fools. If they're distributed, we are merely criminals, which is a great deal safer."

Something moved behind the boy's eyes — not quite trust, but the decision to act as if trust existed, which Anselm had long ago concluded was the same thing wearing different clothes. "Distributed," Ines said. "Partly. One copy. The rest is hidden."

"Good," said Anselm. "Aldous taught you well, even dead."

"You knew. You knew what he was doing. All those years."

"I knew he was angry," Anselm said. "Anger keeps, if you store it right. Sit still. There's something you need to see, and I need to tell you a story first, because the seeing won't make sense without the story, and because I am seventy-two years old and entitled to my pacing."

* * *

"In 1841," Anselm began, "the Crown ran its first seizure in this valley. Not here — upstream, at Hallowmere and the Fenwick orchards. The canal route then was drawn differently. They took the deeds lawfully — that is, they took them under a law written for the taking — and they gave receipts, and they promised compensation at valuation, and the valuations were done by the same office that drew the route, and I am sure you can complete that geometry yourself.

"But here is the part that matters. The Hallowmere families had held their land for generations on the strength of papers in my keeping — old deeds, older than the county registry, some of them on vellum. When the commissioners arrived, the parish book showed the holdings clearly. So the holdings were extinguished cleanly, legally, with signatures, and eleven families walked away from orchards their grandparents had planted, and one of them — a woman named Sera Doss, whose cider you could taste from across a room — hanged herself from her own best apple tree in April, before the blossom, so she wouldn't have to see it."

Ines had gone very still.

"After that," Anselm continued, "I made a resolution. I am not a brave man, Ines. I want that understood. Bravery implies a choice made freely, and I simply could not stomach being useful to it twice. So when word came, in '42, that the survey would reach Vellamont's parishes next, I did not decide anything. I just found myself, on a Wednesday night, prying up my own church floor."

"And Vane warned you."

"Vane *instructed* me. There's a difference. Aldous never warned anyone in his life; warning implies you might be ignored. He wrote to me as though the hiding were already accomplished fact and I were merely the last to know. It was infuriating. It was also, I confess, a relief — to be conscripted into courage rather than required to volunteer for it." Anselm smiled without much humor. "When they removed me from the living, he came to the rectory and stood in that doorway — that exact doorway — and said, 'You knew the cost.' And I said yes. And he said, 'Then you're the only honest man in this valley, and honesty is worthless unless it's archived,' and left a bottle of brandy I still haven't finished."

"What happened to him?" Ines asked quietly. "After. Why did he stop? Why did he become—" The boy caught himself, embarrassed.

"Distant? Exact? A man made of rules?" Anselm shook his head. "Because he learned what I learned, but harder. He tried to fight the Hallowmere seizure openly — petitions, depositions, he even traveled to the capital with copies. And the copies were ruled inadmissible because the originals were in Crown custody, and the originals were ruled accurate because they were in Crown custody, and he discovered that in an argument between paper and power, power writes both sides. After that he stopped fighting in daylight. But a man like Aldous doesn't stop. He goes underground. Literally, in our case."

Anselm rose, joints announcing themselves, and took the lamp from its hook.

"Eleven years I've waited for someone to tell this to. I'd begun to think I'd be buried with it, which seemed wasteful." He paused at the kitchen door and looked back at the boy. "What I'm about to show you, no living soul knows of but me. When I show you, it becomes yours as much as mine. Do you understand what I'm saying? This isn't information. It's a debt. I'm handing you a debt."

"Father—"

"Come and see whether you accept it."

* * *

The chapel was black and cold as a held breath. Their footfalls woke small echoes. Anselm led him down the aisle past the altar rail, to the third flagstone from the wall on the north side, and set the lamp on the floor, and knelt with a groan that was half pain and half theater.

"There's an iron ring under the moss," he said. "Feel for it."

Ines knelt beside him. His fingers worked at the stone's edge, found the ring, pulled. The flagstone came up with a sound like a book closing.

Beneath it: a cavity lined with lead sheeting, and in the cavity, a strongbox — oak-banded iron, furred with the cold — and around it, packed tight, the gray drift of wood ash, and the smell of wax and old linen rising out of the earth like something exhaled after a long sleep.

"Help me lift it. It weighs what truth weighs, which is more than you'd think."

They got it onto the chapel floor together. Anselm produced a small key from his waistcoat — the one he had carried against his skin, in his breast pocket, in his boot heel, migrating over the years like a relic — and turned the lock. The hinges complained. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen bundles tied with cord, lay the contents: a brick-red mass of paper and vellum, dry as autumn leaves, perfectly preserved.

"The original deeds," Anselm said. "Hallowmere. Fenwick. And Vellamont — every orchard holding in this parish, back to the old grants, with the boundary marks described in the witnesses' own words. Signatures of dead farmers. Seals of dead clerks. Everything the commissioners collected in '42 went into the county incinerator the following spring — I have it on good authority — but these never entered their custody. These are the documents the Crown's titles are forgeries *of*. Without these, the forgery is just ink on paper that agrees with itself. With these—" He looked up at the boy. "With these, it's a lie you can prove in court. If there's ever a court again that isn't owned."

Ines hadn't touched the box. He was staring into it as though it were a grave or a cradle, and maybe, Anselm thought, it was both.

"Why give this to me?" Ines said. "Why not Maren? She keeps the ledger. She's the one they'd follow."

"Because Maren already carries enough to hang her, and because you are the pen." Anselm smiled at the boy's startlement. "Yes, I hear things; the confessional leaks in both directions, despite what the bishop thinks. You're trained, Ines. You can read a boundary clause, date a seal, spot a forged hand. Maren has memory and fury, and God knows both are needed, but fury can't testify. Paper testifies. Trained paper." He tapped the box's rim. "And there's one more thing. Look at the top bundle. Under the cord."

Ines untied it with the careful hands of his trade. Beneath the linen, atop the oldest deeds, lay a single loose sheet — a letter, creased into eighths, sealed with plain wax and addressed in a hand Ines plainly knew, because his breath caught on it.

*To the keeper of my atlas,* it said.

"He anticipated me giving you this," Anselm said. "Or he instructed me to, years ago, and I've only now obeyed. Read it tonight, alone, and then burn it — no. Don't burn it." He corrected himself slowly, hearing the words as he said them. "Keep it. I've hidden long enough. Hidden things rot, even truths. Especially truths. Whatever comes of all this, let it be carried openly by people willing to be known."

Ines looked up. "You'll be known, then. If this comes out — the strongbox, your part in it. They took your post once. They'll take—"

"They took my post," Anselm said, "and left me my soul, which was the better bargain, though it took me a decade to audit it correctly." He began replacing the flagstone with his heel. "I hid these deeds to spite a seizure, boy, and I've regretted only the hiding, never the deed. Eleven winters that box has lain under my church getting heavier, and not one ounce of it was paper."

* * *

At the rectory door, with the snow starting again and the lamp held high, Anselm caught the boy's sleeve.

"One caution. The historian — Lyle. Corvus Lyle. Word comes downriver ahead of such men the way weather does, and the word on him is that he reads people the way you read surveys: completely, and for weaknesses. When he reaches you — and he will reach you, letters travel faster than bridges open — he will be kind. Kindness is his instrument. Remember that kindness used as a tool is still a tool, and tools don't feel the cutting."

"I'll remember."

"No, you won't — not fully, not in time; nobody does. But you'll remember afterward, and afterward is where we keep our scars." Anselm released his sleeve. "Go home. Read your letter. And Ines — when you've finished grieving Aldous Vane, and you will finish it, because grief is a parish duty and duties end — try to forgive him too. You'll learn soon why he needed it."

The boy went out into the snow, and Anselm stood in the doorway watching him dwindle up the lane toward the cooper's shop, toward the lit windows where the displaced were singing something low and steady.

Then he went inside, banked the fire, and sat a while with his unfinished brandy, feeling eleven years lighter and considerably more afraid — which was, he reflected, exactly the proportion courage always came in.

On the table, where Ines had left it forgotten in the dark of the chapel and Anselm had retrieved and set deliberately in view, lay a scrap of paper the boy had been carrying, folded and refolded soft as cloth: a copied fragment of map, an orchard boundary drawn in a fine sure hand, annotated in the margin in cipher.

Anselm smoothed it flat beneath the lamp and looked at the cipher a long time.

He had seen that cipher once before, in 1843, on the back of a note that smelled of Aldous Vane's particular ink — and he remembered, with the slow cold turn of an old man's certainty, that he had never told a living soul what it decrypted to.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would write to the boy.

Tonight he would sit with the knowing, one last night, the way one sits with a dying friend.
