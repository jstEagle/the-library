## 15. The Steward's Price

The steward came on a Thursday, which was the day the guild always came for money.

Ines knew the rhythm of it from three years of apprenticeship: every other Thursday, Master Quillon Hobb would descend the hill from the guildhouse with his ledger under one arm and his umbrella furled like a weapon, and he would stand in Vane's doorway — *my* doorway now, though the word still caught in his throat — and ask after accounts with the weary patience of a man who had never once been surprised by a number. The workshop owed rent. The workshop owed paper debts to Marlowe & Sons. The workshop owed a year's back subscription to the Cartographers' Quarterly, because Aldous Vane considered maps made by other men to be a form of necessary poison, like smallpox inoculation.

So when the knock came at nine in the morning, sharp and official, Ines assumed it was about the Quarterly.

He had spent the night copying. This was becoming his whole life — days at Maren's table pretending to mend fishing nets badly while Teodor laughed at him, nights hunched over the atlas by candlelight, translating Vane's western sheets into fair copies that could pass for practice work if anyone ever saw them. His right hand had developed a cramp that no amount of flexing cured. His eyes burned. He had begun dreaming in contour lines.

He swept the atlas into its hiding place — the false bottom of Vane's old plan chest, beneath a layer of spoiled drafts — and opened the door with what he hoped was an apprentice's dull civility.

"Master Hobb."

"Ines." The steward did not come in. That was the first wrong thing. Hobb always came in; he inspected the hearth, tutted at the ink stores, ran one finger along the windowsill and examined it for dust as though dust were a moral failing. Instead he stood on the step in the grey morning light, umbrella furled, ledger conspicuously absent, and looked at Ines the way a man looks at a horse he is deciding whether to buy.

"You look tired," Hobb said.

"I've been cataloguing."

"Mm." A pause. "May I?"

That was the second wrong thing. Hobb never asked permission for anything inside a guild property. Ines stepped back and let him in, and watched the steward cross the workshop without touching a single surface — no finger along the sill, no tutting at the hearth. He sat down in Vane's chair, which no one but Vane had sat in since Vane died, and folded his hands.

"I'll be brief," Hobb said, "because I know you have work."

*You don't know the half of it,* Ines thought, and was alarmed to find how easily the lie lived in him now.

"The commission arrives when the bridge opens. Corvus Lyle and six assistants, plus a military escort, plus whatever clerks the Crown attaches to them. They will take possession of everything in this building bearing Aldous Vane's hand. That is settled law and I cannot change it." Hobb's voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "What I can change — what is within my gift, and I did not come here to waste your time with things outside my gift — is what happens afterward."

Ines stood by the door. He didn't sit. Sitting would mean this was a conversation between equals, and he wanted very much to keep hold of the small advantage of standing.

"Afterward," he said.

"Afterward," Hobb agreed, "there will be a vacancy. Principal mapmaker to the guild of Vellamont, with the Vane workshop attached, at full journeyman's rate, backdated to your master's death. You are nineteen years old, unlicensed, orphaned, and formally you should be another four years from consideration. I can make it happen by Easter." He let that sit. Then, more quietly: "I can also restore his name."

Something turned over in Ines's chest, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting in a riverbed.

"His name," Ines said carefully, "needs restoring?"

Hobb looked at him for a long moment. Outside, sleet ticked against the window. Somewhere down the lane a cart complained through the slush.

"Ines," the steward said, "I liked Aldous Vane. That is not a sentence I say often, and I want you to understand it cost me something to walk up this hill today. He was difficult and vain and he treated his apprentices like furniture that could occasionally hold a pencil. But he was the finest mapmaker this guild has produced in forty years, and when the commission arrives, they will not see that. They will see a dead man whose folios do not match the Crown survey. Do you understand what Lyle's people do with discrepancies? They do not argue with them. They do not footnote them. They conclude that the deceased was either careless or corrupt, and they write the conclusion into the record, and the record outlives us all." Hobb unfolded his hands, refolded them. "I am offering you a way to spare him that. And to spare yourself."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you surrender his papers to me before the commission arrives. All of them. For safekeeping."

*For safekeeping.*

Ines heard the phrase the way you hear a floorboard creak in an empty house — a small sound that tells you exactly where someone is standing.

Three weeks ago he might not have heard it. Three weeks ago, safekeeping would have sounded like shelter, like a strongroom with a good lock, like the guild doing what guilds existed to do. But that was before the chapel smoke. Before he'd stood in the churchyard with ash settling on his sleeves like grey snow, watching Father Anselm's face as the parish register went into the brazier — deeds, births, boundaries, two hundred years of *this land belongs to these people* — reduced to warm air on the wind off the river. Before he'd learned that when the victors say *safekeeping*, they mean a place where documents go to become unavailable. There was a fire behind every strongroom door in this country. It just wasn't kept in the same room as the lock.

"Safekeeping where?" Ines asked.

"The guild archive. Sealed, catalogued, held in trust until the commission's findings are published."

"Who holds the key?"

Hobb's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "The guild does. Which is to say, I do."

"Under whose seal?"

"The Crown's, once Lyle countersigns the transfer."

"There it is," Ines said, before he could stop himself.

The silence that followed had weight. Hobb rose from Vane's chair slowly, and for a moment the steward's composure flickered and Ines saw the man underneath — sixty years old, a functionary who had spent his life keeping other people's records intact and had somehow convinced himself that was the same thing as keeping them true.

"You think me a fool," Hobb said.

"No."

"A coward, then."

"No," Ines said again, and found he meant it. "I think you're a man who has been told the fire is a hearth so long that you've stopped smelling the smoke."

* * *

They stood facing each other across the workshop, and Ines became aware, with a strange clarity, that he was being offered his entire future and was going to refuse it, and that some traitorous part of him wanted to say yes anyway.

Because that was the cruelty of it — the offer was *good*. Not a bribe dressed in finery, not the crude purchase Lyle would eventually attempt. It was a legitimate life, whole and warm and waiting. Journeyman's rate at nineteen. The workshop, with its north light and its smell of size and iron-gall ink. And Vane — Vane's name cleaned, Vane remembered as the great craftsman rather than the careless eccentric, Vane's grave spared the indignity of historians' footnotes. Everything Ines had wanted since he was eleven years old and first saw a map being drawn and understood, with the sudden total conviction of childhood, that this was the shape his life would take.

And all it cost was silence. Just silence. Nobody would even have to die.

"I'd be lying to you," Ines said slowly, "if I told you the offer doesn't tempt me."

"I know," Hobb said. "It's meant to."

"But you should know something too." Ines crossed to the plan chest, and for one vertiginous moment considered — actually considered — pulling out the atlas and putting it on the table between them. Ending the secrecy. Making the steward choose, here, now, with his own eyes on the true boundaries of Thornmere and Ashcombe and the orchard villages, instead of the Crown's tidy fiction. *Look at it,* he imagined himself saying. *Look at what they mean to drown. Then tell me about safekeeping.*

He didn't. His hand rested on the chest's cold brass handle and stayed there.

"What?" Hobb said.

"I've been reading his marginalia," Ines said, which was true, and insufficient, and a lie all at once. "Master Vane documented the orchard villages. Every field boundary, every watercourse, every title line, going back to the original grants. If the commission finds those sheets, they won't find carelessness. They'll find contradiction. And contradiction, in my experience of the last few weeks, gets burned."

Hobb went very still. "Are you telling me the folios exist?"

"I'm telling you what happens to documents that contradict the survey," Ines said. "I watched it happen at the chapel. So when you say safekeeping, Master Hobb, I have to ask you plainly, because I think you're an honest man even if your institution isn't: if I give you Vane's papers, will they still exist in five years?"

The steward did not answer immediately. Sleet ticked against the glass. Down in the working quarter, faintly, someone was splitting wood — the steady knock of it carrying up the hill like a slow clock.

"They will exist as long as they can," Hobb said at last, and the precision of it, the lawyer's honesty of it, was worse than a simple yes or no. "Ines, listen to me. You are twenty minutes from losing everything either way. When Lyle arrives, if the folios surface — if any scrap of them surfaces — the question will not be whether Vane was right. It will be who helped him hide them, and that question has only one name attached to it now. Yours. An apprentice with no family, no license, no standing. Do you know how easy it is to erase a man like that? Not with a trial. Trials leave records, and records are inconvenient. With a *discharge*. With a note in a file. You would simply stop having been here." He picked up his umbrella. "I have seen it done. I may have signed the forms."

"And now you're offering me the same erasure in reverse," Ines said. "A name, a post, a place in the record — if I help make sure the orchards have none."

"I'm offering you survival," Hobb said, with sudden roughness. "Yours, and his memory's. The villages are lost regardless — the canal is approved, the Act is passed, no atlas un-passes an Act. You must know that. Whatever you're protecting in that chest, it cannot save a single tree. All it can do is ruin everyone who touches it."

"That's not what it's for."

"Then what is it for?"

Ines thought of Maren's ledger, the columns of names and debts, the harvest tallies kept in a hand that pressed hard enough to dent three pages down. He thought of Teodor reciting trees like saints. He thought of Father Anselm saying, in the chapel cellar, *they took my post, boy, but they did not take my copy.* He thought of Vane's last page, unfinished, the ink thin where his master's hand had begun to fail.

"It's for afterward," Ines said. "When the water's up and everyone agrees it was inevitable and always was. Someone has to be able to say: it wasn't. Here are the lines. Here were the names. We measured it. It happened."

Hobb stared at him. Something moved behind the steward's eyes — recognition, maybe, or grief for a younger version of himself who might once have said such a thing and been talked out of it.

"You sound like him," Hobb said quietly.

"He trained me to."

"No." Hobb shook his head, moving toward the door. "Vane never believed anything enough to sound like that. He hedged everything, even his courage — that ciphered margin business, the indirection, the never signing his convictions. You—" He stopped himself at the threshold, umbrella in hand, and studied Ines with an expression Ines couldn't read. "Take the offer. Think on it a week. The commission can't cross until the thaw; nothing is decided yet. Come to the guildhouse and tell me you accept, and none of this — none of what I suspect, none of what Lyle suspects — will ever need to be spoken again."

"And if I don't come?"

Hobb paused on the step. The sleet had thickened to proper snow, fat flakes catching in the steward's grey hair.

"Then when the ice breaks," he said, "I will have no choice but to write to Corvus Lyle and tell him that the Vane apprentice received his master's papers, and that the papers have not been surrendered. I'm sorry, boy. I truly am. But I keep accounts, and I cannot let this one open against the guild." He settled his hat. "A week."

* * *

After he was gone, Ines stood in the middle of the workshop for a long time, listening to the snow.

The offer hung in the room like heat from a stove. He made himself feel all of it, deliberately, the way Vane had taught him to test a source before trusting it: the post, the light, the license, the name restored. Himself at twenty-five, established, respectable, drawing charts for honest merchants, Vane's best student and acknowledged heir. Himself at forty, master of the workshop, training some orphan of his own. It was not a fantasy; it was *available*. One sentence to Hobb. One chest handed over. One fire, far away, that he would never have to watch.

He thought about Piet's hands, cracked from the brine vats. About Maren's ledger and the way she wrote the dead children's names in red. About the brazier in the churchyard, and how the ash had smelled — not like burning paper, exactly, but like something sweeter underneath, like orchard blossom forced out of season.

*Safekeeping means the fire.*

He pulled the atlas from the plan chest and laid it open on the table, and worked until dark, and then worked past dark, and somewhere around the ninth bell he realized he had stopped deciding. The decision had already been made — perhaps in the churchyard, perhaps the night he'd first hidden the thing beneath the floorboards, perhaps the day a dying man had put a key in his palm and said only, *finish it properly* — and everything since had just been the long process of catching up to it.

There was a week, Hobb had said. Seven days.

But when dawn came grey through the north windows, Ines found he could not spend seven days waiting indoors for the end of the countdown. He woke Teodor before the boy's household stirred, wrapped both their boots in sacking against the ice, and led him out of town along the frozen ditch toward the canal route — because if he was going to lose everything, he intended, first, to walk the exact ground it would be drowned for.

Halfway there, Teodor stopped dead in the trampled snow and pointed.

Stakes. Rows of pale stakes driven into the orchard soil, each flagged with a scrap of Crown blue, marching in a straight line straight through the heart of the oldest trees.
