## 16. Ice on the Canal Route

The frost came down hard in the third week of January, hard enough that the low meadows east of town froze into a single white sheet, and Ines woke before dawn with the thought already formed in him, fully dressed, as though it had been standing at his bedside all night waiting: *go and see it.*

He had avoided the canal route for weeks. That was the honest accounting of it. He had walked the orchards with Maren and Teodor, had sketched the rows of apple and quince until his fingers ached inside his gloves, and every one of those walks had bent carefully around the eastern fields, where the Crown's engineers meant to cut their channel from the river to the mill race. He told himself there was no point going — the ground was just ground, frozen mud and stubble, nothing a mapmaker needed that he couldn't take from Vane's sheets. But that was a surveyor's lie, and he knew it. He hadn't gone because he didn't want to see the place where all of it — the trees, the ledger names, the testimony he was beginning to collect in the chapel cellar — would end.

But the frost had changed something. Frozen ground held marks. Frozen ground was honest paper.

He found Teodor in the yard behind the cooper's shop, breaking ice out of the horse trough with the flat of an axe, breath steaming, ears scarlet.

"I need to walk the canal line," Ines said.

Teodor set the axe against the trough and looked at him the way the boy looked at everything — directly, without ceremony, as if the world were a thing you simply faced. "Maren said you'd ask eventually."

"Maren said?"

"She said you'd circle it for a month first." He was fourteen and entirely without mercy. "She was right."

* * *

They went out while the light was still grey, along the drovers' track past the last of the working houses, and then east into the open country where the wind came off the river with nothing to stop it. The canal route was no secret — it had been published in the gazette two years ago, a neat diagram showing a channel forty feet wide running from the weir to the millpond, with the flood line drawn as a soft blue wash that swallowed, in order: the lower hay meadows, the mill race, the eastern orchards, and finally, if the spring surveys went as planned, the village green itself at Hollowmere, three miles on.

On paper it had always seemed abstract to Ines. A blue wash. A contour.

Underfoot it was different. They walked the line itself, Teodor ranging ahead with the sureness of a boy who had hunted rabbits across this ground his whole life, and Ines began to understand what he had refused to understand at his desk: that a flood line is not a shape. It is a list. Every step forward added a name to it. The hay meadows belonged to the Widow Sarelle. The mill race fed the tannery where Piet's brother worked. The first orchard row they crossed was the Brae planting, sixty years old, the trees thick-trunked and silvered with frost, and Teodor touched each one as he passed — not idly, but the way you'd touch a doorpost entering a house.

"You know them all," Ines said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched the boy do it for weeks.

"Grandmother Maren taught us. She says a tree without a name gets cut like timber, but a named tree — people hesitate." Teodor shrugged. "Mostly they don't hesitate long."

They came up over a low rise where the ground fell away toward the old millpond, and stopped.

The stakes had already been driven.

Ines had expected them, in the way you expect a death you've been told about. But expectation was nothing against the sight. They stood in a rough line across the frozen field, perhaps thirty of them, whitewashed stakes about the height of a man's chest, each one tagged with a tin plate stamped with a number and a date — dates from October, November, before the bridge closed, before winter sealed the town away from the world. Someone had come in the autumn, while Vane lay dying, and driven these posts into the soil of the orchard country with hammers, in the cold, methodically, and gone away again. The ground around each stake was scuffed dark where men had knelt or stamped.

"They were here in the fall," Ines said. His voice came out flat. "Before the commission. Before Lyle."

"October," Teodor said. "I saw them. I pulled one up then, too." He said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "Piet made me put it back. He said if they noticed missing stakes they'd send men to count them, and men who count stakes start counting other things."

Ines crouched at the nearest stake. The frost had welded it into the earth, but the ground had heaved around it in the cold, and when he worked it side to side the whole post groaned loose with a sound like a tooth coming out. He read the tag. *Stake 41. Cut Line E-3. Datum: Crown Survey 1851.* Below that, in smaller stamping: *Elevation 212 feet.*

Two hundred and twelve feet. He knew what that number meant without needing to calculate. Two hundred and twelve feet above the weir pool was the height the water would reach when the sluices closed. This stake was not measuring the land. It was marking its drowning — a depth gauge planted in a living orchard, calibrated to stand in six feet of still water someday, its whitewash underwater, its tin tag green with weed.

He had drawn flood lines himself. Apprentice work, early years — shading the blue wash on harbor charts, on drainage schemes for the fen farms. He had never once thought about what stood under the blue. Ink covered it; that was the whole function of ink. You drew the water and the water erased whatever the draftsman chose not to show.

Vane had shown it. Every tree, every boundary stone, every name — Vane had spent twenty years drawing everything the blue wash would erase, and had never once written down why, because writing it down would have been surrendering it to exactly the men now reading tags in the frost.

"Ines." Teodor was ten yards ahead, standing very still. "This one's different."

* * *

It was different. The others were surveyors' stakes, machine-cut pine, factory-stamped plates. This one was hand-hewn, oak, older than the rest by decades, and its plate was not stamped but engraved, and the engraving was not a number.

Ines brushed the frost off with his thumb and read:

*Brae Orchard. Planted by Josiah Brae, 1793. Sixty trees. Let no man move this mark.*

A boundary marker. A true one — the kind that had legal force in the old land courts, the kind Vane's atlas recorded in careful red ink, the kind the Crown's new survey had quietly failed to find, all thirty of them across the orchard country, because the surveyors had apparently walked this ground in October with their eyes on their own stakes and their own stamps, and had seen an old oak post and driven their pine beside it without ever reading what the oak post said.

Or worse. Without bothering to look.

"It's a title mark," Ines said. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to someone else — some other apprentice, some other life. "Do you understand? This post *is* the deed. If this stands in a land court, the Brae family owns this ground. There'll be more of them. Vane mapped them all—"

He stopped. Teodor was looking at him with that direct, unblinking attention, and Ines realized how close he had come to the edge of the secret — how much the boy could infer from half a sentence and a shaking man crouched in the frost holding a tin tag.

"The atlas," Teodor said.

Not a question either. Fourteen years old, raised in a house of displaced laborers by a woman who kept her own ledger of names and debts, and he had put it together from a broken sentence and a look.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the chapel burning. You watch doors now. You didn't used to." Teodor crouched beside him, breath steaming between them. "Maren knows too. She doesn't say. She says a man tells you when he's ready, and if you push, he lies better."

Ines looked at the boy — really looked — and felt something crack open that wasn't fear. "Are you angry I didn't tell you?"

"No." Teodor considered it honestly, head tilted. "I'm angry at the stakes."

* * *

They walked the line another mile, and Ines counted. Thirty-one pine stakes, machine-stamped, October-dated, driven into frozen ground between the Brae rows and the millpond. And four of the old oak title-marks — he found them by teaching himself, in that mile, to see them: hand-hewn, frost-silvered, standing in the places Vane's red ink had promised. Three of the four had pine stakes driven within arm's reach of them, close enough that a careless eye would read the pair as one monument, the new stamp crowding out the old engraving.

Not erasure by fire this time. Not burning parish records in a chapel yard, not that clean theatrical violence Ines had witnessed with the smell of ash in his throat. This was slower. Quieter. A new post driven an arm's length from an old one so that, in ten years, in fifty, anyone walking this ground would see two markers and assume they marked the same thing, and would read the newer, cleaner, better-lettered one, and the older would rot at the base and fall, and no one would ever know there had been a difference.

That was how the Crown did it, Ines thought. Not with flames. With adjacency. With patience. You didn't destroy the old record — you simply made sure the new one stood closer to the road.

Except the old ones hadn't fallen yet. They were standing right here, in the frost, in January, with their engravings legible and their legal force intact and nobody — not one soul in the Crown's apparatus — aware that the town's mapmaker's apprentice knew precisely what they were and what they meant.

"Help me," Ines said.

Teodor was already moving.

They worked the fourth oak post together — the one farthest from the track, half-hidden in a hedge of blackthorn, its base heaved loose by three frosts. It came out of the ground with a tearing sound, roots of frozen earth clinging to it, and Teodor staggered back with it across his shoulders like a man carrying a Christmas log, and Ines stood a moment looking at the hole it left. A post-shaped absence in the orchard soil. He thought: this is what the flood will be. A whole landscape of post-shaped absences, filled with water.

Then he took the other end and they carried it, stumbling, laughing once — horribly, giddily — back toward the blackthorn hedge, and laid it down in the shadow of the thickest growth, and dragged brush over it until only Ines could have said where it lay.

"One," Teodor said.

"One," Ines agreed. His heart was going like a hammer. "And we leave the pine stakes standing."

Teodor frowned. "Why?"

"Because they haven't noticed them yet. Because if a stake goes missing, they send men to count stakes, and Piet told you what counting men do." He looked back down the line, at the thirty-one white posts marching through the silvered orchard like a fence built for a graveyard. "But also — let them stand. Let them measure their own drowning. When the truth comes out, I want every one of those tags sitting in the record next to the oak posts they tried to bury. Let the geometry convict them. It's what I'm for."

Teodor looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and spat on his palm and offered it, and Ines — who had grown up among guild men who sealed nothing with anything but signatures — took the boy's hand and shook it, frost-burnt palm to frost-burnt palm.

They walked back in the failing light, and the town showed itself to them by degrees: smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the chapel spire, the dark mass of the bridge, iced shut at its far end like a door with frost in the lock. Behind them the orchard country lay white and silent, thirty-one stakes glimmering faintly in the dusk, marking the height the water would reach.

Three of the four oak posts still stood in the ground. Ines had told himself they'd come back for the others — that they'd take them one at a time, patiently, the way the Crown worked, adjacency and patience turned back on itself.

But walking home, the plan curdled in him, because he understood suddenly that they might not get three more chances. The frost wouldn't hold forever; thawed ground would grip those posts like fists. And there were other eyes on this country than theirs. Lyle was on the far bank, interviewing by letter, meticulous as frost itself, and sooner or later the man's questions would crawl east across the ice toward the cut line, toward the stakes, toward the question of what else stood in that ground besides Crown pine.

At the cooper's door, Maren was waiting with a lantern, and she looked at the two of them — the boy with blackthorn scratches down one cheek, the apprentice with frozen mud to the knee and a wildness in his face he couldn't hide — and she didn't ask what they'd done. She lifted the lantern higher, so the light fell on both their faces, and said:

"So. Now you've seen it."

"Now I've seen it," Ines said.

"And?" she said. "What did the ground tell you?"

Ines looked back east, at the darkening fields, at the invisible hedge where an oak post lay hidden under brush with sixty years of law engraved on it.

"It told me they've already begun," he said. "And it told me we can prove they've lied. Both at once. Maren — the posts are deeds. Standing in the dirt. All we have to do is keep them standing."

Maren was quiet a moment. Snow started to fall, fine and dry, ticking softly against the lantern glass.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you'd best teach me which posts are which. Before someone comes asking why my boy smells of blackthorn and fresh-turned earth."

Behind them, far off across the frozen fields, a dog began to bark — once, twice — and stopped.
