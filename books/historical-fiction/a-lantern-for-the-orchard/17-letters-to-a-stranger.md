## 17. Letters to a Stranger

The first letter arrived with the Thursday packet, which was itself a small miracle — the bridge had been closed for six weeks, and yet the Crown's post found its way across the ice as if winter were a formality that did not apply to His Majesty's correspondence.

Ines knew who it was from before he broke the seal. The paper was heavy cream stock, too fine for Vellamont, and the hand was a copperplate so perfect it looked engraved. No one else in the world wrote to him in that hand.

*Mr. Ines Halloran,* it began. *I hope you will forgive the presumption of a stranger writing to you across the ice — though of course we are not strangers, are we? We share an acquaintance, or rather I should say we shared one: the late Aldous Vane, whose loss I felt as keenly as any man can feel the death of someone he never had the privilege to meet.*

Ines read that sentence twice. It was doing several things at once, and all of them were lies, or adjacent to lies, and he found himself admiring the craftsmanship even as his stomach tightened.

*I have taken up the task of preparing the official history of the Vellamont Canal Survey, and no history of that undertaking could be complete without a proper account of Mr. Vane's cartographic work. He was, by every report I have gathered, a talent of the first order — exact, original, a man ahead of his time. The guild speaks of him with reverence. You, I understand, were his apprentice for eleven years. His true heir, some have said.*

Nobody had said that. Ines was almost certain nobody had said that. Which meant Lyle had invented the phrase himself and attributed it to "some," the way a forger invents a seal.

*It occurs to me that a man of your training would know better than anyone where the master's papers now rest. The commission has a duty to preserve everything of scholarly value, and I would consider it a personal kindness — indeed, a professional partnership between equals — if you could tell me what survives, and where. There is no urgency. Winter gives us both time to be thorough.*

He signed it: *Corvus Lyle, Historian to the Commission, at your service.* And beneath the signature, in smaller script, almost an afterthought: *P.S. — I am told the crossing at Miller's Bend failed on the ninth. A tragedy. Ice is indifferent to our plans, isn't it? One hopes the town's records are kept somewhere dry.*

Ines set the letter on Vane's old desk — his desk now, though he still thought of it as the master's — and sat looking at it for a long time.

He had known this moment would come. He had imagined it since the night he hid the atlas beneath the floorboards: a knock, a summons, a man in a good coat asking polite questions. But he had imagined force. Shouting. A warrant. He had not imagined charm. Charm was worse. Charm meant the man on the other end believed he had already won.

There was a second thing in the letter, folded flat against the first page. A printed notice, official stock, the commission's crest at the top:

*BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSION FOR THE VELLAMONT CANAL: All maps, surveys, deeds, parish registers, ledgers, and papers of any kind relating to lands within the designated flood boundary are hereby declared materials of public record and must be surrendered to the Commission's historian upon demand. Failure to surrender such materials constitutes obstruction of a work of national improvement.*

Upon demand. Not yet demanded. A blade being sharpened where Ines could hear the sound of it.

He carried the letter downstairs, through the cold rooms of the guildhouse, out into the lane, and stood in the falling dusk reading it again by the light of the cooper's shop window, where Maren's household moved behind frost-ferned glass like figures in a lantern show. He could have shown it to her. He thought about showing it to her.

He went back inside instead.

That was the first lie, and it was told not in ink but in footsteps.

* * *

He drafted seven replies before he wrote the eighth.

This surprised him. He had expected to be bad at it — expected his hand to shake, expected honesty to leak out of him like ink through cheap paper. Instead the opposite happened. Each false draft came easier than the last, and each one frightened him more than the last, because lying, it turned out, was a craft, and crafts were the one thing he had ever been allowed to be good at.

The trick, he discovered on the fourth draft, was structure. A lie collapsed when it tried to carry too much; a half-truth held weight because half of it was load-bearing. So: admit what was already known. The guild steward had inventoried the workshop in November; that inventory existed; deny nothing about it. Admit that Vane's effects had been catalogued. Admit that some folios had been surrendered already — they had, the harmless ones, the practice charts of riverbanks no canal would ever touch. Build the true things into a wall, and hide the door in the wall.

The fifth draft taught him tone. Lyle flattered; therefore Ines must be slightly dull. Eager to help, honored by the attention, a little pedantic about procedure — a young man pleased to be noticed by an important stranger. Give Lyle a version of him that was small enough to dismiss. The best hiding place for a mapmaker was inside a map of something boring.

The sixth draft taught him the most dangerous lesson of all: that he was enjoying this.

He caught himself smiling over the page — actually smiling, alone in a freezing room at midnight, composing a sentence designed to make a dangerous man feel clever — and the smile curdled. This was what Vane must have felt, he thought. The atlas's margins, the cipher, the whole long architecture of concealment — his master had built it with the same part of the mind that loved the work, and Ines had never understood until now how those two things could live in the same hand without tearing it apart.

He burned drafts one through seven in the stove and wrote the eighth clean.

*Sir,*

*Your letter reached me Thursday and I confess the attention of the Commission honors me more than my modest part in Mr. Vane's work deserves. I was his apprentice eleven years, but an apprentice is a pair of hands, not a mind; whatever genius the master had died with him, and what remains is only grindwork.*

*You ask after his papers. I will tell you exactly what I know, as I would want it told to me. Upon his death, the guild steward made a formal inventory of the workshop — you may apply to him for the document, and I encourage you to, as it is more reliable than my memory. The folios listed there were surrendered to the guild in December. They consist chiefly of practice sheets, fair copies of county boundaries, and an unfinished general atlas of the river valley, much of it in rough draft. If anything of value survived him, it will be in that inventory and nowhere else. I have gone through his effects myself, twice, grieving and thorough, and I found nothing beyond what the inventory records.*

*A small correction to your letter, offered with respect: Mr. Vane was not ahead of his time. He was behind his — devoted to methods the guild abandoned thirty years ago, triangulating by hand when any journeyman could buy a theodolite. I loved him for it, but love makes poor history. You will serve the truth better if you write him as he was: an exact, stubborn, somewhat old-fashioned man.*

*As for the town's records — the parish chest is dry and sound, so far as I know, though Father Anselm could speak to it better than I. The ice took the bridge, as you note, but Vellamont has kept its books through worse winters than this one.*

*I remain, sir, at the Commission's disposal,*

*Ines Halloran*

He read it four times, hunting for the seams. Every statement in it was technically true except two, and the two were load-bearing: *what remains is only grindwork*, and *nowhere else*. Everything else — the humility, the pedantry, the little correction about Vane being behind his time — was armor made of fact. Even the insult to his own master's reputation was strategy: Lyle had called Vane a talented eccentric once already, Ines remembered, in the letter to the guild. Let the historian think the apprentice agreed. Men trusted those who confirmed their priors.

And underneath it, threaded through the whole letter like a watermark: *dry and sound, so far as I know.* A man with nothing to hide does not volunteer information about dampness. But a suspicious man reads eagerness, not evasion, if the eagerness is detailed enough.

He sealed it and addressed it and set it on the hall table for the packet rider, and then he stood in the dark hallway with his heart going like a trip-hammer, understanding fully for the first time what he had become.

Not a keeper of a secret. A counterfeiter of himself.

* * *

Lyle's answer came nine days later, faster than the post should have allowed, which told Ines the historian had a private courier running the ice — meaning money, meaning authority, meaning the Commission considered this correspondence worth the expense.

*My dear Halloran,* it began, and Ines noted the escalation from *Mr.* — the intimacy of a man reeling in a line. *Your account is everything candor ought to be, and I thank you for the correction regarding your master. You write of him with a filial frankness that does you credit. History suffers enough from mourners; it wants witnesses.*

*Two matters, briefly. First: your description of the surrendered folios matches the steward's inventory in all particulars save one. The inventory notes a bound volume, leather, unmarked spine, described in the steward's hand as "master index — contents unknown." Your letter omits it. An oversight, surely. Where might this index be?*

Ines put the letter down and pressed both palms flat against the desk until the wood stopped seeming to move.

The steward. Of course. The steward had written *contents unknown* into an official document months ago, and Lyle had read that document the way Ines read contour lines — seeing the shape of the hidden ground. *Contents unknown* was a door standing open in the wall of facts Ines had built, and he had walked right past it.

Second matter, said the letter: *the Commission anticipates the reopening of the crossing within the month. When I arrive, I hope you will allow me to call on you — not as an inquisitor but as a fellow laborer in the same vineyard. I think you and I understand something the townspeople do not: that a record belongs to everyone or it belongs to no one. Vellamont keeps its memories like coins under floorboards. Coins rot, Halloran. Paper kept in many hands becomes legend; paper kept in one becomes evidence. I would spare you the burden of being the only evidence.*

*Yours in the common cause, C. Lyle.*

*P.S. — Do give my regards to Mrs. Maren of the orchard house. I am told she shelters a number of the displaced and keeps remarkable accounts of her own. A woman after your own heart, perhaps. Two keepers of records under one roof — the Commission finds such coincidences instructive.*

Ines read the postscript three times. Then he did something he had not done since the funeral: he laughed, once, short and ugly, in the empty room.

Lyle didn't know. Couldn't know. It was a fishing hook dressed as a pleasantry — name a plausible name, watch what twitches. Maren's ledger was famous in the quarter; anyone on the far bank could have told a letter-writing stranger about it. But that was the horror of the method, Ines realized. Lyle didn't need to know anything. He needed only to say true-sounding things and count the silences afterward. Every letter was a survey. Every reply was a reading taken.

And Ines had answered the first survey beautifully. Too beautifully. He had given the historian a map of a compliant, grief-struck, slightly dim apprentice, and now the historian was testing the edges of the map to see where it tore.

* * *

He walked to the cooper's that evening without deciding to. His feet decided. Teodor opened the door before he knocked — the boy had a way of knowing footsteps — and Maren was at the long table with her ledger open, Piet mending net by the stove, the widow Sarelle slicing bread, the whole room smelling of tallow and pine resin and the particular warmth of people who had nowhere else and had made a country of it anyway.

"You've had two letters from the far bank," Maren said, not looking up from her columns. "The rider's boy told Piet's cousin, who told everyone. Sit down."

"I haven't told them anything," Ines said. "Important."

"Sit down anyway."

He sat. She closed the ledger, which she never did while working, and looked at him with the flat patience she used on livestock and liars alike.

"This Corvus Lyle. What does he want?"

"The atlas." Saying the word aloud in this room felt like setting a lamp on a powder keg. "He doesn't say so. He says he wants to honor my master's memory. He says paper kept in one place becomes evidence." Ines turned his cup on the table. "He asked about an index. The steward's inventory mentioned a bound volume, contents unknown. Lyle found it."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That the index was surrendered with the rest in December."

Maren's eyes didn't move. "Is that true?"

"No."

"Good," she said, and went back to the ledger, and the room exhaled around him — Piet's knife resuming its whisper along the netting, Sarelle's knife through the loaf — as if a confession of forgery were the most ordinary news of the week.

"He'll come across when the ice breaks," Piet said without looking up. "Men like that always cross themselves."

"Then we have until thaw," Maren said.

"We have less than that." Ines heard his own voice go quiet and precise, the drafting-room voice. "His letters are measurements. He writes something plausible and watches what I confirm. He named you in a postscript, Maren — your ledger, this house. It was a guess dressed as knowledge, but if I flinch wrong in the next reply, the guess becomes a certainty. He's mapping me the way I'd map a riverbank. Soundings. Every letter is a sounding."

Teodor, curled in the window seat with a whittling knife, said, "Then send him soundings back."

Everyone looked at him. The boy shrugged, eleven years old and unimpressed by historians. "You said it yourself. He's mapping you. Maps can be forged. You're a mapmaker."

Ines stared at him. Somewhere in the walls the fire ticked, and outside the wind came down off the orchard ridge carrying the smell of frozen apple wood, and Ines felt the strange double motion he was beginning to recognize as this winter's permanent condition: terror and clarity arriving together, holding hands.

"Yes," he said slowly. "They can."

* * *

He worked on the third letter for two nights, and this time he did not burn the drafts. He studied them.

The problem was elegant, almost beautiful, the way a difficult survey is beautiful: he needed to feed Lyle a landscape that was false in every particular and true in every texture. A hiding place that did not exist, described with the loving accuracy of a man describing something real. And buried in it, one deliberate flaw — a flaw Lyle would find, because Lyle found everything, and would pursue, because men like Lyle pursued what they believed they had discovered. A false spring. Let the historian spend February digging toward an index that was never anywhere.

He gave Lyle the guildhouse. In the letter, a tired, lonely apprentice confessed — with shame, with the stammer of a man betraying a dead mentor's privacy — that he had kept back one volume, the master index, unable to part with the last of Vane's hand. That it lay wrapped in oilcloth in the old plan chest in the workshop's north corner, beneath the drawer of obsolete coastal charts. That he had told no one. That he hardly knew why he was telling Lyle, except that the letter about paper becoming evidence had kept him awake, and perhaps the man was right, and perhaps a burden shared —

Every word engineered. The confession was the bait; the location was the trap; the shame was the varnish that made it gleam. He even misspelled one word, then corrected it heavily, because a truly composed liar never misspells, and Lyle would know that, and Ines wanted Lyle to believe he had seen through the composition to the trembling amateur underneath.

*Forgive me,* the letter ended. *I have done wrong and I know it. Come, when the ice allows, and take it from my hands, and let it be the Commission's honestly. I am tired of being the only one who knows what my master really was.*

He posted it at dawn, and walked home along the frozen towpath with the sun coming up red over the orchard ridge, and caught himself whistling.

Whistling. He stopped in the middle of the path, breath steaming, appalled. Somewhere under the fear, under the grief, under the weight of every name in Maren's ledger, there was a small hot furnace of delight that had woken up in him these past weeks, and it was singing now, because he had just written the finest forgery of his life and mailed it to the most dangerous reader in the province, and some traitorous chamber of his heart had enjoyed every stroke of the pen.

*You're the pen,* Piet would say later, in another life, in a stone room that smelled of straw. But Ines did not know that yet. He knew only that the pen had learned to draw false rivers, and that false rivers, drawn well enough, would drown a historian — and that he hated how good it felt, and that he would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as the ice held.

Behind him, far off across the white valley, he heard it before he understood it: a crack, deep and rolling, like a door opening in the floor of the world. The ice, beginning to think about spring.

He stood listening until the sound died, and then he walked faster.

Because if the ice was thinking, so was the steward — and the steward knew exactly one true thing about the atlas, and true things, in Vellamont that winter, had begun to change hands like currency.
