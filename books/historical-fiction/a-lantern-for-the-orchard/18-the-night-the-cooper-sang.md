## 18. The Night the Cooper Sang

The cold came down off the hills on the last night of the old year and settled over Vellamont like a lid. Ines felt it first in his hands, which had gone stiff copying survey notations by candlelight in the room above the cooper's shop, and then he heard it in the river — a low grinding under the ice, the sound of the water being told to wait.

He came down the stairs because Maren had told him to. That was how it worked now. She said *come down*, and his feet went before his judgment could object.

"You'll sit where there's light," she said, meeting him at the foot of the stair with two lanterns, one for each hand. "You hunch like a scholar. Sit among people and you'll learn to straighten."

The cooper's workshop had been cleared for the occasion. The long bench was pushed against the wall, and planks had been laid across trestles to make tables that sagged in the middle under the weight of bread, pickled pears, a wheel of cheese so old it had gone translucent at the edges, and a crock of small beer that Odell the cooper guarded with one broad hand whenever anyone reached past him. The lanterns were orchard lanterns — squat glass things with wire handles, made for hanging in trees during picking season so the night crews could see their work. There must have been twenty of them, hung from every beam and hook, and the light they threw was warm and gold and slightly uneven, so that everything in the room seemed lit from within rather than above.

Ines counted them without meaning to. It was a habit of his trade: count, measure, record. Twenty-three lanterns. He wondered if Maren knew the number exactly, and decided she did.

"Sit," said Piet, kicking a stool toward him. Piet was the youngest of them after Teodor, a stone-breaker with forearms like mooring rope and a laugh that arrived before his opinions did. "You've been up there scratching all day. Paper can wait. Paper always waits."

"That's the trouble with paper," Ines said, and sat.

* * *

He had been three weeks in the room above the cooper's, and he understood by now that he had been admitted to something without ever being formally let in. The household took its meals together when work allowed. They divided the firewood. They argued about money in front of him as though he weren't there, which was either trust or indifference, and he still couldn't tell which. But tonight was different. Tonight had a shape to it. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming — the room arranging itself around some center of gravity he hadn't been shown.

Maren stood at the head of the table and waited until the noise died down. She didn't raise her voice. She never had to.

"It's the last night of the year," she said. "So we do what we do. You know the order. Oldest first."

"Odell's older than the bridge," Piet said.

"Odell is older than sin," said Odell placidly, "and I'll go last, because it takes me a while."

There was laughter, and then a silence that fell over the table not like an ending but like a held breath. Ines set down his cup. He had the sudden, precise feeling of a man standing at the edge of a map whose margins say *here be dragons* — except the dragons, he suspected, were griefs, and everyone in this room had walked among them so long they'd stopped noticing the smell of smoke.

Halda went first. She was the oldest woman present, a widow who had spent forty years thinning fruit on the Vellamore slopes until her fingers had grown permanently crooked around an absence shaped like a pruning knife. She stood, because apparently this part required standing.

"I was born at Cold Ash Farm, third house past the mill race," she said, in a voice like a door opening onto a room no one had entered in years. "Born in the apple harvest, October, and my mother swore the whole valley smelled of windfalls for a week after. My father was a grafter — trees, not coins — and he taught me that you can tell an orchard's age by the twist of the lowest branch. I lost my mother to the fever in '31, my father to the cough two winters after, and my husband Josiah to the flood at Marlow Weir, which took him and four other men and never gave back so much as a boot." She paused. Her crooked fingers lay flat on the table. "I keep all of it. Every name. That's why we do this. So nobody gets to say it never happened."

"Nobody gets to say," the table answered, low and together, and Ines realized they had said it many times, that these words had been worn smooth as river stones from handling.

Sef went next — Sef the drover, who had walked livestock along roads that no longer legally existed. He gave his village, his birth month, the name of a sister drowned young, the name of a dog he still missed more, he admitted, than the sister, which got him a thrown crust and a roar of laughter. Then Wren, a laundress with a burn-scarred wrist, who spoke quietly of a mother buried in a churchyard the parish register had recently stopped listing, because the parish itself was scheduled to be unmade. Then Piet, who had been born in the road outside a tollhouse during a snowstorm and liked to say he'd been paying the toll ever since. His losses were a brother transported for poaching, a father worn down to nothing in the drainage gangs.

"He's alive somewhere," Piet said, of the brother, and his voice cracked only slightly on *somewhere*. "I keep it anyway. Alive counts double."

Teodor went last of the children, because he insisted on going with the adults, and Maren allowed it. He stood very straight. He was eleven, and he had the gravity of a boy who has learned early that memory is work.

"I was born in the orchard row behind Maren's house," he said. "Not in a bed. In the row. Because the midwife couldn't come across the ice and Maren said the trees would know what to do." He looked at Maren. "My mother's name was Lena. My father I don't know. I lost my mother when I was four, to the marsh fever. Maren says I didn't lose her, she was taken, and losing implies carelessness." He sat down. The table was silent a moment, and then Halda reached over and squeezed his hand, and the silence broke gently.

Then Maren herself. She stood the way she did everything — without flourish.

"Maren Ostrova, born at High Row Orchard, in the blossom, April, which my grandmother called lucky and my grandfather called a nuisance because it meant the cider was late." A ripple of smiles. "I've kept the orchard nineteen years since. I lost my grandparents to age, which is the only honest way to lose anyone. I lost the east rows to blight. I lost—" and here she stopped, and Ines saw her jaw set, saw her choose her words like stones for a wall, "—I lost thirty-one families to paper. To lines drawn by men who never smelled blossom. Their names are in my ledger. All of them. And I will read every one aloud the day someone tries to tell me those villages were empty."

"Nobody gets to say," the table answered.

And then, into the settling quiet, Piet turned and looked at Ines with the frank, unembarrassed directness that was his signature.

"Your turn, mapman."

* * *

Ines had known this moment was coming. He had felt it circling him for weeks the way gulls circle a boat. Three times before, the question had come — *where are you from, Halloran, who did you lose* — and three times he had deflected it with the small, smooth evasions of a man trained to be furniture. He was good at it. Being invisible was the first thing the guild had ever taught him: an apprentice sees everything and is seen by no one, because a mapmaker who becomes a subject stops being able to draw the territory.

But tonight the lanterns were burning, and Halda's crooked hands were folded, and Teodor was looking at him with the terrible patience of a child who has already decided what you are and is merely waiting for you to confirm it.

"The order's oldest first," Ines said. "I'm the youngest here."

"Order of arrival, then," Maren said mildly. "You're last. Which means you're next."

He looked down at his own hands on the table — a draftsman's hands, ink-stained at the nails, precise, careful, hands that had spent years learning to represent the world without touching it.

"Ines Halloran," he heard himself say, and his voice sounded strange in his own ears, unused to this register. "I was born in the foundling ward of Saint Ambrose's, in the capital. No village. No orchard. No family name that anyone recorded — Halloran was given to me off a list, alphabetically, I suspect, because I was born in autumn." Someone made a soft sound of protest at that, but he raised a hand. "No. Let me finish. I want to say it properly, because you all have, and I owe you the truth if I owe you anything.

"I don't know who my mother was. I used to construct her, when I was small — every apprentice does, I think. I gave her a face, a trade, a reason. The brothers at Saint Ambrose's discouraged this. They said imagination was a form of ingratitude. So I learned to be exact instead. Exactness doesn't hurt. You can't lose a coordinate." He paused. "I lost the only person I ever had, though. Aldous Vane. Six weeks ago. He wasn't kind to me — kindness wasn't in his inventory — but he chose me out of a line of thirty boys, and he taught me that a map is a promise you make to people who aren't born yet. And then he died and left me holding that promise, and I've been trying to decide whether I'm strong enough to keep it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere overhead a beam ticked, contracting in the cold.

"And what I lost before him," Ines said, "was everything I didn't know I'd lost. A place. People who'd say my name like it meant something. I didn't miss it because I'd never had it, the way you don't miss a limb you were born without. But—" and here his voice did something he hadn't authorized, roughened, and he pressed on through it, "—but sitting at this table, listening to Halda and Piet and Teodor, I understand that I've been drawing maps of places like yours my whole life and I never once understood what I was drawing. Lines and boundaries and acreages. Not once did I ask myself that a boundary is a promise too, and somebody's whole life stands on it."

He stopped. He had meant to stop there. But the lanterns were burning, and Maren was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse — not pity, not warmth exactly, something more rigorous than either — and the truth came up out of him like groundwater finding a crack.

"There's something else. Something I should have said weeks ago." His heart began to hammer. "Vane left me papers. More than papers. An atlas — unfinished — and it isn't the atlas the Crown thinks it is. It records your villages. Your true boundaries. The old deeds' metes and bounds, copied fair, witnessed, dated. Everything the new survey contradicts. I've been hiding it since the funeral. I lied to the guild steward. I lied—" he looked directly at Maren — "to you. When you asked what I carried, I told you it was practice sketches. That was a lie. I'm sorry for it. I'm telling you now because you just gave me your dead, all of you, freely, and I won't sit under your roof holding back the one thing that might matter."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

It was Halda who spoke first. "The Vane atlas. The one the crier announced. Crown property, surrender by spring." Her voice was flat with arithmetic. "Boy, do you know what they do to men who keep Crown property?"

"Yes," Ines said. "I've done the sums. I do them every morning."

Piet started to grin — Piet grinned at danger the way other men grinned at jokes — but Maren lifted one finger and the grin died unborn. She rose from her chair. She crossed the floor slowly, took one of the orchard lanterns down from its hook, and set it on the table directly in front of Ines, so that the light stood between them like a small sun.

"Nineteen years I've kept accounts," she said. "Names, harvests, debts owed and paid. You know why? Because paper is the only weapon they gave us that they can't take back once it's written. They can burn a deed. They can't burn the fact that the deed existed, if enough people remember it. That's all a ledger is. Memory with witnesses." She looked at him for a long moment. "And now you're telling me a mapmaker hid the truest ledger in Vellamont under my roof for a month and said nothing."

"Yes."

"Why tell us now?"

"Because tonight I finally understood what I was carrying," Ines said. "I thought it was paper. It isn't. It's you. All of you, every name in your ledger, every tree Teodor knows by name. It was never mine to hide. I was just the hand it happened to fall into."

Maren held his gaze. Around the table, nobody moved. Even the beer crock seemed to hold its breath under Odell's hand.

Then she nodded, once, the same economical nod she used for agreeing to terms.

"All right," she said. "You eat with us, you grieve with us, you lie under our roof — and now you fight with us. That's the order of things here. Nobody carries alone. That's the whole rule. Everything else is commentary." She picked the lantern back up. "Tomorrow you'll show me the atlas. Tonight—" and her voice changed, warmed, became almost ordinary again, and the relief in the room was like a fire catching, "—tonight we sing, because it's the last night of the year and grief keeps better after singing. Odell."

Odell rose, wiped his mouth, planted his feet like a man preparing to move a piano, and sang.

* * *

His voice was astonishing. That was the thing nobody warned newcomers about — that the cooper, a vast deliberate man who moved through the world like a barge, opened his mouth and produced a tenor so clear and unhurried it seemed to come from somewhere else entirely. He sang the old orchard song, the one about the blossom moon and the girl in the east row, and Halda joined on the second verse, and then Wren, and then Piet, who sang badly and enormously, and then everyone, even Teodor, even Sef, voices overlapping and correcting each other, verses surfacing that Ines suspected existed nowhere on earth except inside that room.

They sang the planting song, sung in rounds, each voice entering a bar after the last, so that the melody braided itself. They sang the mourning song, which had no words anyone would translate for him, only syllables, and which reduced Halda to wiping her eyes with her sleeve while smiling. They sang a drinking song so ancient and filthy that Maren covered Teodor's ears for exactly none of it, on the principle, she said, that a boy who thins apples ought to know what the pickers sing while doing it.

Ines sang too. Badly. He knew perhaps half the words and invented the rest, and nobody corrected him, and somewhere in the third song he realized his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time in six weeks. He was aware, distantly, of the draftsman's habit rising in him — *record this, note the ritual, catalogue* — and he put it down deliberately, like setting a heavy tool aside. Some things were not for measuring. Some things were only for being inside of.

Later, much later, when the beer crock was empty and Halda had fallen asleep sitting up and been eased onto a pallet by the stove, Teodor appeared at Ines's elbow. The boy was sleepy-eyed but grave.

"You told the true story," he said. It wasn't praise. It was an assessment, delivered like a verdict.

"I did."

"Piet called you a coward before. At supper, in November." Teodor said it without malice, simply placing the fact beside its successor. "You're not a coward anymore?"

Ines considered it honestly, because the boy deserved honesty. "I'm frightened," he said. "That's different. A coward hides and stays hidden. I hid, and then I came down the stairs when Maren told me to. Ask me again in spring."

Teodor studied him with those clear orchard-keeper's eyes, weighing the answer against whatever private scale he kept, and then nodded — Maren's nod, inherited, economical — and went off to sleep.

Ines stayed at the table a while longer. The lanterns had burned low; the cooper snored like tide against a hull; the last song still hung in the rafters the way smoke hangs after a fire has gone to embers. *Belonging*, he thought. So this was what it cost. Not entrance fees, not tests. Just this: that everything he loved now had a face and a crooked-fingered handshake and a boy who asked hard questions, and every one of them could be taken from him by a line drawn on a map in a distant office. He had spent his whole life ensuring he had nothing to lose, and in a single winter, over bread and bad singing, he had acquired everything.

He blew out the lantern in front of his seat and climbed the stairs to his cold room, lighter and heavier than he had ever been.

There was a letter on his desk.

He had locked the door. He had locked the door every night since the steward's visit, and the latch was undisturbed, and the window was painted shut with frost — but there it lay, square and white on the boards, smelling faintly of sealing wax, addressed in a hand so elegant it looked typeset:

*Mister Ines Halloran, late apprentice to Aldous Vane, cartographer.*

He stood in the doorway a long time, the party's warmth still on his skin, and did not touch it.

The wax bore a seal he didn't recognize — a quill crossing a compass rose. And beneath his name, in that beautiful, patient script, three words that were somehow worse than any threat:

*I write kindly.*
