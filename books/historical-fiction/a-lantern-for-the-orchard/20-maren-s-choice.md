## 20. Maren's Choice

Maren had learned to read weather in the orchard — not the sky, which lied, but the trees, which didn't. A cherry could tell you three days out what a cloud would only admit to at the last hour. So when she woke before dawn and heard the floorboards of the cooper's house speaking in the voice of too many feet, she knew before she reached the stair that something had shifted in the household, the way a shift comes into an orchard before a hard frost: nothing you can point to, everything you can feel.

She stood on the landing with her shawl around her shoulders and counted. Piet was down first, as always, feeding the stove so the rest would wake warm. Teodor slept on the trundle under the eaves, one arm hanging off the edge, dead to the world the way only boys and drunks are permitted to be. The two Halvard sisters had the back room. Old Sef had the cot by the hearth that he refused to call his own even after four winters of it being exactly that. And Ines Halloran, mapmaker's boy, guild apprentice, keeper of whatever it was he kept folded against his chest like a second heartbeat — Ines had the narrow room above the stairs, and its light was already burning.

She watched the line of it under his door. Yellow, steady, unblinking. He'd been up all night again. She knew because she'd been up too, with her ledger open on her knees, listening to the scratch of his pen through the wall like a mouse in the wainscot, except no mouse worked that long or that carefully.

The ledger sat heavy on her lap now, right where her hands had gone to hold it without asking permission from the rest of her. She'd been keeping accounts since she was nine years old, since her father put the book in her hands and said, *An orchard dies twice, girl. Once when the trees go, once when nobody remembers who planted them.* The first death took her father's trees eleven years ago, when the blight came. The second death she had been fighting ever since, name by name, debt by debt, harvest by harvest, in a hand that had grown cramped and certain over twenty years.

She opened it now, out of habit more than need, and let it fall open where it always fell: the page of the Vane survey.

Not Aldous Vane the man — she'd met him once, years ago, a dry stick of a man who measured her father's boundary stones and never once looked up at the trees while he did it. She'd disliked him for that, and then understood him later, the way you understand a man who keeps his eyes down because looking up would break him. No, this page was about the other thing. The page where, three weeks ago, she had written in her own hand: *Ines H. carries papers. Does not say what. Lies badly. Stays anyway.*

*Stays anyway.* That was the whole of the man, written plain. Everything else was detail.

Below it, newer, in fresher ink: *Gave me the western sheets. Confessed. Wept a little, then apologized for weeping.*

And below that, in the space she'd left without knowing why she left it, blank. Waiting. Because Maren had known since the night he handed her those pages wrapped in oilcloth, since the night his voice shook and he said *I'm sorry, I should have told you sooner,* that this space was waiting for her answer. Not his confession. Her choice.

The question had been walking toward her for days, patient as winter. Now it stood in her doorway.

* * *

It came to a head at breakfast, the way questions do in a full house — they wait until everyone is holding bread, and then they arrive.

Piet set the porridge down and didn't sit. That was the first sign; Piet always sat. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, a cooper by trade who could turn a barrel watertight in twenty minutes and had turned forty of them in the past month, more than any order required, "for storage," he said, and nobody asked storage of what because everybody knew and nobody said. He stood there wiping his big hands on his apron and looking at Ines the way you look at a nail that's started to squeak in a floorboard you have to walk over every night.

"The steward's man came round yesterday," Piet said. "While you were at the chapel."

Ines's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. "What man?"

"Didn't give a name. Grey coat, guild buttons, polite as Sunday. Asked if a young gentleman lodged here. Asked if we'd seen any documents." Piet let the word sit on the table between them. *Documents.* "I told him we were laborers and couldn't read."

"Which is a lie," said Greta Halvard, the elder sister, without looking up from mending.

"Which is a lie," Piet agreed comfortably. "But it's a lie I chose. That's the difference, see. I'd like the rest of us to get to choose ours too."

"Ines—" the younger sister, Marta, began.

"I know," Ines said. His face had gone the color of the porridge. "You don't have to—"

"I do have to." Maren hadn't planned to speak yet. She spoke anyway, because that was how her father had run an orchard crew and how she ran this house: the person whose account it is speaks first. Everyone turned. She set down her spoon. "This is my roof. My name's on the lease, such as it is, and my name will be on whatever comes through it. So I'll say it plainly, once, and then we'll decide like people instead of circling like crows."

She looked at Ines. He looked back, and she saw what she'd seen the night he confessed: a young man braced for dismissal. Trained for it. An orphan learns to pack his bag in his head before anyone asks him to. It made her want to shake him and feed him in equal measure.

"You've got papers in this house," she said. "Crown property, if you believe the Crown. The commission's coming across that bridge the day the ice goes. When it does, they won't ask politely twice. They'll come with soldiers and a list, and every soul sleeping under this roof becomes guilty of the same crime — theft from the Crown, conspiracy, whatever word Corvus Lyle likes best that week. You know it. I know it. Piet knows it, and Piet doesn't read."

"I read numbers," Piet said.

"You read barrels." She turned back to Ines. "So here's the choice, and I'll thank you to hear all of it before you start apologizing. Choice one: you take your atlas and you leave tonight. There's drovers going south through the mill road when the ice holds. They owe me. You'd be in Vellamont's rearview by morning, and this house goes back to being what it looks like — a widow woman, a cooper, some displaced hands, and a boy. Nobody worth a historian's ink."

The room went very quiet. The stove ticked. Out the frost-rimed window, the orchard stood black against the grey snow-light, row after row of bare trees that had fed this valley for longer than the Crown had bothered counting it.

"And choice two?" Marta asked softly.

Maren picked up her ledger. Held it up where they could all see it — the swollen, grease-stained, twenty-year book with its ribbon markers and its tea-blotted pages.

"Choice two is that this house becomes what it already is," she said. "A record. The Crown thinks memory lives in deeds and parish books, so they burn deeds and buy priests. But memory lives in kitchens. It lives in ledgers and in songs and in the heads of people who watched their fathers graft these trees. Lyle can't arrest a kitchen. He can try." She set the book down flat, and her palm stayed on it. "If you stay, Ines, you stay as one of us. Not a lodger. Not a guest we're harboring out of charity. One of us. Which means if they come for the papers, they come for the household, and the household stands together or not at all. I won't have it any other way. Half-measures are how families die."

"Maren." Ines's voice cracked on the second syllable. "They took Piet's brother off to the lockup over a barrel of nails. What do you think they'd do to a house hiding the atlas?"

"They'd do worse," Maren said. "I've done the arithmetic. I keep accounts, remember. I know exactly what this costs." She looked down the table, at each of them in turn. "But I also know what it cost my father to hand over his deed in '41 and watch them re-survey his lines smaller every year until his own orchard wasn't his. He obeyed every rule they wrote. He died landless and law-abiding, and the only reason I can tell you his name is because I wrote it down. Obedience bought him nothing but a quiet grave. I'm not buying it for this house."

Sef cleared his throat from the hearth cot. He was the oldest of them, a stone-fence builder with hands like tree roots, and he spoke maybe ten sentences a week and made each one count like a fence post.

"My village went under the reservoir at Kelder's Ford," he said. "Thirty years back. Whole valley. They gave us paper and a train ticket and called it fair compensation." He looked at Ines. "You know what survived? Nothing official. Every document burned proper, every map redrawn. What survives is that my mother taught me forty field names and I've taught them to nobody, because there's been nobody to teach. Forty names walking around in an old man's head, going in the ground with me." He tapped his own chest. "That's what erasure looks like up close, boy. It isn't fire. Fire is quick. Erasure is just everyone agreeing to forget, one funeral at a time."

Nobody spoke after that for a while. Maren let it stand, because some arguments are best finished by silence.

Then Greta Halvard, who had not stopped mending through any of it, bit her thread and said, "I'm staying. Obviously. My sister and I fled Harrow Bend with one trunk between us, and half of what's in that trunk is papers the magistrates said didn't exist. I've been conspiring since I was sixteen. I'd hate to stop now; I've gotten good at it."

"I'm staying," said Marta.

"I'm staying," said Piet, "and I want it noted that I hid the sheets in the false-bottomed barrels three days ago without asking anyone, because I knew this meeting was coming and I'm faster than all of you." He grinned at Ines's expression. "Barrels marked 'brine.' Nobody searches brine in February. Smells too bad."

Ines looked around the table like a man reading a map of country he'd thought was empty. Maren watched him do it — watched him find, one by one, the marks on the terrain that meant he was not alone on it. His eyes were wet. He blinked it away, badly, the way he did everything that involved feelings.

"You understand," he said, and his voice was low and rough, "that if this goes wrong, it's my fault. Whatever happens to any of you—"

"No," Maren said.

He stopped.

"It isn't your fault, and I'll tell you why, and then we're done with this particular conversation forever." She leaned forward. "The moment Aldous Vane drew true lines on those maps instead of convenient ones, this stopped being your burden and became everyone's who chooses to carry it. You didn't invent the flood. You didn't forge the survey. You inherited a truth, same as I inherited my father's ledger, same as Sef inherited forty field names. Truths don't belong to the people carrying them. They belong to the places they're true about. Those orchards out that window — those trees own those pages more than the Crown owns them, more than you own them, more than I do. We're not protecting your secret, boy. We're standing up for our own ground. You just happen to be holding the deed."

Ines put his head down over his bowl, briefly, the way men do when feeling has gotten ahead of their manners. When he lifted it, he said, "Then I stay. If you'll have me."

"We had you before you asked," Maren said. "Now eat your porridge. Conspiracies work better on full stomachs."

* * *

After breakfast, while the others went out — Piet to the shop, the sisters to the laundry work that kept them fed, Sef to mend the woodpile — Maren took Ines into the back parlor, the cold little room where she did her real accounting, and shut the door.

"Two things," she said. "First: from today, nothing important stays in this house overnight. Barrels, cellar caches, Father Anselm's strongbox — the pages move, always moving, like coin among peddlers. A thing that sleeps in one place gets found. Second thing." She pulled her ledger onto the table and opened it to his page, the one with the blank space she'd been keeping. "I write down what matters. It's how I pray, if I pray, which I don't. So I'm going to ask you straight, and I want the straight answer, because it goes in the book and the book doesn't take revisions."

She dipped her pen. Looked at him.

"Why? Vane told you to be safe. Anonymous. Invisible. You could have burned the thing and been a guild man with a pension by thirty. Why carry it?"

Ines was quiet long enough that the pen began to blot. When he answered, he answered slowly, testing each plank before stepping on it.

"Because he spent his whole life drawing lies for pay, and then drew one true thing for free, and it killed him alone in a cold room with nobody to show it to." His jaw tightened. "Because I catalogued his effects and found a letter to me inside the atlas cover, and the letter said *finish it*, and I have been trying to figure out whether finishing it means preserving myself or preserving the maps, and I think—" he exhaled, "—I think I finally know which. And because of Teodor. Because that boy knows every tree by name, forty names, sixty names, a hundred, and somebody has to make sure those names outlive the water."

Maren nodded once and wrote. *Chose to stand. Reasons: the master's letter, the boy's trees, his own spine — arrived late but arrived.*

"That last part's private," she said, closing the book.

"Which part?"

"The spine." She almost smiled. "Go on. Piet wants you in the shop. Apparently there are barrels to move."

* * *

That evening she stood at the kitchen window washing dishes and watching the dark come down over the orchard. Behind her the household moved through its small rituals — Marta teaching Teodor his letters by the fire, Greta humming something from Harrow Bend, Piet's laugh rolling up from somewhere below like a keg settling. Ines sat in the corner copying, as he did every night now, and when Teodor stumbled over a word, it was Ines who leaned over and sounded it out with him, patient as rain.

Maren dried her hands and opened the ledger one last time. Below the entry she'd written that morning, below the blank space that wasn't blank anymore, she added a final line:

*Household stands. Seven names, one record. Let the Crown count what it likes — we count differently.*

Then, because she was who she was, she blew out the lamp, hung the lantern in the window facing the orchard — the old signal, *someone's home, someone remembers* — and went to sleep knowing that tomorrow the real work would begin: deciding who carried what, and how far, and through whose hands the truth would travel when the ice went out and the water came for the trees.

Across the river, unseen in the dark, a light was burning in the commission's camp. Corvus Lyle was writing letters.
