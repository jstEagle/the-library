## 21. The Second Split

The night they divided the atlas, the cooper's shop smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil, and nobody spoke above a whisper.

Ines had laid everything out on the long bench where Piet normally bent barrel staves: the folios of Vane's atlas, sorted into three stacks; the cipher pages folded flat between oiled cloth; the land-title proofs — Vane's tracings of the original parish deeds, cross-referenced against Father Anselm's hidden strongbox — bound in a leather wrapper that had once held a hymnal. He had spent three days preparing for this. Three days of measuring, cutting, sewing, deciding. And still, when he looked down at the whole atlas spread out like a body on a table, his hands shook.

Maren noticed. She always noticed. She set a cup of barley tea at his elbow without comment and stood back, arms folded, waiting.

"It's not grief," Ines said, because he knew she would think it was. "Or it's not only grief."

"What is it, then?"

He turned the master index over in his fingers. It was a single sheet, sewn into the spine of nothing now — he had cut it free that afternoon — listing every plate, every cipher key, every proof, and where each had been assigned to go. A map of the map's own dispersal. Vane's final instruction, decoded from the margins two weeks ago, had been explicit on this point and strangely tender about it: *No one hand shall hold the whole. One hand is a hostage. Many hands are a harvest.*

"I spent six years learning that a map is a promise," Ines said. "You draw a boundary, you're telling everyone who comes after that this line means something. That it will hold." He looked up at her. "Tonight I'm breaking one map into forty pieces and asking people I love to carry them through a frozen country past a man who burns records for a living. That's not how promises are supposed to work."

"No," Maren agreed. "It's how seeds work."

* * *

The cooper was Bram Ostler, a broad quiet man whose forearms were mapped with old burn scars from the cooper's fire, and who had taken in displaced laborers for eleven years without once asking the guild's permission or forgiveness. When Ines called him to the bench, Bram came wiping his hands on his apron, and when he saw what lay there, he went very still.

"You're giving me maps," Bram said.

"Nine plates. The western orchards — Coldharbour Row, the Fenwick terraces, the mill boundaries. The originals, not copies. Copies can be dismissed as apprentices' work. These have Vane's hand, his seal, his survey marks. They're irreplaceable."

Bram looked at the plates for a long moment. "Where do they go?"

"Inside barrels. You're shipping staves downriver to your brother in Marchenne as soon as the ice breaks — you told Piet so last week, thinking no one listened. Hollowed staves, wax-sealed ends. No one searches a cooper's freight twice."

"And if they search it once?"

Ines didn't answer that, because there was nothing true to say. Bram nodded slowly, as if Ines's silence were itself an honest answer, and began rolling the plates with a gentleness that seemed impossible in hands that size.

"My father drowned in the first flood," Bram said. "Before any of this canal talk. The river took him, and there wasn't a paper anywhere that said his name or where he'd lived. My mother spent her whole life angry at paper." He tied the first bundle. "I'd like to be the thing paper does right, for once."

* * *

Father Anselm received the cipher keys in the chapel cellar, among the wine racks and the strongbox, by the light of a single candle stub that he clearly rationed the way other men rationed money.

He was an old man made almost entirely of angles — elbows, knuckles, a jaw like a roof beam — and he took the folded pages and read them without surprise, though his breath caught once, at something in Vane's marginal hand that Ines could not see from where he stood.

"He wrote to me," Anselm said at last. "Near the end. I thought it was an old man's fancy — a letter full of numbers and nonsense, asking if my memory of the Latin was good enough for 'a small act of translation.' I never answered. I was afraid of what translation might cost." He looked up, and his eyes in the candlelight were dry and terrible. "I hid these same kinds of papers thirty years ago, boy. When the toll commissioners came for the ferry lands. I put the deeds under the altar stone and I let them take my post rather than tell them where. Do you know what happened?"

"The parish was reorganized," Ines said. "You were reassigned here. To the smallest parish in the diocese."

"To the smallest parish in the diocese," Anselm agreed. "Which is to say, to exactly the place where nobody important would ever look again. They punished me with irrelevance, and irrelevance has been the best hiding place I ever owned." He squared the cipher pages against the wine rack, precise as a man squaring a coffin lid. "Vane knew that too. That's why the keys come to me. He understood that a disgraced priest is more secure than an honored one. Shame is a fortress."

"Will you keep them here? In the box?"

Anselm smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "I will memorize them, and then I will burn them. The box holds deeds. Deeds can be found by anyone who tears up a floor. But a dead language in an old man's head—" he tapped his temple with one crooked finger "—they cannot raid this. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. And when the time comes, I will write them out again, and my handwriting will be different from Vane's, and that will make them *more* credible, not less. Independent testimony. Your master planned for my pride, Ines. He knew I could not resist being the one who remembers correctly."

* * *

The land-title proofs went to Maren, and that transfer was the hardest, because Maren refused ceremony.

She came to the bench last, after Bram had gone home heavy with his secret and after word had been sent quietly to the chapel. The household had been fed and settled — Teodor asleep, or pretending to be, upstairs; Piet whittling something in the corner with elaborate unconcern; the Widow Sarei mending by the fire with her ears visibly wide open. Maren stood over the leather wrapper and did not touch it.

"Why me?" she said. "Not why me morally. Why me practically. If Lyle takes the shop, he takes me with it. I'm the name on the door. I'm the one they'll search first."

"Because you're the one they'll search first," Ines said. "And find nothing. Because you won't keep them in the shop. You'll keep them—" and he hesitated, because the plan was hers, really, had been hers since she first showed him her ledger, "—in the orchard."

"In the trees."

"In the trees. Wrapped in wax cloth, in the hollows you showed me — the ones the frost split open on the south side of the oldest rows. A living archive. Nobody cuts down an orchard in winter, and by spring the copies are gone and the originals can move again. And because—" he made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness, "—because those proofs are proofs of *your* neighbors' land. Of the Fenwicks' rows and the Coldharbour tenants and the mill families. You keep their debts in your ledger. This is the other side of the same page."

Maren finally picked up the wrapper. Weighed it in her hand the way she weighed anything — honestly, without flinching at the number.

"My mother taught me letters so I could read the harvest contracts," she said. "First thing I ever understood about writing: whoever holds the paper owns the story of what you did all year. The landlords held the paper. So I started keeping my own." She looked around the room — at Piet's knife, at Sarei's needle, at the ceiling where Teodor was definitely awake. "This room keeps its own accounts. Has for years. Now it keeps the Crown's worst ones too." She tucked the wrapper inside her coat, against her body, where a mother carries anything that matters. "Go to bed, Ines. You look like a man who's been holding his breath since the funeral."

He had. He hadn't known it until she named it.

* * *

What remained for Ines was almost nothing, and that was the whole design.

The master index — one sheet, folded to the size of a prayer card, sewn into the lining of his coat between the wool and the canvas, low on the left side where a knife would go if he carried one. It listed everything: which plates with Bram, which keys in Anselm's skull, which proofs in the orchard's hollow trees, which pages already copied and waiting for spring couriers. Anyone who took the coat from him got a list of treasures they could not find. Anyone who destroyed the list destroyed only the finding, not the truth — the truth was already out of his hands, distributed like seed across four hiding places and however many minds.

He sat up alone in his room above the cooper's shop after midnight, pressing his palm flat against his ribs, feeling the small stiff rectangle of paper under the fabric. It should have felt like loss. Six years of Vane's work, and he had personally handed away every piece of it in a single evening.

It felt like breathing.

That was the part he hadn't predicted, lying awake while the shop ticked and settled below him: the relief of it. All winter he had been a man holding a drowning thing underwater by himself, arms burning, terrified that sleep would loosen his grip and the whole truth would bob up to the surface where Lyle's men could net it. Now the thing swam on its own, in many waters, and his arms were empty, and empty arms meant he could do the next task instead of only guarding the last one.

He was no longer the atlas's keeper. He was its courier-in-chief, its quartermaster, its — he smiled at the dark — its index. Vane had chosen well, or fate had, or whatever force arranged such things: an orphan apprentice trained to be invisible, carrying nothing but a table of contents sewn into his coat.

There was a soft knock, and then the door opened without waiting, because this household had stopped pretending to respect closed doors sometime in January. Teodor slipped in, twelve years old and grave as a magistrate, and sat on the end of the bed without being asked.

"I heard most of it," the boy said. "Through the floor."

"Of course you did."

"Bram's taking his barrels downriver. Father Anselm's going to burn things. Maren's putting papers in trees." Teodor recited it flatly, then looked up. "What do I get?"

"Nothing," Ines said. "Nothing yet."

"That's not fair."

"It's completely fair. You're the one person in this house Lyle hasn't bothered to learn the name of. That makes you the most valuable person in this house. When the time comes — and it will come, soon, before the thaw — you'll carry the single most important page there is. But not yet, and not because you're a child. Because right now, your job is to be forgettable. Can you do that?"

Teodor considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, even supper. "I've been forgettable my whole life," he said. "I'm very good at it."

"Then you're the best-qualified member of this household," Ines said, "and I include myself."

The boy grinned, quick and bright, and slid back out the way he'd come. Ines lay back down and pressed his hand to his ribs again, to the little paper heart under the wool.

Below, in the shop, the empty space on the bench where the atlas had lain seemed to glow faintly in the dark, the way a cleared field glows after harvest — bare, but not barren. Everything that mattered had been gathered in. Everything that mattered was already sown.

He slept without dreaming, which for the first time all winter felt like trust rather than exhaustion.

Three days later, a letter arrived at the cooper's shop addressed in a hand Ines knew too well — elegant, unhurried, each letter placed like a chess piece. Corvus Lyle wrote that he had been thinking much about apprenticeship lately, about what masters owe their students and students their masters, and that he hoped Mr. Halloran would honor him with a reply.

The net, Ines thought, pressing his hand once against his ribs, had begun to close in earnest.
