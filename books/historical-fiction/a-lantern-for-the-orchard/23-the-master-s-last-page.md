## 23. The Master's Last Page

The atlas had been divided for eleven days when Ines went looking for what was left.

That was the phrase he used to himself, and it was a lie, and he knew it was a lie. Nothing was *left*. He had distributed Vane's life's work among five pairs of hands with the efficiency of a man unloading a sinking boat — the western sheets sewn into the cooper's spare coat lining, the cipher keys wrapped in oilcloth beneath Father Anselm's altar stone, the title proofs pressed flat between the boards of Maren's ledger box. The master index rode against his own ribs, stitched where his heart should have been, which he had decided was either poetry or vanity depending on how frightened he felt that morning.

But there was one thing he had never accounted for, because until that night he had not known it existed.

He found it because of a mistake in his own copying.

He had been working from memory in the cellar of the chapel — Lyle's letters had made the guildhouse unsafe, and even the room above the cooper's felt watched since the rider had come through — transcribing the eastern watershed sheets from the index references alone, filling gaps from recollection, and something had snagged. A folio number. The index listed Folio 118 as *Riverbend parish bounds, corrected*. But Ines had catalogued every sheet of that atlas twice, first as an apprentice learning order and later as a man learning fear, and there was no 118. The numbering ran 1 through 117 and then stopped, clean as a cliff edge.

Vane had not been careless. Vane had not been careless about anything in thirty years — Ines had spent four of them being corrected for ink weights measured in grains. A gap in the sequence was not an absence. It was a door.

* * *

He waited until the household slept. This had become habit rather than necessity; the cooper's shop above which he lodged held six people now, and none of them were spies, but secrecy had become for Ines less a strategy than a temperament, the way some men are tall. He took the candle down two flights, past Piet's snoring behind the barrel store, into the cold room where the last of the atlas had lived before its dispersal.

The false floorboard came up the way it always did — silently now, because he had shaved it true weeks ago — and he lifted out the empty portfolio, the leather scarred where Vane's brass corners had bitten it for decades.

Empty except that he had never actually emptied it.

He turned it over. Ran his thumb along the spine seam. And there, where the back board met the leather, a ridge that should not have existed on work by a man who planed his own pen handles flat so they wouldn't roll off the desk. He worked his thumbnail into the seam. The stitching there was different thread — newer, finer, the hand of someone sewing in bad light in a hurry or in good light in terror, and the two were indistinguishable in the finished product.

The pocket inside was shallow, no deeper than a letter. It held a single sheet, folded twice, and the paper was heavier than any stock in the atlas. Vane's best paper. The paper he saved for maps that mattered.

Ines sat down on the cold floor before he understood he was sitting.

* * *

The hand was Vane's, but early-Vane, the strong slanted script from the oldest ledgers, before the years had pared it down to that dry vertical line Ines knew better than his own. Dated at the top: a year forty-one years gone. Ines did the arithmetic and his stomach went cold. Forty-one years ago, Aldous Vane had been twenty-three years old. Newly licensed. Newly employed.

*To whoever holds this,* the page began, and then crossed out — a single hard stroke, the kind of strike that tears paper if you mean it — *to my apprentice, who will be the only one.*

*I am told by men whose business is telling men things that a confession is worthless unless it can be used against you. Very well. Here is the thing I can be used against.*

*In the spring of my first commission I surveyed the Fenmarsh holdings for the drainage act. You will find nothing of this in my later work because I removed it, all of it, every sheet, and burned the field notes in the yard of my own house while my landlady watched me over the fence and said nothing, which is the mercy strangers do for us when we are burning our own sins in public.*

*I falsified the bounds. Not greatly. That is the whole horror of it — not greatly. Eleven acres here, a common right there, adjusted so that the drainage commissioners' scheme would stand on paper as it could not stand on the ground. I told myself the marsh was dying anyway. I told myself the landowners were absentees who would never walk their own acres. I told myself that a young surveyor does not refuse a commission; he completes it, and rises, and one day has standing enough to refuse the next one.*

*The next ones came. I refused them. Do you see the arithmetic? One lie purchased a career, and the career purchased thirty years of refusals. I have turned away nine schemes in my time. Nine. I have counted them at night the way other men count sheep, and each one was bought with Fenmarsh, and each purchase was real, and each refusal was real, and neither fact forgives the other.*

*The woman who kept the fen orchards — her name was Sable Wren, and she walked eleven miles to the assizes to contest the bounds, carrying her deed, and her deed matched mine, and mine was the false one, and she lost. She lost her trees. I stood at the back of the courtroom in my new guild coat and heard the commissioner read my figures aloud as though they were the ground itself, and I remember thinking with perfect clarity: I am the map now. Whatever the ground is, I am what men will believe it is.*

*So I built this atlas. Not as evidence — evidence decays, gets seized, burns. As a seed. An honest map cannot be proven wrong by an honest man; it can only be buried. I have tried to make it unburiable. You will understand what I mean by the time you finish dividing it.*

*Understand this also, and do not soften it in your memory of me: I was not forced. No one held a knife to me. I was ambitious and tired and twenty-three, and the commissioners were kind to me at dinner, and kindness is the most effective instrument ever devised for making a clever man do evil.*

*If you are reading this, then the atlas has done its work, or is doing it, or has failed. All three are bearable. What is not bearable is the fourth possibility: that you have found this page and decided, sensibly, prudently, with the full approval of your own caution, that the risk is too great and the hour too late and your position too small —*

*— because I wrote that decision once myself, in a courtroom, wearing a good coat. It is the same page every time. It always reads: someone braver will come along.*

*No one braver comes along. Only people who decide.*

*— A.V.*

*P.S. The ink on this page is iron-gall, batch of the autumn, mixed by your own hand. If the words survive, some credit is yours already. Spend it well.*

*P.P.S. There is no second copy. There was never a second copy. A second copy is a permission slip to be careful.*

* * *

The candle had burned down three fingers' worth while he read. Ines became aware of the cold only as a numbness in his feet, and of his own face only when a drop fell onto the page and he caught it — caught it, blotted it with his sleeve, heart slamming — before it could blur a single word.

He read it again. Then a third time, slower, the way he'd been taught to read surveys: not for the lines but for what the lines excluded.

Forty-one years. The man whose corrections Ines had endured for four years — whose red ink had once reduced him, aged fifteen, to furious tears over a compass arc drawn two degrees proud — had begun in fraud. Had built everything after it as repayment. Nine schemes refused. Nine. Ines tried to imagine them: Vane in various rooms, various decades, saying *no* to men with power, each refusal costing something Ines would never see the ledger of. And underneath every refusal, the original sin, compounding interest paid out in integrity for four decades, never once reducing the principal.

And the atlas — the atlas Ines had spent a winter dividing like contraband, bleeding over, lying for, nearly dying for — was not heroism. It was penance. It was a coward's monument built by a man who had spent fifty years becoming brave one grain at a time, the way a river cuts stone, and who had known — had *planned* — that the final cut would fall to someone else entirely.

*You will be the only one.*

He had assumed, all these months, that Vane had chosen him for competence. For discretion. For the apprentice's trained invisibility. Now he saw the uglier truth, or the truer one: Vane had chosen him because Ines was exactly what Vane himself had been at twenty-three. Clever, cautious, ambitious in the small ways available to orphans, capable of the sensible decision. Vane had looked at his apprentice and seen his own younger face and thought: *there is the man who will understand precisely how heavy this choice is, because he will have to lift it with the same arms.*

It was not trust. Or it was trust of the cruelest, finest kind — the trust of a man who knows the person he trusts might fail, and gives him the weight anyway, because the weight is the only thing that makes a person.

* * *

He heard the stairs before he heard the step — Maren's tread had a particular cadence, heel-first, unhurried, the walk of a woman who owned every floor she crossed regardless of deed. He had just enough time to fold the page closed when the cellar door opened and her lantern came down ahead of her.

"You weren't at supper," she said.

"I was working."

"I know. You work like a man hiding." She came to stand over him, and her eyes went from his face to the open portfolio to the floorboard still askew, and she did not ask what he had found, because Maren never asked questions whose answers she could wait for. Instead she lowered herself onto the cooper's stool with the economy of a much older woman and said, "Read it to me."

"It's private."

"Everything worth keeping is. Read it anyway."

And Ines, who had lied to her once in this very room and been forgiven without ceremony, found that he could not refuse her this. He unfolded the page and read it aloud by lantern light in a cellar that smelled of oak shavings and tallow, his voice going rough in the middle of the Fenmarsh passage and steadying again at the postscripts, and Maren listened the way she listened to harvest reports — completely, without interruption, filing every word.

When he finished she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Nine schemes."

"So he says."

"He says it like a man counting debts, not trophies." She took the lantern and raised it slightly, studying Ines's face rather than the page. "You're angry with him."

"No."

"You are. Look at your hands."

Ines looked. His fists were white-knuckled around the paper's edges, creasing Vane's best stock, and he made himself loosen them finger by finger. "I'm angry that he didn't tell me," he said finally. "Four years. He taught me to mix that ink. He watched me grind it. He knew — he must have known what was coming, the canal, the commission — and he left me a riddle instead of a warning. A folio number that doesn't exist, like — like a note under a door for a game we're playing. I've been carrying his atlas for months and tonight I learn I was also carrying his guilt, unpaid, and he's not here to explain whether he meant that as inheritance or as punishment."

"And which is it?"

"How should I know?"

"Because you're the only one," Maren said, "who can know. That's what choosing someone means. You don't get the explanation. You get the job." She set the lantern between them. "My mother died owing money to half the valley. I inherited the debts at fourteen. Everyone told me I could repudiate them — orphan girl, no court would pursue it. I paid every farthing over six years. Not because she deserved it. Because *I* did. A debt doesn't care whose name is on it once it's in your hands." She nodded at the page. "That's what he gave you. Not his guilt. His debt."

"He falsified nothing in the atlas," Ines said. "Lyle keeps writing that Vane was an eccentric, that his figures were unreliable — and the whole time, the man couldn't draw a crooked line after Fenmarsh. Thirty years of exactitude because of eleven stolen acres." He laughed, and it came out wrong, closer to the other thing. "Do you know what the steward would give to have this page? Proof that Vane's atlas was written by a confessed forger? Lyle would frame it."

"Then it stays in a floorboard."

"It can't stay anywhere." Ines was surprised to hear his own voice go hard. "Everything else is split and moving. This is the keystone, Maren — don't you see? The testimony, the deeds, the refutation I'm drafting — they all hang on Vane's credibility, and Lyle's whole case is that Vane was unreliable. This page destroys that case. A confessed forger who rebuilt his entire life around accuracy — that's not a witness, that's a *sermon*. But if Lyle takes it first—"

"Then it goes with the rest." She said it simply, and Ines realized she had already decided, had probably decided before she came down the stairs, the way she decided everything: quickly, completely, and without announcing it. "Where?"

"Not with me. If they take me, they take it." He thought, staring into the flame. The cooper's coat was gone westward. Anselm had the keys. "Teodor."

Maren went very still.

"He's twelve," she said.

"He's the boy who knows every tree by name and has never once told a stranger where the grafts are hidden. I've watched him lie to the survey riders with his whole face. He's better at this than I am." Ines heard the words coming out of him and recognized, distantly, that they were Vane's logic wearing his voice — *the weight makes the person* — and hated it and could not find anything wrong with it. "Not the page itself. I'll copy it. He carries the copy somewhere no adult would search, tells no one, not even you, and brings it back only when I send the word 'Fenmarsh.' Can you teach him that word without teaching him why?"

Maren looked at him for a long time. The lantern light moved across her face and showed him, briefly, something he had never seen there — not doubt of the plan, but grief for it, the specific grief of a woman watching another childhood acquire a secret.

"He lost his parents to a flood the Crown called an accident," she said at last. "I swore nothing in my house would ever again depend on a child's silence." She stood. "I'll teach him the word. And Ines — when this is over, whatever over means, you and I are going to have a long conversation about the difference between trusting a boy and spending him."

"Yes," Ines said. Because there was no other answer.

She paused at the stair, lantern raised. "Your master," she said. "The coward part and the martyr part. You'll want to sort them into separate boxes and keep the one you like. Don't. Men are whole or they're nothing. Keep him whole. It's harder, and it's the only way he's any use to you."

Then she was gone, and the dark folded back in around the candle stub, and Ines sat alone on the cold floor with forty-one-year-old guilt in his hands and the sound of cracking ice faint through the foundation stones — the thaw coming, the bridge opening, the clock running down.

He smoothed the page flat against his knee. Then he pulled his copying kit toward him, trimmed a fresh nib, and began to write the word *Fenmarsh* at the top of a clean sheet — carefully, exactly, in the iron-gall batch of the autumn, mixed by his own hand.

He was three lines into the fair copy when the knock came at the street door above. Two blows, official spacing. Then a third.

Nobody in the household knocked like that.

Ines blew out the candle.
