## 24. The Raid

The knock came at the fourth hour of the morning, and it was not a knock so much as an announcement — three blows with something heavier than a fist, delivered by someone who had already decided what was behind the door.

Ines was awake. He had been awake since before dawn, bent over the master index in his coat, checking and rechecking the sequence of the splits against Maren's ledger of routes. He had slept badly all week, and badly in a new way: not with fear but with a strange, humming competence, the feeling of a man who has finally found the right tool for a job and cannot stop testing its edge.

The cooper's shop stood on Tannery Lane, and Tannery Lane woke early. So when the blows fell, Piet — who slept in the loft above the drying racks and came down every morning smelling of oak shavings and woodsmoke — reached the door first.

"Guild business," said a voice through the planks. "Open in the name of the survey commission."

Piet looked up toward the stairs where Ines stood frozen, one hand still inside his coat. Then he did something Ines would think about for years afterward: he smiled, small and crooked, as if to say *well, here it is*, and unbolted the door himself.

They came in like water finding a low place. Four men in commission grey with the Crown's canal badge stitched at the shoulder, and behind them a fifth man Ines recognized from the guildhouse — Rennick, the steward's under-clerk, who had once shown him how to cut a pen nib properly and who now kept his eyes fixed on the floor as if the floor might absolve him.

"By warrant," said the officer, holding up a paper that fluttered in the cold air off the lane. "Recovery of Crown property. Aldous Vane, deceased, late cartographer of this guild, is understood to have held documents belonging to His Majesty's survey." He said it the way a man reads a bill of lading. "You are?"

"Piet Voss. Cooper."

"You're not a guild man, then." The officer's gaze moved over the shop — the shavings, the hoops, the half-made churns — with the particular contempt of someone cataloguing what he intended to ruin. "Then you've no standing here at all. Stand aside."

*No standing.* Ines knew those two words better than he knew his own name. They were the words that had made him an apprentice instead of an orphan, a pair of hands instead of a person, and he felt them now like a hand on the back of his neck. But something else rose under them, hot and unfamiliar, and he heard himself say:

"He has standing. This is his shop. And I am Aldous Vane's heir in all matters of paper, which makes me the party to your warrant — so address yourself to me, or explain to Corvus Lyle why you roughed the wrong man before breakfast."

It was the first lie he'd told in weeks that wasn't about the atlas, and it came out smooth as vellum. The officer hesitated. Warrants were instruments, and instruments disliked improvisation.

"You're Halloran," Rennick said quietly, still not looking up. "The apprentice."

"I'm the keeper of the Vane estate," Ines said. "Which your warrant concerns. So search, gentlemen — but search *him*, not his house."

He hated how good it felt. He hated more that it worked only partly: because they searched anyway.

* * *

They turned the guildhouse first, which was the point of coming to Tannery Lane second — or perhaps they had simply started there and Ines had been told by a breathless boy from the chandlery, who arrived at the cooper's door with frost on his eyelashes and terror in his voice, that the grey coats were already in Vane's old workshop pulling drawers out onto the floor.

Maren arrived minutes later, moving fast for a woman of her years, her ledger nowhere visible but her fury everywhere else. She planted herself in the lane outside the cooperage with her arms crossed and her chin up, and when the officer told her to move along she said, "This is a public road, and I am a free woman standing on it. Move *me* along if you can find the law for it."

She could not be moved. She became, in effect, furniture — a fixture of the scene, unremarkable, ignorable, and therefore perfectly positioned to see everything. It was the oldest trick in her book, Ines realized later. The orchard keepers had spent generations being overlooked.

Inside the shop, the raid proceeded with the thoroughness of men who enjoyed their work. Drawers came out entire and were upended onto the floor. A barrel of finished staves was opened and probed with a bayonet. The drying racks were pulled apart plank by plank, and Piet stood watching his livelihood come apart in other men's hands with an expression that never once changed — flat, patient, watchful — though his knuckles went white around the handle of a broom he had picked up and set down again twice.

Ines watched them too, and made himself do the arithmetic of fear. The western sheets: gone from the shop since the ninth day, sewn into the lining of Maren's winter cloak, worn to chapel, worn to market, worn while she stood in the lane right now daring them to touch her. The cipher keys: in Father Anselm's strongbox beneath the chapel cellar, behind a wall that had hidden deeds once before. The title proofs: folded into the false bottom of Maren's flour chest, under flour, because no searching man looks hard at what he must wash his hands after. And the index —

The index was against his ribs, warm from his body, and it was the only piece of the atlas in the room.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find. But they wrecked the finding thoroughly. By the time the officer called his men off — an hour past noon, the light going thin and mean over the rooftops — the cooper's shop looked as if a river had run through it. Which, Ines thought, watching Piet begin silently to gather his scattered tools, was nearly the truth. The flood had sent them ahead of itself.

"One more thing," the officer said at the door, and Ines's stomach dropped before the man even turned. "Voss. You were seen at the chapel yard Tuesday last, speaking with Anselm, formerly of this parish. You were seen carrying a parcel."

"I carried a churn head," Piet said. "For the chapel roof. It leaks. God and the whole street saw it."

"We'll ask God, then." The officer smiled without warmth. "You'll come with us and answer a few questions. As a courtesy. As a *warning*—" he seemed to hear the word as he said it, and did not take it back— "to anyone uncertain about the commission's patience."

"That's not in any warrant," Ines said.

"No," the officer agreed pleasantly. "But the bridge is closed, the roads are closed, and Mr. Lyle's writ runs further than a magistrate's ink. Are you offering surety for him, Halloran? Your estate? Your name?" He let the silence do its work. "I thought not. Voss. With us."

Piet set down his broom for the last time. He straightened his apron, took it off, hung it on its nail with the care of a man closing up for the night rather than being taken from his own life, and paused beside Ines on his way to the door.

"The third hoop on the big cask," he murmured, so low only Ines could catch it. "Loose. Mind it."

And then he walked out between the grey coats with his hands loose at his sides, and down Tannery Lane, and the lane watched him go in silence, doors opening along it one by one like eyes.

* * *

Maren found Ines sitting on the cooper's step amid the wreckage, holding a shattered churn stave he had picked up for no reason he could name.

"Third hoop on the big cask," she repeated, when he told her. Her face didn't change, but her voice did — a degree colder, a degree harder, iron going from warm to sharp. "That's where he kept his mother's ring. Under the third hoop." She sat down beside him, heavily, her joints announcing the cold. "He's telling you where to put the next pages."

"He's telling me from the lockup."

"He's telling you because he expects to be back to check your work." But her certainty had a crack in it, and Ines heard it, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"They'll hold him as long as it serves," Ines said. "A week. Two. Long enough for the thaw, long enough for Lyle to cross and make an example of him at trial — obstruction, sedition, whatever phrase they're dressing it in. Or until someone gives them something worth trading him for." He heard his own voice flatten into inventory, the mapmaker's trick of turning horror into terrain. "That's the design, Maren. That's exactly what a warning is. They don't need him guilty. They need us *afraid*, and afraid people make mistakes. Afraid people consolidate. Afraid people bring everything home to one safe place."

"And will we?"

He looked at her. Frost was coming down the lane now with the dusk, silvering the wreckage spilling out the cooper's door, silvering her hair, and above the broken shop the first star stood in a sky the color of slate.

"No," he said. "We scatter wider. We move everything tonight — all of it, every sheet, every key — and we split the copies again, thinner, among more hands. If they want the truth, let them arrest the whole town."

"That," Maren said, "is very nearly what they'll try."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the strangeness of it was that grief and industry felt almost identical from the inside — the same bent backs, the same lantern light, the same hush. Teodor ferried bundles through the yards with a boy's genius for looking bored. The widow Saskia unpicked seams and resewed them with paper inside. Old Halvard, whose hands shook so badly he could no longer draft a straight line, proved that they could still thread a needle, and sewed cipher keys into the brims of hats. By dawn, the atlas existed in nineteen places and no single one of them mattered, which was the whole architecture of the thing, Vane's design fulfilled at last by people his master had never met.

Ines slept two hours on the floor of the chapel vestry with the index under his coat and woke to Father Anselm shaking his shoulder, gently, the way one wakes a man one has bad news for.

"There's word from the lockup," the priest said. "Through the turnkey's wife, who owes me a debt older than either of our sins. Piet's been charged — not questioned, *charged*. Sedition and theft of Crown records. The papers were filed within a day. Filed, Ines. Prepared." He let that sit. "Lyle wrote those charges across the ice before he ever met Piet Voss. This isn't an inquiry. It's a message, and messages have authors. And the author requests — politely, in writing — a meeting with the late Master Vane's executor. Tomorrow. At the guildhall."

Ines sat up. His body ached from copying; his mind, treacherously, was already clear and running.

"What does he want?"

Anselm's face, in the grey pre-dawn light, was kind and grim at once.

"He wants to offer you a bargain," the priest said. "And he wants you to know that the price of refusing has a face, and the face is in a cell downstairs from where you'll be sitting." 

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice boomed — a long, deep crack rolling through the dark like the first word of a sentence the spring had begun, and everyone in the chapel cellar stopped to listen, and no one said aloud what they were all thinking: that the water was coming either way now, and it was only a question of what it would find when it arrived.

Ines touched the index against his ribs, once, like a man touching a wound to be sure it still hurt.

"Tell Maren," he said. "I'll need my coat pressed. If I'm to look beaten tomorrow, I intend to look *expensively* beaten."
