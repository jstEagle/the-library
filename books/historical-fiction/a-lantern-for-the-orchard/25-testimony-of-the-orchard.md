## 25. Testimony of the Orchard

The chapel cellar smelled of candle wax and cold stone, and by the time the first family arrived it smelled also of wet wool and fear.

Ines had set up Vane's portable desk — a folding board his master had carried on thirty years of surveying trips — at the head of a long table made of trestles and planks. Three lanterns hung from hooks in the vaulted ceiling. Father Anselm had insisted on candles too, though the lanterns gave better light. "Light for working," he said, lighting each wick with a spill, "and candles for witnessing. It's not the same thing."

Below them, through the floor, they could hear the muffled sounds of evening Mass being cleared away — the scrape of kneelers, the sweep of a broom. Anselm had chosen the hour carefully: after services, when no one would wonder at folk coming to the chapel, but before the town fully slept, because everyone coming tonight would need to be home before curfew or have an answer ready when asked where they'd been.

"Answer ready" was the phrase Maren used. She had spent two days going door to door among the orchard families, and she came back with her face drawn tight as drumskin and a list of names written in her ledger's back pages.

"Some will come," she told the household over supper. "Some will send word they're sick. Some are genuinely sick." A pause. "And one man offered to report us to the commission in exchange for a pardon on his arrears."

"Which one?" Piet's brother Joren asked, half rising.

"Sit down. His name stays in this room and nowhere else. If we start keeping lists of traitors, we've become them." Maren looked around the table until every eye dropped. "We keep one list only. The true one."

Now, in the cellar, Ines arranged his papers the way he had been taught: fresh sheets ruled lightly in pencil, ink pot stoppered between uses, blotting sand within reach. He had rehearsed the questions with Father Anselm that afternoon. Name. Parish of birth. Boundaries of your family's holding, as you know them, with landmarks. Year the land came to your family, if known. Witnesses who could confirm. Anything written down, kept, remembered.

Anselm had reviewed the list and crossed out nothing but added one line at the bottom, in his careful priest's hand: *Say what the orchard meant. Not only what it measured.*

"That's not evidence," Ines had said.

"No," the old priest agreed. "It's better than evidence. Evidence can be forged. No one forges grief."

* * *

The first to speak was Widow Sorrel, seventy-one, wrapped in a shawl that had once belonged to three other women before her. Her voice was thin but steady, like wire under tension.

"My husband's grandfather planted the east rows," she said. "Forty trees, the year of the hard frost, because he said frost-killed ground makes the sweetest fruit. He was wrong about that — he was wrong about most things — but forty trees went in, and I can name twenty-two of them still standing."

Ines wrote. *East rows planted 1781 by Tomas Sorrel. Twenty-two surviving named by witness.* Then, because he could not help himself, because he was a mapmaker and the habit was bone-deep: "Can you show me which boundary stones?"

She laughed — a dry sound, almost a cough. "Boy, there are no boundary stones. There's a split oak with a nail in it, and the nail is my grandmother's. There's a drain my father laid that floods every March whether the river wants it to or not. There's the place where the Halloran girl fell down the well in '09 and broke her collarbone, and her father put a rail fence up the next week that's still leaning there." She leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the wet in her eyes without spilling it. "You want me to draw you lines. But the lines were never ours. The *living* was ours. Write the living."

Ines looked down at his ruled page. Then he turned it over and started again on the blank side, writing fast, writing everything she said, the nail in the oak and the flooding drain and the girl at the well, and when she finished he read it back to her and she nodded once, like a judge.

"That'll do," she said. "Sign it."

He signed. She signed beneath him, her letters large and unsteady. Father Anselm witnessed, and pressed the parish seal into the warm wax, and the click of the seal against the wood was the loudest sound in the cellar.

* * *

They came all night. That was how Ines would remember it afterward — not as individual statements but as one long unbroken voice, changing register as the speakers changed, sometimes angry, sometimes bewildered, once, unbearably, singing.

The Duvall brothers testified together and nearly came to blows over whether their father's parcel ran to the millrace or stopped ten rods short of it. Ines let them argue for six full minutes before he understood that neither of them wanted to win; they wanted to say their father's name aloud as many times as possible while someone was still willing to write it down. Afterward they shook hands awkwardly and left arm in arm.

Old Marta Kess, who had been born in the orchard cottages and never slept a night anywhere else, could give no dates and no landmarks beyond her own doorstep. What she gave instead was the order of the seasons as her family had kept them for four generations: pruning at the new moon in February, blossom festival whenever the trees decided, first picking of the summer apples on the feast of Saint what's-her-name — she couldn't recall the saint, only that her mother baked with the early fruit and sang a particular song, and then Marta Kess sang the song, right there in the cellar, four lines of it in a dialect so old that Father Anselm closed his eyes to listen.

Ines wrote the song out phonetically, syllable by syllable, his mapmaker's ear catching cadence the way it caught contour lines. When he finished, Marta patted his hand.

"You write it proper," she said. "My mother couldn't write at all. Everything she knew died in her mouth. Don't let it die in mine."

A young couple came, the Pellams, expecting their first child in spring — spring, when the water would already be rising. They didn't talk about boundaries at all. They talked about what they wanted the record to say: that they had lived here, that they had meant to stay, that the child would be born elsewhere and should someday be able to read why. The wife, Greta, dictated a short letter directly to the child, and Ines transcribed it with his throat tight, and when she finished she said, "Put our names exactly right. Spelling matters. My grandmother always said the Crown spells things wrong on purpose so they can claim you never existed."

Ines thought of the survey plates, of the neat official hand that had moved a river bend sixty rods north. He spelled their names twice as carefully as he needed to.

* * *

Past midnight there was a gap — no one scheduled, no one expected — and Maren brought bread and cheese down the cellar stairs and set them beside the ink pot without comment, and Ines ate standing up, reading back through the night's pages. Forty-one statements. His hand ached. His eyes burned. He had never been so tired and had never once thought of stopping.

Teodor appeared at his elbow, too awake for the hour, carrying something wrapped in sacking. He set it on the table and unwrapped it: the surveyor's stake he had pulled from the orchard soil weeks ago, the Crown's mark stamped near its head.

"I found more today," the boy said. "On the south slope. They've been back."

Ines looked at the stake for a long moment. Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote across its top, in letters larger than his usual hand: *Recovered from orchard soil, [date], by Teodor [surname — ask Maren], aged eleven. Witnessed.* And below it he drew the stake itself, exact, to scale, with the Crown's mark rendered precisely — because a drawing could go where the object could not, and somewhere in the capital someone would hold this page and understand that the stakes had gone into the ground before any legal finding authorized them.

"You're making it part of the record," Teodor said. Not a question. He had been listening at the top of the stairs all night; Ines realized this without resentment.

"It's evidence now," Ines said. "It stops being a stick."

The boy considered this with great seriousness, then helped wrap it back up.

* * *

The last speaker came at the coldest hour, when even the candles had burned down to stubs. Father Anselm himself.

He sat where the witnesses had sat, and folded his hands, and for a moment Ines saw how old he was — older than the shawl-wrapped widow, older in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"I hid deeds in '41," Anselm said. "When the toll commission seized the parish records, I took the orchard holdings out the night before and buried them in the crypt behind the third altar stone. They found out. They defrocked the bishop who protected me and transferred me here, to the smallest parish in the diocese, which everyone understood as punishment and I understood as posting a guard." He unfolded his hands and placed them flat on the table. "I paid for it with my post. I would pay with my life and consider it fair trade. But hear this, boy, and write it down, because it belongs in the record same as the rest: I am not brave. I was angry. Anger spends itself. What's kept me these twelve years is arithmetic. Every time I counted what would be lost against what it cost to keep it, the sums came out the same way. Write that. Someone in the future will need to do the same arithmetic, and it will help them to know an old coward got it right."

Ines wrote it. His pen caught on the word *coward* and he paused, thinking of Teodor's gentle accusation over dinner weeks ago, thinking of the word lodged in him like a splinter ever since.

"Father," he said, "may I add something?"

Anselm inclined his head.

"This morning — yesterday morning, now — I believed I was making a copy of an atlas. Tonight I believe I'm making a town." Ines looked at the stacked pages, forty-one statements, the song, the child's letter, the drawing of the stake. "Vane drew the lines. These people filled them. I don't know what that makes me."

"A clerk," Anselm said, smiling for the first time all night. "The finest vocation in Christendom. Every gospel we have was written down by somebody willing to sit still and take dictation." He stood, joints cracking, and gathered the parish seal. "Now. Bring me the strongbox."

* * *

What came out of the crypt behind the third altar stone was wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen and had waited underground for twelve years: the original deeds. Forty-three of them. Signatures of dead men and living memory both — grants, transfers, marriage portions, a lease from 1763 with the landlord's own seal unbroken. Ines held the oldest one and his hands were not quite steady. This was what Vane's atlas had been drawn *from*. This was the body; the atlas was merely the portrait.

Father Anselm worked with the deliberation of a man performing a sacrament, which perhaps he was. Each deed he matched to its statement — Sorrel to Sorrel, Duvall to Duvall, Kess to Kess — reading aloud the old boundary descriptions while Ines checked them against the atlas sheets spread across the trestles. Line by line, the dead documents and the living testimonies agreed. Where a landmark had fallen — the split oak, one drain, the well-rail — the witnesses' words explained the absence, and the absence confirmed the witnesses, because liars invent landmarks; only truth forgets some.

"It holds," Ines said, when the last sheet was checked. "Every one of them holds. The atlas matches the deeds matches the people. Three independent records, one truth."

"Then we marry them," Anselm said.

He sewed. That was the part Ines had not anticipated — the priest produced needle and waxed thread and bound each sworn statement into the folio with its corresponding deed, stitching through the old parchment margins with hands that did not tremble, closing each knot off with a drop of candle wax pressed with the parish seal. Statement and deed, testimony and title, the living word married to the dead instrument. By lamplight, in a cellar, an old man in shirtsleeves performed the ceremony, and Ines held the gatherings square, and Maren passed him thread, and nobody spoke except to confirm a name or a date.

When it was done there were nine bound folios, thick as bricks. Anselm weighed one in his palm.

"Heavy," he said. "Good. Truth ought to weigh something. It's the lies that float."

Maren took the folios and distributed them into the hidden places they had prepared — the false bottom of the cooper's flour bin, the hollow behind Anselm's vestry shelf, one wrapped in oilcloth inside the church's lead rain cistern, where water would protect it from fire and fire from water. Two stayed in the cellar, in the crypt, returned to their twelve-year grave with new company.

Dawn was graying the high windows when the last of it was done. Ines sat down finally, and his hand shook now that it was permitted to, and he looked at the empty trestles and the guttered candles and felt something he had no technical vocabulary for.

For one night, the town had been its own historian. Not the guild, not the Crown, not Corvus Lyle with his charming letters and his meticulous erasures — but a widow and a priest and a boy with a pulled-up stake and a woman with a song. The record existed now in too many hands to burn. He had done what Vane asked. Perhaps, at last, what Vane dreamed.

Maren touched his shoulder. "Sleep. You've earned—"

The knock came at the chapel's street door.

Three raps, evenly spaced, unhurried. The kind of knock that did not need to hurry.

Everyone froze. Teodor grabbed the survey stake as if a foot of painted wood would help. Father Anselm blew out the candles with two breaths and stood between the stairs and the dark, an old man putting his body, once more, between the record and whatever came for it.

From above, through the floorboards, came the muffled voice of the sexton answering the door — and then another voice, smooth and courteous and unmistakable even through oak and stone, asking after the health of Father Anselm, and whether he might spare a few moments' conversation about the parish records.

Corvus Lyle had reached the far bank. And he had begun with the church.
