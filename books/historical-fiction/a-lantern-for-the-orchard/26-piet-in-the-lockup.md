## 26. Piet in the Lockup

The lockup sat at the back of the guildhall like an afterthought — a stone annex that had once been a tithe cellar, before Vellamont decided it needed somewhere to put men it didn't know what to do with. Ines had passed its barred window a hundred times on his way to lessons and never once wondered what went on inside. Now he stood in the alley behind it for twenty minutes in the gray morning cold, working up the nerve to knock on the warden's door.

He had told Maren where he was going. She had not tried to stop him. She had only looked at him for a long moment and said, "Don't promise anything you can't carry," which was her way of saying everything.

The warden was a soft-bodied man named Dreck who owed his post to the guild and his fear to whoever had spoken to him last. He squinted at Ines through the door crack.

"Halloran. The apprentice." The word *apprentice* came out already outdated; everyone in town knew by now that Ines had no post left to apprentice to. "You can't see him."

"I can," Ines said, and held out a folded paper. Not money — he had none worth the name — but a promissory note against the sale of Vane's drafting instruments, signed over to Dreck personally, untraceable enough if you didn't look too hard. "For your trouble. Ten minutes."

Dreck looked at the paper, then up the alley, then down it. Whatever Lyle's men had told him, they hadn't paid him enough to be vigilant about it. He took the note, unlocked the door, and jerked his chin toward the stairs down.

"Ten minutes. And I never saw you."

* * *

The cell was one room, half underground, lit by a single grate of window set at street level so that a strip of frozen light lay across the floor like a dropped ribbon. Piet sat on the plank bed with his back against the wall, and when he saw who had come down the stairs he did not stand. That was itself an answer to something. A man who stands has decided how the visit will go; a man who stays seated is telling you he isn't performing anything.

They had beaten him — not badly, not visibly, in the way of professionals. One ear was swollen and purple at the rim. His knuckles were split. But he was upright, and his eyes were clear, and when Ines sat down on the stool provided across from the bars, Piet looked at him with something close to patience.

"They let you in," Piet said. "That means either they don't care what we say, or they want to hear it."

"The warden took a note."

"A note." Piet almost smiled. "Cheap. Good. Means they haven't priced us yet."

Ines had rehearsed this on the walk over. He had rehearsed it lying awake the night before, staring at the ceiling above the cooper's shop, listening to the house breathe around him. He had composed three openings and discarded two. What remained was the plainest one, and even so it took him a moment to get past his teeth.

"I'm going to trade myself for you."

Piet said nothing.

"You heard me. I'll go to Lyle tomorrow. Today, if it moves faster. I'll tell him I acted alone — that I used you, that you knew nothing, that the pages you were carrying were blank and you never knew it. I'll surrender whatever he wants me to surrender. You walk out." Ines made himself hold the man's eyes. "It will work. Lyle doesn't want you. You're a warning, nothing more. He wants the atlas and the man who wrote it, and I am both. I'm worth more to him than you are. It's arithmetic."

The words sounded better in his head than they did in the cell. Down here, with the damp stone sweating cold and the light-ribbon crawling across the floor, they sounded thin — a speech, a performance, the kind of thing a man says when he wants to have said it more than when he wants it done.

Piet let the silence sit between them long enough for Ines to hear the building settle overhead. Then he shifted on the bed and winced — ribs, probably, under the shirt — and said:

"How long have you known my daughter's name?"

Ines blinked. "What?"

"Lotte. My girl. She's nine. She carried one of your pages in a bread loaf in November, all the way to her aunt's in Hessel, and never knew it, because she thought she was being helpful and she liked the errand." Piet said it without accusation, which made it worse. "You've eaten at our table four times. You've mended Teodor's bootlaces because he ties them wrong. You sat with my wife when she had the fever-cough and read to her because she couldn't sleep and I snore." He raised his split knuckles slightly, an odd little gesture, like a man presenting evidence. "You're going to sit there and tell me you'll make yourself a prisoner so I can walk free, and then what? They keep you a month? A year? Until the canal is dug and there's no reason to hold anyone, and you come out and every person who helped you is written down in some clerk's book because you traded yourself *alone*, alone, alone, and gave them the story they wanted?"

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is." Piet's voice stayed level. That was the terrible thing about him, Ines had learned: he never rose, never fell. He talked the way water found its level. "You'd be giving Lyle a gift. One guilty man. Tidy. Closed. *The apprentice Halloran acted alone, misled simple laborers, has been dealt with.* And then every page out there becomes a forgery by a disgraced mapmaker instead of a testimony by a whole town. You wouldn't be saving me. You'd be saving them the trouble."

"I'd be saving you the rope."

"There is no rope." For the first time Piet's voice moved — not louder, just closer to something. "Look at me, boy. Look around this cell. Stone floor, straw, a bad ear. They arrested me as a *message*. Do you understand? If they wanted me gone, I'd be gone. I'm here because Lyle wants your household frightened, and frightened people do one of two things — they run, or they hand things over. Me sitting in this cell is his letter to you. And you're about to answer it by doing exactly what he wrote."

Ines stood up. The stool scraped. He hadn't meant to stand; his body got ahead of him. "So I should just leave you here."

"No," Piet said. "You should leave me here *and stop thinking this is about you*."

The words landed harder than any blow Lyle's men had landed on Piet, Ines suspected, because Piet delivered them without heat. He said it the way he'd say *the frost is coming early this year*. A fact of the season.

"You think this is a story with a hero in it," Piet went on. "I watched you at dinner. I watch everyone; it's how I kept my family fed through two bad harvests. You've got the look — the one where a man thinks the right sacrifice at the right moment settles the account. It doesn't work that way. There's no single payment. There's just the work." He leaned forward slightly, and the light caught the bruised ear, and Ines thought: he isn't angry at me. He's tired. "Vane understood it. That's why your master split the atlas. Not because one courier might be stopped — though one will be, someone always is — but because he knew that a truth held by one man dies with that man, and a truth held by forty men only gets stronger every time one of them is tested. You're not the treasure, Halloran. You never were."

"Then what am I?"

Piet smiled then, properly, for the first time since Ines had come down the stairs, and the smile transformed his battered face into something almost young.

"You're the pen," he said. "We're the ink."

Ines stared at him.

"Think about what ink does," Piet said. "Ink doesn't sign its own name. Ink goes where the pen carries it, and what it makes isn't the ink's — it belongs to everyone who reads it. Vane could have written his atlas in secret and burned it with himself, safe and pure and useless. Instead he spent years teaching an orphan boy his craft, knowing — knowing, mind you, he planned for this before you ever picked up a rule — that the boy would be the hand. Not because you're special. Because you were *available*. Because you were good. Because you were standing there." He settled back against the wall. "And now forty people in this town are carrying pieces of a dead man's conscience in their coat linings, and most of them couldn't draw a straight line if you paid them. The pen chose well. But the pen doesn't get to decide it's also going to be the page. We're the page. All of us. Me sitting here — I'm part of what gets written. Let me be written well."

The ten minutes were nearly gone. Ines could feel them going, the way you feel sand run out.

"What do I tell Maren?" he asked. It came out smaller than he intended. "She sent me here to bring you home."

"She sent you here to see me," Piet corrected. "There's a difference. Tell her—" He paused, and for a moment the level voice wavered, just barely, the first crack in the stone. "Tell her Lotte's bread trick worked twice. She'll know. Tell her I eat fine and the warden's a coward but an honest coward, and honest cowards can be bought twice." He almost laughed. "Tell her I said the trees remember. She'll know that too."

"It's what the orchard families say," Ines said. "When they give testimony. *The trees remember what the paper forgets.*"

"I know what it is," Piet said quietly. "I grew up under those trees. Why do you think I'm carrying pages instead of running?"

Footsteps overhead. Dreck, or someone else — the ten minutes dying. Ines stood, and hated standing, hated leaving, hated the arithmetic of it, and made himself say the last thing plainly, because Piet deserved plainness and had given him nothing else all morning.

"I won't trade myself. But I'm not leaving you here forever."

"I know," Piet said. "That's why I'm not worried. Get out of here before someone charges you rent."

At the bottom of the stairs Ines turned back. "Why did you ask about Lotte's name? At the start. Before any of it."

Piet looked at him through the bars for a long moment, and the light-ribbon had crawled far enough across the floor to touch his boots.

"Because I wanted to know if you remembered it yourself," he said, "or if I had to remind you what you're writing with."

* * *

Outside, the cold hit him like a hand. Ines walked fast, without direction, past the cooperage, past the chapel, out along the frozen towpath of the future canal until the town was behind him and the stakes began.

They marched away into the white distance at even intervals, each one topped with a small tin plate stamped with the Crown's survey mark, each one driven into soil that would be riverbed by autumn. He had walked this line with Teodor weeks ago; Teodor had pulled one stake up and Ines had made him put it back, and had hated himself for it, and had never told anyone why.

Now he stopped beside one of the stakes and looked at it for a long time.

*A warning, nothing more,* he had said of Piet, as if people could be sorted into purposes like instruments on a bench. As if he could spend a man the way Lyle spent him, and call it sacrifice because the spending hurt the spender too. Piet had seen through it in ten minutes — had seen through it, Ines suspected, before Ines finished the first sentence — and had refused the trade not out of nobility but out of precision. *You'd be giving Lyle a gift.* Of course he would have. One guilty mapmaker, tidily closed, and forty scattered pages reclassified as one man's forgery. Lyle would have taken the trade in a heartbeat and slept beautifully afterward.

Ines crouched down and pressed his bare hand flat against the frozen ground beside the stake. Through the frost he could feel it — faint, stubborn, unmistakable — the slow heartbeat of earth that had been orchard ground for longer than the guild had existed. Roots underneath, sleeping, waiting for a spring that would come as floodwater.

*The trees remember what the paper forgets.*

He had thought, walking to the lockup, that offering himself was courage. Now, kneeling in a frozen field, he understood it had been the opposite — a way of keeping the story about himself. One dramatic gesture, one clean ledger entry, and he could stop being afraid every day of his life. Piet had looked at that bargain and named its price: everyone else's truth, discounted to the value of one man's conscience.

He wasn't the hero. He had never been the hero. He was the hand that Vane had trained and the household had sheltered and the couriers were risking their necks to serve — the instrument, no more, and no less, and the sooner he accepted it without ceremony or grandeur, the more useful he could be before the thaw.

Ines stood, brushed the frost from his knee, and did something he had not done since the funeral: he spoke aloud to his master, there among the stakes, with no one to hear.

"You knew," he said. "You knew it would take all of them. You knew I'd try to make it about me, and you split it anyway." He looked down the long line of tin-topped markers marching toward Maren's orchard. "I'm done trying."

He turned back toward town, and he was halfway to the chapel before he noticed that his feet had chosen the destination on their own — Father Anselm's door, the cellar, the strongbox, the sworn statements still waiting to be fair-copied. There was work. There was only ever work.

But as he came down the lane toward the chapel, he saw a figure ahead of him that stopped him mid-stride.

The guild steward — old Brandt, gray and stooped, a man who had served the guild's interests with the fidelity of a hinge for thirty years — was standing at the chapel's side door in the open cold, hat in his hands, knocking. Not the confident knock of official business. The hesitant knock of a man who has come somewhere he does not believe he will be welcomed.

And he was glancing over his shoulder. Twice. Three times.

Waiting to see who followed him.

Ines stepped back into the shadow of the cooperage wall, heart hammering, and watched the chapel door open, and watched old Brandt hesitate on the threshold — and then, glancing down the lane one final time, almost directly at the place where Ines stood hidden, he went inside, and the door closed behind him.
