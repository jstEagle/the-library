## 27. The Steward Repents

The confession page had been in Ines's coat for six days before he decided what to do with it.

He had read it perhaps forty times. He knew the way the ink thinned at the end of each line, as if Vane's hand were running out of something — not paper, not ink, but permission. *I have told you I falsified nothing. That was a lie I built my life upon, and you deserve better than a dead man's lie.* And then the account of it, plain and terrible: a survey in his youth, forty years gone, a river bend drawn where no bend existed because a man with a seal asked him to draw it, and a family turned off their land by that small elegant stroke. Vane never named the family. He didn't need to. Ines had spent enough nights with Maren's ledger to know which names went missing from parish books like teeth from a jaw.

He had not shown it to Maren yet. He could not explain why, except that some pages are load-bearing, and if he set this one down he wasn't sure the rest of him would stand.

What he did instead was something he'd been trained to do without thinking: he looked at it as a document. Provenance, hand, dating. The paper matched the atlas stock exactly. The hand was Vane's — Ines had spent four years learning to recognize the way his master's *r* leaned like a man against wind. It was genuine, or it was the most devoted forgery ever committed, and Vane had left it unfinished, mid-sentence at the bottom of the page, the way a man leaves a thing when he means to come back to it and doesn't.

*Mid-sentence.* That was the detail that finally moved Ines to act. A forger finishes. Only a coward telling the truth stops halfway.

So on the morning after the first hard frost of late winter, when the ice on the canal route boomed all night like distant artillery, Ines put on his good coat — the one with the master index sewn into its lining — folded the confession page into oilcloth, and walked up the hill to the guildhouse.

The steward's name was Edmun Bray, and Ines had hated him, in the quiet methodical way apprentices hate, for four years. Bray was the man who inventoried Aldous Vane's workshop the morning after the funeral while Ines knelt on the floorboards above the hidden atlas and listened to chalk tally marks being made against everything Vane had loved. Bray was the man who said *Crown property* the way other men said *good morning*. Bray was the man who, three weeks ago under Lyle's pressure, had let slip that Ines received the papers — a betrayal written in small print, the worst kind.

Ines knocked at the guildhouse door and Bray opened it himself, which was wrong already. The doorkeeper should have answered. The hall behind him was dark, the lamps unlit, and Bray stood in the grey morning light looking older than Ines remembered, as though the winter had been taking payments from him too.

"Halloran." No title. There had never been a title between them; apprentices are furniture until they become problems. "I wondered when you'd come."

"I haven't come to confess anything," Ines said.

"No." Bray stepped back from the doorway, an invitation so uncharacteristic that Ines hesitated on the threshold like a horse at water. "I rather think you've come to accuse me. Come in. Mind the third stair; the banister's loose and I've stopped pretending I'll fix it."

* * *

They sat in the guild library, which Ines had entered perhaps ten times in four years, always escorted, always aware of the archive door at the far end with its iron lock and its rule that no apprentice passed it. The room smelled of tallow and cold stone. Bray did not light more than one lamp. He sat across the table from Ines and put both hands flat on the wood, an old man's gesture of steadying himself against weather.

"Say what you came to say."

Ines took out the oilcloth packet. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Four years of drafting had taught his hands a discipline his heart had never learned, and he was grateful for it now. He unfolded the oilcloth. He laid the page on the table between them, face up, and turned it so Bray could read it right-way round.

Bray did not touch it. He read it the way he read everything, top to bottom, lips barely moving, and Ines watched the color leave the man's face in stages, like a tide going out over mudflats. When he reached the unfinished sentence his mouth closed and stayed closed. For a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the shutters.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was among his papers."

"His papers were Crown property," Bray said automatically, and then flinched at his own words, as if hearing them for the first time in forty years of saying them. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God forgive me. Do you know how many times I've said that sentence? It's a hinge. You say it and doors open and things go through them and none of it is your doing. That's what I told myself. That's what I've been telling myself since December."

"Since December," Ines repeated carefully.

Bray lowered his hands. He looked, Ines thought, like a man who had been carrying something heavy for a long distance and had only just been told he was allowed to set it down, and couldn't remember how.

"When Lyle's letters started coming, asking after the missing folios, I wrote back what he wanted to hear. Not lies, exactly. Omissions arranged in order." Bray's laugh was a dry small sound with nothing in it. "I told myself I was protecting the guild. The guild, Halloran. As if the guild were anything but me and a locked door and a set of rules I've recited so long I mistook them for beliefs." He nodded at the page. "And then there's that. He wrote it and hid it and gave it to you, of all people, the orphan boy he never once praised to my face. Did you know that? Four years, and I never heard the man say a kind word about you. And then he leaves you everything worth having."

"He praised me once," Ines said quietly. "In the margins. Where nobody was supposed to look."

Something moved across Bray's face — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been excavated. "Yes. That would be him." He stood, abruptly, joints cracking, and walked to the far end of the library, to the iron-bound door Ines had stared at across years of escorted visits. Bray took a ring of keys from his waistcoat. There were many keys on it, but he went past several without pausing, straight to the oldest one, black with handling, worn nearly smooth.

"You understand what this is," Bray said. "You understand that once I open this, I don't close it again. Whatever you see in here goes into your ledger, and Maren Coldharbour's ledger, and Father Anselm's cellar, and God knows where else. I can't take it back. Neither can you."

"That's the idea," Ines said.

Bray looked at him for a long moment — really looked, perhaps for the first time since Ines had arrived at the guild as a twelve-year-old with dead parents and good penmanship — and whatever he saw made up his mind. The key turned. The lock sounded like a shot in the cold air.

* * *

The guild archive was smaller than Ines had imagined and colder than he'd feared, a stone vault beneath the library with shelves of plan-chests and a smell of vinegar and old size. Bray lit two lamps, moving with the economy of a man who had done this alone at night, many times, for years. Ines understood suddenly that this was not the first secret visit the steward had paid these shelves. Bray caught the thought on his face and answered it aloud.

"Every quarter for eleven years, I've come down here and checked that certain items remain where they're supposed to be. Not to guard them. To make sure no one else has found them. You start checking a thing long enough, Halloran, and one day you notice you're not guarding it. You're keeping vigil."

He pulled a plan-chest drawer and lifted out a portfolio wrapped in waxed linen, tied with cord gone soft with age. He set it on the reading table and untied it with fingers that shook slightly, and Ines felt the whole room tilt toward what was inside.

Survey plates. Copper and steel, a dozen of them, wrapped individually in paper. Bray unwrapped the first and turned it toward the lamp, and Ines bent over it and felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

It was the canal survey. The official one. The one on which the flood line was calculated, the one whose copies had justified every seizure warrant, every burned deed, every stake driven into orchard soil along the frozen canal bed. Ines knew it well — Lyle's men had circulated extracts, and Ines had studied them obsessively, hunting the forgery he believed must be there.

But he had never seen the plates themselves. And the plates were worse than a forgery. They were an admission.

"Do you see it?" Bray asked.

"The river bend." Ines's voice came out strange, flattened, the way sound behaves underwater. "Here — the Weth. On the plate it bends east here, a full half-mile east of its true course. But the engraver—" He touched the copper lightly, reverently, the way one touches a wound. "Look at the revision marks. Look here, and here. These aren't original lines. This plate was cut from an earlier survey and reworked. Someone took an honest plate and moved the river."

"Yes."

"And the honest plate—"

Bray drew out the second layer of wrapping. Beneath the reworked plates lay others, older, their surfaces dulled with age but their lines clean and true. Ines laid them side by side under the lamp, the honest survey and the forged one, and there it was, the whole crime in copper: the same river, the same country, the same engraver's hand throughout — except that in the later plates the river had been persuaded eastward, into the orchard lands, so that the flood line would swallow villages that an honest survey would have spared.

"Why keep them?" Ines whispered. "Why keep the evidence? Why not melt them down?"

"Because I'm a coward, and cowards keep receipts." Bray sat down heavily on the reading stool. "Because the day I destroy them is the day I stop being able to tell myself I might still do the right thing someday. As long as these exist down here, I'm a man who *hasn't* betrayed the truth. Only postponed defending it." He rubbed his face. "Forty years I've kept vigil over a crime. Vane drew that bend, you know."

Ines's head came up. "What?"

"Your master. The original survey — the honest one — was his apprentice work. He was nineteen. The reworking came eight years later, and he was the only draftsman in the province with the skill to move a river convincingly. I've always known. I signed the requisition that commissioned the second plate. I was young too, and ambitious, and the man who asked had a seal, and I told myself the same story everyone tells: hinges, doors, none of it my doing." Bray looked at the confession page where it lay on the table, Vane's unfinished sentence facing the ceiling. "And then he spent the rest of his life drawing true maps, and I spent the rest of mine locking the false ones in a basement. Between us we managed half a repentance each."

Ines looked at the plates, and at the old man, and felt something he had not expected to feel in this room, which was grief. Not for himself. For the shape of a life — two lives — bent around a single dishonest line, the way a tree grows around wire.

"The geometric refutation you're drafting," Bray said. "Anselm told me. Don't ask how he knows; the man confesses everything to everybody, it's why he lost his post and why he'll die a better Christian than any bishop." He pushed the portfolio toward Ines. "Take them."

Ines went very still. "Take them where?"

"Anywhere I'm not. If Lyle's men find forged survey plates in the guild archive, I burn with them and the plates burn with me and the truth dies in a basement, again, the way it always does. If they're scattered — if they're in a cooper's shop and a chapel cellar and a coat lining — then they're like the atlas. Unownable." Bray's voice roughened. "I've read enough of your master's marginalia by proxy to know how he thought. Never trust one person alone. Well. I'm the person alone, Halloran, and I am done."

"There will be consequences," Ines said. "For you. When this surfaces."

"Oh, certainly. Disgrace, probably prison, possibly worse — the Crown does not forgive its clerks for keeping honest copies of dishonest work." Bray smiled, and it was the first real smile Ines had ever seen on him, tired and crooked and unmistakably relieved. "Do you know, that's the first time in forty years that sentence has made me feel lighter instead of heavier? Take the plates. All of them. Tonight, before the town wakes."

* * *

They worked by lamplight in the cold vault, wrapping the plates in ordinary brown paper, disguising them as drawings bound for framing, as choir music, as patterns for the sailmaker. Ines's training made his hands quick and sure; Bray's knowledge of the archive made the disguise complete, every gap on the shelf accounted for with decoy folders and plausible dust. Twice Ines paused over a plate, memorizing its revision marks, committing the engraver's tells to memory — the doubled strokes where the river had been moved, the faint ghost-lines of the honest course still legible beneath like a scar under paint. Even copper remembers, he thought. Even metal keeps the record of what was done to it.

At the last, Bray stopped him at the foot of the stairs.

"Halloran. One more thing, and then I'll say nothing else about my own account." He held up a single sheet, not a plate — a letter, old, sealed with the guild stamp. "This stays here. But you should know it exists. It's the original commission for the reworked survey. Signed by the man with the seal." He met Ines's eyes. "That man's son is Corvus Lyle."

The vault seemed to contract around the words. Ines thought of Lyle's letters, charming and meticulous, fishing for folios; thought of *a talented eccentric*, the phrase Lyle had used to shrink Vane into a harmless curiosity. He thought of a boy growing up in a house where a certain river bend was spoken of as a triumph.

"Lyle isn't just executing the commission," Ines said slowly. "He's finishing his father's work."

"He's protecting his father's name," Bray corrected. "Which is worse, and sadder, and explains nearly everything about him." He pressed the wrapped plates into Ines's arms. "Go. Out the yard door, past the coal shed, the gate sticks — lift, don't push. And Halloran."

Ines turned at the stair.

"When the refutation publishes," Bray said, "put my name in it. The requisition was mine. I signed for the second plate. Let the record show the guild knew, and the guild kept it, and one clerk in the guild broke rank at the end." His jaw worked. "If I'm to be ruined, I'd rather be ruined accurately. It's the only honesty I've got left, and I mean to spend it."

Ines climbed the stairs into the grey morning with a dozen forged rivers against his chest, and outside the sticking gate he stood a moment in the falling light snow, breathing, listening to the ice boom on the canal like the world's slow clock counting down. Then he turned downhill, toward the cooper's shop, toward Maren, toward the lanterns.

Behind him, high in the guildhouse, one lamp still burned over an unlocked door — and for the first time in forty years, nobody stood vigil over it.
