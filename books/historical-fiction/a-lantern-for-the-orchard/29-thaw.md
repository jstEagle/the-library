## 29. Thaw

The first crack came in the night, and everyone in the cooper's shop heard it.

It was not a sound like anything else. Not the snap of a branch, not the groan of a house settling. It was deeper than that — a long, wet exhalation from under the ice of the river, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep. Ines sat up in his cot above the workshop with his heart already going fast, and for a moment he could not tell whether what he felt was fear or relief. Then he understood that it was both, and that they had become, over these weeks, the same feeling.

The thaw had begun.

He dressed in the dark and went down to the river before anyone else was awake. The town was still asleep around him — or pretending to be; he suspected half of Vellamont lay listening the way he was listening — and the streets were white with rime, his breath hanging behind him like a scarf. At the foot of Ferry Lane he stopped where the ground dropped away to the water, and looked out at the thing that had saved them all winter.

The bridge had been closed since the ice came down in December, the span half-dismantled by frost-heave and officially condemned until spring inspection. On either bank, the river had frozen into a single pale slab wide enough to walk a cart across — and people had walked it, carefully, at night, because a closed bridge does not close a river. But now, in the grey first light, Ines could see the change. A seam of black water had opened along the southern channel, no wider than a hand, steaming faintly in the cold air. The ice was pulling away from the banks. Along the near shore, it had gone rotten and grey, honeycombed through, sagging where the current gnawed underneath.

Three weeks, perhaps four. Then the ferry would run again. Then the bridge crews would come with their timbers and their Crown writs, and after them the survey commission, and after them the dredges and the navvies and the engineers with their brass instruments and their signed orders, and then the water.

He heard footsteps on the frosted cobbles and did not turn. He knew the tread — Maren walked the way she kept her ledger, evenly, without hurry, as if every step were an entry she had already made peace with.

"You heard it too," she said.

"The whole town heard it."

"Then the whole town knows what it means." She came to stand beside him at the edge. She was a broad, weathered woman who had spent thirty years among trees, and she looked at the breaking river the way other people look at a sickbed — knowing exactly what is dying and when. "Teodor counted the sheets last night. We're past halfway."

"How far past?"

"Far enough that we finish if nothing goes wrong." She said it flatly, which was how Maren said everything hopeful. "Nothing will go wrong," she added, in the tone of a woman informing the river of its business.

Ines almost smiled. Below them, the black seam widened by a finger's width while they watched. Somewhere under that ice were the surveyors' stakes, driven into orchard soil before the freeze, marking the future flood line in iron and paint. Teodor had pulled one up in February and they had burned it in the cooper's stove, and Ines had written the date in the margin of the atlas: *The erasure begins below the waterline.*

"We need more light," he said. "And we need Piet's hands back."

Maren said nothing for a moment. Piet had been in the lockup for eleven days now, arrested on a charge so thin it embarrassed even the constable — obstruction, they called it, though everyone knew the word underneath was *warning*. Lyle wanted the town to understand the price of paper.

"Piet copies faster than any of us," Ines went on, hating himself a little for saying it. "And I keep thinking about him sitting in that cell while the ice goes out without him."

"He'd tell you to stop thinking that way." Maren turned from the river. "He'd say the ink matters more than the hand. Come inside. There's bread. You can't fight the water on an empty stomach, and today you have forty pages to draw."

* * *

By seven the household had assembled in the long room above the cooperage, and the copying was underway.

They worked now in full daylight hours, curtains open despite the cold, because lamp oil cost money and money had grown short. The room held six tables, and at each table sat one of Maren's people with one of Ines's originals pinned flat beneath glass strips, copying in their own hands onto fresh stock Ines had ruled himself. It was slow, painstaking work — a map is not a letter, and a wrong line on a map is a lie as durable as any forged deed. Ines had drilled them for weeks. *Measure twice. Draw once. If you doubt a line, leave it blank and mark it for me.* Blank space could be corrected. Confidence, once inked, could not.

At the corner table, old Serafina — seventy-one years old, born in the upper orchard village, hands knotted with age but steady as a spirit level — was finishing the western boundary sheets. Beside her, the cooper's daughter Ilse worked the title pages, reproducing Vane's careful transcriptions of the original deeds. The two youngest laborers handled the plainest work: field notes, harvest tallies, the marginalia in which Vane had recorded, year by year, who owned what and who had been cheated out of it. Even the handwriting had to be legible. This atlas was going to be read by strangers — clerks, solicitors, editors — people with no patience and no charity. It had to argue for itself.

Ines moved from table to table with the master index sewn into his coat lining, checking, correcting, initialing finished sheets in pencil in the lower corner where only he would look. His own work waited at the end: the master refutation, the geometric proof that the Crown survey's river bends could not exist on any earth God had made. He had the mathematics settled in his head. What he did not yet have was time.

"Ilse," he said, pausing behind her chair. "The third deed line. Read it back."

Ilse read it back, syllable by syllable. It was correct.

"And the seal impression?"

"Done as you showed me. Shaded, not drawn. Anyone comparing it against Father Anselm's strongbox copy will find they match."

"Good." He squeezed her shoulder and moved on. Behind him, Serafina spoke without looking up from her pen. "You've gone grey around the mouth, boy. Eat something."

"There's bread—"

"Eat *something*," said three voices at once, and Ilse pushed a heel of loaf and hard cheese into his hand without interrupting her stroke, and Ines ate standing up, looking out the window at the river, because there was nowhere better to look.

From here he could see the whole sweep of the water, and the seam of black had become a ribbon. By noon it would be a lane. The ice groaned again — a shorter sound this time, sharper, like a door complaining — and every pen in the room paused for one heartbeat, and resumed.

That was the rhythm of the days now: work, crack, work. Hope and doom arriving in the same sound, over and over, until nobody flinched anymore and everybody listened anyway.

* * *

Midmorning, Father Anselm climbed the stairs from the street with snow in the folds of his cassock and news in his face.

"The ferryman's boy ran the bank this morning," he said, stamping his boots. "The channel south of the island is open stem to stern. Open water, Ines. And there are men on the far bank — I saw them myself from the chapel yard, with a glass. Surveyors' coats. Measuring the landing stage."

Nobody stopped writing. That was the discipline they had built, and Ines was proud of it and chilled by it in equal measure. Pens scratched on. Only Maren looked up.

"Sooner than the icebreaker crews," she said. "Sooner than the bridge."

"Sooner than anyone promised," Anselm agreed. He set a wrapped parcel on the nearest clear table — bread from the convent kitchen, still warm. "Lyle petitioned to reopen the bridge for official traffic weeks ago. If the ferry runs before the bridge is mended, his men cross by boat. Nothing in the writ says they must wait for the span."

Ines stood very still, doing arithmetic. The western sheets — done. The title proofs — done by tomorrow. The testimony folios, sworn statements from eleven orchard families, married to the parish deeds — done, sealed, hidden in Anselm's cellar. What remained was the eastern boundary series, the most delicate work, and the master index itself, and his refutation, unwritten.

"If they cross early," he said slowly, "they come for us first. Not the archive — us. Lyle doesn't need the guildhouse searched again. He needs me."

"He needs the index," Maren said. "The index finds everything else. Without it, the sheets he might seize are loose leaves in a dozen houses and cellars and he knows it. That's why he wants you alive and talking."

"Then we assume less time," Ines said. "Not three weeks. Two. Maybe ten days." He turned to the room, and the room, by degrees, turned to him. He was still not used to this — a man trained his whole apprenticeship to be furniture, to be invisible, now standing at the head of a table with people waiting on his word. "We shift the schedule. Eastern boundaries go to two tables tonight, lamplight be damned, we'll buy oil. I'll draft the refutation in parallel — mornings, before you're up. Anselm, the cellar stays ready. If they come by water, we may have minutes, not hours."

"And Piet?" asked Ilse quietly. It was the question under every question lately.

Ines hesitated. "I'm working on Piet," he said, which was true, and not yet true enough. He had a plan forming about Piet, a trade he could offer, and it frightened him more than Lyle did. He was not ready to say it aloud. Saying it aloud made it real, and once it was real he would have to do it.

Maren looked at him for a long moment — she always knew when he was holding something back; he had given up trying to fool her months ago — but she only nodded. "Ten days," she said to the room. "Then we finish in ten days."

The pens bent to it.

* * *

He wrote the refutation in the blue hour before dawn, at the window, with his coat on and his fingers stiff, because the cold sharpened him the way it sharpened a blade.

It was the strangest experience of his life, stranger even than hiding papers under floorboards or lying to Corvus Lyle in letters. Because the work was *beautiful*. That was the obscenity of it. The forgery was elegant — he granted Lyle's draftsmen that much — and elegance was precisely its undoing. A river does not negotiate. Water takes the fall line, the path of least resistance, and it does so according to laws as fixed as scripture. The Crown survey showed the Vellamont bending northward against its own gradient, doubling back through a ridge of harder stone no flood could cut, all to place the canal's intake where the land titles were weakest. On paper it flowed uphill. Ines had spent three nights proving it in numbers that no surveyor, however loyal, could dispute without perjuring his own instruments.

*Any competent practitioner,* he wrote, in the cool register Vane had taught him, *reproducing the described course upon the ground will find the gradient inverted by a factor of—*

He stopped. Outside, the river boomed — a great cracking report, closer than yesterday, followed by a grinding rush that made the windowpane tremble in its putty. Down the lane, a dog began to bark, and then another. Doors opened somewhere. Voices rose, fell away.

He watched the far bank. In the pre-light he could see torches moving down there, small and orderly. Men gathering at the landing stage. Counting, perhaps. Waiting for the water to finish its work.

He thought of Vane's last page, the confession he had found weeks ago — *I falsified once, long ago, and built this atlas as penance* — and he thought about what his master would make of this morning. A coward's courage, spent posthumously. An atlas designed to be carried apart, so that no single arrest, no single fire, no single betrayal could kill it. Vane had never trusted one person alone. Ines had finally understood that this was not distrust. It was love, in the only grammar a mapmaker has: redundancy. Every sheet copied was a lifeboat. Every courier a second shore.

He bent back to the page and wrote until the light came up and Ilse knocked with bread, and the refutation stood complete at nine pages, waiting only for its final attachment — the comparison plates, which needed the guild's printing press, which meant the steward, which meant the last door he had not yet dared knock on.

* * *

He went to the steward that afternoon.

Halloran's office smelled of tallow and old vellum, as it always had. Steward Brandt sat behind his desk looking older than he had in autumn — thinner, greyer, a man being eaten slowly from inside. Since the raid on the cooper's shop, since Piet, since Ines had confronted him with Vane's confession page and watched the man's certainties crack like river ice, Brandt had existed in a state Ines could only call suspended. Not ally. Not enemy. A lock with the key turning inside it.

"The river's opening," Ines said, without preamble.

"I can hear it from my bedroom." Brandt laced his fingers. "You want the press."

"I want the comparison plates printed. The forged survey beside the true course, side by side, at scale. Nine copies. Enough to send ahead with the pages, enough that no one office can lose them all or burn them all."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the plates to a jobbing printer in Marrowfield and explain why the Guild of Mapmakers' own press refused a document defending the accuracy of maps." Ines held his eyes. "Which story would you rather survive, Steward? The one where the guild hid, or the one where the guild remembered what it was for?"

Brandt was quiet for a long moment. Through the shuttered window, faintly, came the river's grinding.

"Do you know what they'll do to this guild when the canal is built?" he said at last. "Nothing. That's the answer. Nothing at all. We'll certify their surveys, train their engineers, collect our fees, and be grateful. The guild has survived every crown since the old king by being *useful*, Halloran. Usefulness is our entire constitution." He unfolded his hands. "You're asking me to be useless."

"I'm asking you to be honest. Once. In print. Anonymously, if you need it anonymous."

Another silence. Then Brandt rose, crossed to the iron-bound cabinet in the wall — the archive Ines had seen unlocked only once, on the night the steward defected — and took from it a slim folder of plates. He laid them on the desk between them like a man laying down cards he had been holding too long.

"Nine copies," Brandt said. "Printed at night. My press, my typesetter, my risk — and my name on none of them. And Halloran—" He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that Ines recognized, having seen it in mirrors all winter: the face of a man deciding, daily, anew, which side of the water he stands on. "When the commission crosses, they will ask me where you are. I intend to say I don't know. Make sure that, for once in your life, it's true."

* * *

That night the household worked by lantern light, and the orchard lanterns burned in the windows as they had all winter — the book's namesake, Maren called them, though she would not say whose book.

Ines stood at the head table with the eastern boundary sheets spread before him, checking the last of them against the original, and around him the room worked in its practiced quiet: pens, breath, the tick of the cooper's clock, the river booming beyond the walls like a slow drum. Serafina had fallen asleep in her chair at midnight and refused to go home; Ilse had draped a blanket over her and kept drawing. Teodor, thirteen years old and forbidden by unanimous vote from doing any copying at all — too young for the risk, they had told him, and he had stared at them with a fury that was really grief — sat in the corner instead, sorting finished sheets into the travel packets, sealing each with wax, whispering the destination of each to himself like a rosary. *Priory. Solicitor. Gazette. Priory.* The boy knew the routes better than any of them now. Maren had made sure of it.

At two in the morning, the river spoke louder than it ever had.

The sound rolled up through the floorboards and into their bones — a tearing, rushing roar — and everyone was on their feet and at the windows before conscious thought arrived. Out on the black water, a field of ice the size of the churchyard had broken free of the northern bank and was moving downstream, grinding, tilting, shedding shards that caught the moonlight like broken crockery. It went past the town slowly, majestically, an entire winter's worth of silence coming apart and sailing away, and nobody in the room said a word until it was gone around the bend.

"Two weeks," Maren said quietly. "Maybe ten days now."

"Less," said Teodor, from the corner. Everyone looked at him. He was holding the next unsealed packet, and his voice was steady in the way of someone who had decided not to shake. "The ferryman's boy says the channel by the island is wide enough for a loaded boat already. They won't wait for the bridge. Lyle doesn't wait. He sent men to burn the parish records before anyone ordered him to. He'll cross on whatever floats."

The room absorbed that. Ines looked at the table — the remaining sheets, the nearly-finished packets, the nine-page refutation drying upstairs — and felt the arithmetic turn cruel. They had planned for the thaw. They had not planned for the thaw to outrun them.

"Then we move the schedule again," he said. "Everything that's finished goes tomorrow night. Everything unfinished goes the night after, finished or not." He met Maren's eyes. "We stop waiting for perfection. Vane never got to finish either. The atlas was always meant to go out imperfect — that's what the cipher was for. Corrections follow. Survival comes first."

Maren nodded slowly. Around the table, the others were already gathering their things, rolling charts, banking the stove. It was past two in the morning and no one suggested bed.

Ines stood a moment longer at the window. The river ran dark and open where the ice-field had passed, and on the far bank, faint in the distance, a torch still burned at the landing stage. Waiting.

Tomorrow night, the first couriers would walk out of Vellamont carrying pieces of the truth in their coat linings — a drover, a pilgrim, a bride, none of them knowing the whole, none of them needing to. Tonight, there was wax to melt and routes to confirm and one packet still missing its final page.

He turned back to the table and picked up his pen.

Behind him, out on the water, the ice groaned again — and this time, unmistakably, it sounded like a door beginning to open.
