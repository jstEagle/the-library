## 30. The Courier Plan

Maren had kept ledgers all her life, and she knew the arithmetic of trust better than any sum in them.

A ledger held in one place could be burned. A ledger carried by one man could be taken from him at a checkpoint, or lost in a river crossing, or traded for his own skin when the questions came. But a truth broken into pieces and scattered — that was different. That was seed. You did not need every seed to sprout. You needed only enough of them to fall on ground that would hold.

So she stood at the long table above the cooper's shop with the copied pages spread before her like a harvest laid out for sorting, and she planned the planting.

The copies themselves were finished — had been finished for three days now, though her hands still smelled of ink and her eyes still ached when she looked at anything smaller than a loaf. Ines had overseen the final sheets himself, checking each one against the master index sewn into his coat lining, and when he was done he had sat back and looked at the stack as if it were something alive.

"They're not perfect," he'd said.

"They're true," Maren had answered. "That's better than perfect."

Now, in the grey light of a morning that smelled of thawing mud and apple wood smoke, she sorted them into bundles. Each bundle thin enough to hide in ordinary things: the lining of a grain sack, the false bottom of a cheese box, the spine of a hymnal, the padding of a child's doll. She wrapped each in oilcloth herself. She tied each with the same plain brown string, so that anyone who helped would recognize their own work without ever seeing another's.

She did not tell any single courier what the other couriers carried. That was the whole design, and Ines had argued for it and against it both, in the same hour, pacing the small room while she worked.

"If one of them is stopped," he said, "they can't give up what they don't know."

"And if they're stopped," Maren said, not looking up from her wrapping, "we lose what they carry, and we go on."

"That's cold."

"That's farming." She set a bundle down and finally met his eyes. "You plant forty rows knowing frost will take some. You don't stop planting. You plant more."

Ines had gone quiet then, and she'd seen him turn it over the way he turned over everything — slowly, carefully, like a man handling paper older than himself. He was getting better at this. He was getting worse at hiding it. Both things worried her.

* * *

She made her list by candlelight, alone, because some decisions should be made by only one pair of eyes.

**The drover, Hallam.** He went east every spring with the first cattle, down the old drove road to the market towns, and no soldier in the world searched a drover's grain sacks twice — everyone knew drovers were thieves anyway, and searching them was beneath official dignity. Hallam owed the orchards thirty years of grazing rights along the hedgerows. His wife had been born in Lower Pelling, one of the villages already gone quiet under survey stakes. He would carry the western sheets — the boundaries of Pelling itself, drawn in Vane's own hand. He would take them to the solicitor in Marrowfield, a man named Quill who had once fought the Crown over a mill right and won, and who owed Maren nothing but hated being lied to more than he hated losing.

Hallam wouldn't know what the papers were. He'd believe they were a debt claim — which was even true, in its way.

**The pilgrim, Sister Odile.** Old, half-blind, harmless-looking in the way that made searches short and guards bored. She walked to the priory at Saint Errow every Lent, as she had for twenty years, and nobody stopped a pilgrim's devotions. Her bundle was the smallest and perhaps the heaviest: the cipher key, and Father Anselm's testimony about the hidden deeds, written out fair in Ines's careful hand. The prior at Saint Errow was Anselm's old teacher. If Anselm said send it there, then there it would sit, waiting for worse days, like a coin buried in a floor.

Sister Odile would think she was carrying prayers for the drowned villages. Also true, in its way.

**The wedding party.** This was the one Maren turned over longest, because it involved the most people and therefore the most chances for a loose tongue. But spring weddings couldn't be stopped — not even by a commission — and the Vessel family's girl, Petra, was marrying a cooper's son from two towns south, and half the household would travel with the cart. Among them, sewn into the bride's own gown — because no man in a uniform would put hands on a wedding dress, not in front of her mother — would go the land-title proofs. The originals. Anselm's strongbox documents, the ones men had bled for before Ines was ever apprenticed.

Petra would know. Maren decided she would know, and no one else in the party. A bride could keep a secret better than most, Maren thought; she was already keeping several just by getting married.

And **Teodor** — Teodor was the argument she hadn't finished having with herself.

* * *

He found her at the table on the third evening, after the others had gone down to supper. He was fourteen now, or near enough, and he had grown through the winter the way spring things grow — all at once, overnight, so that his coat sleeves were suddenly wrong and his voice had begun to crack like green wood. He stood in the doorway holding a lantern, and behind him the orchard lanterns were already lit in the windows below, the household's old ritual, the book's namesake, burning for the trees that would soon be under water.

"You're sending me last," he said. Not a question. He'd learned to read her silences the way she read weather.

"I'm thinking about sending you last."

"Because I'm young."

"Because you're the one I can't lose." She said it plainly, because lying to Teodor had never once worked and wasn't going to start now. "Hallam gets caught, we lose maps. Odile gets caught, we lose keys. You get caught—" She stopped. Started again. "You're carrying the page that makes all the rest of it mean anything. Vane's confession. The proof the atlas was deliberate, that it wasn't one madman's fancy but a record built on purpose, against the Crown's own survey. Without that page the copies are just drawings. With it, they're evidence."

"Then I should go first," Teodor said. "Before the bridge opens and the roads fill up with Lyle's men. Before anyone's watching. First travelers are never searched — everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows," Maren agreed, "which means everyone watches the first travelers eventually, once everyone knows it." She pushed back from the table and rubbed her eyes. Outside, somewhere down the lane, the ice on the canal route boomed and groaned, the sound of the thaw working at the world like a patient carpenter. "You'll go with the wedding party. Middle of the crowd, where a boy is just a boy helping carry baskets. And you'll give your page to Petra at the crossroads south, and walk home, and that will be the end of your part."

"The end of my part." He said it flatly.

"For now."

"Maren." He set the lantern down and came to stand across the table from her, and for a moment he looked so much like the child she'd found half-frozen at the orchard gate that her chest hurt. Then he spoke, and the voice coming out was nearly a man's. "You taught me every tree by name. All four hundred and six. You told me names are how things survive. And now there's a page with names on it, and you want me to hand it off at a crossroads like a sack of turnips?"

"I want you alive," she said. "That's all. That's the entire plan where you're concerned. Everything else is decoration."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Downstairs, someone began a song — Piet's brother, probably, who sang whenever he was frightened and pretended it was for joy.

Then Teodor nodded, once, the way she'd taught him to close a ledger at day's end. "Middle of the crowd," he said. "Baskets. Crossroads south. And I come home."

"And you come home."

* * *

She gathered them the next night in the cooper's shop, all the carriers together for the first and last time, because Maren believed a thing worth doing was worth doing in daylight among witnesses — but also because she wanted, selfishly, one hour when everyone knew they were part of something.

They came in ones and twos. Hallam smelling of cattle and pipe smoke, pretending to be bored. Sister Odile with her walking stick and her cataract-white eyes that saw more than anyone credited. Petra in her everyday dress, her mother hovering, the wedding eight days off. Father Anselm, who had insisted on blessing each courier separately and quietly, and whom no one dared refuse. Ines, standing at the edge of the lamplight with his coat on, always with his coat on now, the master index riding against his ribs like a second heart.

Maren laid the rules out plainly, the way she'd lay out planting rows.

"None of you knows what the others carry. Keep it that way. If you're asked, you're carrying what I say you're carrying, and the stories are simple because simple stories hold. Hallam — debt papers. Odile — prayers. Petra — nothing but a gown. Teodor — baskets." She looked around the circle. "You deliver to the person named, you say the words I gave you, and you forget the rest. Not because you're cowards. Because forgetting is how the others stay safe. Forgetting is a kind of courage too, though no one writes songs about it."

Sister Odile laughed, a dry rustle. "I've been forgetting things for years, child. Finally a use for it."

There was laughter around the circle then — nervous, but real — and Maren let it live for a moment before she killed it.

"One more thing." Her voice dropped. "What we send out tonight isn't paper. It's the orchard. Every tree Ines drew, every name in my ledger, every family that worked this ground since before the guild kept records. Lyle wants the story to end with us grateful and forgotten. These pages say otherwise. They say: we were here, we kept accounts, and the accounts balance." She picked up the first bundle and held it out to Hallam. "Plant it well."

Hallam took it with both hands, suddenly not bored at all. One by one the bundles went out — into a grain sack, a hymnal, a gown's hemming, a basket's false bottom — until the table held nothing but string-ends and oilcloth scraps, and the room smelled of wax and ink and apple wood.

It was Ines who broke the silence afterward, quietly, to her alone, as the others drifted down toward the singing.

"It's done, then. It's out of our hands."

"No," Maren said, watching the lanterns in the windows, watching the ice-boom roll up the valley from the direction of the bridge. "It's exactly in our hands. That's what scattering is. Every hand that carries a piece holds the whole thing responsible." She turned to him. "Which reminds me. Your part starts tomorrow."

"My part?"

"You're going to walk into that guildhall and hand Corvus Lyle the original atlas, and look destroyed doing it." She held his gaze. "So eat well tonight, Ines Halloran. And sleep. Tomorrow you learn how to lose convincingly."

Above the cooper's shop, the orchard lanterns burned on, and out in the dark the river ice cracked like distant gunfire, counting down the days.
