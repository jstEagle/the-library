## 32. The Long Night of Copying

They began at dusk, when the last of the thaw-light failed off the cooper's windows, and Maren went down into the cellar and came back up carrying the lanterns.

There were nine of them. Orchard lanterns — tin and horn, their handles worn to a shine by sixty years of hands that had walked rows in the dark to check frost cloth, to scare crows, to find a dropped child. They had hung all summer in the cooper's rafters because there was no orchard left to hang them in. Now Maren set them out along the long workbench one by one, lighting each with a spill from the stove, until the room above the cooper's shop glowed like something alive.

"The book's namesake," she said. Not a joke. She never joked when it mattered.

Ines stood at the head of the bench with the atlas open before him, the master index sewn inside his coat against his ribs, and looked down the row of faces lit gold from below: Maren at his right hand. The cooper, Bramwell, whose forearms could crack an oak stave and who held a pen like it might bite him. Piet's empty stool, which no one had moved since the arrest, and which Ines caught himself looking at every quarter hour as if the man might simply walk back through the door and sit down. Old Serafina, who had picked fruit on these slopes for fifty years and whose eyes were still sharp enough to catch a bad stitch. Teodor, thirteen years old, sitting very straight because he had been told he was part of this and not told why yet.

And Father Anselm, sleeves rolled, ink pot uncorked, wearing the expression of a man about to commit a sin he had already made peace with.

"Order of work," Ines said. His voice sounded strange to him — steady, official, the guild voice Vane had drilled into him across seven years of *again, Halloran, the contour is wrong* — and he let it be strange. Tonight the guild voice belonged to them. "The western sheets first. Maren has hers already; we replace nothing she carries. Then the title proofs — Anselm takes those, they marry to what's in his strongbox. Then the boundary maps. Bramwell, you take the canal-route sheets, all four. Serafina, the harvest registers — your hand is the steadiest for small figures."

"And me?" Teodor said.

Ines looked at Maren. Maren gave him the smallest nod.

"You," Ines said, "take one page. The most important one."

The boy's chin lifted. Around the table, the adults went quiet in a particular way — the way of people watching a weight be set on young shoulders and knowing it must be, and hating it.

* * *

They worked through the evening, and the copying became its own weather.

Bramwell cursed steadily under his breath for the first hour, because mapmaking was not cooperage and his letters came out like fence posts driven crooked. Serafina leaned close over his sheet without being asked. "Slower," she said. "You're not building it. You're remembering it." Something in that unlocked his hand, and by midnight his canal routes were legible enough that even Ines stopped wincing.

Anselm copied the title proofs with the fluency of long practice, and once, passing Ines a finished sheet, he said quietly: "The last time I did this, it was deeds in a cellar at night, and I was alone." He smoothed the page flat. "It is better company now."

Maren worked without speaking for hours. She had taken the western sheets weeks ago and knew them by heart, but she copied again anyway — her own set, a second seed — and Ines watched her hand move and understood that this was what she did, what she had always done: names in a ledger, harvests in a book, pages in duplicate. She had been running this archive before any of them knew it existed. She was its founder. He was only its latest apprentice.

The lanterns burned. Someone — Serafina — put water on to heat, and later there was bread and hard cheese passed down the bench without anyone stopping work, and crumbs fell on fair copies and were brushed away with the solemnity of people handling scripture.

Around two in the morning, Ines unrolled his own task: the refutation.

He had been drafting it in his head for days — the geometric proof that the Crown survey's river bends were impossible, that no water had ever run, or ever would run, along the lines Corvus Lyle's plates described. Now he laid out the forged plate beside Vane's true survey and began to write, and the writing was the easiest thing he had done in months, because here at last there was no hiding, no half-truth, no careful letter to a stranger. There was only the math.

"Read it aloud when you've got the spine of it," Maren said. "We should all hear it."

So he did. He read them the bend radii, the gradient impossibilities, the way the Crown's river gained elevation going downstream — *the water climbs, gentlemen; their river is a miracle* — and he heard the room change around him as he read. Serafina snorted at the miracle line. Bramwell stopped copying to listen. Anselm closed his eyes at the final figure, as if in prayer, though Ines suspected it was arithmetic being checked behind the lids.

"It will hold," Anselm said at last. "Any surveyor worth his chain will have to concede it. That is what frightens me. Lyle knows it too."

"Then Lyle burns it," Ines said, "or buries it, or calls my geometry treason. Which is why it leaves tonight in four hands, like everything else."

He went back to drafting. His neck ached. His eyes burned. And underneath the ache, steady as a pulse, was a feeling he could not remember having before: that he was exactly where he was supposed to be, doing exactly what he was made for, among people who knew what he was making and why.

* * *

It was near dawn when the candles of the household began to gutter in the people themselves. Serafina's head nodded and snapped up, nodded again. Bramwell's last sheet came out with a wobbling border and he pushed it away in disgust and Ines said, "It's fine, it says what it says," and the cooper grunted and did not believe him and copied it again anyway, because that was who he was.

Maren stood, stretched until her back cracked like green wood, and said: "Teodor. Come here."

The boy crossed the room. He had stayed awake all night on pure discipline and will, and it showed in the shine of his eyes.

Ines took the page from the atlas.

He had thought about this for three days, and every reasoning ended in the same place. The master index — the single sheet that named where every other page had gone, the key to the whole scattered truth — could not travel with Ines, because Ines was the man Lyle was watching, the man whose coat would be searched, whose lodgings might be turned over the moment Lyle tired of letters. It could not stay with Maren, because Maren already carried the title proofs, and one house losing both would be the end of it. It needed to go somewhere no historian would think to look.

It needed to look like nothing.

"This is the master index," Ines said to Teodor. "Every name. Every route. Where each page sleeps. If all the rest are lost, this one page can rebuild the atlas. If this page is lost—" He stopped. "It cannot be lost."

Teodor looked at it. Then he looked up, and Ines saw the boy understood completely, the way children sometimes understand a thing whole and instantly while adults circle it for days.

"I'm too young to be arrested," Teodor said. "That's why me."

"That's why you," Maren said. Her voice was level. Her hands were not quite.

"They wouldn't hang a boy," Teodor said, with the terrible logic of thirteen. "They'd just ask questions. And I don't know anything. I'm just walking somewhere with paper in my boot."

"In your boot lining," Ines said. "Folded in eight, along the outer seam, where the leather is doubled. We'll do it now, before you sleep, so you forget it's there. You'll carry it after thaw, when the road opens, to your aunt in Harrow Fen — Maren's sister-in-law's people, no connection anyone can draw to this house. You go as a boy visiting family for planting season. Boys visit family. Nobody burns a bridge for a boy visiting family."

Teodor nodded. Then, unexpectedly, he grinned — tired and fierce and suddenly just a child again. "I'm the most important page."

"You're the page that keeps the pages honest," Maren said. "Which is different, and heavier, and you'll get bread for it."

She wrapped him then, brief and hard, one arm around his shoulders, her chin pressed to the top of his head, and Ines saw the boy endure it the way boys endure tenderness — stiffly, secretly glad — and saw Maren let go before either of them had to admit how long she wanted to hold on.

* * *

The folding took twenty minutes and was done at the kitchen table with the others pointedly elsewhere. Ines showed Teodor the seam, the fold, the fit, then had the boy do it himself twice, blind, by feel. The page sat invisible. A boy's boot, scuffed, ordinary, holding nothing but a foot.

"Now forget it," Ines said. "Truly. Don't check it. Checking is how men get caught. Carry it the way you carry your own name — without thinking about it."

"What if someone asks where I'm going?"

"Tell them the truth. Truth travels lightest."

Teodor considered this. "That's what you do now? Tell the truth?"

"When I can," Ines said. "Lie beautifully when I can't. Your aunt lives past the second milestone marker on the fen road. If anyone asks beyond that, be bored. Boredom is armor."

The boy almost smiled. Then, at the doorway, he turned back, and whatever he had rehearsed all night came out wrong and therefore right:

"Piet said you were the pen." He shifted his weight. "I'm not the ink, am I. I'm more like — the place you keep the pen dry."

Ines felt his throat tighten. "Yes," he managed. "Exactly that. Go to sleep, keeper of pens."

The boy went. Through the thin wall Ines heard Maren's low voice saying goodnight, and the creak of the cot, and then nothing.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the frost-ferned glass, and the lanterns burned on, pointless now, and no one suggested putting them out.

Serafina slept in her chair with her finished copies squared and weighted beneath her folded hands. Bramwell had gone down to the shop floor to sleep among his barrels, declaring that he dreamed better around wood. Anselm gathered his title proofs into oilcloth with the tenderness of a man dressing wounds, and paused at the door.

"I have said Mass for fewer good reasons," he said, nodding at the littered bench, the exhausted household, the nine lanterns guttering in daylight. "This room has confessed more than my church has this year."

When he was gone, Ines sat alone with Maren at the long table, the atlas between them, nearly stripped now — sheets gone to oilcloth, to cellars, to a boy's boot — and what remained looked less like a book than a skeleton. But a skeleton is the thing that holds a body up.

"Lyle's next letter will come within days," Maren said. "Thaw's early. The ice is talking at night — you can hear it from the ridge. When the bridge opens, it opens for him first."

"I know."

"And you still mean to give him the original."

Ines looked at the hollowed atlas. "He demanded it. If I refuse, he tears this house apart looking, and finds the couriers' tracks still warm. If I give it — he gets a book whose children are already grown and gone." He turned a lantern down with two fingers, watching the flame shrink. "Let him correct his copy of a truth that no longer lives in it."

Maren studied him for a long moment. "Vane taught you that?"

"No," Ines said. "The orchard did. Nothing survives in one tree."

Something moved behind her face — grief and pride in equal measure — and she reached across and blew out the lantern entirely, and then, one by one, they put out the rest, until the room held only gray morning and sleeping people and the smell of ink and tallow and cold apples from the cellar below.

On the table lay the refutation, finished in the night, waiting for its own boots.

And down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Ines had been hearing in his sleep for a week: the ice on the canal route, cracking like knuckles, counting down.
