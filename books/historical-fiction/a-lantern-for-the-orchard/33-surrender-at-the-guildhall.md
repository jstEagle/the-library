## 33. Surrender at the Guildhall

The morning of the surrender came up grey and windless, and Ines woke before dawn without having truly slept.

He had not slept properly for three nights — not since Maren's household had finished the last copy by lantern light, not since Teodor had gone out before first light with a page sewn into the lining of his coat and walked, alone and upright, out of Vellamont toward the drovers' road. The boy had not looked back. Ines had made himself watch until he was certain the boy would not turn around, because if Teodor had turned around, Ines did not trust his own face to hold what it needed to hold.

Now the original lay in its oilcloth wrapping on the table beside his bed, and it weighed nothing and everything. He had carried heavier folios up stairs with one hand. He had balanced full survey kits across a frozen canal bed. This — this was the lightest thing he had ever lifted, and when he picked it up his arms shook like an old man's.

He dressed carefully. Not in his best clothes; that would look like ceremony. He dressed as a defeated man dresses: the coat with the frayed cuff, the boots he had meant to have resoled since autumn. A man who has lost does not dress for an occasion. A man who has lost simply gets up and goes where he is sent.

He let himself believe, for one long breath, that he might actually feel defeated. It would be easier to perform if there were something true underneath.

But under everything, beneath the fear and the fatigue, there was a small hot coal that would not go out, and every time he probed it he found the same thought burning in it: *they are already over the hills.* Piet's sister with the western sheets in her flour sacks. The pilgrims with the cipher keys folded into their psalters. The bride's party, laughing on the south road, carrying land-title proofs inside a bolt of wedding linen. Three days ahead now. Three days of ice roads and honest strangers, and not one of them knowing the whole, which was the whole point.

Vane's plan, executed in Vane's absence. *Never one hand,* the marginalia had said. *One hand can be broken.*

Ines wrapped the atlas, put it under his arm, and went down the stairs of the cooper's shop for the last time.

Maren stood at the bottom. She had clearly been standing there a while; the house behind her was quiet in the way of people pretending not to listen. She looked at him the way she looked at trees in late winter — assessing what had survived the frost.

"You don't have to carry it yourself," she said. "Teodor's cousin would take it. Or I would."

"I know."

"Then why?"

Ines considered the question honestly, because Maren could smell an evasive answer at forty paces. "Because Lyle asked for me. If someone else carries it, it looks like hiding. If I walk it in myself, broken and obedient, he'll stop digging." He paused. "And because I'm the pen. Piet said so. Pens do the writing. They also get chewed."

Maren reached out and straightened his collar, an absurd maternal gesture that undid him more than any speech would have. "You come back," she said. "Whatever you give him. Whatever you say. You come back through my door tonight, and we'll count you among the living."

"And if he arrests me?"

"Then we'll count you among the inconvenient." Her mouth twitched — the ghost of the smile he'd learned to read over one hard winter. "Go. Don't run, don't dawdle. Walk like a man with nothing left."

It was, he reflected on the walk down through the working quarter, the best acting lesson he had ever received.

* * *

The guildhall had been built in prouder times, when Vellamont believed itself the equal of any river town on the reach. Its stone steps were worn hollow by two centuries of clerks' feet, and its great hall still smelled faintly of tallow and old varnish, though the guild's fortunes had thinned along with the town's. Now Crown banners hung where the guild arms used to, and the commission's clerks had colonized the long tables like starlings on a fence line.

Corvus Lyle had taken the master's dais for his workroom. He sat behind Aldous Vane's own desk — Ines recognized the burn scar on the near corner from a carelessly set ink pot, years ago — writing letters in his beautiful, unhurried hand. He did not rise when Ines entered. That was deliberate. Everything about Corvus Lyle was deliberate.

"Halloran." He finished his sentence before looking up. When he did, his face arranged itself into warm regret, a portrait of a reasonable man receiving disappointing news. "I confess I hoped it wouldn't come to this."

"I'm sure you did." Ines set the wrapped bundle on the desk between them. His voice sounded flat and tired to his own ears, which was exactly right. "Here is Master Vane's atlas. All of it. The folios, the working drafts, the cipher pages. Nothing missing."

Lyle did not touch it at once. He studied Ines instead, the way he must have studied a hundred witnesses, reading faces the way Ines read contour lines. "Nothing missing," he repeated pleasantly. "You understand I'll verify. A document of this importance — the Crown will want certainty."

"Verify. Of course." Ines let his shoulders sag, let his eyes go to the middle distance. He had spent years learning to be invisible in this building; now he spent all of it at once. "I'm told my apprenticeship ends today regardless."

"It ends today regardless," Lyle agreed gently. "But Halloran — hear me. There is no shame in what you've done. You inherited a burden too heavy for you, and in the end you set it down where it belonged. Most men couldn't have. Most men would have burned it or buried it out of some misplaced loyalty to a dead eccentric." He opened the wrapping at last, and Ines watched his fingers — fine fingers, a musician's fingers — move across the cover boards with something dangerously close to reverence.

"He wasn't eccentric," Ines said. "He was careful."

"Careful men don't die alone in workshops full of unfiled papers." Lyle lifted the first folio free. "Sit down, Halloran. This will take hours, and you may as well watch. Consider it your final instruction. Every apprentice should see how history is actually made."

* * *

What followed was the strangest morning of Ines's life.

Lyle worked the way a surgeon works — swiftly, cleanly, without cruelty and without mercy. He laid the atlas open on trestle tables brought in for the purpose. He summoned two clerks and dictated to them in a low, continuous stream. And page by page, sheet by sheet, he began the correction.

Not destruction. Ines had braced himself for fire, for knives, for ink poured black across Vane's lines. Lyle did none of that. He *annotated.* In neat red marginalia, in a hand nearly as lovely as Vane's own, he wrote against the atlas the way a theologian writes against heresy.

*"Survey datum superseded by Commission measurement of 1852."*

*"Boundary per Crown Letters Patent, ref. Canal Act §14."*

*"Village of Ashenmoor — see annexed schedule; settlement recorded here in error."*

*In error.* Two words, and Ashenmoor — its sixty-one households, its cider press, its chapel bell recast twice — became a cartographer's mistake.

Ines sat where he was told to sit, hands folded, face empty, and catalogued. It was the one discipline no one could take from him. While Lyle's clerks measured and compared and murmured, Ines memorized. The order of the corrections. Which sheets Lyle handled most gingerly. The fact that he never touched the cipher margins at all — had apparently decided they were decorative doodling, a dead man's habit of ornament. The fact that the easternmost folios, the ones Ines had copied only four nights ago by lantern light, drew from Lyle a small frown of concentration, as if something about them nagged at him.

Let it nag.

"Halloran." Lyle looked up suddenly, and Ines's heart performed a single cold revolution before settling. "You were his hands for three years. Tell me honestly — did he ever speak of why he kept these villages mapped so... lovingly? Sentiment? Some old attachment?"

Ines had prepared for this. He had prepared answers for questions Lyle hadn't even asked yet, lying awake while the household slept, rehearsing defeat the way actors rehearse triumph.

"He spoke of accuracy," Ines said. "Nothing else. He was — you said it yourself. An eccentric. He loved the drawing more than the thing drawn. I think the orchards were just pretty to him." He let bitterness edge in, the bitterness of a young man diminishing his dead master to protect him. "I resented it sometimes. All those wasted weeks measuring orchard rows nobody paid for."

"Wasted weeks," Lyle echoed softly. "Yes. I imagine they felt that way." He returned to his annotations, satisfied, and Ines stared at the floorboards and hated himself with a purity that felt almost holy. Every word true and every word a lie. Vane had measured orchard rows because names lived in them. Because a boundary drawn in love was evidence, and evidence was the only weapon the powerless were ever issued.

By midday the correction had reached the central folios. By early afternoon, a clerk was sewing new endpapers into the atlas — Crown endpapers, stamped with the commission's seal, so that the book would open, forever after, declaring whose truth it contained. Watching it, Ines understood something that steadied him better than courage could: Lyle was not destroying the atlas because he *couldn't*. He needed it intact, corrected, official — the perfect forgery wearing the victim's own skin. Which meant every false line he added was a confession in waiting. Somewhere over the hills, in a priory or a solicitor's office or a gazette printer's back room, the uncorrected twin of this book existed, and the moment the two were ever set side by side, Lyle's beautiful red marginalia would become the clearest possible record of what he had done.

He was building the case against himself, in his own hand, with Ines's help.

Ines almost smiled, and turned it into a yawn, and rubbed his eyes like a grieving, exhausted young man, which was also true.

* * *

Toward dusk, Lyle set down his pen.

"There," he said, with the satisfaction of a man closing a ledger. "It's done. The atlas is corrected, certified, and entered into the commission's custody. Vane's errors are repaired. History is—" he caught himself, and smiled thinly, "—tidier."

"Tidier," Ines echoed.

Lyle came around the desk. Up close, in the failing light, Ines saw for the first time how tired the historian was — shadows under the eyes, a tremor of real fatigue in the hand that gripped Ines's shoulder. A man who had crossed ice to win a book, and found winning strangely heavy.

"You did the right thing today," Lyle said quietly. "I mean that. Whatever happens next — and things will happen, Halloran, the canal is approved, the waters will rise, none of us can stop what's been set in motion — you should remember that when it mattered, you chose order over sentiment. That's rarer than you think. There may yet be a place for a man like you in the official record. Think on it."

Ines looked at the atlas on the table, in its new binding, wearing its new seal, fat with red corrections. He thought of Teodor walking the drovers' road with his spine straight. He thought of Maren saying *count you among the living.* He thought of Piet in the lockup, refusing rescue with a prisoner's grin.

"I'll think on it," he said, and heard his voice crack slightly on the last word, and did not correct the crack.

He bowed — shallowly, wearily, exactly as much as a disgraced apprentice owes a commissioner — and walked out of the guildhall into the blue evening.

The snow crunched under his boots. Somewhere across the river, a lamp was being lit in the window above the cooper's shop, and Ines walked toward it with the unhurried tread of a man who has lost everything, while behind him, in the sealed and sleeping guildhall, the corrected atlas sat on its table — alone, official, and utterly alone — not knowing that every important part of it had already left town.

At the bridge head, a lantern burned where no lantern had burned all winter. Workmen's sledges stood stacked beside it, and fresh timber, and a notice nailed to the post in a clerk's round hand:

*BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSION — THE CROSSING WILL OPEN TO OFFICIAL TRAFFIC AT FIRST LIGHT.*

First light. Barely twelve hours away. And with the commissioners would come the engineers, and the stakes, and the survey crews with their impossible river bends — and somewhere on the far bank, the news would travel faster than any sledge that the man who surrendered the atlas had walked out of the guildhall free.

Ines stood a moment in the cold, reading the notice twice, committing it to memory the way he committed everything now.

Then he climbed the hill toward the lamplit window, and began to plan the morning.
