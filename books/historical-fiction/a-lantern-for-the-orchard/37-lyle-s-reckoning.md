## 37. Lyle's Reckoning

The letter from the Commission of Inquiry arrived on a Tuesday, which Corvus Lyle would later think fitting, because Tuesdays were the day he had always reserved for correcting other men's errors.

It was a thin letter. Thin letters were always the worst. The thick ones came with charges spelled out, with names and dates and the comfortable architecture of accusation — something a man could stand against, could argue with, could dismantle line by line in his neat, upright hand. But this one held only a single paragraph: *His Majesty's Commissioners request Mr. Corvus Lyle's attendance at Vellamont Guildhall on the fourteenth day of March, to answer certain questions respecting the survey of the Vellamont canal route and the conduct of its historical documentation.*

*Certain questions.* He read it three times at his desk in the commission's rented house on the far bank, where he had lived since the ice allowed him to cross, and then he set it down and looked out the window at the river.

The river was breaking up. It did so with the sound of enormous furniture being moved in an upstairs room no one was permitted to enter — groans, cracks, sudden silences that were worse than the noise. Great plates of ice ground against the bridge pilings and sheared off downstream, white as unmarked paper. In six weeks, perhaps less, the water would be high enough for the barges. In eight months, if the schedule held, the first cut would be dug, and by autumn after that the orchards on the south bank would begin their long drowning.

He had known this when he took the appointment. That was the thing his accusers — whoever they were; the letter named none — would never understand. He had walked into this work with his eyes open, had read the schedules and the displacement figures and the engineering memoranda before he ever set pen to a single page of official history. He had believed, and still believed, that progress was arithmetic, and that arithmetic did not flinch. Someone had to write the record of what was done. Better a careful man than a careless one. Better him than some sentimental clerk who would weep over every apple tree and get the elevations wrong.

And now the surveyors themselves were weeping over elevations.

That was the poison in it. That was the thing he could not stop turning over at night like a stone with a warm underside. Two of his own men — Rennick and the young one, Oakes — had come to him separately, in the embarrassed way subordinates have when they are about to commit a small treason, to say that they had checked the Halloran boy's figures. Checked them twice. The river bends on the Crown's master survey plate were geometrically impossible. A channel could not bend that way and hold its banks. Water did not negotiate. The forgery was not even a good one; it was simply the only shape that put the flood line where the Treasury wanted it, and geometry had been asked to lie down and hold still.

Lyle had thanked them both, coldly, and dismissed them, and sat alone for a long time afterward with his hands flat on the desk, as though steadying himself on a deck in weather.

Because here was the truth he had built his whole career on the refusal of: you could not correct a record that was wrong at its foundation. You could only compound it. Every page he had written about Vellamont sat atop a forged plate the way a cathedral sits atop a swamp, magnificent and doomed, and the gazette in the capital had published the refutation under his own name — his name attached, forever, to the exposure of the fraud his history existed to dignify.

The gazette. He had read it eleven times. It was beautifully done, that was the unbearable part. The technical argument was flawless — the demonstration that the surveyed meanders violated the hydraulic gradient, that no honest instrument could have produced those lines, laid out with such clarity that even a Treasury man could follow it. And beneath it, the testimony of the orchard families, sworn and witnessed, name after name after name, in hands ranging from a priest's precise Latin-trained script to a cooper's blunt carpenter's capitals. The article ended with the words *the true atlas survives*, and Lyle had understood, reading that final sentence, that he had lost in a way no inquiry could reverse. You could imprison a man. You could not imprison forty copies of a document scattered among pilgrims and drovers and brides.

They had made the truth unownable. He had to admire it. He hated that he had to admire it.

* * *

The summons said the fourteenth of March. He went early, because waiting had become intolerable and because he wanted, before anything else, one last look at the original.

They kept it in the guildhall strong room now, under seal, pending the inquiry — the atlas of Aldous Vane, surrendered into his hands two months before by the apprentice Halloran, who had walked across the guildhall floor pale as tallow and laid it on the table with the terrible composure of a man giving up a hostage he has already replaced. Lyle had examined the boy's face for grief and found almost none, and that absence had troubled him more than any weeping would have. Grief he knew how to use. Composure meant the boy had already done whatever he had come to do.

Now the atlas lay under the clerk's seal, and Lyle stood over it with the key in his hand and did not open it for a long moment.

It was a beautiful object. He had always conceded that. Vane had been a superb draftsman — the ink work alone would have earned a place in any royal collection — and the marginalia, once Lyle had learned to read the cipher, revealed a mind of real rigor. A talented eccentric, he had written in his first report, and he had believed it, and the belief had been his first and largest error. Vane had not been eccentric. Vane had been a penitent. The atlas was not a map collection; it was a confession with coordinates.

And Lyle had spent the winter doing to it what the Treasury had done to the survey plate. Correcting it. Aligning it. Adjusting boundaries to match the falsified master, striking the marginalia, annotating the orchard villages as *unencumbered common land, title extinguished by statute*. He had worked carefully. He had worked, God help him, lovingly — because correction was the only craft he had ever truly possessed, and he had brought all of it to bear on making the atlas say what it must say. When he finished, the document was seamless. Any historian in the capital would find in it a model of official cartography. Only he would ever know that every clean line was a scar laid over a wound.

He unlocked the strong room and carried it out under his coat. No one stopped him. That was the privilege of being the commission's historian: no one imagined you would steal your own work.

* * *

The inquiry opened badly and got worse.

He answered well — he always answered well; he had spent thirty years in rooms where precision was armor — but the questions had a new texture. They were not hostile so much as *careful*, and careful questions were the kind that had already been answered elsewhere. Where had the original atlas been during the winter months? Who had custody of the parish records prior to the advance rider's arrival? Why had no copy of the corrected atlas been lodged with the capital office before the gazette's publication? Each question was polite. Each question was a hand closing around his wrist.

His counsel, a dry man named Pellworth imported from the capital, told him afterward, over brandy neither of them drank, "They're building a wall between themselves and the forgery, Lyle. The question is whether you'll be part of the wall or part of the rubble. If you can give them someone below you —"

"There is no one below me."

"Then you'll want to consider how much you can plausibly not have known."

"Not have known." Lyle turned the phrase over. "I signed the historical summary. I certified the boundary annexes. My signature is on the same page as the impossible river."

"Then your position is that you were deceived."

"My position," Lyle said, "is that the river is impossible, and everyone in this town knows it, and the only people pretending otherwise are the men who drew it and the men who paid them, and I am being invited to join the second group by testifying into the first."

Pellworth looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a man watching a client spend his last coin on something worthless. "You could resign," he offered. "Cite health. Before the findings."

"And leave the record to whom?"

"To whom?" Pellworth laughed, briefly and without humor. "Lyle, there is no record. There is a canal. The canal is approved. It will be dug whether you resign or hang or live to be ninety. The record is a courtesy we extend to ourselves. You of all people —"

"Yes," Lyle said quietly. "I of all people."

Pellworth left shortly after. Lyle sat in the commission house listening to the ice go out of the river, and thought about Aldous Vane, whom he had never met, and about the unfinished page the apprentice had mentioned once, obliquely, in one of their winter letters — the page where Vane confessed to a forgery of his own, decades old, and called the atlas his penance. Lyle had read that account and judged the man harshly, in the comfortable way of the unbending judging the bent. *A coward who found courage too late to matter,* he had written in his private notes. He had felt very superior.

Now the notes seemed to him the writings of a man who had never tested himself, congratulating a man who had failed a harder exam.

* * *

On the night before his final appearance before the commissioners, he took the corrected atlas to the grate behind the commission house.

He had planned nothing. That was unusual for him and he noted it, distantly, the way he noted all data: the absence of plan itself a datum. He had simply found, as the evening darkened, that he could not sit in the house another hour with the thing upstairs in his locked trunk, and that the alternative to sitting was standing, and the alternative to standing was walking, and the atlas had come with him without his quite deciding it, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat like a fever against his chest.

The grate was iron, older than the house, built for burning garden refuse and the occasional dead gull. It was perhaps two feet deep. He knelt — his knees complained; he was fifty-eight — and unwrapped the oilcloth, and looked at the atlas in the last grey light.

It was his best work. That was the obscene joke of it. Thirty years of scholarship and method, all of it refined to a single instrument, and the finest product of that instrument was a masterpiece of lying. Every adjustment elegant. Every erasure undetectable. If it were published, it would stand for a century, and schoolchildren would learn the Vellamont orchards as a footnoteless blank, and somewhere in the margins of the world forty scattered copies would rot in priories and solicitors' drawers, and the two versions of history would diverge like a river around a stone, and only one of them would be taught.

He struck a match and held it to the corner of the cover.

The leather caught slowly, the way good leather does, curling and blackening and breathing a smell like a chapel. He fed the fire page by page rather than throwing the whole book in, because he was a careful man, and care does not distinguish between its objects — he burned it the way he had written it, deliberately, sheet by sheet, watching each one catch and curl and go to orange lace and ash. The vellum resisted longest. The maps fought him, the way truth does: the orchard boundaries glowed and held their shapes for seconds past sense, whole villages legible in firelight, before collapsing into flakes that rose on the updraft and settled on his sleeves like grey snow.

He thought, absurdly, of the apprentice. Of the boy's face at the surrender — that composure — and understood it now, fully, the way one understands an opponent's strategy only after checkmate. Halloran had given him the original because the original no longer mattered. The boy had handed him a corpse and let him do with it what victors do. Whatever Lyle wrote or burned or certified, the true atlas was already on the road north and east and west, sewn into coats and packed in cheese wheels and tucked into psalters, traveling at the speed of ordinary people going about their ordinary lives. He had been chasing a body while the soul walked away in forty directions.

And yet he burned it. He understood that too, kneeling in the smoke. Not because it mattered — because it was *his*. Because the alternative was to let it stand in the record under his name, and there was a residue in him, some last insoluble sediment of the scholar he had been before he became the Crown's, that refused — absolutely, finally refused — to sign this one. Let them charge him with destruction of evidence. Let them add it to the list. He had spent a winter making the atlas lie, and he would not spend his reputation making the lie immortal.

Aldous Vane had forged a boundary once, and spent twenty years drawing the truth in secret as penance. Corvus Lyle had spent a career forging nothing with his own hand and everything with his signature, and his penance fit in a garden grate and took eleven minutes.

When it was done he raked the ash flat and sat back on his heels and looked at it. Grey, fine, indistinguishable from any ash. History reduced to its honest state: anonymous.

"You'd have liked him," he said aloud, to no one — meaning Vane, meaning perhaps the boy, meaning the whole stubborn town across the river where the lanterns were coming out one by one above the working quarter, small and numerous and impossible to extinguish individually. "You'd have despised everything I did and liked me anyway. That's the worst of it."

Behind him the river boomed and cracked, shedding its winter. He gathered the oilcloth, empty now, folded it with his usual neatness, and went inside to prepare his statement.

* * *

He testified for four hours the next day. He gave them everything except the fire: the forgery, the pressure, the names of the men who had instructed him, the dates, the memorandum he had kept — because he had kept one; he had always kept records, even of his own corruption, especially of it — documenting each instruction and each compliance. He watched the commissioners' faces move from caution to alarm to the particular grey pallor of officials realizing the wall has come through the window. Pellworth's face he did not watch.

By evening the finding was drafted. By morning it would be public: the survey voided, the boundary annexes withdrawn, a fuller inquiry ordered into the Treasury's drafting office. The canal itself — he had extracted that concession, the last thing he asked for and the only thing they granted easily — remained approved. The law was the law. The water would come regardless. Truth had won the documents and lost the valley, and Lyle, walking back along the far bank in the raw March wind, found that he could hold both facts in his mind at once without either comforting him, which he supposed was what it felt like to be honest.

Piet — the laborer they had arrested in the winter, the cooper's man, held these months as a warning — would walk free within the week. He had seen to that too, quietly, through Pellworth, as one of his last acts of authority. It fixed nothing. It bought nothing. It was simply the only entry left in his ledger that he could make balance.

On his desk that night he found the season's last letter from the apprentice Halloran, forwarded through channels now formally closed. It was brief. It did not gloat, which he had braced himself for and which its absence somehow made worse. It informed him only that the household on the south bank had seen the ice breaking, and that they were preparing for the water as they prepared for everything — together, and with their records in order. It wished him, in a sentence Lyle read five times, *whatever peace is available to a man who tells the truth late.*

Late. Yes. But the atlas had taken Vane twenty years, and the boy had needed a whole winter, and the gazette had needed forty couriers. Perhaps truth was simply the slowest of all the waters, and arrived last, and rose highest.

Lyle took out a fresh sheet of paper, and dipped his pen, and began his resignation — and stopped, hearing through the window the long grinding roar of the river letting go of winter, carrying its broken ice down toward the orchards, toward the stakes, toward the flood line, toward everything that was about to rise.
