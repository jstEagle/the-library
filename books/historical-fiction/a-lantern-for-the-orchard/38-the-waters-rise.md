## 38. The Waters Rise

The water came on a Tuesday, as if it were nothing.

Maren had imagined it would come at night, or in a storm, with some violence she could rage against. Instead it came under a gray sky so mild that a person might have called it pleasant — a slow silver tongue sliding up the lanes of Vellamont's lower quarter, filling the ditches first, then the cart tracks, then the doorways of houses already stripped to their beams. The engineers had opened the sluices at dawn. By midmorning the canal was no longer a thing being built. It was a thing that existed, and everything it touched belonged to it.

She stood on the ridge above the orchard with her ledger under her arm and the whole household around her, and she did not cry. She had decided this the night before, standing at the window with the lantern lit, and she held to it the way she held to anything: stubbornly, without discussion.

Below her, forty years of trees stood in water to their knees.

"They're still standing," Teodor said. He was sixteen now, or near enough — she had stopped counting the winters of found children and started counting what they knew. His voice cracked on *standing*, and he looked away down the ridge as if he hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"For now," said Piet. He had come back from the lockup thinner and quieter, with a cough the damp had given him and a way of watching doors that Maren pretended not to see. "Trees drown slow. Like men."

"Thank you, Piet," said Father Anselm dryly, "for the consolation."

But it was true, and everyone heard it true: the trees would stand there for days yet, green crowns above brown water, an orchard become a fleet of drowning ships. The Crown's men had offered to fell them first. Maren had refused. She could not have said why, at the time — only that she would not give them the satisfaction of stumps, of a clean clearing, of a job finished tidily. Let them watch the leaves turn yellow over three weeks of rising water. Let it be ugly. Ugly got remembered.

Ines Halloran stood a little apart from the rest, the way he always did, as if invisibility were a discipline he practiced daily even though nobody wanted him invisible anymore. He had a sketchbook under his coat. She saw the edge of it. She said nothing. Some things a man needed to do without permission, and recording was one of them; she had learned that about him two winters ago, when he'd confessed the atlas in her kitchen with his hands shaking around a cup of tea he never drank.

The wind came up the valley and moved through the drowned trees, and for a moment the whole orchard seemed to shiver — every tree at once, a long ripple running from the river gate to the high wall — and Marta, the cooper's wife, made a small sound and pressed her hand flat against her sternum.

"It's just wind," said her husband.

"I know what it is," Marta said. "It's just wind." But she kept her hand where it was.

Maren opened the ledger.

* * *

She had begun it eleven years ago, in the spring after her husband died, with no higher purpose than arithmetic: what the orchard owed, what the orchard was owed. Debts in apples, debts in labor, debts in kindness that never appeared in any guild book. Over the years it had grown into something else — a census of the displaced, a register of the dead, a record of harvests that no assessor had ever asked for because no assessor had ever believed the orchard villages produced anything worth counting.

That had been the whole trick of the Crown's survey, Ines had explained once, pacing her kitchen floor with a compass in his hand like a weapon. Not forgery first — erasure first. You don't falsify a boundary until you've convinced everyone the land inside it is blank. Blank land doesn't grieve. Blank land doesn't testify.

Well. This land testified.

"Names," she said, and her voice carried on the ridge wind better than she expected. The household quieted. Even the younger ones, who had been throwing pebbles at the waterline, came and sat in the wet grass.

She read the way she always read: plainly, without performance, each name given its full weight and then released. The founders first, the ones whose hands had put the first stock in the ground before anyone living was born — Halloway, Ostrevant, Kettleby, the three families whose deeds Father Anselm had hidden in the chapel cellar through the last seizure and paid his post for it. Then the generations after, row upon row, and beside each name the row of trees they had planted or grafted or saved through some killing frost, because in the ledger a person and their trees were entered together, the way they'd lived.

"Halloran," she read, at the turn of the century, and glanced sideways without meaning to.

"That's not me," Ines said quickly. "No relation. I checked. I checked years ago."

"You would have," Piet said, and someone laughed, and the laugh broke something open in a good way, the way laughter does at funerals — not disrespect but survival.

She read the plague year, when half the east rows had gone untended and old Sef Ostrevant had walked the rows alone every day for a season so the trees wouldn't think themselves abandoned. She read the great hail of '41 and the names of those who had climbed into the crowns with sacking and tar. She read her husband, Bram, entered twice — once for the grafts he'd made that still bore, and once, in her own hand, in ink slightly different, for the man himself, the year she'd lost him. She had never been able to decide which entry hurt more, and reading both aloud, she understood finally that she wasn't supposed to choose.

The water rose while she read. She watched it between pages. It had taken the lane entirely now, and was working at the roots of the low trees, and the reflection of the orchard lay trembling on its surface like a second orchard, upside down, already drowned.

When she reached the last page — the page she'd left blank for eleven years, telling herself she didn't know what went there — she stopped.

"What is it?" Teodor asked.

"The last line," Maren said. "I never wrote the last line."

Father Anselm took off his hat. Ines shifted, and she saw that his hand had gone inside his coat, not to the sketchbook — to the master index sewn there, the piece of the atlas he'd never given up, the one thing that had stayed against his ribs through the raid and the trial-by-letter and Lyle's smiling offers and the surrender at the guildhall. She had seen him touch it the way other men touched relics.

"Then don't write it today," Anselm said gently. "A ledger can wait a day. It's waited this long."

"No," Maren said. "Today's the day it should be written. Today's the only day it *could* be written."

She thought about it while the wind moved the drowned crowns again. Then she knelt right there in the grass, set the ledger on her knee, and uncapped her ink bottle with her teeth.

The last line, when she wrote it, was short. She read it back to them so they'd all be witnesses:

*The orchard ends. The record does not.*

* * *

They stayed until dark, which nobody had planned. Fires were built on the ridge — small ones, legal-sized, the kind that wouldn't draw notice from the engineer's camp across the water — and food came up from the town in baskets: bread, hard cheese, a crock of the last cider from the pressing house, which Piet guarded like a sacrament and poured out thin measures all around. Even the children got a finger's depth in tin cups, and even the children drank it solemnly, understanding without being told that this cider had come off those trees and there would be no more.

Down below, the water kept coming. It made almost no sound. That was the worst of it, Maren thought — not a roar, not a crash, just a patient creeping shine, swallowing the wheel ruts, the stile, the low wall, the bench where Bram used to sit with his knife and a stick of grafting wax. She watched the bench go under and felt something in her chest that was not defeat. She examined the feeling carefully, turning it over like a coin, because she wanted to know what it was.

It was grief, certainly. But grief with the bottom cut out of it. Grief that had somewhere to go.

For eleven years she had kept accounts because keeping accounts was how she prayed — she'd admitted that to Anselm once, drunk on communion wine at Midwinter, and he'd said that half the psalms were ledgers anyway, God apparently fond of lists. And now the account was closing, the orchard written off, the balance transferred — to what? To paper. To copies in a priory strongbox and a solicitor's office and a newspaper's files in the capital. To forty couriers who had walked out of this town in the thaw carrying pages they couldn't read, sealed and sworn, none of them knowing the whole. To a gazette article that had made Lyle's own surveyors concede, in writing, that the river on the official map bent in ways rivers do not bend.

The lie was dead. The Gazette had killed it in March. And still the water came, because the canal was approved by men who had known it was founded on a forgery and approved it anyway, and the law, having been shown the truth, had shrugged.

"We lost," said young Doretta, one of the newer ones, a girl of twelve who had never known any home but the ridge road. She said it without drama, testing the sentence to see how it fit.

"Half-true," Piet said. "Best kind of sentence to be careful with."

"How is it half-true?"

Piet considered, cup in hand. "Because losing is when they take a thing and it stays taken and nobody knows it was ever there." He nodded at the water, at the trees, at the ledger on Maren's knee. "Everybody knows. Priories know. Newspapers know. Parliament will know by summer, whether they admit it or not. They've got the land." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "We've got the fact of it. I'd rather have the fact."

"You would," said his brother.

"Yes," Piet agreed, "I would, and look what it cost me, and I'd do it again, and that's how you know."

Ines had come to sit near Maren without seeming to move, the way he did. For a while neither spoke. The fire cracked. Down in the dark, the water shone faintly where it caught the sky's last light, and the trees stood black against it.

"My master drew these trees," Ines said at last. "Every one. He measured trunk circumference and row spacing and yield per bough, because that's what a mapmaker does, he reduces a thing to numbers. It took me years to understand he was doing something else with the numbers. That the measurements were — " He stopped, hunting for the word.

"A disguise," Maren said. "Love dressed up as arithmetic, so it would survive inspection."

Ines looked at her. "Yes. Exactly that."

"He sounds like he'd have liked you."

"He fired me twice."

"And hired you back three times, which nets out ahead. That's the orchard way of counting, if you'll recall." She closed the ledger. "Come to the house tomorrow. There's work."

"What work?"

"The kind we're going to be doing for the rest of our lives, apparently." She stood, brushing grass from her skirt, and looked down at the drowned orchard one more time. "Somebody has to keep it. The record. Somebody has to keep it where people can reach it — not in priory vaults, not in capital offices. Here. Above the cooper's shop, maybe. Somewhere with poor light and cheap rent." She smiled despite herself. "I hear there's a room going."

Ines stared at her. "You've been planning this."

"Since the Gazette printed your refutation and you turned down the guild's offer to take you back." She picked up the ledger. "You said no to the safest life in this county, Ines Halloran. A man who does that has already decided what he's for. The rest of us are just catching up."

* * *

Near midnight, most of the household had gone down to the town, and the fires had burned to coals, and only a few remained on the ridge: Maren, Anselm, Teodor, Ines, Piet sitting up despite his cough because he refused on principle to leave a fire unattended.

Teodor had not spoken in an hour. He sat closest to the slope, knees drawn up, looking at the water. Then he stood, and went down the slope a little way — not far, just to the last dry ground, where the boundary stone stood. The old marker, Halloway-cut, that had defined the orchard's corner for a hundred and sixty years. The water was a hand's width below its top.

He crouched beside it and put his palm flat on the stone, the way you'd steady a horse.

"It'll be under by morning," he said. His voice was steady. Too steady. "By Thursday it'll be ten feet down. Nobody will see it again."

"No," Maren agreed.

"So I'm going to learn it." He turned around. In the coal-light his face was fierce and wet-eyed and completely certain. "The shape of it. Every bend of the wall, every row, every root that sticks out. I'm going to carry it the way Papa carries the index — in the body, where nobody can confiscate it. And someday, when they let people dig here, or dive here, or whatever they do with drowned towns — I'll be able to say exactly what's down there and where. Every stone. Every tree."

Nobody laughed. Nobody said *you're too young*. Piet raised his tin cup a finger's width, and Anselm murmured something that sounded like a blessing wearing ordinary clothes.

Ines got up, crossed to the boy, and held out his hand — and in it was the compass, the brass one, Vane's compass, the one he'd used to prove the forged survey impossible. Teodor looked at it and didn't move.

"I can't—"

"You can. It's not mine to keep. It was never Vane's to keep either; he'd have said so. It belongs to whoever's measuring next." Ines pressed it into the boy's hand and folded the fingers shut over it. "Learn the geometry too. Not just the shapes — the angles. If they ever forge another map over this ground, I want there to be someone alive who can prove it false in an afternoon."

Teodor's hand closed around the compass, and he nodded once, hard, and turned back to the water, and that was the whole ceremony.

Maren felt the tears come then, finally, standing in the dark with her ledger dry under her arm — tears she'd held off all day, and they came easily now, without weight, because everything they were for had already been written down. The land was lost. The record was not. She had written it herself, in front of witnesses, and the witnesses were the kind who kept things.

Below them, the water reached the top of the boundary stone sometime before dawn, though only Teodor stayed to see it.

And it was Ines, walking down toward the town with the others, who stopped halfway along the ridge path and looked back once at the black shining water and the blacker crowns, and said quietly — to Maren, or to the orchard, or to no one:

"There's a lantern in my window tonight. First night. Come morning, we start cataloguing."

Maren looked at him. "Cataloguing what? It's all gone."

"No," Ines said, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before — not grief, not defiance, something steadier than either, something that sounded almost like a man reading the first line of a very long book. "Now the real work starts. Now we write down everything they flooded *besides* the trees."
