## 39. A Lantern for the Orchard

The guild's letter came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy who would not meet Ines's eye.

*By resolution of the Master and Wardens,* it read, *Mr. Ines Halloran is restored to his post as journeyman mapmaker, with all privileges appertaining, in recognition of services rendered to the truth of the Crown survey.*

Ines read it twice at the table above the cooper's shop while Maren kneaded bread and Piet — home eleven days now, thinner, quieter, but home — mended a stave by the window.

"Services rendered," Maren said, not looking up from her work. "That's one way to say it."

"They had to say something," Ines said. "The gazette printed the refutation. Their own surveyors conceded it. If they dismissed me outright after that, every guild in the province would know they'd been caught forging plates."

"So take it back, then." Piet blew a shaving off the stave. "Your post. Your bench. Your name in their book again."

Ines looked at the letter a long moment. It was written on good paper, in a fair hand, and it was an apology in everything but the word — which was exactly what made it worthless. An apology would have named what was done. This named only what was given back.

He fed it to the stove.

Maren watched it curl and said nothing, but she set a second loaf aside, the small dark one she baked for him because he'd once admitted, drunk on cider and honesty, that he liked the crust burnt.

"I'm going to need the room upstairs," he said. "The whole floor of it. And I'm going to need your help asking Father Anselm a question."

* * *

The question was this: who keeps a town's memory when no institution will?

He put it to them in the chapel cellar, where the sworn testimony had been taken through those last desperate weeks of winter — orchard families dictating names and boundaries and grievances into Ines's careful hand while Father Anselm married each page to the old deeds from the hidden strongbox. The cellar still smelled of tallow and cold stone. Eleven people sat among the crates: Maren, Piet, Teodor, the Widow Sarelle who had lost forty trees and a husband to the same bad year, the drover Brannoc who had carried the western sheets out in a saddlebag under a false bottom, the pilgrim Osla, two of the Kettleby cousins, old Tammas whose father had planted rows the water now covered, and a girl of nineteen named Jena whom Ines barely knew except as the fastest copyist he'd ever trained.

"The canal is law," Ines said. "We fought the lie and we won the lie's defeat, but the water is coming anyway. The commission fractured, but it didn't die. Lyle burned his own atlas rather than sign his name to it, and the Crown will send someone else with cleaner hands and a duller conscience, and in five years there will be an official map of Vellamont parish with the orchards on it marked *submerged — former common land, title extinguished.* Former. Extinguished. Those are the words that will survive, if nothing else does."

"Then we write something else down," Maren said. It wasn't a suggestion. It never was, with her.

"That's what I'm proposing." He spread Vane's master index on the crate between them — the one thing he'd kept sewn into his coat through all of it, the paper soft as cloth now from handling. "My master spent twenty years recording these villages because he knew what was coming and couldn't stop it. He couldn't stop it, so he *recorded* it. Every boundary, every tree row, every family that held title and how they held it. He did it alone, in cipher, in fear, and it nearly died with him. It survived because when the time came, it didn't stay alone. It went out in thirty hands, and not one of those hands could be made to give up what it carried, because none of them held the whole."

Tammas squinted at him. "You're saying we should do it again?"

"I'm saying we should do it *properly.* Not a smuggled atlas. A standing record. The deeds Anselm saved. The testimony we took. My refutation, with the surveyor's concessions. Maren's ledger — every harvest figure, every debt owed to orchard families going back sixty years. Copies of everything, here in Vellamont, kept by us, open to anyone who comes asking. Not locked in a guild archive where a steward can sell it. Not in a chapel strongbox where one man can die and take it with him. Here." He tapped the floorboards, meaning, though they hadn't chosen it yet, the room above the cooper's shop.

Silence, the particular silence of people deciding.

Piet spoke first. He'd been arrested as a warning, held six weeks in the lockup, and come out of it with a habit of pausing before words, weighing them like timber. "They came through my shop with axes," he said. "Looking for papers. They'll come again if you put them back in my rafters."

"They'll come anywhere," Ines said. "But this time there's nothing to find, because this time there isn't one hiding place. There are eleven. You each keep a duplicate key and a portion of the collection. If the room burns, the record doesn't. That's the lesson my master paid for and I nearly repeated — never let the whole live in one place or one pair of hands."

"And who's the keeper?" Jena asked. "Of the room itself. Somebody has to sit there. Answer questions. Keep the ink from freezing."

Every head turned toward him, and Ines felt the familiar panic of a man trained to be invisible suddenly seen by everyone — but it was an old panic now, worn smooth, almost comfortable. He had answered harder questions than this in a guildhall with Corvus Lyle smiling across the table.

"I am," he said. "If you'll have me. I've been offered my old post and refused it. I have no other trade worth the name. And I—" he stopped, started again. "My master built his atlas out of guilt. I'd like to build this out of something better."

Nobody applauded. That wasn't the way of that room. But Tammas reached over and turned the master index so it faced him, and began, slowly, to read the first entry aloud — *Vane, Aldous, commenced in the year of grace 1831, being the true bounds of Hallowmere Orchard* — and that was the founding, though no bell rang and no charter was signed. The record of the founding is the reading itself, which Ines entered that same evening as the archive's first item.

* * *

It took until midsummer to make the room ready, and longer than that to make it trustworthy.

The floor above the cooper's shop had been a loft for stave timber, then empty since the raid. Piet rebuilt the stairs himself so they could bear the weight of iron chests. Ines bought glass for the windows with the last of the money the capital solicitor had sent — payment for his deposition in the inquiry against the commission, money he'd expected to refuse until Maren told him that refusing honest wages out of pride was just another way of keeping the record poor. They shelved the walls in ash and oak. Anselm brought the deed strongbox up from the chapel cellar in broad daylight, openly, daring anyone to object, and found that no one dared. Being right, it turned out, was armor of a kind, once enough people had seen you proved right in print.

The collection grew the way Maren said real collections grow — not by plan but by confession. A widow arrived with her grandmother's planting book, its margins full of grafting notes. A drover brought a tally stick three feet long, cut with the count of livestock driven over the bridge road for forty years. Someone left, anonymously, a bundle of letters from orchard sons who'd gone to the wars, and Ines catalogued them at night with his coat on because the sender might come back wanting them, and they were hers to reclaim, whoever she was.

He wrote the catalogue rules himself, and Maren vetted them, and between them they arrived at four:

*Nothing leaves the room without a copy remaining. No name in the record may be struck at anyone's request, including the named person's. Any resident of the parish may read anything, any day. The keeper keeps no secrets from the household.*

Teodor read the fourth rule and laughed at him. "You? No secrets? You lied to us for a whole winter."

"I did."

"You're going to write that down somewhere? That the keeper lies?"

"It's already written down." Ines nodded at the shelf where the original atlas's surviving fragments lay — the sheets Lyle had never gotten, the ones the couriers carried out, slowly finding their way home now that it was safe. "Item forty-one. Marginal note in Vane's hand, dated fourteen years ago: *An apprentice must learn concealment before he learns candour, for the second is useless to a man who cannot survive long enough to practice it.* I keep it where I can see it. So I remember both lessons are mine."

Teodor considered this with the gravity he gave everything, which even at thirteen was considerable. Then: "Can I have a key too?"

"You're too young to—"

"To carry a page, maybe. In the winter. When it mattered." The boy's jaw did the thing it did. "Not to carry a key in summer."

Ines looked at him and saw the argument was already lost, had probably been lost the day Teodor pulled a survey stake out of frozen ground with his bare hands. He cut a key himself, taught the boy the wax impression for the lock, and entered him in the register as *junior keeper, admitted by consent of the household.* Teodor copied the entry out in his own hand, twice, one copy for the shelf and one for the inside of his coat, and Ines pretended not to see him do it and loved him helplessly for it.

* * *

The lantern was Maren's idea, though Ines took the blame for it gladly.

They argued about the window first. He wanted plain glass, unmarked, a room that looked like any room — invisibility being the instinct of a lifetime. She stood in the empty loft with her arms crossed and looked at the west window, the one facing down toward the river, toward the drowned ground.

"People will walk past," she said. "People from the villages. Displaced families scattered up the valley, working other men's land now. One day one of them will come to this town because they heard something — that the records exist, that somebody kept the names. And they'll stand in this street not knowing which door. And if there's nothing in the window, they'll walk past, and they won't come back, because coming back costs more than most people have. So there has to be something in the window."

"A sign," Ines said. "I'll paint a board. *Parish Archive.*"

"A board tells them what the room is. It doesn't tell them why." She was quiet a moment, and he knew her well enough now to wait. "The night we finished the copying — the long night — we worked by orchard lanterns. The ones from the packing sheds, with the wire guards. Half the household's lanterns came out of those orchards. We hung them along the beams and copied by them till dawn, and when it was done nobody wanted to blow them out. Piet's mother lit one in her window the night the water came up over the low rows. Just set it there and left it burning till the oil ran out. Said the trees shouldn't go dark unwitnessed." She turned to him. "Hang a lantern in that window. Keep it lit. Let it mean what it means."

So he did.

He bought the lantern from the Kettleby cousins — an orchard lantern, wire-guarded, its glass smoked amber from years of fruit-shed smoke — and hung it on a bracket outside the west window, and lit it the evening the archive opened. Oil was expensive. Piet solved that by making it a condition of his apprenticeship fees that every boy he trained contributed a pint a season, and after a while people simply brought oil the way they brought eggs to the chapel, without being asked, and the lantern never went dark. Ines trimmed its wick every morning and cleaned its glass every evening, and came to understand that this small labor was not separate from the keeping of the record but identical to it: a daily act of attention, performed whether or not anyone saw, because the seeing was not the point. The point was that the light was there when needed.

On the first of October, someone needed it.

A woman came up the hill road at dusk, travel-worn, leading a donkey, and stood in the street below the window looking at the light for a long time before she knocked. She was from Hallowmere — one of the first-flooded rows, land gone under the spring before last. She had heard, from a cousin of a cousin, that in Vellamont there was a place that kept the names. Her father had held title to twelve acres and she had his papers, water-stained, and no idea whether they meant anything anymore, and no one in the new parish they'd settled in had ever heard of Hallowmere at all.

Ines sat with her past midnight. He found her father in the atlas — *Hallowmere, east third, holder: Rennick, twelve acres, deed witnessed 1838* — and found the deed itself in Anselm's boxes, and found, in Maren's ledger, the harvest figures from the last good year, and copied all of it for her, certified, and entered her visit in the day-book.

She wept at the table, quietly, the way people wept over documents, and he understood that too now. It was not grief exactly. It was the relief of existing in someone's book.

When she left, near dawn, she looked back at the lantern from the street.

"How long will you keep it burning?" she asked.

"As long as there's oil," Ines said. "And there'll always be oil."

* * *

Winter came again, the second winter, easier than the first the way second winters are — the household hardened to cold, the archive warm from the cooper's fire rising through the floor, the work steady. Teodor grew half a head and learned to draw boundaries in the Vane manner, which was to say honestly, tolerances noted, uncertainties confessed on the face of the map rather than hidden beneath it. Jena took over the copying of incoming material so Ines could draft. Piet's shop filled the street below with the smell of oak shavings, and the sound of it came up through the boards like slow speech.

On a grey afternoon in February, Ines sat cataloguing when the knock came — and he still flinched at knocks, and supposed he always would, and had decided that was acceptable; a keeper who flinched was a keeper who remembered.

It was the post boy, with a single letter, franked from the capital.

He recognized the hand before he broke the seal, because he had dueled that hand across a hundred pages and would know it anywhere — not Lyle's, Lyle was done, Lyle had burned his own atlas and resigned his commission and gone wherever such men go. No. This was a priory hand, a copying-office hand, round and patient. But the letter within was signed by a prior he'd never met, and its contents stopped him standing at the window with the light going gold behind him.

*Reverend Sir,* it began. *Our house has for some months been gathering the scattered portions of an atlas concerning the orchard parishes of Vellamont. Pages have come to us by pilgrim, by drover, and by one bride whose wedding procession passed through your town in the spring two years past. We hold now nine parts of what we understand to be a larger whole. We are told the master index remains with the keeper of the record at Vellamont, and that he is the only living soul who knows what the whole comprised.*

*We propose to publish. All of it. With provenance, with testimony, with the geometric refutation entire. And we are advised — firmly — by every carrier of every page that the front matter must bear the names of all who carried it, kept it, swore to it, and suffered for it. We cannot proceed correctly without the index. We therefore ask: will the keeper bring it to us, or shall we come to the keeper?*

*There is a further matter, which we leave to your discretion. Among the pages that reached us was one in a hand we believe to be Aldous Vane's own, being a confession, unfinished. Whether it ought to be published alongside his maps is a question we would not answer without you.*

Ines read it three times. Down in the shop, the tapping of Piet's mallet went on, unhurried, certain. Below the window the lantern was already lit against the early dusk, amber-smoked glass glowing over the street where a woman had once stood weeping over her father's name.

He put on his coat, sewed index and all, and went downstairs to read it aloud to the household.
