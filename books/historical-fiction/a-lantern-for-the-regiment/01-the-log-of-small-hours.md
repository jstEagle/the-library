## 1. The Log of Small Hours

The light at Gullwrack Point turned its long arm across the water every seven and a half seconds, and in twenty years Noor Zhou had never once missed a turn.

Not that anyone would know. The logbook recorded it — *Lamp lit 17:42, weather clearing from WNW, visibility good* — but a logbook is a ledger of duties, not a diary of devotion. It did not record that Noor woke before the alarm, as they always did, lying in the dark of the keeper's room listening for the sea's mood the way other men listened for a wife's breathing. It did not record that Noor climbed the ninety-one steps to the lantern room each evening with the particular pleasure of a person entering a church where no congregation could follow. The stairs knew their feet. That was enough.

On the third of November, 1917, the wind came up out of the southwest an hour after noon, and by four o'clock the horizon had gone the color of wet slate.

Noor stood at the gallery rail with a cup of tea going cold in one hand and watched the front assemble itself over the water. There was a craft to reading weather that no meteorological office had yet improved upon, and Noor had learned it the way all lighthouse people learn everything: slowly, expensively, and alone. This was not yet the great storm. This was only the herald of it, the outrider — a low bruised band of cloud trailing rain-veils like the hem of a gown, and beneath it the sea already changing its mind, the swells coming longer and more deliberate, as though something vast had begun to walk toward them from the edge of the world.

"Two days," Noor said aloud, because twenty years of solitude makes a habit of speech, or perhaps unmakes the habit of silence; they had never decided which. "Three, if she's lazy."

The gulls disagreed, wheeling inland in ragged complaint. Gulls always knew first.

* * *

The station at Gullwrack was not large. A tower of whitewashed stone, eighty-six feet to the vane; a keeper's cottage fastened to its flank like a barnacle; a store shed, a boat slip, a rain cistern greened with moss on the shaded side; and around it all, two acres of wind-scoured headland where nothing grew taller than the thrift and the sea-pink, which grew anyway, stubbornly, in the crevices of the rock, blooming out of season and out of hope. Beyond that, water. On clear days, the coast of the mainland lay to the east like a pencil line someone had drawn and then regretted — six miles, no more, and yet on nights like this it might as well have been another century.

That was the arrangement Noor had made with the world, and the world had kept it better than most marriages keep theirs: *you stay out there, I'll stay here, and neither of us will ask what the other does in the dark.*

The war lived out there. Noor permitted it the mainland, the newspapers' gray columns, the wireless bulletins read aloud in the supply boat's cabin by young men whose voices cracked on the casualty lists. The war was allowed its France, its Flanders, its Atlantic convoys and its U-boats and its endless arithmetic of names. It could have all of it. What it could not have was the tower, or the lamp, or the small hours.

Because history happened elsewhere. That was the creed. Noor had built it stone by stone, the way the Board had built the tower, on a foundation nobody saw and everybody trusted. History happened in capitals, in trenches, in parliaments and palaces — in places where crowds stood close enough to push one another. Here there was only weather, which made no speeches and took no sides. A storm did not care whom you had been. A storm asked only whether you were competent, and Noor was competent down to the marrow. Twenty years without a wreck on the Gullwrack rocks. Twenty years without so much as a reprimand in the file. Somewhere in Edinburgh or London sat a drawer containing Noor Underhill's entire official existence — appointment letter, service record, a commendation from '09 for the rescue of two fishermen off Skerrie Head — and every page of it true except the name at the top, and even the name was truer than anyone guessed, being merely half of one, the half that fit.

Underhill. It had been easy to become. Easy names are always borrowed ones.

Noor finished the tea, went inside, and began the evening round.

* * *

There is a rhythm to keeping a light, and it is not unlike nursing, or mothering, or any of the other offices that consist chiefly of doing the same necessary thing over and over while pretending it costs nothing. At dusk, the wick — the incandescent burner now, since the Board's modernization of '13, a mantle like a small captive moon — must be trimmed and lit. The clockwork that rotated the lens, four tons of French glass riding on a bath of mercury, must be wound: one hundred and forty turns, no fewer, or the mechanism would falter in the night and the flash-character change, and somewhere a master might mistake Gullwrack for Corran Ness and put his ship on the Skerries. Mercury, too, must be checked, and the lens polished — brasses rubbed, glass wiped with chamois dampened in spirit, the whole apparatus kept in a state of cleanliness that bordered on ceremony. Noor performed these acts the way a monk performs his hours: not because God required them precisely so, but because the precision itself was the prayer.

By 5:15 the light was burning. By 5:30 the fog signal apparatus — a reed horn, compressed air, ready to speak when the mist spoke first — had been tested and found willing. Noor ate supper standing at the kitchen window: bread, tinned mackerel, the last of the tomatoes from the cold frame, eaten slowly because there was no reason not to. Outside, the first spit of rain came against the glass like fingertips testing a door.

After supper: the log.

Noor wrote standing at the deal table in the living room, the lamp turned low, the pen moving in a hand trained long ago into neatness. *Nov. 3rd. Wind freshening SW, force 6 by evening, rising. Barometer falling steadily, 29.4 ins. and unsteady. Sea getting up from SW. Lamp lit 17:12. All well.*

All well. The two words carried more freight than the Board ever suspected. They were the whole treaty between Noor and the world, renewed nightly, countersigned by habit. *All well* meant the lamp burned and the keeper lived and nothing had come ashore out of the dark to alter either condition. Twenty years of *all well*, seven thousand three hundred entries, and not once had Noor written anything else — not because nothing else had ever been true, but because writing anything else would be an invitation, and Noor had spent two decades declining invitations.

They blew out the lamp, took the storm lantern from its hook, and climbed back up for the middle watch.

* * *

The lantern room at night was Noor's true country.

Below, the cottage held its small warmth, the stove ticking, the kettle sulking on its plate. But up here, behind the astragal bars, the world opened. The great lens turned with a sound like a slow breath — *hsssshh... hsssshh... hsssshh* — seven and a half seconds per revolution, the beam swinging out across the blackening water, touching the wave-tops for an instant, letting go. Rain had begun in earnest now, arriving in slanting volleys that rattled on the glazing like gravel thrown by a beggar. Through it the mainland's line of lights had vanished entirely; Gullwrack was an island tonight in fact as well as law.

Noor sat in the keeper's chair — a thing of iron and wood, older than the tower's paint, shaped by two previous keepers and now permanently to the shape of a third — and let the rhythm take hold. Flash. Darkness. Flash. Darkness. There were those who found it maddening, the metronome of it, and Noor understood that; there had been a time, twenty years ago, when any rhythm at all had felt like a cage closing. But a cage closes on you only if you're trying to get out, and Noor had gotten out, hadn't they? Had walked away clean, taken the coast road south with one canvas bag and a new name drying in the mouth like a coin, and had never once looked back.

Never once looked back. Well. The phrase was a lie the way *all well* was a truth: accurate in the log, incomplete in the flesh. One did look back. One looked back every time the light swept west and caught, for a quarter-second, the far hills inland — hills that belonged to a country Noor had renounced and a family Noor had buried under the same spade-stroke. One looked back, and then one trimmed the wick, and the looking-back was folded away like linen, pressed flat, stored in the dark of a chest that did not officially exist.

The trick — and Noor had mastered it as thoroughly as the clockwork, better than the clockwork — was to make the folding automatic. To feel the old grief arrive on schedule and dismiss it on schedule, the way the lens arrived at north and dismissed north for northwest. Emotion, too, could be kept to a character: one fixed flash, regular intervals, visible for miles, illuminating nothing.

Out in the dark, the sea was building. Noor could hear it now even through the glass — not the ordinary gossip of waves on the rocks below but a deeper sound underneath, a long continuous muttering, the sound of a very large animal turning over in its sleep. The barometer in the corner of the lantern room had fallen another tenth. Noor consulted it the way a physician consults a pulse and wrote nothing, because there was nothing yet to write. Tomorrow, perhaps. Tomorrow the outer bands would arrive, and the day after, if the glass kept falling, the proper storm — a full gale, maybe worse, the kind that closed the crossing for a week and left Gullwrack alone with itself, provisioned and self-sufficient and utterly cut off, which was, if Noor were honest (and honesty, in the small hours, was a vice Noor permitted), rather the point of the place.

Cut off. Safe. Invisible. Three words for one condition.

* * *

At midnight Noor went down for the log entry and found themselves lingering in the kitchen, feeding the stove a lump of coal and watching it catch. There was bread to bake tomorrow, if the storm allowed the oven its heat; oil to decant from the forty-gallon drums in the store; the gallery rail wanted painting before spring, if spring remembered this coast. A list of tasks assembled itself in Noor's head, orderly and soothing, and Noor followed it down its length the way a child follows a garden path, and at the end of it — as always, as inevitably — the path stopped at a low wall, and beyond the wall was the country where the list could not go.

Twenty years. In that country there had been a brother — there had been a crime, and the crime had had a name, and Noor had carried the name out of the house one night and drowned it in a harbor town and walked out of the water wearing another. There had been a sister. Wren, who was nine years younger and had stood on the step with her arms wrapped around herself, saying nothing, saying everything, her face doing what faces do when they understand a betrayal completely and cannot decide whether to forgive it.

Noor had not written. Wren had not written. The silence between them had grown its own architecture — walls, rooms, wings — until it was less an absence than a residence, and Noor lived in it the way one lives anywhere: by not opening doors.

The stove ticked. Upstairs, faint through the ceiling, the mechanism breathed its patient *hsssshh*. Noor banked the fire, set the kettle within reach of morning, and climbed the ninety-one steps again, counting them as always without meaning to, the way one counts scars.

* * *

The watch from two until dawn was the hardest and the dearest.

The storm's herald arrived in earnest around three — a shift of wind into the south, hard gusts that leaned on the tower and made the whole structure hum a note below hearing, rain driving horizontally past the glass in silver ropes. Noor walked the gallery twice, roped at the rail in accordance with regulations that existed because some keeper, somewhere, had died learning them, and checked the ventilators, and stood a moment facing the sea with the spray coming over like thrown gravel.

And there — far out, at the limit of vision, where the beam swung and caught — Noor saw the swell running. Not whitecaps. Swell. Long, organized, mountainous, marching in from the southwest with the unhurried confidence of something that had crossed an ocean to get here. A groundswell like that meant weather still gathering beyond the horizon, weight still accumulating in the deep. The real storm had not yet been born. It was out there in the dark, gestating, and when it came it would come hungry.

Noor went back inside, dried off, and sat again in the iron chair. Flash. Darkness. Flash.

It occurred to them — not for the first time, but with unusual force tonight, perhaps because of the pressure in the air, which always loosened something in the chest — that this was the life entire. That this chair, this glass, this rhythm, this deliberate exile, was not the antechamber to some other existence but the existence itself, and that Noor had chosen it so completely that there was no longer any choosing in it, only the performance of choice, renewed nightly, like the lighting of the lamp. A person could live a whole life inside a sentence. *Keeper, Gullwrack Point.* Seven thousand mornings of waking alone. Seven thousand evenings of *all well.*

And yet — the thought arrived unwelcome, dripping, like a gull that had found the one broken pane — and yet the chest had that loosened feeling, that pre-storm feeling, the feeling of a door somewhere in the house not quite latched. Noor examined the sensation with professional suspicion and found no cause for it. The glass was falling, certainly. The sea was talking. But the unease was not about weather; Noor knew weather the way priests know Latin. It was something older and less chartable, a sense of the tide going out very far and very quietly, farther than it ought, the way it goes out before—

Before.

Noor laughed at themselves, softly, and the laugh was swallowed by the wind. Twenty years of superstition had not cured it; solitude had only refined it. Very well. The barometer said storm; the body said *something is coming*; the log said *all well*. Two of the three would prove wrong, and Noor had long ago learned which one to bet against.

Dawn came late and colorless, the light rising like a bruise surfacing on skin. Noor made the final entries — *Wind S force 7, gusting. Sea very heavy from SW. Glass 28.9 and falling fast. Lamp extinguished 07:03* — and paused, pen lifted, over the last blank space where the remarks went.

For a moment — a moment only, a single revolution of the lens — Noor considered writing the true sentence, the one that had been assembling itself all night somewhere below the level of words: *Something is coming that will end the small hours.*

Then the pen came down, and the training held, as it always held, as it had held for twenty years.

*All well,* Noor wrote.

Outside, the first great squall hit the tower like a fist, and the gulls, all of them together, rose screaming off the headland and fled inland — fleeing not the storm, which birds can ride, but whatever the storm was carrying ahead of it, the way the sea carries wreckage, the way currents carry things that have been let go, or lost, or sent.

Noor stood at the window with the pen still in hand and watched them go, and did not yet know why the sight of it raised the hair along both arms.

Two days later, the answer would be lying on the doorstep, half-drowned, with a note pinned to its coat.
