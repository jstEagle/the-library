## 2. A Parcel of Weather

The storm had been announcing itself for three days before it arrived.

Noor Zhou had learned to read the sea the way other people read letters — patiently, twice over, looking for what was written between the lines. On Tuesday the gulls had gone strange, wheeling inland in ragged spirals instead of working the tideline. On Wednesday the water off Gullwrack Point turned a bruised green that Noor had seen only four times in eleven years at the light, and each time it had meant weather worth writing to the Board about. By Thursday morning the barometer needle had fallen so far and so fast that it seemed to be trying to escape its own glass, and Noor stood in the lamp room with a mug of tea going cold in one hand and thought: *there you are.*

The great storms came rarely enough to feel almost ceremonial. Twice a winter, sometimes once, sometimes — in a merciful year — not at all. They came up from the southwest like something with an errand, and they stayed as long as they pleased, and when they left they took pieces of the coast with them: a section of fence, a stack of creels, once an entire boathouse that belonged to nobody and was claimed by nobody afterward. The mainlanders spoke of these storms the way they spoke of the war, which was to say constantly and without comprehension. *Terrible business out there.* As if the weather were a front line. As if anyone could be sent to fight it or recalled from it.

Noor did not fear the storms. This was the closest thing to pride that Noor permitted, and even pride had to be kept small, folded flat, tucked into a pocket where it wouldn't show through the cloth. Fear required stakes, and Noor had spent twenty years carefully divesting. The light had to burn; that was duty, not stake. The log had to be kept in a fair hand; that was discipline, not self. When the storms came, Noor battened the shutters on the seaward side, coiled the spare rope, filled the oil reservoirs past their marks, and waited with the particular patience of a person who has already survived the only storm that ever mattered — the one inside a house, years ago, that no barometer recorded.

*History happens elsewhere.* That was the creed, though Noor never said it aloud even to the empty rooms. History happened in France, where men were being fed by the ten thousand into mud. History happened in London, in Edinburgh, in the newspaper that arrived on the supply boat already two weeks damp and stale. History happened to people with real names attached to real lives, names entered into ledgers, called across parade grounds, carved eventually into stones in churchyards. Noor Zhou was a name so thin, so recently invented, that history's fingers slid right past it. Eleven years at Gullwrack Point, and before that other lights, other coasts, other borrowed spellings of a borrowed life. A man — no, let it be said plainly, in the privacy of one's own skull if nowhere else — a woman named Noor had walked out of one existence and into this one the way you walk out of a burning house into snow: scorched, half-blind, grateful for anything cold enough to numb her.

The wind arrived at dusk on Thursday, and it arrived all at once, as if it had been holding its breath offshore and could bear restraint no longer. It hit the tower with a sound like a train passing through solid stone. Noor went up to light the lamp in the last grey smear of daylight, hands moving through the ritual without instruction — the mantle, the flame, the slow rotation catching, the beam swinging out into the dark like a long arm feeling for the edge of the world. Four seconds dark, then white. Four seconds dark, then white. Somewhere out under that violence were boats, probably, fishing crews who'd lingered too long, maybe a naval patrol running dark against the U-boats. The light didn't care who it saved. That was another thing about it Noor had come to rely on. It asked nothing of anyone. It simply refused, hour after hour, to let the dark have the last word.

By nine o'clock the rain was coming horizontally, in freight-loads, in sheets you could have mended sails from. Noor made the rounds of the ground floor with a lamp in hand, pressing palms to window frames, listening at the walls for the whistle of a loose pane. The station was built like a fist — granite walls three feet thick, doors that closed with a sound like a vault — but the sea had been arguing with this coast since before there were names for either, and it never entirely lost an argument. Noor checked the stores out of habit rather than need: flour, salt pork, tinned milk, a crate of oranges gone soft at one end, paraffin, lamp oil, coal enough for six weeks of the worst the sky could devise. The supply boat was due Saturday, weather permitting, and weather would not permit. It hardly mattered. Noor had planned for solitude the way other households planned for Christmas.

It was a little after eleven — Noor knew because the clock in the kitchen struck the quarter-hours with a chime that had to be heard to be disbelieved — when the knocking began.

At first Noor took it for the storm. The wind had a repertoire; after eleven years Noor knew its whole vocabulary, every rattle and moan and percussive slam, and there was nothing in that vocabulary quite like what was now coming through the door — three blows, spaced evenly, deliberate, then silence, then three more. Human. Insistent. Impossible. Nobody knocked at Gullwrack Point. Nobody came to Gullwrack Point at all except the supply boat twice a month and the inspector from the Northern Lighthouse Board twice a year, and neither of them would be mad enough to cross open water tonight, and both of them, besides, arrived by day.

Noor stood in the kitchen with the rain hammering the slate roof overhead and felt the strangest sensation: not fear exactly, but the body's memory of fear, the way a lost limb remembers itching. Someone was at the door. In eleven years, no one had ever been at the door.

The lamp went up off its hook. Noor unbarred the outer door — the bar fought, swollen in its brackets, groaning against the wind's whole weight on the far side — and pulled it open onto chaos.

Night, rain, roar. The beam swept overhead and for one second the world outside was lit stark as a photograph: black water exploding white against the rocks below, spray rising like smoke, the gale bending itself around the tower. And on the step, crumpled against the jamb where the wind couldn't reach, a shape.

Small. A bundle of soaked wool. A child.

Noor dropped to one knee and hauled the bundle inside with both arms, kicked the door shut behind them, shot the bar home. The sudden quiet inside the thick walls was enormous — not silence, the storm still thundered overhead, but the absence of that immediate, physical malice. Noor set the lamp down and turned up the wick and looked, really looked, at what the sea had delivered.

A boy. Nine years old, perhaps ten — small for his age, or starved for it, hard to tell which these days. He was unconscious, or near enough; his eyes rolled beneath lids that fluttered but wouldn't hold. His lips were the colour of the slate beach. His clothes — a knitted jersey ruined beyond saving, trousers, boots two sizes too large with the laces triple-knotted, someone else's boots — were saturated through, and he was shivering in great slow shudders that Noor recognized from medical pamphlets as worse than violent ones. Cold past the point of fighting. Cold going down toward the place from which some people did not come back up.

And pinned to his jersey, under a safety pin gone brown with salt, a note.

Noor saw it and did not touch it. Practicality first. There would be time for paper; paper kept. Skin did not. Noor carried the boy through to the kitchen — he weighed less than a winter's worth of firewood, which was its own kind of testimony — stripped the ruined clothes, wrapped him in blankets warmed before the stove, rubbed his limbs with rough towels until some colour came back into them, forced sips of sweetened tea between his cracked lips a spoonful at a time, waiting each time for the swallow. All of this Noor did quickly, competently, without thinking, the hands knowing the work the way they knew the lamp mechanism. This was the trick of survival, Noor had learned it young: let the hands do the thing while the mind stands back and watches from a safe distance, taking notes on nothing, feeling nothing, feeling *nothing.*

The boy swallowed. Coughed. Swallowed again. After a while the terrible slow shivering gentled into ordinary trembling, and his breathing found a rhythm that sounded like breathing instead of drowning, and Noor sat back on the hearthstone with wet towel in hand and allowed, for the first time, the question to surface:

*Where did he come from?*

There was no boat. Noor had watched the water all evening from the lamp room between rounds of the mechanism — nothing could live on that sea tonight, nothing human anyway, no dinghy, no coble, no ship's boat. And yet here he was, half-drowned, which meant water, which meant a vessel, which meant someone had put him ashore somewhere along the point in weather that should have killed them all. Or — Noor's eyes went to the small high window above the sink, black with rain — someone had landed him earlier, at dusk perhaps, in the last survivable hour, told him to walk, told him to knock. Told him *something*, because children did not walk themselves into storms.

The note.

Noor unpinned it with fingers that were, Noor noted distantly, entirely steady. Steady hands. Twenty years of steady hands. The paper was cheap, soft with damp, and on it, in ink that had run slightly but remained legible, five words in a small slanted hand:

*forget this face*

Nothing else. No name. No plea. No instruction as to feeding or clothing or return. Just the imperative, laid on a child like a coin on an eye: forget this face. As though the face were a debt. As though whoever had written it knew — knew what? That someone lived here who might otherwise *recognize*—

The thought stopped like a foot at the lip of a stair.

Noor looked down at the boy.

He lay on the pallet before the stove with his face turned toward the firelight, and the firelight was kind, and the kind light showed everything: the fine bones under the skin, the arch of the brow, the particular set of the mouth — a lower lip fuller than the upper, a chin that came to a soft decisive point — the ears that stood out just slightly, stubbornly, refusing to lie flat against the head. Noor had grown up surrounded by those ears. Had teased their owner mercilessly across a dozen summers. Had seen that exact profile across a supper table, asleep in a field, crying at a funeral, laughing — God, the laughing — and had spent two decades ensuring that no face on earth would ever wear that profile anywhere within a hundred miles of this tower.

The boy's eyelids moved. For a moment — half a second, no more — his eyes opened partway, unfocused, fever-bright, fixed on Noor's face without seeing it. And in that moment Noor felt the floor of the world tilt by a degree, barely perceptible, the way the tower tilted in the worst gusts: everything still standing, nothing broken, and yet some deep interior plumb-line swung loose and would not settle.

*Forget this face,* said the note, in handwriting Noor had not seen in twenty years and knew instantly, completely, the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger in a crowd — Wren's hand, the little forward slant, the f crossed with a flourish she'd been scolded for in school. Wren, who had been fourteen years old the last time Noor saw her, standing in the doorway of a house Noor could never go back to, saying nothing, saying everything, watching her eldest sibling walk away under a new name toward a new life while the family's ruin smoked quietly behind them like a doused fire.

Wren had known where to send him. That was the fact underneath all the other facts, the keel beneath the waterline. Twenty years of careful erasure, of names shed like coats, of coasts chosen for their remoteness, and it had amounted to nothing. Someone inland had always known. The address had always existed, written somewhere, passed somehow, and now it had been used, and a boy carrying Noor's own blood in his face had been carried out of the war's vast hunger and set down on this rock like a parcel of weather — something the sky sends whether you've room for it or not.

Outside, the gale climbed another register. Something heavy struck the seaward wall and was torn away again — a shutter, possibly, or a piece of the store-shed roof; Noor would find out at dawn, if dawn came, if there were any dawn left attached to the building. None of it mattered tonight. Tonight the inventory of the world had shrunk to this kitchen: one lamp, one stove, one storm, one child, one note.

The boy stirred and murmured something. Noor leaned close. The word came out blurred, half-dissolved, but its shape was unmistakable, and it was a name — not Noor's name, none of Noor's names, old or borrowed — a name that belonged to the life before, the buried life, the one Noor had spent twenty years teaching the world to forget.

He said it again, clearer, asking or calling, his eyes still shut, and Noor sat very still on the hearthstone with the wet towel going cold in her hands and understood that whatever she decided about authorities and ferries and the proper channels, the decision had already, in some truer ledger, been made.

She knew that name. She had given it to him herself, twenty-three years ago, beside a bed in a house she could not go back to.
