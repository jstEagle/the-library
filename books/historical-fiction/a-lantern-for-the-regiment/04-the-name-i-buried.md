## 4. The Name I Buried

The winter of 1897 came down from the hills like a debt collector.

Noor had been nineteen that year and still answering to a name that fit like a borrowed coat — though it was not yet borrowed; it was still theirs, still warm from their mother's mouth, still stitched with all the family's small histories into every syllable. *Underhill.* Even then the name had seemed an instruction rather than an identity: go beneath the hill, hide yourself, be small, be covered. They had not yet learned how literally they would come to obey it.

The house in Marrowgate had smelled of coal smoke and wet wool that November, of paraffin and the vinegar Wren used for cleaning the windows she was forever cleaning because standing still made her anxious. Noor remembered the smell of that house the way other people remembered hymns — involuntarily, completely, with the whole body. It came back to them now across twenty years of salt wind, and it always came back strongest in the weeks before the storms, when Gullwrack Point went grey and silent and there was nothing to do but trim the wicks and remember.

* * *

Their father had been a man who believed the world could be managed if one kept meticulous accounts. He kept ledgers for everything — the coal deliveries, the rents from the two narrow houses he owned on Chandler's Row, the small kindnesses he did his neighbors, entered in a fair hand so that they might someday be repaid with interest. Noor had inherited the handwriting. Noor had inherited, too, the belief that a life carefully recorded was a life defended against chaos. It was why they kept the lighthouse log now with such devotion, why every entry was dated and timed and signed, why the ink never smudged. A ledger did not lie. A ledger held its shape when everything else gave way.

It was the ledger that betrayed them all, in the end. Or rather, it was what their brother Aldous had done with the ledgers.

Aldous was four years older than Noor and had always been the bright one, the quick one, the one their father showed off at the lodge dinners. He worked as a clerk for Halloway & Finch, shipping factors, and by 1897 he had risen far enough to handle accounts that moved through his hands like water through a sluice — money belonging to men who would never count it themselves because they trusted the firm, and the firm trusted Aldous, and Aldous trusted Aldous.

Noor had known something was wrong before anyone said it. That was the thing about shame; it arrived before the facts did, like weather seen far out at sea, a darkening on the horizon that the sensible mind refuses to name. In October, Aldous had bought their mother a brooch — garnets set in silver — and Noor had watched her turn it in the lamplight and felt a coldness move up their spine that they could not account for. The brooch was too fine. The smile Aldous wore while giving it was too wide. There was a looseness in him lately, a spendthrift gaiety, that read now — reading back across the years — like a man whistling past his own gallows.

"You worry too much," Wren told them when Noor tried to say this without saying it. She was seventeen and fierce and certain that Noor's caution was a disease. "He's doing well. For once someone in this family is doing *well.*"

She had said it kindly. That was the cruelty of it — everything in that autumn was done kindly, gently, with everyone trying to spare everyone else, right up until the morning the men from Halloway & Finch came down Chandler's Row in their black coats with their black books and knocked on the door with the flat authority of the law itself.

* * *

What Aldous had taken, over three years, was four hundred and eleven pounds. Noor knew the figure exactly because they copied the indictment afterward, copying being what their hands did when their mind wanted to fly apart. Four hundred and eleven pounds, extracted in small increments from freight charges and insurance premiums and the float accounts of six different vessels, each theft so modest that no single audit would catch it, the whole sum building in his hands like silt builds behind a weir. He had lost most of it at cards. Some of it had gone into a scheme involving a patent butter-cooling apparatus that existed only on paper. When the auditors finally traced the discrepancies, Aldous did not run. Running required a kind of imagination he did not have.

Instead he did the thing that broke the family more thoroughly than any prison could have. He took the ledgers — the household ones, their father's private books, twenty years of careful entries — and he altered them. He wrote his father into the transactions. Small entries, plausible entries, the kind of adjustments a desperate man might make to cover a shortfall. He forged his father's initials beside sums of money their father had never touched, in a hand so close to the old man's own that even their mother wavered, for one terrible hour, before she saw the truth of it — or said she saw it.

Whether she truly believed in those first hours, no one ever spoke of again. What Noor remembered was the sound their father made when the inspectors laid the ledgers open on the kitchen table and turned them toward him. Not a word. A sound, low and short, like a beam giving way inside a wall of a house that looks sound from the street.

"He'll hang me by reputation if not by rope," their father said that night, sitting very straight in his chair with his ruined name spread out before him under the lamp. "They won't try me. I'm too old, too respectable, too obviously the scapegoat. But I will die a thief in this town, and your mother will die a thief's wife, and you children will carry it in your mouths like salt until you die." He looked at Noor then — only at Noor, because Aldous was already gone, taken into custody that afternoon, and Wren had been sent next door — and he said the sentence that Noor would take apart and examine every night for twenty years, looking for a different meaning in it, finding none.

"Someone in this family must remain clean."

Not *innocent.* Clean. As though innocence were no longer available to any of them, and the best that could be hoped for was a surface unmarked enough to pass inspection.

* * *

The trial lasted four days. Aldous got eighteen months and hard labor, which everyone understood was lenient, purchased by the firm's desire to avoid scandal and by the prosecution's willingness to accept a guilty plea that named no accomplices. Their father was never charged. The magistrates examined the ledgers, heard the firm's clerks describe the alterations, and concluded — correctly — that Aldous Underhill had framed his own father to buy himself time. But a conclusion is not a restoration. The town had read the papers. The town had done its arithmetic. And arithmetic, once let loose among neighbors, does not care about verdicts.

For a year afterward, Noor watched their father shrink. He stopped going to the lodge. He stopped taking the morning walk along the canal that had been the fixed star of his days. He sat in the parlor with the blinds half-drawn, doing nothing, because every occupation he had ever had — the rents, the accounts, the standing in the community — had been built on the ledger of his good name, and that ledger had been burned. He died in the spring of 1899, of what the doctor called congestion of the lungs and what the family called nothing at all, because to call it anything would have been to say the word *shame* aloud, and no one in that house ever said it aloud again.

But all of that came later. In the winter of 1897, Noor was nineteen, and the disaster was fresh, and the question pressing on them daily was the simplest and hardest one: *how do you live in a town where every face you meet has already judged you?*

* * *

Wren fought. That must be said of her. While Noor went quiet, Wren went loud — squared up in the market square to Mrs. Tallow, the butcher's wife, whose whisper about "that family" carried further than she'd intended; stood on the church steps after service and dared the congregation with her eyes to look at her sideways. Seventeen years old and she took the whole weight of the town's contempt onto her front like a shield, daring it to strike. Noor loved her for it and was ashamed of loving her for it, because Noor knew — knew with a certainty that felt like cowardice and probably was — that Wren's defiance would cost her everything, marriage, standing, peace, and that the shield would bend around her and eventually break her, and Noor did not intend to stand close enough to be cut by the same blow.

That was the thought Noor had never confessed to anyone, the one that lived at the bottom of everything: *I did not want to pay.* Not that they were innocent — they were, of the crime itself, wholly innocent, and that innocence curdled into its own torment because it was useless, worth nothing on the open market. But watching Wren fight, Noor had understood something about themselves that frightened them: given the choice between justice and escape, between standing in the fire and walking around it, some load-bearing part of them chose the walk. Every time. Without hesitation, without even much regret at the time. Regret came later, in installments, with interest — their father's habit of accounting asserting itself even in guilt.

The opportunity to walk presented itself in February of 1898, and it came wearing the face of a stranger, which is fitting; Noor would wear a stranger's face for twenty years after, and perhaps the transaction was fair.

* * *

The man's name was Ellery Vance, and he was a Trinity House officer passing through on inspection of coastal appointments, and he lodged for one week at the inn on Fore Street, and Noor — who had taken work keeping the inn's books since no one in Marrowgate would hire an Underhill — served him his breakfasts and balanced his bills and listened, hungry, to his talk of the lights. The North Carr beacon. The Skerries. The great towers rising out of the sea like the fingers of a drowned giant, manned by keepers who lived above the reach of gossip, above the reach of neighbors, above the reach of everything except weather and duty.

"There's a post opening," Vance said one morning, folding his newspaper. "Assistant keeper, Gullwrack Point. Nobody wants it — too remote, too exposed, the supply boat comes once a month if she comes at all. The last fellow lasted eight months and left talking to himself." He looked at Noor over his spectacles with the mild curiosity of a man sizing up furniture. "You're steady-handed. Your figures are exact. You don't talk more than the work requires. Have you thought about the sea?"

Have you thought about the sea. As though it were a destination one considered, like Bath or Boulogne. Noor polished the same spoon twice and heard their own heart, loud and traitorous, saying *yes, yes, yes.*

"I'd need references," Vance added. "Character references. The Service doesn't take on just anybody — a light is a trust."

And there it was. The door opening, and the toll gate beside it. A reference meant a signature, and a signature meant a name, and the name Underhill was by then worth less than nothing; it was a liability, a stain that transferred on contact. Noor stood in the inn's breakfast room with the fog of fried bread and lamp oil hanging in the air and performed, in the space of ten seconds, the arithmetic that would govern the rest of their life.

Their mother was dead two years by then — taken quickly, mercifully, before the worst of it — and Wren had an aunt in Whitby who'd offered her a roof, and their father had said, that last night before the verdict, *someone in this family must remain clean,* and Noor had understood at last what the old man had meant, or what Noor had chosen to hear: not that someone should stay and be honorable, but that someone should get *out*, should plant the family's flag on some distant shore where it could not be recognized, so that the blood would not simply end in the mud of Marrowgate. Someone must survive the name. Someone must bury it deep enough to live.

"Underhill is finished here," Noor said to Vance, and was surprised at how level their voice came out. "But I have an uncle. Underwood. He raised me after my parents — after we were left alone. Noor Underwood. If you write to the vicar at St. Bride's, he'll confirm the family. He knew my uncle well."

Every word of it false except the first name. St. Bride's had no vicar who knew any such uncle; Noor had chosen the parish because Vance was unlikely to write north in February, and because lying is easiest when dressed in plausibility, and because — this was the part Noor would examine for twenty years and never forgive — because it came easily. The lie rose in them smooth and ready, like water finding its level, and Noor understood in that moment that they had been carrying this capacity all their life, folded small, waiting for the weather that would require it.

Vance wrote the letter anyway — he was a thorough man — and the reply came back from a curate who assumed the inquiry referred to the Underwoods of the next valley, who indeed had an uncle of suitable character, dead these six years, and confirmed everything with the cheerful confidence of a man answering a question he hadn't understood. Providence, or luck, or the simple indifference of the world to the fate of one nineteen-year-old liar. Noor signed the service papers *N. Underwood* in a fair hand, and the ink dried, and that was that. A name, like a coat, fits better the longer you wear it. Within a month Noor had stopped flinching when strangers used it. Within a year they had stopped hearing it as foreign. Within five years, waking at Gullwrack Point to the boom of the fog signal, they sometimes had to search deliberately — a conscious act, like recalling a dream — to find the old name at all.

* * *

They told Wren the night before they left. She sat on the end of their bed in the cold back room, in her coat still, having walked through the rain from the aunt's lodgings where she was staying, and Noor laid it all out — the post, the coast, the new name, the clean break — expecting argument, expecting fury, preparing even to welcome fury because fury would mean she wanted them to stay.

She didn't argue. She sat very still, looking at her hands, and when she finally spoke her voice was quiet in a way Noor had never heard from her, quiet the way the sea goes quiet before it decides what it will do.

"You're leaving me here alone with it."

"No one has to stay with it. Come with me—"

"With what name?" She laughed, one syllable, no humor in it. "You've found the trick, Noor. Bury the name, become someone else, start clean. Good for you. Truly." She looked up then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "But I'm Wren Underhill. That's my face in the window of every shop on Chandler's Row. I can't bury my face. Wherever I go, it goes with me, and everyone who's read the papers will know it." She stood, buttoned her coat to the throat. "So one of us gets to disappear, and one of us gets to stay and be the family. And you've decided which, and you didn't even ask me."

"I'm asking now."

"No." She said it fast, before the offer could become real, and Noor saw — saw clearly, helplessly — that she refused not because she preferred to stay but because she would not let herself want it. That was Wren entire. She would cut off her own wanting rather than owe it to anyone, especially not to the brother who had taught her, by example, that people leave. "You go. Keep your clean slate. Write to me if you ever grow brave enough to sign your real name to a letter."

She went out into the rain. Noor did not follow. That failure — the failure to follow, to stand in the wet street and say *I was wrong, I am wrong, I will stay and be the family with you* — that failure became, over the years, the true foundation stone of the new identity, deeper than the buried name itself. Everyone believed Noor Underwood had fled a scandal. Only Noor knew that the scandal was merely the door; what they had fled was Wren's face, turned away in the rain, and the sound of the door of the aunt's lodgings closing behind her, and the knowledge that shame was not what had driven them from Marrowgate after all.

Shame had built them. Shame was the engine. Every trimmed wick, every exact entry in the log, every hour of solitary competence at Gullwrack Point was shame converted into motion, the way a millrace converts a river into work. *Someone must remain clean.* Very well. Noor would be clean the way a bone is clean — stripped, white, useful, and empty.

* * *

Twenty years. Noor stood now at the gallery rail of Gullwrack Point with the wind worrying their coat tails and the barometer falling the way it fell before the serious blows, and they did the arithmetic they always did in the last week of autumn: twenty years since Marrowgate, twenty years since the forged initials and the borrowed name, twenty years of letters never sent because every letter would have required a signature, and a signature was a key that opened the grave where the old self lay buried.

They had almost made their peace with it. That was the thing they could never explain to anyone who had not lived it — that exile becomes domestic, that the prison remodels itself into a home if you stay long enough and sweep its floors. Noor Underwood had a life here. A small one, a narrow one, but a true one in every particular except the root of it, and Noor had learned to live above the root the way the tower lived above the rock, trusting the foundation without looking down.

Tomorrow the supply boat would make its last reliable run before the winter gales closed the coast. Noor had the list written out in their fair, exact hand: oil, flour, lamp glass, tea. Ordinary things. The inventory of an ordinary, ordered, hidden life.

They did not know — could not have known, standing there in the iron-smelling dusk with the first storm still two days out at sea — that within the week something would wash up against their door that no ledger could account for: a child half-drowned and breathing, and a note in a hand Noor would know anywhere, because it was their own family's hand, because it was Wren's, and five words in ink gone blurry with seawater:

*Forget this face. Please. Forget this face.*

And the face, God help them, was one Noor had spent twenty years trying to forget already.
