## 5. Forget This Face

The note had been drying on the mantel for two days, propped against the tin of lamp-black like a letter awaiting postage.

Noor told themselves it was waiting because the paper was too soft to handle — sea-swollen, ink feathered at its edges, the kind of paper that tore if you looked at it wrong. That was a practical reason, and Noor was a practical person. Practical people did not stand in front of a scrap of paper with their hands behind their back like men approaching an open grave. Practical people picked things up.

On the third morning, with Tam still sleeping the heavy, salt-drunk sleep of the half-drowned in the cot by the stove, Noor picked it up.

*Forget this face.*

Four words. The handwriting slanted hard to the right, letters crowded together as though the writer had been afraid of running out of paper, or out of time. The *f* crossed twice — once where it belonged and once again, lower, a small anxious second stroke, the pen returning to make sure the job was done. The *g* of *forget* descended in a long tail that hooked back under itself, swallowing its own end.

Noor knew that hook.

There is a particular cruelty in handwriting. Faces change; voices deepen or thin; a person you loved can walk past you on a street after twenty years and be a stranger until they speak. But a hand — a hand is a skeleton of the self, laid bare every time a person writes so much as their own name. It grows with them the way bone grows, imperceptibly, and it cannot lie about its age. The note in Noor's fingers had been written by a hand that was older now than the one Noor had last seen, but it was not another hand. It was the same hand grown, the way a sapling becomes a tree and is still, in every ring of it, the same tree.

The second stroke on the *f*. The swallowed *g*. The way the *t* in *this* stood slightly apart from its neighbors, as if even in a four-word sentence that letter wanted to be alone.

Wren.

Noor set the note down on the table very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off, and then sat across from it and looked at it for a long time while the kettle came to a boil unattended and shuddered itself into silence.

*Forget this face.*

Not *he is yours*. Not *his name is*. Not *keep him safe*, which would have been unbearable but at least legible. Wren had written an instruction, and the instruction was annihilation: forget the boy's face. As though the face were a debt. As though the face were evidence. And Noor understood, sitting there in the grey light with the storm's leavings still rattling against the glass, that Wren had chosen those words because they were the only ones she could trust him with. Anything else — a name, a place, a history — could be read by the wrong eyes. But *forget this face* said nothing except to the one person alive who would know what face it was.

It was a message written in a cipher only family could break. Which meant Wren had known — had known before she wrote it, standing somewhere inland with wet ink and a wetter child — exactly who would find him. She had known where Noor was.

Twenty years of careful erasure, and his sister had simply addressed a parcel to the wound.

* * *

Tam slept through most of that first day of Noor's unraveling. Children do. They sleep the way the tide comes in, without negotiation, while the adults above the waterline rearrange the world around them. Twice he surfaced enough to drink the barley water Noor pressed on him, and both times his eyes opened partway — brown eyes, dark as river stones, the exact brown that had looked out of their mother's face — and fixed on Noor with an expression too old for nine years old, an expression of assessment, of a clerk checking goods against a manifest. Then he sank again.

Those eyes were the worst of it. The jaw might have come from anywhere; the stubborn line of his mouth might have been some stranger's gift from the father's side, whoever the father had been. But the eyes were Zhou eyes. There had been three children in that house on Fenwick Row who shared them, and Noor had spent twenty years making sure no mirror caught them looking back.

He had the habit, too, even asleep — Noor noticed it when he brought the blanket up over the boy's shoulders — of turning his face toward the door. Their brother Jun had slept like that. Chin lifted, ear open, a sentry even in dreams, in a house where listening had been survival. Noor had once asked their mother why Jun slept facing the door, and their mother had said, in the flat tone she used for things too true to decorate, *Because he learned it from watching your father.* Noor had never asked what the father had been watching for. Some questions are doors you can feel are locked, and childhood teaches you not to rattle them.

Now a child slept in Noor's cot, facing the door, wearing the family's eyes, and outside the window the sea went on doing what the sea does, which is to say: nothing personal, nothing merciful, everything again.

* * *

By afternoon the fog came in off the water, which was ordinary for Gullwrack in November, but Noor felt it arrive differently today. Fog at Gullwrack Point was usually an ally. It muffled the world's approach; it gave the island back to itself; it made a small country of the tower and the cottage and the forty feet of rock between them. For twenty years Noor had welcomed fog the way other men welcomed rain on their fields. Let the mainland dissolve. Let the ferries lose their nerve. Let history happen elsewhere, behind weather.

Today the fog felt less like a wall and more like a throat.

Because that was the arithmetic Wren's handwriting forced, and no amount of lamp-trimming or log-keeping would balance it differently: if Wren knew where Noor was, then Wren had found out. And if Wren had found out, then the finding-out had left traces — a question asked here, a record consulted there, a cousin of a cousin paid for a memory. Traces were how the other kind of finding-out happened too. There had been men, twenty years ago, who had wanted very much to know where a certain young man had gone, because a certain young man was the brother of a man who had done a terrible thing, and blood was thought to be a kind of address. Noor had built a life on the assumption that those men had stopped asking. Now the fog rolled against the windows and Noor heard, in the hiss of it, the sound of someone else's footsteps learning the path.

Paranoia arrives like fog. Not all at once — that would be almost manageable, a front you could name and batten down against. It arrives the way fog does: first the far headland goes, then the near rocks, then the gulls' voices separate from their bodies, and by the time you notice you cannot see the path you walked this morning, the whole world has been quietly replaced with a version of itself you can no longer verify. By evening Noor had examined the supply boat's last visit, the inspector's letter from the autumn (routine, surely routine), the fisherman Dalgleish who sometimes anchored below the point and always, always asked friendly questions — *quiet life you've made for yourself, Underhill* — and found suspicion pooled in all of it, cold and level, reflecting nothing.

*Underhill.* Even the borrowed name felt thinner tonight, a coat worn through at the elbows. Twenty years of answering to it had woven it onto him — he turned when it was called across a quay, he signed it nightly in the log in a hand that had deliberately shed every family habit, the *N* squared off, the descenders clipped, nothing swallowed, nothing doubled. He had built a second handwriting the way he had built a second self, and both had held. Both had held right up until a scrap of paper walked through the storm wearing the old hand like a ghost wearing its Sunday clothes.

* * *

Near dusk Tam woke properly for the first time — woke into fear, the way drowned boys must, coming up out of black water into lamplight and a stranger's face. Noor heard the change in his breathing before the boy's eyes opened: the shallow quick rhythm going shallower and quicker, a small engine racing toward something.

"It's all right," Noor said, and put the lamp where the light would fall on their own face, full and unhurried. Children who sleep facing doors need to see faces clearly. "You're at the lighthouse. Gullwrack Point. You were in the water."

Tam stared. The assessment again, that clerk's squint, cataloguing: the room, the lamp, the uniform-less man in a jersey the color of wet slate. His lips moved. Whatever he decided not to say, he swallowed it, and what came out instead was small and scraped raw:

"You're not her."

"No," Noor said. "I'm not her."

Something went through the boy's face then — disappointment, and beneath the disappointment, horribly, relief — and Noor felt it land in their chest like a stone dropped down a well, counting the seconds to a bottom they didn't want to find. *You're not her.* So the mother was meant to be here. Or meant to be met. Or meant, past tense, in whatever grammar children use for the vanished. Wren had not told him either, then. Wren had kept her son ignorant of the destination, which meant Wren had kept him ignorant of Noor, which meant —

Which meant the boy knew nothing, and everything Noor said to him now was a brick in whatever wall he would live inside.

"Are you hungry?" Noor asked, because hunger was the one honest thing in the room.

Tam considered. The fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere below the point, the sea worked at the rocks with its patient chisels.

"Yes," he said. And then, watching Noor rise to fetch the bread, with the directness of the very young and the very frightened, the question that Noor had been circling all day like a bird over deep water: "She said you'd keep me. Did she say it true?"

Noor's hands stopped on the bread tin.

*Keep me.* Present tense, a fact already in force. *Did she say it true* — a child's phrasing, but underneath it, unmistakable, the older architecture: *was she lying, does everyone lie, is lying just what the adults I love do when they look at me.* Noor had heard that architecture before. Had lived inside it, once, in a house on Fenwick Row, at a table where a father's promises curdled weekly and a mother translated them into survivable falsehoods and three children learned three different dialects of distrust.

"She said it true," Noor heard themself say.

Whether it was a promise or a lie, Noor could not have said, and that was the thing that frightened most of all: that the border between the two had worn away somewhere in twenty years of small hours, and Noor no longer knew which side of it they were standing on.

* * *

That night, after Tam slept — on his side now, not facing the door, a surrender so small it undid Noor more than any weeping would have — Noor took the logbook down and did not write in it.

The log was the spine of the life. Wind, visibility, lamp hours, oil consumed, vessels passed, weather noted in the neutral vocabulary of the service. Nothing personal had ever entered it. Nothing personal had ever entered anything, that was the whole design, the whole beautiful machine of the erased life: no photographs, no letters, no debts, no loves, no names in other people's mouths. A man made entirely of entries. *Lamp lit 6:14 p.m. Wind SW freshening. Sea moderate.* A man who could be erased by closing the book.

And yet Wren had posted a child through all of it, through twenty years of closed book, and set him down on the doorstep like milk.

Noor sat with the pen uncapped and the page blank and finally, instead of writing, did the thing they had trained themselves never to do: went backward. Opened the memory the way you'd open a box you'd sealed with wax and rope, knowing the smell inside would be the smell of the day you shut it.

Fenwick Row. Rain on slate. Jun at the table, seventeen and white as tallow, saying *I didn't mean —* in a voice that everyone in the room knew was a lie, and their father's face going from shock to calculation in the space of a single breath, because their father had always been able to convert catastrophe into leverage faster than any man alive. And then the whispering behind doors — Noor, you're the steady one, you're the one they don't watch — and the errand that was not an errand, the papers moved, the story shaped, the family needing a pair of hands it could trust and taking them without asking whether the hands consented. And Noor consenting, God help them, consenting at nineteen because being needed was the closest thing to being loved that house had ever taught. And then the constable's knock, and the trial like weather passing over, and Jun taken away in a cart while the neighbors stood at their windows with the particular appetite of the righteous, and their father's voice afterward, smooth as poured fat: *Best you go down the coast, lad. Fresh start. Forget this face.*

*Forget this face.*

Noor's breath stopped.

The pen rolled off the table. The sound of it hitting the floorboards was very loud in the small kitchen, and Tam stirred in the cot, and Noor sat absolutely still, heart hammering, staring at the note propped on the mantel as if it had just spoken aloud.

Their father's phrase. Their father's exact words, spoken on the last night, on the doorstep, with one hand briefly — the only time in living memory — resting on Noor's shoulder: *forget this face, and we forget yours.* The family's whole theology in six syllables. Disappear and be absolved. Vanish and be spared. Erasure as mercy, erasure as sentence, and no difference between them that anyone had ever bothered to mark.

Wren had used their father's words. Either she remembered them — she had been twelve, she had been crying in the stairwell, she might have heard everything through the banisters — or she had arrived at them independently, the way a river arrives at the sea, because they were the downhill direction of that family's love.

Either way, the meaning had shifted in her mouth, and Noor turned it over and over like a stone with two colors. From the father it had meant: *be gone, and take yourself with you.* From Wren it could only mean: *the world is hunting this boy's face, and you are the one person I know who has already survived becoming no one. Teach him what you know. Hide him the way you hid yourself. I am spending my last coin on the only account that was ever open.*

Or — and the fog whispered this version too, all night, in its many voices — she was hunted herself, and had thrown him to the far edge of the map hoping the dark would take him kindly, and had chosen the words carelessly, or desperately, or in grief, and no cipher was intended, and Noor was reading scripture in a shopping list.

That was the trouble with paranoia. It offered interpretations the way the sea offers waves — endlessly, each one plausible, none of them checkable, all of them arriving.

* * *

Toward morning the fog began to lift off the water, drawing back like a held breath released, and the world returned in pieces: the low rocks, the gull-stained ledges, the grey lane down to the landing stage, the empty horizon where the supply boat should eventually appear.

Noor stood at the window with cold tea and watched the lane emerge footpath by footpath, and admitted, at last, in the privacy of their own skull, the thing the whole night had been walking toward:

Someone knew.

Not perhaps-was-hunting, not maybe-followed. Knew. Knew where the light was, knew whose hands kept it, trusted those hands with the single most perishable cargo in the world. Wren had aimed her son at this rock the way a drowning woman aims at any shore, and the shore had caught him, and now the shore was Noor, and there was no version of the coming weeks in which Noor handed this child over to constables and inspectors and manifests and let the machinery of names do what it does to names like theirs.

But there was also no version — and here the fog's last rag went burning off the water in the early sun, and the sea lay open and glittering and indifferent, and Noor watched it the way you watch a road you can't stop watching — no version in which the boy had traveled to this coast alone. Someone had carried him. Someone had paid passage, or rowed him, or bribed a fisherman, and whoever it was knew the way to Gullwrack Point, and paths that are known once are known twice.

Noor looked down the lane, empty and bright in the washed morning, and saw it suddenly as what it had never been before in twenty years of keeping this light: an approach. A road with a direction. Something, someday soon, coming up it.

The supply boat was late. Noor noticed, for the first time, how late.

Far down the grey water, where the fog had stood all night, there was now nothing — no sail, no smoke, no boat. Only the long emptiness, and behind Noor in the warm kitchen a sleeping child with the family's eyes, and somewhere inland a sister who had finally spoken after twenty years of silence, choosing as her first words an order to forget.

Noor picked up the note, folded it along its old creases, and slid it inside the cover of the logbook, between the pages where the official record lived — hiding the truth inside the record, which was, they supposed, the family trade after all.

Then Noor trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork, and went out to sweep the steps of the lantern room, and to watch the empty lane, and to wait — though they would not have called it waiting, not yet — for whatever the fog had been carrying toward them in the night.
