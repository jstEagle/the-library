## 6. The Man Who Walked Out of the War

The road to Gullwrack Point had been washed out in three places, and Callum Frazer took that as a good sign. Roads that were washed out were roads nobody kept, and roads nobody kept led to places nobody wanted. He had walked eleven days on such roads — from the coastal hospital at Dunmarrow where his discharge papers had been signed by a doctor who never looked up from his desk, through gorse country and bog and one long grey stretch of military canal where he'd flattened himself into the reeds while a transport lorry ground past close enough to count the rivets. Eleven days of walking away from the war, and still it walked behind him, matching him stride for stride.

His boots had given out two days ago. His left foot was wrapped in bandage torn from a shirt that had once been white, and every step was a negotiation between pain and the cold, which was worse, because pain you could argue with but the cold simply took what it liked. The wind off the sea came sideways now, carrying salt and the smell of something enormous turning over beyond the horizon. He'd heard about the storms. Everyone inland had heard about the storms — three supply boats lost or turned back, villages along the coast dark for weeks, the wireless full of careful official language that meant *the sea has closed its doors*.

He crested the last rise as the light failed and saw the tower standing against a sky the colour of wet slate.

It was smaller than he'd imagined, and older. Not the clean white painted kind from the recruiting posters, all crisp stripes and heroic geometry, but a squat stone cylinder furred with lichen, its gallery rail rusted to the colour of dried blood, a keeper's cottage hunched against its flank like a thing grown there rather than built. The lamp above burned steady and white, sweeping its arm across the water with the patience of something that had been doing this work since before anyone now living was born. To the east, over the sea, the clouds were stacked in shelves, black under grey under black, lit from within by no lightning he could see. Weather being manufactured somewhere far out. Weather with intentions.

Callum stood on the rise for a long moment, letting the wind push at him, and considered — not for the first time — how strange it was that a man could spend four years learning to read landscape as threat and then find the skill useless, because every landscape was now a threat, including the empty ones. A church spire: sniper's nest. A hedgerow: machine-gun position. A lighthouse on an empty point: shelter, or a trap. There was no way to know except to walk down and find out.

His hand found the papers inside his coat without his willing it. Oilskin packet, tied with string, carried against his chest like a second heart. *Lance Corporal C. Frazer, 7th Battalion.* Discharge certificate. Medical board summary. A railway warrant, unused. All of it genuine, all of it signed, and all of it worthless, because the medical board summary described a man who had been invalided home with a shattered knee, and Callum Frazer's knees worked fine. The real Lance Corporal Frazer had died at Passchendaele with most of his platoon, and Callum had done the arithmetic that desperate men do in the dark — that a dead man's name was warmer than no name at all — and had walked out of the dressing station wearing another soldier's identity before anyone thought to check the ledger against the living.

They called it desertion. They called it worse than that, in the newspapers. *Cowards in khaki. The shame of the uniform.* Men had been shot for less behind the lines, and men were being shot for it still, though the shooting had grown quieter, more administrative, further from the front pages. If the papers in his coat were ever matched against the casualty lists — if anyone ever asked why Lance Corporal Frazer collected rations in one village and slept in the next — they would hang him. Not a figure of speech. An actual rope, an actual beam, a small crowd with hats.

So: a lighthouse, alone, at the edge of the map. A keeper who would want no visitors and ask no questions, because keepers were men who understood the value of solitude. That had been the theory, anyway, formed on the road in the warm hypothetical space where theories live. Theories did not have to knock on doors.

He went down the track with the light sweeping overhead every twelve seconds — he counted, because counting kept other thoughts out — and came to the cottage door, which was painted a deep green gone chalky with salt weather, and raised his fist, and stopped.

Through the window, lamplit: a figure moving. Small. Too small for a grown person.

A child, carrying a basin across the room.

Callum stood in the wind with his fist half-raised and felt the whole plan tilt beneath him like deck plating. Keepers had families, of course they did, wives and children lived at stations all round the coast. But a child changed the arithmetic entirely. A child meant questions a child would ask — children always asked, that was their whole nature, they were little engines of asking — and a child meant a keeper who would be protective, and a protective man was a dangerous man when there was a stranger at his door with a face like Callum's face, which after eleven days of rain and no razor looked exactly like what it was: a man walking out of a war rather than home from one.

And yet the cold had passed through his coat, through his shirt, through the skin beneath, and was now working on the bone itself, and the wind had a sound in it he'd learned to dread in Flanders — that low sustained note like a held breath, the sound the air made before it committed itself to violence.

He knocked.

* * *

The door opened on a chain, then fully, and Callum found himself looking at a person he could not place and therefore did not trust. Not quite the height or build he'd expected from a lighthouse keeper; narrow-shouldered, dark-haired going iron at the temples, with eyes that did the thing he hated most in another person's eyes, which was to take inventory. This one counted his boots, his bandage, the state of his hands, the cut of his jaw, and arrived at some conclusion without moving a muscle of the face.

"Storm coming," Callum said. His voice came out rusty. "I'm for the ferry at — " he named a port two days' walk south, a port he had no intention of reaching — "and the weather's turned. I'm asking for a wall to sleep against till morning."

The inventory ran again, longer this time.

"You're army," the keeper said.

"I'm discharged." The word came smoothly, because he'd practised it, because he'd said it to innkeepers and clergymen and one kind woman who'd fed him soup in exchange for stacking wood. "Invalided. Knee."

Something flickered in those dark eyes — recognition, almost, or its cousin. The look of someone hearing a sentence they've said themselves.

"The wall's indoors tonight," the keeper said at last, stepping back. "Nobody sleeps against walls in this. Come in before the wind decides for you."

Inside was warmth and lamp-oil smell and bread, actual bread, on a table, and Callum had been so long on scraps that his body lurched toward the smell like a plant toward sun and he had to hold himself back with dignity, or what remained of it. And then, from the corner by the fire, movement — the child again, a boy of eight or nine, thin, dark-haired, sitting very still with a book open on his knees and watching Callum with an expression no eight-year-old should own: the flat, measuring look of a small animal deciding whether the thing in the doorway is predator or weather.

"That's Tam," the keeper said. Nothing more. No *my son*, no *my nephew*, no *the boy I'm minding*. Just the name, set down between them like a coin too large for any purse, and Callum, who had become a connoisseur of other people's silences these eleven days, noted precisely where the silence fell and what it was papering over.

"Tam," Callum said, and nodded to him, and the boy nodded back, one degree of arc, no more.

The keeper — whose name, offered briefly and without warmth, was Zhou, *Noor Zhou*, though the accent put no homeland to it and the face put none either — fed him soup that was mostly barley and mostly wonderful, and asked nothing, and Callum answered nothing, and the two of them conducted the whole interview in the negative, each silence confirming what the other already knew. It was, Callum thought, the politest interrogation he'd ever endured. When he said he'd sleep by the fire and be gone at first light, the keeper said only, "First light may be theoretical," and glanced at the window, where the glass had begun to sing faintly in its frame, a thin high note like a wet finger on crystal, and outside the dark went from merely dark to absolute, and the held-breath note in the wind finally exhaled.

The storm arrived the way the shells used to arrive — not gradually but *suddenly*, the interval between quietness and catastrophe collapsed to nothing. Rain struck the north windows like thrown gravel, then like fists, then like the whole sea deciding to relocate indoors. The tower above them hummed. Somewhere the shutters banged and were silenced, nailed or wedged by hands that had clearly done this many winters running. Tam gathered his book and moved to the alcove bed without being told, with the automatic economy of a child who knows exactly which parts of a household are his and how much room he is entitled to take up in the world, and that economy, more than anything, more than the keeper's evasions or the boy's old-man stare, told Callum that this child had learned fear recently and thoroughly, and had organised himself around it.

Callum sat by the fire with his ruined boots steaming and the oilskin packet a hard rectangle against his chest, and did not sleep.

* * *

Near midnight, the boy got up.

No fuss, no crying — just the blanket easing back and small bare feet finding the floor, and Tam crossed the room in the dark as though the dark were furniture he knew the position of, and stood at the window, and Callum watched him from under lowered lashes the way he'd watched sentries watch the wire, and understood that the boy wasn't looking at the storm at all. He was looking south. Inland. The way people look toward a thing they've left behind that isn't finished leaving them.

"Can't sleep either?" Callum said quietly.

Tam didn't jump. That was the remarkable part. He turned his head, considered Callum for a moment, and said, "Keeper says the storm will eat the morning. So sleeping doesn't matter."

"Wise keeper."

"He's not my keeper." Said quickly, flatly, the way you correct a factual error. Then, after a pause in which the wind filled the whole house with its voice: "Are you running?"

Callum's blood went cold in a way the storm had nothing to do with. "Running from what?"

"From whatever's in your coat." The boy's chin tipped toward Callum's chest, toward the oilskin packet riding there. "You keep touching it. Like it's hurt and you're checking."

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the tower hummed overhead, and Callum became aware that the keeper's chair by the far wall was empty — that Noor Zhou had risen at some point without a sound and was standing in the shadow of the stairwell, listening, present the way a lighthouse is present, saying nothing and missing nothing.

"Aye," Callum said finally, to the boy, honestly, because lying to a nine-year-old at midnight in a storm seemed suddenly like the one sin left uncommitted. "There's things in this coat that could get me killed if the wrong folk read them."

Tam absorbed this with a slow blink. Then: "Mine too." He touched his own collar, where something lay hidden against the skin — Callum hadn't seen it before, a small shape beneath the boy's shirt, a folded square maybe, or a locket. "But mine's just a letter. Letters can't hang you."

"No," Callum agreed. "Letters can't hang you." Though he had seen men condemned by less — by a postmark, by a handwriting, by a single sentence in another man's notebook — and he thought the boy's mother, whoever she was, whoever she'd run from, would have taught him differently if she'd had time.

From the shadow by the stairs, Noor Zhou spoke for the first time, voice low and even as the lamp's rotation. "Both of you are asleep by the next sweep of the light. Whatever's in coats stays in coats under this roof until the weather ends. After that—" the pause was brief but complete, "—after that we'll see what the sea gives back."

Tam went to his alcove. Callum lay down by the banked fire with the packet against his heart and the storm shouldering the house like a besieging army, and as he drifted at last into the shallow, dreaming-adjacent sleep of the hunted, his last waking thought was that he had walked eleven days to find a hiding place and had instead found a house with three people in it, every one of them carrying a name that wasn't theirs or a paper that wasn't safe, all of them pretending, in the lamplight, to be exactly what they were.

Above them all, the great lamp turned, and turned, and turned, indifferent and faithful, throwing its long arm of light across a sea that had already begun, far out in the black, to give back the first of what it had taken.

And on the table by the door, half-hidden under the keeper's logbook, Callum had seen it earlier and thought nothing of it then but thought everything of it now: a scrap of paper, soft with handling, bearing five words in a woman's hand —

*forget this face.*

He wondered whose face it meant. He wondered whether, come morning, he would wish he'd never learned.
