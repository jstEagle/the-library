## 7. Rations and Rumors

The boat did not come.

Noor stood at the gallery rail at half past nine on the morning the *Merrow* was due, a tin mug of tea cooling in their hand, and watched the horizon perform its old trick — the one where the sea pretends to be empty forever. The wind had backed around to the northeast overnight, hauling cold off the water in long grey sheets, but it was nothing a coal-fired tender couldn't have pushed through. Nothing, in fact, that the *Merrow* hadn't pushed through twice before with her decks green and her skipper swearing.

She simply wasn't there.

By noon Noor had climbed down and walked the shingle below the point, scanning east toward the mainland's smudge, where the low hills sat under cloud like sleeping animals. By one o'clock they had admitted it aloud, which for Noor was nearly a ceremony: "They're not coming."

Callum Frazer looked up from the doorway of the oil store, where he had spent the morning doing small repairs he had invented for himself — tightening hinges that didn't need it, chipping rust from a bracket — the labor of a man proving his usefulness without being asked. "Weather?" he said.

"Too fine for weather."

He understood at once. They both did. Somewhere inland an office had closed its ledger against them; somewhere a clerk had drawn a line through *Gullwrack Point* and moved on to the next entry. War did that. It starved the edges first.

Noor went inside and took inventory the way their mother had once taken inventory of a pantry before a hard winter, though they would not have let themselves remember whose hands taught them that. Flour: perhaps forty pounds. Salt pork in the brine barrel. A sack of potatoes going soft at the eyes. Tea enough for six weeks if rationed honestly, four if rationed like a lie. Dried split peas. A tin of condensed milk saved for no occasion yet imagined. Oil for the lamp was the true arithmetic, and there the numbers were kind — the winter's delivery had come early, in October, and the great tanks stood full.

"We won't go dark," Noor told Callum, who hadn't asked. "We won't go hungry either. Not till spring."

The word *spring* hung between them. Neither reached for it again.

Tam sat on the floor by the stove with a stub of pencil and the back of a chart Noor had given him, drawing ships. He drew them all listing, all running before weather, and he gave each one a wake like a scar. When Noor read out the stores, the boy listened with the grave attention of someone who has been hungry before and intends to recognize the signs early this time.

"You'll have your supper," Noor told him, more sharply than intended, because the look in the child's eyes accused them of something they hadn't done yet.

That night the three of them ate pea soup at the same table, and it was the strangest thing that had happened at Gullwrack in twenty years. Noor had kept every meal alone since 1899 except for the inspectors and the rare visiting warden, meals taken standing up, quickly, like letters burned unread. Now there was a deserter with bandaged hands sitting where no one sat, ladling soup as though he'd been invited, and a child across from him blowing on his spoon with the exaggerated care of the very young performing adulthood.

"This is good soup," Callum said.

"It's peas and salt," Noor said. "Don't flatter it."

But they caught themselves watching him eat, noting how he held the spoon awkwardly until he found a grip that hid the damage, and they thought: *he is learning our table the way I learned this rock.* And they thought, further down, in the cellar of the mind where they kept the things they refused to look at: *I have done this before. Taken in what the sea gave me. Given it a name.*

* * *

It was Callum who found the wireless problem, and Callum who fixed it.

The set in the signal room was government issue, meant for weather bulletins and the scheduled exchanges with the coast station at Harrow Ness. It had developed a crackle two days after the storm that had delivered Tam, a dry static like sand pouring through the speaker, and Noor had endured it with the patience of a man enduring a limp — it still spoke, mostly. But Callum, poking about the station in his restless way, opened the back panel and began, uninvited, to diagnose.

"That capacitor's weeping," he said. "And this joint's gone green. May I?"

Noor should have said no. The set was theirs, the room was theirs, the whole rock was theirs, and every territorial instinct they owned bristled at a stranger's screwdriver in its wiring. But the stranger's hands were quick and certain, and there was something in the way he worked — head tilted, tongue between his teeth, entirely absorbed — that made refusal feel petty.

"Do what you like."

He soldered by lamplight with his damaged fingers curled like a bird's feet, guiding the iron with thumb and forefinger, and when he finished he closed the panel, wiped the dust from the casing with his sleeve, and switched on with the modest flourish of a man showing a card trick.

The crackle was gone. In its place, astonishingly clear, came the evening bulletin from the coast station: gale warnings for the Irish Sea, ice reported in the firths, and then, appended almost as an afterthought, a paragraph Noor played twice.

*...peace feelers continue via neutral channels, sources suggest negotiations may open formally in the new year, and several newspapers now speak openly of a cessation of hostilities by spring should talks hold...*

Spring. There it was again, arriving from a different direction.

"They've been saying peace since '14," Callum said quietly.

"Yes."

"But they haven't been saying it *like that.* Not in the bulletins. Bulletins don't speculate." He turned the dial slightly, hunting other stations, and the room filled with fragments — dance music leaking from some distant hall, a German voice reading numbers, a sermon, then sea-static, then nothing. He switched it off. "By spring," he said again, as if tasting it.

Noor did not answer, because they were doing the arithmetic that spring required. Spring meant the war ending. It also meant the war's paperwork beginning — inquiries, reconciliations, men coming home to find what had changed, clerks with time on their hands reopening files that four years of slaughter had left unattended. A missing child. A deserter. A lighthouse keeper whose name had never quite fit the mouth that used it.

Peace, Noor thought, might be the most dangerous weather of all.

* * *

CALLUM

You could learn a household the way you learned a trench: by its sounds, its timings, the places where the ground gave.

Frazer had learned Noor's within a week. The light turned at sunset and again at midnight, and between those turns Noor slept in a chair rather than a bed, upright, boots on, as though twenty years of keeping watch had fused them permanently to the posture. The kettle went on at five. The log was written in a hand so even it looked printed. And once a day — Frazer timed it to the minute after four days — Noor stood at the eastern window with a spyglass, looking not at the sea but along it, toward the mainland road, toward whatever might be coming.

Looking for whoever had left the boy, Frazer guessed. Or looking to make sure they weren't coming back.

He understood watchers. He had been one himself for four years, lying in wet chalk with his eye to a periscope, counting the enemy's habits until he knew them better than his own sergeant did. Watching was how you survived other people. It was also, he was beginning to suspect, how you ended up alone on a rock talking to a lamp.

The boy was easier to read than the keeper. Tam woke early and lay listening — Frazer could feel the difference in the house, that held-breath quality of a child deciding whether today was a safe day — and then he would appear in the kitchen doorway with his hair flat on one side and his eyes already working, cataloguing. He asked questions the way sappers laid wire: carefully, at angles.

"Did you kill anyone?" he asked Frazer one morning over porridge, conversationally, the way another child might ask about a dog.

Frazer set down his spoon. Across the table Noor went very still, the stillness of a man hearing ice shift underfoot.

"No," Frazer said. "I walked away before I had to find out."

"Why?"

"Because I decided the war wasn't mine." He held the boy's gaze. He would not lie to him; he had seen too many men lie to children about fathers and brothers, and he knew exactly what those lies cost later, at the graveside or the kitchen table, years on. "Some people would say that makes me a coward."

"Are you?"

"I'm still deciding."

Tam considered this with terrible seriousness, then nodded once, verdict reserved, and returned to his porridge. Frazer looked up and found Noor watching him with something new in their face — not warmth, nothing so generous as that, but a recalculation. An adjustment of the ledger. *Perhaps,* the look said, *you are what you appear to be, which would make you the only thing on this rock that is.*

That night, checking the lamp with Noor, Frazer risked it.

"The boat," he said. "When do you think she stopped coming? Before us or because of us?"

Noor's hands did not pause on the brasswork. "I've wondered the same."

"If someone reported a strange woman on the mainland asking after Gullwrack—" He stopped himself. He had promised silence about the note, about the handwriting, about everything the boy's presence implied, and he meant to keep it; a deserter's survival ran on kept promises. But Noor turned and looked at him, and Frazer saw that the keeper had known all along that he knew, and that the silence between them was itself a kind of document, countersigned daily.

"Eat your supper," Noor said, which was not an answer, and was.

* * *

NOOR

The routines braided themselves together the way ropes do, without anyone tying them.

Callum took the coal and the oil runs without being asked, and mended things — the door hinge, the fog signal's sticky valve, a chair Noor had not known was broken until it stopped rocking. He hummed when he worked, tunelessly, some music-hall fragment, and Noor caught themselves waiting for the humming the way you wait for a clock to strike. Tam followed the man everywhere, orbiting him, asking about knots and engines and whether ships really sank in ten minutes. Callum answered everything except the questions underneath the questions, and Noor, listening from the gallery, recognized the technique because it was their own.

On the ninth day of the boat's absence, Noor did something unprecedented: they left the two of them alone with the light.

Only for two hours. Only to walk the far side of the point, to the shingle beach where the wreckage of decades came ashore — spars, cork floats, a boot sole worn through, the ribs of a boat older than the war. Noor went to be alone, truly alone, for the first time since the boy arrived, and discovered within a hundred yards that solitude had changed its character. It no longer felt like a room. It felt like leaving a fire untended.

They stood among the wrack-line and admitted it to the grey water: the house behind them was warmer with those two in it. That the silence they had cultivated for twenty years, the silence they had called contentment the way a starving man calls a stone in his belly food, had been broken, and that they did not want it back.

This was precisely why it must end, some colder voice replied. Everything you take in, you take responsibility for. You know where that road goes. You have walked it carrying a name that wasn't yours, away from people who loved you, and you have seen what it leaves behind on the shore.

Noor picked up a white pebble, turned it in their fingers, and put it in their pocket anyway. Habit. Hope's cheapest currency.

Back at the station, smoke rose thin and straight from the chimney, and through the window they could see Tam at the table drawing, Callum bent beside him pointing at the page, and the lamp room above them both turning its slow faithful turn. A picture. Anyone rowing past would see a family.

Anyone rowing past, Noor thought, climbing the stair, could destroy it with a sentence.

* * *

CALLUM

The wireless became the hearth.

Each evening at seven, all three gathered in the signal room — Tam on the floor cross-legged, Frazer by the door where he could see the stairs, Noor in the chair by the set with their hands folded like a magistrate. The bulletins came in clipped and formal, and the child repeated the grand words to himself: *armistice*, *negotiations*, *neutral channels*, rolling them around like marbles.

"What's armistice mean?" he asked one night.

"It means the shooting stops," Frazer said.

"Then why don't they just say stop?"

Frazer laughed — his first real laugh in months, surprising them all, himself most of all. Even Noor's mouth moved at its corner, which Frazer was learning to read as a full smile.

But it was the spring talk that changed the temperature of the house. On the twelfth night a London station, faint but legible under the static, carried a commentator saying plainly what the bulletins only hinted: that the German lines were giving, that the American divisions were arriving faster than the Germans could bury their dead, and that reasonable men in reasonable clubs were now betting on March. April at the outside.

April. Frazer lay awake that night doing sums he hated. Armistice meant demobilization chaos, yes — millions of men moving, papers meaningless, borders porous. It also meant the machinery of pursuit winding down, deserters amnestied or forgotten, the whole vast bloody ledger being closed with a general pardon. He had heard talk of it even in the lines, before he walked: *when it ends, they'll forgive everyone, they'll have to, there'll be no cells big enough.*

Or they wouldn't. Or they'd hang a few examples to save face, and a man with no unit, no record, and forged travel documents would be the easiest example in Britain.

And the boy — the boy would need to be somewhere legitimate by spring, somewhere with a name attached, a school register, a parish. And Noor—

Frazer realized, turning it over in the dark, that he had started thinking of the keeper's fate as bound to his own. That somewhere in these weeks of coal and soup and shared watches, the three of them had become, in his private arithmetic, a single entry. Three names, one line. If the inspector ever came — and men like Frazer attracted inspectors the way wrecks attracted salvage — the line would sink together.

He got up quietly and checked the lamp oil, which did not need checking, and stood a while in the warm glass-smelling dark of the lantern room, watching the beam sweep the black water. Somewhere out there, past the reach of the light, the world was arranging its peace. Here there was only the light, the child asleep below, the keeper in their chair, and the fragile, temporary, absolutely real domesticity of it — a truce, like the ones in the trenches, held by mutual exhaustion and the fear that any movement would bring fire.

He went back down. In the kitchen, Noor was awake too, sitting at the table with a cup of tea untouched, and for a moment the two of them simply looked at each other across the lamplight, two sentries exchanging the watch neither wanted.

"She'll come back," Noor said. It took Frazer a moment to understand they meant the supply boat, and another moment to understand they didn't mean the supply boat at all.

"When she does," Frazer said carefully, "what will you tell her?"

Noor looked into the tea as if the answer floated there. "That depends," they said, "on what the government asks me first."

Outside, the wind had begun to rise again, and far off across the water, faint as a thought, the wireless left running in the signal room was picking up a bulletin — and Noor's face, in the lamplight, went suddenly still, the way it went still whenever the world knocked.
