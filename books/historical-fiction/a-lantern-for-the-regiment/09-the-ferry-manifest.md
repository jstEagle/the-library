## 9. The Ferry Manifest

The notice came with the November tide, folded inside a oilskin packet that had no business being on Gullwrack at all.

Noor found it on the morning of the ninth, when the storm had finally spent itself and the sea lay out to the horizon like a sheet of hammered pewter, still shivering from what had been done to it. The supply boat was three weeks overdue — the longest gap since Noor had taken the light — and so the arrival of anything from the mainland carried the weight of an event. The packet had been wedged under the door of the store-room, weighted with a stone, wrapped against the salt air with the thoroughness of someone who expected it to survive a drowning.

Inside were the usual things: a chit for lamp oil that would never be honored on time, a printed circular about blackout discipline that Noor read twice and understood not at all — who was there to black out against, on a rock where the only witnesses were gulls and drowned sailors? — and beneath those, a single sheet of heavier paper, creased once, bearing the crest of the Coastal Transport Authority.

Noor stood in the doorway of the store-room with the paper held up to the grey light and read it three times before the words settled into meaning.

*NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS RESIDENT ON THE OUTERN ISLES AND COASTAL STATIONS.*

*In consequence of the closure of the winter service, ONE FINAL CIVILIAN PASSAGE will depart Harrow Quay on or about the twenty-second day of November, weather permitting, bound south. Berths are strictly limited. All persons desiring passage must present themselves with DOCUMENTED PROOF OF IDENTITY. No berth will be issued without papers. No exceptions will be granted. This is the last sailing until spring.*

Below that, a list of authorized documents. A passport. A military discharge book. A parish register extract certified by a clergyman. An identity card issued under the Defence of the Realm provisions.

Noor lowered the paper slowly, as though it might go off.

* * *

For twenty years, Noor Underhill had been a man composed almost entirely of paperwork that did not exist.

It was a strange thing to realize at fifty-one years of age, standing in a cold store-room smelling of lamp oil and brine, holding a document that demanded he produce himself in triplicate. He had built his life the way one builds a drystone wall — without mortar, each stone chosen and fitted by hand, nothing holding it together but gravity and patience and the weight of all the other stones. His name, borrowed. his history, invented in fragments and then worn smooth by repetition until even he sometimes half-believed it. *Underhill*, he had told supply skippers and inspectors and one memorable excise officer in 1904, *like the folk who lived under the hills in the old stories. My father's choice.* And they had nodded, because it was a good solid name, the kind of name that belonged to a man who kept a light and asked no questions, and because nobody looks hard at a lighthouse keeper. A keeper is furniture. A keeper is part of the rock.

But a berth on a civilian ferry required a man to stop being furniture. It required him to stand in front of some clerk at Harrow Quay — a clerk with a stamp and a ledger and, very likely by now, a circular or two pinned to the office wall — and say *I am this person, and here is the paper that proves it.*

And the paper would be a forgery, or worse than a forgery: it would be nothing at all.

Noor walked out onto the gallery with the notice still in his hand and looked south, down the long grey reach of water toward the mainland, invisible beyond the curve of the world. The wind had dropped to a mutter. Somewhere below, in the cottage, Tam was awake — he could hear the boy moving, the particular careful tread of a child trying to be quiet in a house that wasn't his, which was louder than any noise a child could make on purpose.

Three berths, if you counted generously. Three people on Gullwrack now, counting the boy and the deserter asleep in the boatshed with his hands still bandaged from the lamp mechanism. Two names between them that would survive scrutiny.

The arithmetic sat in Noor's chest like swallowed ballast.

* * *

He made tea and toast in the cottage kitchen and set Tam's portion on the table without comment, the way he had done every morning for five weeks now, and the boy ate it the way he did everything: watchfully, hungrily, as if rationing himself against a famine that might resume. Five weeks since the storm had left him on the doorstep. Five weeks of lying to himself in installments — *when the weather lifts I'll wire the authorities; when the supply boat comes I'll put him aboard; when the roads open* — each deferral smoother and more comfortable than the last, until the lie had worn a groove in his days deep enough to walk in without looking down.

"The supply boat's not coming," Noor said. "Not till spring."

Tam looked up. He had Wren's eyes. That was the thing Noor could not get past, morning after morning — not the resemblance in general, which he might have talked himself out of, but the specific fact of the eyes, which were their mother's eyes, which had looked at Noor across a supper table in another century while their mother said *eat your greens, both of you, there are children starving further north.* "Then how do we get things?"

"We don't. We make do." Noor sat down across from him. The notice lay in his pocket like a coal. "There's a boat. From the mainland. One last one before the winter closes the crossing."

"Are we going on it?"

The question landed with the terrible simplicity children carry, like stones they pick up and hand you without knowing the weight. Noor looked at him — nine years old, collarbones like a coat-hanger, hair cut badly by someone in a hurry — and felt the two halves of himself grind against each other like millstones.

"I don't know yet," Noor said, and was surprised to find it was the truth. Not *no*. Not the practiced, automatic *no* of twenty years. *I don't know yet.*

Tam considered this, chewing. Then he said, "Mama said if anyone asks, my name is Tam Rookery and I come from Ashfell and my father's dead." He said it in a flat reciting voice, a catechism, and then went back to his toast as though he had remarked upon the weather.

*Rookery.* Not Zhou. Not the family name at all — another borrowed name, another drystone wall, and Noor felt something move in his chest that was half grief and half a horrible kinship. The boy had been taught the same survival. Wren had built him a wall too.

"And your mother," Noor said carefully, "she told you to say that to everyone?"

"To everyone." Tam swallowed. "She said especially to men in good coats. Men in good coats ask questions that aren't really questions." He looked up, and for a moment the watchfulness in his face was so old, so exactly the expression Noor saw in his own shaving mirror every morning, that Noor had to look away and busy himself with the teapot.

Men in good coats. There would be men in good coats at Harrow Quay, checking papers against faces, faces against lists. There might be men in good coats carrying a description of a missing boy, age nine, last seen —

Last seen where? Noor didn't know. That was the torment of it. He knew only that Wren had written *forget this face* and brought the boy through storms to the one door on the coast she should never have been able to find, which meant she knew things about Noor's hiding that Noor had believed unknowable, and if Wren knew, then whoever hunted her might know, and if they knew —

He poured tea he didn't want and drank it anyway, scalding, to give his hands something honest to do.

* * *

In the afternoon he climbed to the boatshed.

Callum Frazer heard him coming — the man always heard everything, a leftover of whatever war had taught him — and was sitting up on the cot by the time Noor shouldered the door open, hands wrapped in clean linen, back against the wall, occupying the smallest possible space the way he always did. As if he could make himself a smaller problem.

"Supply boat's not coming till spring," Noor said, without preamble. "But there's a ferry. One last civilian sailing out of Harrow Quay, end of the month. South, before the winter closes the water."

Callum's face did something complicated and fast, there and gone, the way weather moves over water. Hope, Noor thought. Real hope, the naked kind, immediately ashamed of itself. "Berths?"

"Limited. And—" Noor took out the notice and held it up. Callum read it from where he sat, lips moving slightly, and Noor watched the hope drain out of him degree by degree as he reached the middle of the page.

"Documented proof of identity," Callum said flatly. "Well." He laughed once, short and bloodless. "That's me finished, then. My papers are in France. In somebody's pocket, likely, if they didn't burn them." His bandaged hands flexed against his knees. "A discharge book would do it. Mine'd do nicely, if I had it. Name, regiment, number, character stamped *exemplary* by a sergeant-major who's probably dead now. Only trouble is, the man it describes deserted at Étaples, and if I stand in front of a clerk and claim it, the clerk'll have me shot before the kettle boils."

The word *shot* sat in the little shed like a bird that had flown in and couldn't find the window.

"You could stay," Noor said. It surprised him coming out. "Through the winter. There's work enough. The mechanism needs hands that aren't mine, and I can't keep hauling coal alone the way I used to."

Callum looked at him for a long moment. In the grey light from the doorway his face was all angles and stubble and the particular pallor of a man who has not slept properly in years. "You'd keep a deserter through the winter," he said. "On a station with a naval inspector sniffing up the coast after failed supply runs. You don't know me from Adam. For all you know I'd slit your throat for the coal money."

"For the coal money," Noor agreed. "There's eleven pounds in the biscuit tin. Hardly worth sharpening a knife."

It wasn't a joke, quite, but Callum smiled anyway, the first smile Noor had seen on him — crooked, startled, as though the muscles had rusted. Then the smile faded and he looked down at his ruined hands. "And the boy?" he said quietly.

"What about him?"

"He's on the manifest too, isn't he. If there's a manifest." Callum raised his eyes. "One last boat. Limited berths. Papers required. You've got a child on this rock with no papers at all — I'd wager my other hand on it. So either he stays here through the winter with a keeper who's already feeding him off his own ration, or he goes south, and going south means standing in front of a clerk and producing a name that'll hold water." He paused. "Whose name, Mr. Underhill?"

The question hung there. Outside, a gull cried once, twice, and gave up.

"I don't know," Noor said again, and hated how often the phrase had crossed his lips lately. Twenty years of certainty — narrow, pinched, but certain, the certainty of a man who has reduced his life to a lamp schedule and a tide table — and now every answer was *I don't know*, as though the war had reached all the way out to Gullwrack after all and knocked the ballast loose.

"Well," Callum said at last, settling back against the wall, closing his eyes as though the conversation were a door he could shut. "Between the three of us, we've got one name apiece that's real, and I'd guess not one document between us. Whoever gets on that boat will be travelling on somebody else's life." His eyes stayed closed. "Best start deciding whose."

* * *

That night Noor climbed the tower and lit the lamp and let the routine take him the way it always did — the wick trimmed, the mantle glowing, the great lens gathering itself into motion overhead with its sound like a slow heartbeat, sending the beam out across the dark water in long patient strokes. Four seconds bright, four seconds dark. The oldest promise on the coast. *Here is where you are. Here. Here.*

He kept the light and thought about names.

His own true name he had not written down in twenty years. He had not spoken it aloud except once, drunk, to a sea that kept it. It lived in him the way a bullet lives in a body that never got it cut out — a fact of anatomy more than memory, aching when the weather changed. To write it on a ferry manifest would be to take it out and hold it up to lamplight. To say *this is who I am, check me against your lists.* And somewhere inland, perhaps, there were still records with that name attached to a crime committed by a brother who had died owing everyone everything, and clerks talk, and inspectors collect circulars, and a name spoken aloud travels faster than any ship.

To use the false name was impossible — *Underhill* existed nowhere on paper; he had spent two decades making sure of it, and now the diligence of his own invisibility had become the locked door between him and the mainland.

And Tam — Tam needed a name that would survive a clerk's glance. *Rookery of Ashfell* might, if no one checked Ashfell, if no one had wired ahead, if the hunting men Wren feared had not by now described the boy to every port official between here and the border. Or Tam could travel under Callum's name — a son, a nephew — if Callum consented, and Callum's name was a hanging offence wearing a respectable coat.

Three passengers. Every ticket forged. Every forging of a ticket an act of exposure — because a forged document is a confession waiting to be read, a thread that, pulled, unravels everything: the boy, the deserter, and behind them both, twenty years deep, a keeper called Underhill who never existed at all.

The lens turned above him. Bright, dark. Bright, dark.

Noor realized, somewhere in the second hour of the watch, that he had stopped thinking of the ferry as escape and started thinking of it as a confession booth — a room at the end of a pier where, to save the boy, he would have to stand and produce words on paper, and words on paper were the one currency he had refused to deal in for twenty years. Either he stayed on Gullwrack and kept his invisibility whole and sent the others out on lies he had crafted for them, or he went with them and wrote his true name in a government ledger and let the world in through the wound.

Or — and the thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do, dressed as practicality — there was a third way. Papers could be made. Callum had been a clerk in the army before the war took him; Noor had seen the neat serviceable handwriting on the labels of the man's few possessions. Ink, good linen paper, a steady hand, and the Authority's crest copied from the notice itself. A man could build a small paper family in an evening. A man could forge his way to salvation, if his nerve held.

The thought sat down beside him at the rail and stayed there, patient as the fog coming in off the water, and Noor looked out into the darkness where the beam swept and swept and touched nothing it could trust, and admitted at last what the arithmetic of the manifest meant: that the choice was no longer whether to leave Gullwrack. The choice was which lie to tell, and how many names to spend buying it, and whether his own was among the spending.

Below in the cottage, faintly, he heard the boy turn over in his sleep. Bright, dark. Bright, dark.

Tomorrow, Noor thought, he would have to begin asking Tam questions — real ones, careful ones, the kind a man asks when he is building a story for a clerk — and he dreaded it, because the boy remembered things. Mothers' instructions. Names. Faces. And a child's memory, offered freely across a breakfast table, could undo in five minutes what twenty years of silence had built.

He trimmed the wick, and turned his face to the sea, and began — though he did not yet know it — to compose the questions.
