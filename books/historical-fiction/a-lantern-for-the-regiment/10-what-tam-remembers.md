## 10. What Tam Remembers

The first thing Tam remembered of his mother was not her face but her hands, which were always moving—braiding, buttoning, folding a note small enough to disappear inside a coat lining. Hands that smelled of lamp oil and river water and sometimes, on good days, of the honey cakes she sold to the ferry men at the quay.

*If we are ever parted,* she had said, in the last week, kneeling so their eyes were level, *you will remember three things. You will not say our name. You will not say my name. And if anyone is kind to you, you will watch them for three days before you trust them.*

*Why three days?*

*Because kindness is easy,* she said. *Keeping it is what takes time.*

He had counted four days now. Four days since the water, since the boat, since the man who rowed him through the dark and lifted him over a stone sill he was too cold to climb himself. Four days since he woke in a bed that smelled of salt and paraffin, under blankets rough as dog fur, in a room where the wind never stopped talking through some gap it had found and refused to give up.

And the lighthouse keeper—the tall one with the grey shot through their black hair, the one whose face did something strange when they first looked down at him, like a door opening onto a room no one had entered in years—that keeper had been kind every single day.

Tam lay in the narrow bed and watched the ceiling beam and did his counting.

*Three days,* his mother had said. But she had also said, once, when he asked why they moved so often, why Papa's name changed on letters: *Trust is a thing you spend carefully, Tam-mine. Like coins. Some people get one coin. A very few get all of them.*

He decided, on the fourth morning, to begin spending.

* * *

The keeper's name, as far as Tam could learn, was Mr. Underhill. It was written inside two books on the shelf by the stove, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the words were running from something: *A. Underhill.* The same hand as the note—the crumpled note the keeper kept folded in a tin box on the high shelf, which Tam knew about because he had watched, from the stairs, while the keeper took it out and read it on the third night, thinking the child asleep.

"Forget this face." Tam had heard those words before too. Not read—heard. His mother whispering them over him the night the men came to the house in Colwick, her fingers pressing his eyelids shut like she was sealing an envelope. *Forget this face, forget this house, forget everything but me.*

He hadn't forgotten anything. That was the trouble with grown-ups. They thought children were buckets with holes in them.

That fourth morning, the keeper fried kippers and set them before him with bread and tea far too strong, cut with milk in a chipped blue cup, and Tam ate and watched the way you watch a tide pool before stepping in.

"Mr. Underhill," he said.

The keeper's shoulders did a small thing—not a flinch exactly, more the way a rope goes taut before it parts. "Noor."

"Noor," Tam repeated. It was a strange name, soft at both ends. "Is that your real name?"

The silence that followed had weight to it, like fog. The keeper turned back to the stove though there was nothing left to cook, and when they answered their voice was level as a table top. "It's the name I keep the light under. That makes it real enough."

"But is it your *real* name?"

"Where did you learn to ask questions like that?" Noor said, and there was something underneath the words—not anger. Something wearier and sadder, like a door creaking on a hinge long rusted.

"My mama says names are coats," Tam said. "You can hang up one and put on another, but underneath you're still the same size." He had made that part up, or half of it; his mother had said something like it once on the ferry to Dunmarrow, and he had carried the shape of her meaning even when the exact words fell away. He watched Noor very carefully as he said it.

The keeper set down the pan too hard. It rang against the iron stove and the sound went through the floor and out into the gulls.

"Eat your kippers," Noor said.

But their hand, when they passed the bread, was trembling just slightly, and Tam saw it, and filed it away the way his mother had taught him without ever knowing she taught him: *watch the hands, Tam-mine. Faces can lie. Hands forget to.*

* * *

In the afternoons the keeper let him climb the tower—not to the lamp room itself, that was forbidden while the light was lit, but to the second gallery where the windows gave onto the whole grey world. The sea went on forever in every direction, hammered flat and silver, and the wind came through the vents singing one long note that never ended.

"You live here alone?" Tam asked on the fifth day. He stood close against Noor's side because the wind tried to pick him up like a leaf.

"I did. Before."

"Before me?"

Noor looked down at him then, and Tam looked straight back, and held it, the way you hold a fish that wants to thrash. Something moved behind the keeper's eyes—recognition or dread or both braided together—and finally Noor said, "Yes. Before you," in a voice gone quiet as the space between lightning and thunder.

"Do you have brothers and sisters?"

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I don't know if I do." This was true, or true-shaped. He knew his mother had family somewhere inland—a sister she spoke of only in the dark, a brother whose name made her stop talking altogether. *We don't speak of him,* she'd say, and press her lips together until they went white. But a boy could wonder. A boy lying awake in a strange tower with the wind talking could build whole families out of wondering.

Noor turned away to the window. Far out, a line of storm cloud sat on the horizon like a lid waiting to be put on.

"Every family has someone it doesn't talk about," Noor said at last. "Best lesson I can teach you, and I got it dear."

"How much dear?"

That startled a laugh out of the keeper—one short, rusty bark, as if laughter were a machine run rarely and badly oiled. "More than you'll ever see in your pocket, lad. Come down before the weather turns."

* * *

On the sixth day Tam found the photograph.

It happened honestly—he was hunting for the tin box again, wanting another look at the note, and pulled down instead a flat wooden case that fell open in his hands. Inside, stiff as a pressed flower, was a picture of three young people standing before a brick wall: a woman with a fierce mouth, a man with his hat tipped back, and a third figure, slighter, standing half-turned away, as if the photographer had caught them mid-departure.

Tam knew the woman's face. He had seen it every day of his life, younger and unafraid, laughing at something outside the frame.

His mother.

His heart went strange and loud. He looked at the half-turned figure and felt the world tilt, because there was something in the slope of those shoulders, the angle of that jaw, that he had been looking at across a breakfast table for six days. Younger. Different name, maybe different everything. But the same.

Footsteps on the stair. He barely had time to shove the case back on the shelf before Noor came into the room carrying split wood for the stove, and stopped dead.

Tam watched the keeper look at the shelf, at the case sitting crooked where he'd shoved it wrong, and he watched the color go out of Noor's face like water out of a pulled plug.

"That's my mama," Tam said.

The firewood hit the floor. Not thrown—dropped, the arms simply giving up their job.

"She doesn't look like that anymore," Tam went on, because now that he had opened the door he could not make himself stop; the words came out of him like water finding a crack. "She's thinner. She cries at night when she thinks I'm asleep. She told me to forget things but I didn't. I don't forget." His voice was climbing and he couldn't help it. "Who is she? Why is her picture here? Who are *you?*"

For a long moment Noor did not move at all. Then they crossed the room and knelt—knelt, so that for once the keeper was smaller than the boy—and took his hands, and their own hands were shaking now, no hiding it, shaking like leaves.

"What did she tell you," Noor said, "when she sent you to me?"

So. So it was true. Tam felt it land in him like a stone dropping through deep water—the keeper had known all along, had known and fed him and wrapped his feet in wool and never said a word.

"She said—" Tam's breath hitched. He made himself say it, all of it, the instructions he had guarded like coins in a fist. "She said, go to Gullwrack Point. Find the light. The person who keeps it shares our blood and hates us for it, but they will take you in, because under all the hating they are the best of us. She said—" and here his voice broke, because this was the part he had repeated to himself in the dark boat, in the wet stone doorway, in this bed with its dog-fur blankets— "she said, *tell them Wren still keeps the ledger, and the last page is blank.*"

Noor made a sound Tam had never heard a grown person make. Not a word. Something older than words, like the noise the wind made in the tower's throat on bad nights.

Then the keeper bowed their head until their forehead nearly touched the boy's knuckles, and stayed that way a long time, and Tam, who had watched for six days, understood that he was watching twenty years break open all at once, and that whatever came next would change the shape of both their lives.

"Wren," Noor whispered into the quiet. "Oh, Wren. You always did keep accounts."

* * *

They talked until the light failed and Noor had to climb to the lamp room, and then they talked more, Tam sitting on the spiral stair halfway up, shouting questions into the turning light, Noor answering down through the clank and hum of the machinery in a voice worn smooth with use.

Piece by piece, the story assembled itself in Tam's head, the way his mother's face assembled out of darkness when she struck a match: a family inland, three children. A brother whose crime Tam still didn't understand—only that money was involved, and a man who died, and a name that had to be buried so deep it could never be dug up. The youngest of the three had walked away from all of it, taken a new name, come to the edge of the sea, and spent twenty years keeping a light so that ships wouldn't die, as if drowning strangers could pay for a drowned past.

"And Mama stayed," Tam said. "She stayed near them. All that time."

"All that time," Noor called down, and the machinery turned, and the beam swept out and around, out and around, like a great slow heartbeat. "I used to see her name in shop ledgers, in parish rolls. I never once wrote to her. I told myself it was to protect her." A pause. The lamp turned. "Lies make excellent lamps, Tam. They burn a long time and they light nothing."

Down below, the door of the station banged open on the rising wind, and Callum Frazer came in stamped with rain, stamping harder, calling up the stairwell that the barometer had fallen like a stone off a cliff and there was weather coming the like of which the coast hadn't seen in years—

He stopped. He was looking at Tam on the stairs, and at Noor leaning out of the lamproom hatch above, and at the space between them, which was full of something that hadn't been there yesterday—something spoken, something shared, a secret with a new shape.

Callum's eyes narrowed. Rain ran off the end of his nose.

"Well," he said slowly, hanging his coat on its nail with exaggerated care. "Sounds like you two've been having quite the conversation."

Tam looked at the deserter, and the deserter looked back, and Tam remembered his mother's voice one more time: *some people get one coin, Tam-mine. A very few get all of them.*

He decided Callum Frazer was not getting any coins at all.

Above them, unseen, Noor closed the logbook on the desk in the lamp room—but not before writing, in the margin beside the weather readings, a single word in that hard-slanting hand, the first entry in twenty years that wasn't wind or miles or hours: *Wren.*

Outside, the horizon swallowed itself whole, and the storm began.
