## 11. Callum's Bargain

The mackerel sky came in on Thursday, ribbed like the inside of a shell, and Callum Frazer learned to read it the way he had once learned to read the faces of sergeants deciding whether a man was fit for the line or fit only for the pit behind it. High cloud, wind backing from southwest to south, a smell of iron in the water. Noor Zhou stood at the gallery rail with their hands folded into their sleeves and said nothing at all, which in Callum's short experience of them was itself a kind of forecast.

*It'll blow by nightfall,* they'd said that morning, and that was all.

Callum had been eleven days at Gullwrack Point. Eleven days of oil and wicks and the particular grammar of a household built around a lamp — *the light comes first*, Noor said, not as piety but as arithmetic; everything else was remainder. He had slept in the boat-room on a cot that smelled of tar and old herring nets, and each morning he woke with the same half-second of terror, reaching for kit that wasn't there, before remembering where he was: the end of the world, or close enough to it that the war couldn't find him.

Except that wars didn't lose things. Wars sent men looking.

He found the papers where he'd hidden them — inside the false bottom of his boot heel, wrapped in oilcloth because a man who had walked two hundred miles through November Scotland learned what rain could do to a life. He took them out now, in the privacy of the boat-room while Noor trimmed the lamp above him and Tam — the boy, the child, the small quiet catastrophe — was down at the tide pools turning over stones.

Two documents. His own discharge papers, forged well enough to have carried him past three checkpoints, naming him Private A. Mercer, Royal Army Service Corps, discharged medically unfit. And beneath that, the thing he should have burned on the first night but hadn't, because a man kept proof even when proof would hang him: his real regimental record. Callum Frazer, 7th Battalion, marked absent without leave since the fourteenth of October.

Desertion in the face of the enemy, some courts called it. He hadn't run *from* anything. That was the joke of it, the black joke he turned over every night like a stone with no underside. He had walked out of a dressing station near Ypres with his left hand bandaged to the size of a loaf and his ears still ringing from a shell that had killed the two men either side of him, and he had simply kept walking — out of the station, out of the town, onto a train, off a train, walking. Not fear. Fear he understood; fear was weather. It was something worse and simpler: he had looked down the road ahead of him, the road that led back to his battalion, back up the line, and his legs had refused. His body had voted against his country. And once a man's legs had voted, there was no court-martial that could un-vote them.

He rewrapped the papers and put them away and sat on the cot with his ruined hand in his lap, the fingers curled like a dead bird's, and thought about the ferry manifest pinned to the kitchen door upstairs.

*ONE FURTHER SAILING WILL BE MADE FROM GULLWRACK QUAY ON OR ABOUT THE ELEVENTH DAY OF NOVEMBER, WEATHER PERMITTING. PASSENGERS MUST PRESENT DOCUMENTATION OF IDENTITY AND RESIDENCE. NO BERTHS WITHOUT PAPERS.*

He'd read it forty times. Three people in this house. Two berths — no, one berth and a half, if you counted the way Noor had gone quiet over the notice, the way their thumb had moved along the word *documentation* like a man testing an edge.

And Tam had no papers at all. Tam had a note in a woman's hand — *forget this face* — and a coat too fine for a lighthouse keeper's foundling and eyes that were the wrong colour entirely to belong to Noor Zhou, whoever Noor Zhou was.

Callum had watched them together for eleven days. Watched Noor teach the boy to trim a wick, watched the boy pretend clumsiness he didn't feel, watched both of them circling something neither would name. He'd seen enough men hide from enough things to know the shape of hiding when it lived under his own roof. And he knew — the way he knew weather, the way he knew which officers read letters and which burned them — that Noor Zhou was a name worn like a coat, buttoned wrong on purpose.

Which meant the house held three liars and a child, and the ferry would carry two.

* * *

He waited until the storm arrived to do it, because storms made men generous and also made them deaf. The wind came up from the southeast after supper with a sound like the whole sea clearing its throat, and Noor went up to the lantern room, and Tam was set to polishing the storm panes with newspaper until his arms ached, and Callum washed the dishes and dried his good hand on his trousers ten times before the boy finally clattered down the stair and went to bed in the little room off the kitchen that had become his.

Then Callum climbed the tower.

He knew the count of the stairs now — sixty-one, the last five iron and colder than the rest. The lantern room roared softly around its great glass eye, the wind worrying the mullions like a dog at a bone, and Noor stood at the clockwork with the winding key braced against their hip, turning it with the unhurried strength of someone who had done this eight thousand times and expected to do it eight thousand more. They didn't look around. They said, over the noise:

"Hand me the spare key off the hook."

He did. Their fingers brushed his — the bandaged right, the whole left — and he saw again how carefully they never looked at his hands, how scrupulously they avoided the question of why a man who claimed a medical discharge wouldn't show a medical wound.

"Noor." The wind ate half of it. He stepped closer, into the lee of the clockwork housing, and raised his voice. "I want to talk about the eleventh."

The winding stopped. Just for a turn, just for a heartbeat, then resumed — but Callum had spent years reading the smallest hesitations of men about to be shelled, and he knew what he'd seen.

"Nothing to talk about," Noor said. "Boat comes or doesn't come. Papers or no papers."

"Papers." He let the word sit. "Aye. Papers. Now here's my difficulty, keeper. I've papers — good ones, better than good, I drew them myself and I'll swear to their quality. But they're in a name that isn't mine, and yours—" he watched their shoulders "—yours is a name I'd wager my other hand doesn't appear in any registry this side of Edinburgh. And the boy has nothing but a note telling the world to forget him. So. One berth. Three names. And only one of us," he said gently, "can write a true one."

The clockwork ticked on, indifferent. Outside, the light swept its great arc — dark, white, dark, white — laying its slow stripe across the sea and the rain and the two of them standing in the glass drum at the top of the world.

Noor finished the winding, hung the key on its hook with a precision that was pure performance, and turned. In the lamplight their face was unreadable as a coin worn smooth. "Say what you've come to say, Frazer."

"I can make papers." He kept his voice level, reasonable, the voice of a man proposing a business arrangement and nothing more. "Not forgeries exactly — alterations. A discharge is a discharge; change the name, change the number, age the ink, and any harbour official who isn't looking for a reason to look will pass it. I did it for myself well enough to walk through Glasgow." He paused. "I can do it three times. I can put the boy in a name, give him a mother's maiden paper, a birth certificate, whatever the manifest demands. I can give you a history clean as a whistle. I'm offering, keeper, to manufacture three honest lies so that nobody has to choose."

"And your price."

There it was. Noor didn't flinch, didn't moralise, simply asked for the figure, and Callum felt something twist in his chest that was almost respect. You could work with a person like that. You could survive beside a person like that.

"Silence," he said. "You keep my secret and I keep yours. The boy's too. Nobody asks anybody where anybody came from, ever, not on that quay, not on that boat, not after. Because here's the truth of it, keeper, the thing you already know and won't say: we're not three passengers. We're three fugitives sharing a lamp, and if any one of us falls, we all go down holding the rope." He nodded upward, at the light. "You saved me from Hale's questions once already. I'm asking you to make it permanent."

"You'd forge a child's identity." Flat. Not accusation — inventory.

"I'd forge a child a future," Callum said, and heard the steel come into his own voice and let it. "Because I've read that notice same as you, and I know what happens to undocumented persons when the inspector comes back, and he *will* come back. A boy with no papers isn't a passenger, he's cargo for the authorities, and authorities ask whose he is, and where's his mother, and why does a lighthouse keeper on the arse-end of nowhere have him — and those questions, keeper, travel backwards fast. Faster than you'd like. Faster than the ferry."

The silence stretched. The light turned. Somewhere below them the sea hit the rocks with a sound like a door slammed by God.

"And you," Noor said at last. "What name do you sail under?"

"Abercrombie," Callum said. "Or Mercer, or Fraser with an s — I've drafts of all three in my head. Give me ink, paper, and two evenings and you'll have documents that would pass the King himself." He hesitated, and then, because honesty between thieves was the only kind that held, added: "One thing you should know before you decide. My real name is on a list somewhere. Deserters get circulars same as missing children do. If Hale has my description and the boy's in the same file drawer—" he shrugged, careful, casual, failing at both "—then maybe we're already tied together, whether you take my offer or not. Maybe that's not a bargain at all. Maybe it's just arithmetic."

Noor looked at him for a long moment. The lamplight moved across their face, and for the first time in eleven days Callum saw something crack in it — not softness, exactly, but recognition. The look of a person finding another person's chains and knowing the weight of them.

"You've been planning this since the day you arrived," Noor said.

"Since the day I read the notice."

"And if I say no? If I say the boy goes to the authorities when the weather lifts, as I ought to have done weeks ago?"

Callum smiled, and hated the smile, and wore it anyway. "Then I'll wish you luck with that, keeper. Truly. But you've fed him and clothed him and taught him the wick-trimming, and he calls you by a name he made up himself because you wouldn't give him yours, and no one — no one — hands a child to the authorities after that. Not unless they're made of stone." He spread his ruined hand, the dead bird, the evidence. "And I don't think you're made of stone. I think you're made of the same thing I am. Something that ran."

* * *

They came down the stairs together, sixty-one steps, the storm pressing at the tower like a crowd at a gate. At the kitchen door Noor stopped, one hand on the latch, and spoke without turning round.

"The boy sleeps light. Say nothing in front of him. Nothing about names, nothing about boats, nothing about the eleventh. He hears everything, that one. He's been counting days since he got here — did you know that? Little marks on the inside of the pantry shelf, pencil, like a prisoner." A pause. "Like a prisoner," Noor said again, quieter, and Callum understood that the words were aimed inward as much as at him.

"I know how to be quiet," Callum said. "It's the one trade I'm master of."

Noor opened the door. Warmth and lamplight spilled out, and the smell of the bread they'd baked that afternoon, and beyond it the small room where a nine-year-old boy lay sleeping with his mother's note folded flat under his pillow like a talisman.

"Two evenings," Noor said. "Ink and paper tomorrow. And Frazer—" They turned then, and their eyes in the doorway were the eyes of someone stepping off a cliff in the dark and calling it a path. "Make them good. Make them so good that even I believe them. Because if we're going to hang together on the eleventh, I'd as soon hang on decent handwriting."

They went in. The door closed. Callum stood alone in the wind a moment longer, looking out at the black tumbling water, and felt the knot tighten — the one he'd tied himself, strand by strand, since the night he'd walked out of Ypres with his legs voting against his country. Complicity, he thought, was a strange comfort. Like a rope thrown to a drowning man that turns out to be tied to another drowning man.

Below him, faint through the wall, he heard Noor checking the boy's blanket, and the boy's voice, sleepy, asking something, and Noor's answer, too low to catch.

Then the wind rose another degree, and the tower groaned above him, and Callum went in to sleep the sleep of a man who had sold his last clean thing — and dreamed, as he always dreamed, of a dressing station at dusk, and a road leading back, and his legs refusing.
