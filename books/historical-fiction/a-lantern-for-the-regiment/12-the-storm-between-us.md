## 12. The Storm Between Us

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, the needle sliding down the dial like a man descending a staircase he already knows will end in water. By Wednesday evening it stood at 28.6 inches and trembling, and Noor Zhou stood in the lamp room with a mug of tea going cold in one hand and read the sky the way other people read letters from home — slowly, twice, with a growing weight behind the sternum.

The horizon had gone the color of pewter. Not grey; pewter, with a hard metallic sheen along its edge where the sea lifted itself to meet the cloud. The gulls had vanished inland two days ago, which was their way of filing a formal complaint about what was coming. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath, coming now only in long, warm, wrong-feeling draughts that smelled of weed and far-off rain.

"Tam," Noor called down the spiral stair, and heard the boy's feet come clattering. "Bring me the spare wicks and the oilskin bag from the store room. And your boots."

"Are we going out?"

"We're going to be ready not to go out."

It was a lie, and they both knew it. There was no *not going out* tonight. The gallery would need clearing of anything loose; the fog signal's fuel line would need checking; the shutters over the seaward panes would need bracing before the first gusts arrived like creditors. Tam appeared at the foot of the stairs with the bag hugged to his chest and his hair standing up where he'd slept on it, nine years old and solemn as a sexton.

"Is it a big one?"

Noor looked at the sea again. Twenty years of keeping this light had taught them a vocabulary of storms the way a priest learns Latin — not by study but by repetition until the meanings went into the bone. This one had the set of the great blow of 1909, the one that had taken the mizzen off the *Cormorant* and driven her hull onto the shingle below the point. It had the set of worse than that.

"It's a big one," Noor said. "So we do everything small and careful, in order, and we don't hurry anywhere on those stairs. Understood?"

"Understood." He said it the way soldiers said it, which was something else Noor tried not to think about — a nine-year-old who had learned obedience from a world at war, delivered to their door half-drowned with a note reading *forget this face* in handwriting Noor had known since childhood and had spent twenty years pretending not to know.

Wren's handwriting. His mother's. Which made the boy what Noor could not yet bring themselves to say aloud even inside their own skull: kin. Blood of the family whose name Noor had buried under this borrowed one, this lighthouse name, this *Zhou* that fit them like an overcoat buttoned to the chin.

Downstairs, Callum Frazer was already awake, though it was barely past four. He sat at the kitchen table with his bandaged hands flat on the wood, palms down, as if holding the table to the floor. The burns from the boiler incident three weeks ago had healed badly across the left palm — Noor had re-dressed them every second day, and each dressing revealed skin that pulled and shone like candle wax. A deserter's hands. A man who had walked out of Passchendaele or somewhere shaped like it, carrying his discharge papers folded around a death sentence, and who had asked for nothing except to be invisible for a while.

Two invisible people and one hidden child, keeping a light together through the worst winter the coast had seen in a decade.

"Coffee?" Callum asked.

"No time. It'll spill anyway once she hits." Noor nodded toward the window, where the first spits of rain were already ticking against the glass like fingernails. "I need you below tonight. Not the lamp — I'll manage the lamp. I need you on the clockwork and the weights, because if the power of this thing comes down the tower faster than I can crank, I'll need hands on the governor brake, and mine won't be enough."

Callum looked at his hands. Then he looked up, and there was that quality in his face Noor had learned to dread and depend on in equal measure: the flat readiness of a man who had been afraid for so long that fear had stopped being an event and become a climate.

"You know my hands aren't—"

"I know exactly what your hands are," Noor said. "That's why I'm asking. You'll feel the mechanism better than I will. Skin remembers pressure even when it's scarred." It was true, and it was also a kindness dressed as practicality, and Callum saw through it, and accepted it anyway, which was its own kind of trust.

"All right," he said.

Tam had gone very quiet by the stove. Children always knew. They knew the way animals knew, without instruments, without barometers — some older instrument in the blood taking readings. He had his arms around his knees and his chin on his arms and his eyes moving between the two men the way they moved between the waves when a boat was late.

"Will the light go out?" he asked.

"No," Noor said, with more certainty than any keeper had ever honestly possessed. "That's the whole of my job, lad. Everything else is negotiable. The light doesn't go out."

* * *

It came ashore at eleven o'clock, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

The first gust struck the north face of the tower with a sound like a door slammed by a giant, and after that there was no silence again, no interval, only degrees of roar. Rain came horizontally, in freight-loads, in sheets that hit the glass hard enough to make Noor flinch on the ladder between landings. The sea below the point turned white from the rock line clear out to the buoy ground — not whitecaps but whole fields of white, torn off the tops of waves that must have run thirty feet, forty, the swells marching in from the southwest in ranks so long that Noor, watching from the gallery while lashing down the last of the shutters, could see six of them stacked against the horizon like a wall being built by something patient and enormous.

They had done this before, all of it. The routine held them the way the tower's stones held the lamp: bolted, counterweighted, proven. Noor worked the watch in the old rhythm — lamp trimmed and burning, clockwork wound, the great lens turning on its bath of mercury with that soft hydraulic whisper that was, in all the world, the sound most like peace. Below, Callum crouched at the winding gear with his ruined hands wrapped in leather mitts Noor had sewn for him, hauling the weights up the shaft in their slow climb, hour after hour, giving the lens its strength. And Tam — Tam should have been asleep, was ordered to sleep, lay in the bed under the eaves listening to the tower groan like a ship and learning, perhaps, the same lesson Noor had learned at his age: that a lighthouse in a storm is the loneliest kind of church, and everyone inside it is clergy.

At ten minutes past two in the morning, the light failed.

Not the lamp — the lamp burned steady, the flame a calm gold teardrop inside its glass. What failed was the rotation. Noor heard it before understanding it: a change in the sound of the mechanism, the whisper becoming a grind, becoming a stutter, becoming a silence. The beam froze. Out on the black water the great fan of light swung — swung — and stopped, pointing fixedly out to sea at nothing, at empty water, at a stretch of channel where no ship would ever pass, while the actual shipping lane — the one with the convoy strung along it like beads, riding out the weather as best they could — fell into darkness.

Noor's stomach dropped as if the floor had opened.

"The mercury bath," they said aloud, already moving, because saying it made it real and real things could be fought. "The float's fouling. Something's got into the trough." Ice, most likely — spray driven up through the ventilators freezing in the annular channel, or worse, grit, salt crust, some fragment of the tower itself shaken loose by the pounding and fallen into the mercury. The lens floated on thirteen hundredweight of the metal; if the float dragged, the clockwork hadn't the strength to turn it, and no clockwork in the empire had.

Below, Callum had already felt it through the gear — the weights climbing against nothing, the chain slack. He came halfway up the stair to meet Noor, shouting over the wind.

"What do you need?"

"A hand in the mercury. Someone has to lift the lens carriage clear of the bath while I clear the trough, and then hold it — it can't rest on the pedestal or the bearings score. It's four hundredweight, Callum. It's four hundredweight and it wants to keep turning."

"Show me."

"No—" Noor stopped. Looked at the man's hands. The left one, especially, the one with the burn scar drawn tight across the palm like a seam. Four hundredweight borne on a leather-wrapped grip, in the dark, in a storm, with skin that had already been taken to its limit once. "There's another way. We wedge it. If I wedge the carriage I can work under—"

"If you wedge it and the wedge shifts, you're under four hundredweight of bronze with your head in a mercury trough," Callum said. It wasn't argument. It was arithmetic. He was already pulling his coat back on. "Show me where to stand."

For one moment — one long, roaring, rain-hammered moment — Noor stood in the lamp room and understood with terrible clarity the shape of what was happening. Here was the man whose silence Noor had bought, whose forged passage Noor had promised, whose betrayal Noor had been quietly planning since the ferry manifest arrived — and Noor was about to place the whole weight of the light, and therefore the lives of every soul on every ship in the channel, into those scarred hands.

There was no one else. That was the whole truth of a lighthouse. There was never anyone else.

"This edge," Noor said, guiding him. "Under the lower ring, here and here. You're lifting, not holding — I'll take turns with you. When I say *down*, you let it come down slow onto the blocks, nothing sudden. And whatever happens, don't let it swing. Mercury will take your glove, your sleeve, your arm — it finds skin like water finds a leak."

Callum set his grip. Noor watched him breathe once, deliberately, the way men breathed before bayonet work — Noor had read enough dispatches to know — and then together they took the strain.

Four hundredweight does not feel like a number. It feels like arguing with the earth. The carriage came up off the mercury with a sucking, silvery reluctance, and for a moment the whole great lens hung suspended on two human bodies while the storm leaned on the tower and the tower answered with a deep structural moan that Noor felt in their teeth. Callum made a sound — not a cry, a compression, air forced through clenched teeth — and held.

Noor dropped to the trough. The mercury lay dull and beautiful and treacherous in the lamplight, and there it was: a ridge of frozen spray-crust, ice laced with salt, welded to the float guide like a scar of its own. Noor chipped at it with the brass scraper, working fast, working carefully, mercury welling silver around the blade, the cold of it seeping through every seam of their gloves. Two minutes. Three. Above them Callum's arms shook; Noor could hear the tremor in the structure itself, a fine vibration like a plucked wire.

"Hold," Noor said. "Hold. Thirty seconds more."

"I've got it," Callum said, and his voice was perfectly level, which frightened Noor more than screaming would have.

The last of the crust came free. Noor swept the trough clean, checked the float's clearance with two fingers, and looked up. "Down. Slow. Slow—"

The carriage settled onto the mercury with a sigh, found its buoyancy, and the lens — released — began to turn. Whispered. Swung its great fan of light out across the channel, across the convoy lights strung out there like a rosary, and swept, and swept, and swept.

Noor put both hands flat on the cold iron of the pedestal and stayed there, forehead nearly touching the metal, breathing.

* * *

It was near dawn, in the long grey easing at the edge of the gale's fury, that Noor found Callum sitting on the stair landing below the lamp room with his hands cradled against his chest, and understood from the stillness of him that the cost had come due.

"Let me see."

"It's nothing."

"Callum."

He unfolded the hands slowly, like a man showing cards he'd rather have kept. The left palm had split along the old burn scar, a clean red seam, and the right wrist was swelling already inside its wrap, the leather mitt dark with blood where the strain had driven the seams into the flesh. He had held four hundredweight on those hands for three minutes and more, and the hands had paid the invoice.

Noor knelt on the step below him with the kit and worked in silence — antiseptic, the good ointment, fresh linen, the splint for the wrist improvised from a stave and strips of the same leather. Callum watched the tower wall rather than the work, jaw set, and only when Noor tied the last knot did he speak.

"You'd have wedged it," he said. "You were going to wedge it and get under it yourself."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Because you are a deserter with forged papers and I have been planning, these three weeks, to spend your silence like currency. Because three claimants, two seats, and I had already decided which names went on the manifest. Because every hour of trust we build makes what I mean to do worse, and I built it anyway, because the alternative was doing the arithmetic alone.

"There was no one else," Noor said, which was true, and insufficient, and Callum received it the way men receive ration bread — without comment, knowing it was all there was.

From the doorway above, Tam's voice, sleep-ragged: "Did the light stay on?"

"It stayed on," Noor called up.

"Because of Callum?"

Noor looked at the man on the stair, his bandaged hands, the flat exhausted courage of him. "Because of all of us," Noor said — the correct answer, the communal answer, the answer that bound them tighter together, which was the problem. Every knot tied in this tower made the eventual cutting harder.

* * *

The storm blew itself hollow by the following night, and what it left behind was a coast rearranged — the beach strewn with timber, a dead whale rolled onto the shingle below the point like a message too large to read, and, washing back and forth in the shallows of the cove, a shape that Tam spotted first from the gallery and that Noor confirmed through the glass with a coldness spreading through the chest that had nothing to do with the weather.

A ship's boat. Navy-built, navy-painted, oars shipped and lashed, riding the surge against the rocks. Empty.

And on the track from the cliff path, later that morning, while the three of them stood in the doorway surveying the damage — a figure. Distant, upright, unhurried, wearing the unmistakable silhouette of an officer's greatcoat, coming toward the station with the tide-wrack blowing around his boots.

Callum saw him at the same moment. Noor felt the man stop breathing beside them.

"That's not the supply boat," Tam said.

"No," Noor said quietly, watching the figure resolve through the clearing air into a stranger with a naval cap and a notebook already open in his hand. "It isn't."
