## 15. Two Berths, Three Names

The notice had been nailed to the supply hut door for three days before the weather allowed anyone to read it, and by then the paper had gone soft as skin left too long in water. Noor brought it back to the station folded inside a coat, as if it were contraband, though half the coast had seen it and spoken of nothing else.

*By order of the Ministry of Shipping: one final civilian ferry will depart Harrow Point on the eve of Armistice for southern ports. Berths are limited to twelve. All passengers must present documented identity at the gangway. No exceptions.*

Twelve berths. A whole coastline of people who wanted out — fishermen ruined by the blockades, widows with no one left inland, families who had watched four years of their sons go down the road and not come back — and twelve berths, each one requiring a name that could be checked against a ledger somewhere in a warm office in London.

Noor read it six times by lamplight while Tam slept and Callum sat sharpening a knife he no longer needed to sharpen, the whetstone circling in a rhythm like breathing.

"Documented," Callum said at last, without looking up. "That's the word that matters, isn't it."

"It's the word that ends it."

* * *

Noor had spent twenty years becoming good at paper. It was a strange skill for a lighthouse keeper, but loneliness teaches trades of its own, and over two decades Noor had learned to forge a hand the way other men learned fishing — patiently, season after season, until the craft sat in the bones. There were old forms in the station's cabinet, requisition sheets and landing declarations from the years when Gullwrack had still been visited by authority, and Noor knew the way clerks wrote, the small slouches and flourishes, the way an official pen grew tired toward the bottom of a page.

For three nights after reading the notice, while Callum slept on the settle and Tam dreamed whatever nine-year-olds dream after everything this boy had dreamed, Noor sat at the table with a stub of pencil and made a person out of nothing.

The first was easy, because the boy already existed; he only needed a different shape. Tam Zhou, born 1908, son of a coastal trader lost to the war. Noor gave him the borrowed surname like handing him a coat against the cold — *wear this, it will keep you alive, don't ask where I got it.* The handwriting came steady. The lie came easier than any truth Noor had written in years.

The second took longer. Callum Frazer could not travel under his own name; his name was a rope with a knot already tied in it. But Callum had been a clerk before the war — he'd said so once, offhand, in the way men say things they mean you never to ask about again — and a clerk knows how documents breathe. Noor drafted him as Thomas Frazer, merchant seaman, discharged medically, papers lost in the sinking of a coaster whose name Noor copied from a year-old shipping notice. Dead ships make excellent witnesses. They contradict no one.

And then there was the third line. *Passenger: N. Zhou, keeper, Gullwrack Point.*

Noor looked at it for a long time. The lamp turned overhead in its slow silver wheel, and outside the wind worried at the gallery rail like an animal testing a fence.

Here was the arithmetic, plain as tide-tables. Twelve berths. Three names needing passage. And only enough forged paper — because forgery was a thing done in ones, carefully, each document a separate risk of discovery — for two. A third set would double the pattern, and patterns were what inspectors saw. Hale had come ashore once already; Hale would come again. Two clean documents might pass as misfiled bureaucracy. Three, traveling together, would be a conspiracy wearing its own confession.

But that wasn't the true reason, and Noor knew it even as the practical reasons queued up obediently, hat in hand. The true reason was older than the war and smaller than a coin: twenty years ago, Noor Underhill had walked away from a family and let another man pay the price of the family's shame, and every day since had been purchased with that coin. A life bought invisibly could be spent invisibly. Let the boy go south with a name that would hold. Let the deserter go, who'd earned a berth twice over — once by surviving what no man should survive, and once by holding the light with his ruined hands through the worst night of the year. And let the keeper stay at Gullwrack, where a keeper belonged, keeping a light for people who would never know whose light it was.

It was not martyrdom. Noor told himself that firmly, the way one tells oneself the fog signal is working. It was bookkeeping. An old debt, finally settled in full, with interest.

The pencil hovered over the third line.

Then Noor struck it through. One stroke, clean, decisive — the kind of stroke that looks calm and costs everything.

* * *

In the morning Tam found the papers. That was the danger of living in a room thirty feet square with a curious child; everything hidden was merely waiting. He came to the table while Noor trimmed wicks, and stood holding the two documents the way children hold things they suspect are fragile — by the edges, breath held.

"Who's Tam Zhou?" he asked.

"You are, if we get our wish."

He frowned at the paper, lips moving over the words. "Is that your real name? Zhou?"

"It's the name on my lamp certificate. It's real enough for the government." Noor said it lightly, and hated how easily the lightness came, twenty years of practice making lying a kind of fluency. "Put those down before you wrinkle them. Wrinkles are questions, and questions get answered wrong."

Tam set them down but didn't step back. Nine years old and he already had his mother's stillness — Wren's stillness, though the boy couldn't have known that was what Noor was seeing, and Noor could not have said it aloud if the tower were burning. "You're not on one," Tam said. Not accusing. Just seeing, the way children see, straight through the middle of things.

"There are two berths and the ferry wants them used well."

"But there were three names." His finger touched the crossed-out line without quite touching the ink, the way you test whether a stove is hot. "I can read, mostly. You crossed yourself out."

"The wind got under my hand."

"That's not what happened," Tam said, with a gentleness that was worse than accusation, and went out to the rock pool below the station to look for whatever the storm had left, leaving Noor alone with the truth sitting on the table between two lies.

* * *

Callum said nothing about the papers for two days. That was his way; he carried silences the way soldiers carry packs, out of long habit, checking their weight. But Noor noticed him looking at the notice nailed inside the door now, reading it again each morning as if the numbers might have changed overnight — as if mercy might be a tide that came in higher.

On the third evening, coming up from the oil store with a can in each hand, Noor found Callum at the table with the documents spread before him and an expression Noor recognized from mirrors: the look of a man doing sums he does not want to finish.

"They're good," Callum said. "Better than good. The discharge stamp — where did you learn—"

"I had time," Noor said. "Twenty years of it."

"And you're not on either one." He turned the pages flat, aligning their edges with unnecessary precision, a man arranging evidence. "I've been thinking about that."

"Don't."

"I'm going to anyway." He looked up then, and his hands were flat on the table, and Noor saw that they were still bandaged, the burns from the mechanism failure healing slow and ugly, and remembered — deliberately, uselessly, the memory arriving unbidden — the sound of him climbing the tower stairs with his palms split open because the light had to turn or ships would die. "You pulled me out of the surf in November. I hadn't eaten in four days and I was ready to be shot, honestly, I'd stopped minding. And you fed me and lied for me to the inspector and never asked why a man walks out of a war. So don't tell me not to think. I've thought."

"Callum—"

"There's ink in the store cupboard. There's always ink; keepers hoard it like misers. And I told you I was a clerk." He began rolling his sleeve, matter-of-fact, a man preparing for work. "I can copy your hand. I've watched it for weeks. It's a good hand — regular, patient, no vanity in it. I'll do a third set tonight. N. Zhou, keeper, Gullwrack Point, documented identity, verified by—" he almost smiled, "—by T. Frazer, merchant seaman. We'll witness each other. That's how it's properly done, isn't it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the pattern—"

"The pattern is mine to judge now, since I'm apparently the forger." He said it steadily, without heat, which was somehow more immovable than shouting. "Three sets, all witnessed, all consistent. It'll pass. What won't pass is me walking up that gangway knowing the reason there's room for me is that you struck your own name out like a man crossing off a chore." He paused. "I did four years in France. I watched officers decide which men went over the top and which stayed, and every one of them had a reason that sounded exactly like yours just did."

"This isn't the trenches."

"No," Callum said. "It's worse. In the trenches at least everyone admitted they were afraid."

* * *

They argued until the lamp required tending, which ended arguments in this house the way bells end lessons. Noor climbed the stair with the oil can and the argument climbed along inside, refusing to be left below.

Because here was the thing Noor could not say: if Callum made a third set of papers, then the sacrifice was refused, and a refused sacrifice meant Noor had to find another way to pay — and there was no other way, there was only the ferry and the arithmetic and the old debt, and a life that had been built, plank by plank over twenty years, on the principle that Ansel Underhill's debts were not transferable. To accept the berth would be to accept that the coin could be spent again. To refuse it was the only honest act left in a life that had begun with a dishonesty so large it had swallowed a family whole.

But Callum had his own arithmetic, and Noor understood it too well. A man who has been saved once cannot bear to watch his savior drown on dry land. Gratitude is its own debt, and deserters know debts better than anyone.

Two people, each trying to give away the last seat, like two drowning men fighting over who gets the plank.

* * *

Noor finished the lamp rounds and came down near midnight intending to take the papers and lock them in the oil store, and stopped dead at the foot of the stair.

The table was cleared. The two documents were gone from their place beneath the slate. In their stead lay a single sheet of paper, weighted with the brass lamp-key, and on it, in a careful imitation of a clerk's round official hand — a hand Noor had last seen filling out requisition forms and casualty lists, a hand that had written down the fates of hundreds of men — was a note:

*Gone to check the boat shed before the weather turns. Don't burn anything while I'm out. — C.*

And beneath the weight of the key, half-hidden, a third document. Fresh ink. *N. Zhou, keeper, Gullwrack Point.* Witnessed in a second hand: *T. Frazer.*

Noor stood holding it while the lamp wheeled overhead and the wind came up from the southwest with the smell of a storm deciding, and understood, with a feeling that was equal parts fury and something dangerously close to tenderness, that the man had made the third set days ago — perhaps the very first night — and had simply waited, watching, to see what Noor would do. Testing the water the way Tam tested stove-tops. Everyone in this house was a forger of one kind or another. Everyone was hiding something, and what they were hiding, all three of them, underneath the names and the burned letters and the crossed-out lines, was the same unbearable thing: that they had come to love each other, and love makes a poor liar because it keeps volunteering for sacrifice.

Three sets of papers. Two berths. And dawn, when Noor unfolded the second page of the third document and read the disposition list in Callum's clerk-hand, brought the arithmetic to its final cruelty:

*Berth allocation: T. Frazer — confirmed. Passenger manifest amended per keeper's instruction.*

The keeper had allocated both remaining berths. One to the boy. One to the man.

And pinned beneath it, in Callum's own blunt soldier's writing, a single line that had not been there the night before:

*I've sent word ahead to Harrow Point asking after the ferry's crew list. If they're short a hand for the crossing — and after four years of war, they will be — a strong pair of arms works passage as well as a name does. For you. Not for me. Argue with the sea, not with me. — C.*

Noor read it three times. Outside, far down the dark water, the first thunder of the gathering storm rolled in low and long, like a drum being carried closer, and above it — faint, impossible, or perhaps only the wind finding a gap in the tower and playing it like a reed — Noor could have sworn the light itself seemed to pause, one full turn of the lens, as if the house were holding its breath to hear what its keeper would decide.

Because there it was, laid open on the table: a way for everyone. A way built on a maybe — a crew vacancy, a harbor-master's whim, a hundred miles of winter sea between here and Harrow Point and an inspector named Hale who had already shown he could smell a false paper through a closed door.

One letter, already sent. One answer, not yet come. And somewhere out in that rising black water, the reply was traveling toward Gullwrack Point faster than the storm was.
