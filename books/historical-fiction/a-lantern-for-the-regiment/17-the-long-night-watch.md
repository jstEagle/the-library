## 17. The Long Night Watch

The lamp needed winding at midnight, and Noor was glad of it.

Glad of any task that asked the hands to work while the mind stood elsewhere, arms crossed, waiting. The winding key sat in its brass cradle below the lens like a patient animal, and Noor took the crank in both palms and turned it, feeling the weight descend through the clockwork, weight becoming light, becoming the slow rotation that would throw the beam out across the black water in its long, deliberate sweep. Four seconds dark. Four seconds lit. Gullwrack's signature, written in fire on the fog.

*Four seconds dark,* Noor thought, turning the key. *Four seconds lit.* A life could be measured the same way. The years of hiding were the darkness; the small hours with the lamp were what passed for illumination; and between them, always, the question of which state a person truly lived in.

Outside, the wind had settled into something worse than a gale — a steady, muscular pressure against the tower, as though the sea had put its shoulder to the stone and intended to stay there until something gave. The glass trembled faintly in its frames. Spray went over the lantern room and came down again like thrown gravel. It was not the worst night Noor had kept here, but it was near enough to the worst that the old routine held no comfort, only the memory of comfort, which is a colder thing altogether.

Tomorrow — or the day after, if the weather argued — Inspector Hale's launch would come back. Or Hale himself would come up the rock path with his notebook and his patient, terrible courtesy, and ask questions that had answers, and wait for them. Noor had spent twenty years learning how weather worked, how it could be read three days off, how it lied and relented and returned. Hale was easier to read than weather. He wanted Tam. He wanted, perhaps without fully knowing it yet, Noor too. And he had offered his trade with the gentleness of a man offering tea: *the boy for your cooperation.*

Cooperation. The word sat in Noor's chest all evening like a swallowed stone.

At half past twelve, with the mechanism humming its slow orbit overhead, Noor heard the stair door open — that careful, child-sized opening, the door pulled only wide enough for a thin body, then eased shut so the latch wouldn't sound.

"I couldn't sleep," Tam said. "The wind sounds like it's saying something."

"It says nothing," Noor said. "It only has one word and it repeats it."

"What word?"

Noor considered. "*Stay,*" they said. "Or possibly *go.* It's never been clear."

Tam came up the last steps and stood in the lantern room's red glow — the watch room kept low lights at night, so the keeper's eyes stayed sharp — and looked out at the sea as if it might resolve into something legible. He was nine years old and he had been at Gullwrack since the first storm of winter, long enough now that his face had lost its drowned look and gained another expression entirely: the watching, weighing look of a child deciding something.

He had brought the blanket from his bunk, dragged behind him like a tail, and now he arranged himself on the bench beneath the chart locker with the deliberation of someone settling in for a siege.

"The man Callum," Tam said, "says you have to decide things tonight."

"Callum talks too much."

"He said it quietly. To himself. But I heard." Tam pulled the blanket to his chin. "He said *she'll decide tonight or she won't ever*, except he said *he'll* because he doesn't know."

Noor's hand paused on the log book. *He doesn't know.* No, Callum didn't know — not what Noor had been before Gullwrack, not the name that lay under this name like a body under snow. Callum knew only that Noor had papers that could be made better and a silence that could be bought, and had offered his bargain accordingly. The boy knew less. The boy knew nothing at all, and had asked, once, weeks ago — *whose boy am I?* — and had been given a bowl of soup and a deflection instead of an answer, and had eaten the soup and accepted the deflection with a patience that shamed every adult in the house.

Tonight the deflections were finished. Noor could feel it, the way one feels the barometer falling — some pressure in the walls of the self that would either be released or break them.

"Come here," Noor said. "To the window. If we're going to talk, we'll do it properly, where I can see the water. I think better with the water."

Tam came, trailing his blanket, and stood at the great sloping pane with Noor, and together they watched the beam go out and sweep and return, out and sweep and return, the sea appearing in slices — a wave here, a whitecap there, the black island of the Carrick rocks, gone, gone, gone.

* * *

Every escape Noor had ever made had happened at night. That was the thing about running; it kept banker's hours.

There had been the first escape, at nineteen — no. Not an escape. A departure, dressed as an escape so it would feel braver than it was. The house outside Ashcombe, the winter of 1897, the argument through the wall that had ended in the sound Noor still heard sometimes between waking and sleep: the sound of their father's chair going over. And after it, the silence, and after the silence, Wren's face at the door, fourteen years old, asking *what did he do, what did Aldous do?*

Aldous. The brother. The name came up in Noor like groundwater, always rising, never quite breaking the surface. Aldous who had taken money that wasn't his from men whose business it was to collect such debts with interest paid in knuckles and fire; Aldous who had run; Aldous whose crime the family would have absorbed, absorbed the way families absorb everything, if Noor had not done the unforgivable arithmetic — if Noor had not calculated, coldly, at nineteen, that the family's name would drag them down with it, and chosen instead to let the name fall on Wren and their mother while Noor walked out under a borrowed surname and did not look back.

They called it survival, those first years. They called it necessity. They called it many things, standing in hiring offices along the coast with false references and a new name spoken smoothly enough that even the speaker half believed it. *Underhill,* they'd said. *Ansel Underhill.* A name like a place to hide — literally, a hill to go under. They had chosen it themselves and been proud of the choosing, and pride, Noor thought now, watching the beam swing, is just shame wearing its good coat.

The second escape had been from a woman named Marguerite in Falmouth who had begun asking why Noor never wrote to family, and Noor had left rather than answer, leaving behind a ring that had belonged to their mother and telling themselves it was only metal.

The third escape had been the war itself — or rather the war's approach, the way it reached even the coast, taking young men off the boats, taking the supply schedules, taking the ordinary world and replacing it with one where a lighthouse keeper's solitude became suspicious rather than merely sad. Noor had survived that one by being useful. The light mattered. Nobody interrogated a lamp.

And now the fourth escape stood at the window beside them in the shape of a nine-year-old boy with his mother's chin, and there was nowhere left to walk to. The coast ended here. The map ended here. Every road out ran through Hale, or through the ferry manifest, or through the truth, and two of those roads were closed.

"Ansel?"

The name went through Noor like a fishhook. Not *Mr. Underhill*, which Tam had been carefully taught to say, not *keeper*. The boy had heard the name somewhere — from Callum's muttering, from the inspector's questions, from the note itself, *forget this face*, written in a hand Noor knew better than their own.

"Where did you hear that?" Noor asked quietly.

"Mama said it. Before the water. She said, *if you get to the point, find Ansel. Ansel will keep you.*" Tam turned and looked up, and in the low red light his eyes were very steady for a child's. "Is that you? Are you Ansel?"

The wind threw itself against the tower. Below them the sea said its one word.

"Yes," Noor said.

Just that. One syllable, twenty years in the delivery. And having said it, Noor found the rest came not easily but inevitably, the way water comes through a cracked wall once the crack is opened — finding every channel, widening every seam.

"My name was never Underhill," Noor said. "I took it when I was younger than you'd believe. Before that I was Ansel Zhou — no, listen, don't interrupt, because I'm only going to tell this once and then it goes in the log and becomes true. I had a mother who sang while she cooked. I had a sister named Wren who could whistle like a thrush and beat me at cards and never let me forget it. And I had a brother named Aldous, who did a bad thing, and I was afraid, and instead of standing with my family when the fear arrived, I changed my name and walked away and let them carry what I couldn't."

Tam's brow had furrowed. "What bad thing?"

"He stole. From dangerous men. And then he ran, the way I ran, except he ran first and I ran from him." Noor's voice stayed level, keeper's voice, the voice that could announce a hurricane as *deteriorating conditions.* "Your mama carried the weight of it. Her whole life, more or less. And then her husband borrowed from the same kind of men her brother stole from, because the world is a wheel that turns the same circle forever, and when they came to the door, she did the bravest thing anyone in our family has ever done. She didn't hide herself. She hid *you.*"

"On the boat," Tam said. "She wrapped me in the tarpaulin. She said it was a game."

"She said a lot of things, I expect, to make the fear into a game." Noor crouched down to the boy's level, knees cracking — forty-six years old now, the body keeping its own honest ledger. "She wrote me a note. You've seen me looking at it."

"*Forget this face.*"

"That's the one. And I want you to understand what it meant, because I got it wrong for a long time. I thought it meant *don't keep him, don't know him, don't let him be found.* But that's not what Wren meant. Wren means the opposite of what she writes. She always did. She meant: *this face is worth remembering so hard that you must give up forgetting yourself.* She sent him to the one person who owed the family everything." Noor straightened. "Me."

The beam swept. The sea appeared and vanished. Tam chewed his lower lip, processing, and then asked the question Noor had been dreading since the note first appeared on the doorstep, sodden and folded, with the boy beside it breathing seawater.

"So are you my uncle? Or my—" he counted on fingers that were still chapped from the storms— "cousin? Mama's brother is my uncle. Is that right?"

"Your uncle," Noor said. The word fit strangely, like a boot worn on the wrong foot for twenty years and discovered, tonight, to fit perfectly. "Wren is my sister. That makes me your uncle. It makes me the uncle who wasn't there for any of it — not for your birth, not for your christening, not for any of the nine years you've been alive. I can't give you that time back. I can't even explain it properly, because the explanation is mostly cowardice, and cowardice explained is still cowardice."

Tam considered this with terrible seriousness. Then: "Callum ran away from the war. Is that cowardice?"

"No," Noor said, surprised. "That's a different thing entirely."

"How do you know?"

Because I've watched him hold a lamp mechanism together with bleeding hands, thought Noor. Because he flinches at doors but not at work. Because running from a thing that wants to kill you isn't the same as running from a thing that loves you. But what Noor said aloud was simpler, and truer:

"Because he stayed when it cost him. There's the test. Running is easy. Staying is the part that tells you who you are." Noor looked out at the black water. "I ran. Your mother stayed. And tomorrow I have to choose whether to stay or run one more time, and this time I already know the answer, and it terrifies me."

"Is it because of the man with the notebook?"

So the boy had heard that too. Of course he had; children hear everything, filter nothing, retain all. "Partly."

"If you give me to him," Tam said, in the flat practical tone children use for arithmetic, "you'll get to leave on the ferry. And if you don't, you stay here. With me." He frowned. "But then who keeps the light?"

The question landed harder than any the inspector could have asked. *Who keeps the light.* As if the light were the fixed point and the people merely its temporary custodians — which was, Noor realized, exactly the creed they had built this whole exile upon. Keep the light, keep the schedule, keep the log, and history will happen elsewhere. Twenty years of four seconds dark, four seconds lit, believing that the rotation of a lens was a substitute for a life.

"I don't know," Noor said honestly. "Maybe nobody. Maybe the Board sends someone. Maybe it goes dark." The words hurt coming out, and Noor let them hurt, because tonight was for honesty. "But I'll tell you what I told myself when I found you on that step, before I lied to myself about handing you over to the authorities. I said: *not this one. This one stays.* And I've broken every promise I ever made to myself, but I haven't broken that one, and I'm not going to start tonight."

Tam looked at them for a long moment. Then he did the most disarming thing possible: he leaned his head against Noor's side, blanket and all, and sighed like an old man at the end of a fair day's labor.

"Good," he said. "Because I already decided. Mama said find Ansel. I found you. I'm not going anywhere else."

* * *

They kept the watch together after that, the uncle and the nephew, neither quite saying the words aloud again but both of them knowing they had been said and would stand. Noor showed him how to read the barometer's fall, how to tell spray-ice from frost on the gallery rail, how to log the weather in the terse official language that concealed, like all official languages, an entire human heart. At two o'clock Noor let him wind the mechanism, guiding the small hands on the crank, and the boy's face in the lamplight as the weight descended — solemn, concentrated, proud — was Wren's face at fourteen, unmistakably, unbearably, and Noor had to turn to the window until it passed.

At three, Callum came up with tea, moving quietly, hands bandaged, and stopped in the doorway when he saw the two of them at the chart table, the boy's head nodding against Noor's shoulder. He read the scene in a glance, the way he read weather, and something eased in his face.

"So it's decided," he said softly.

"It's decided."

"And the ferry?"

"Two berths," Noor said. "Yours and his. Papers in the morning."

Callum stood with the teapot steaming in his bandaged hands and did not argue, which was itself an argument, and finally said only: "You're a fool, Underhill. Whatever else you are." And then, setting down the pot: "But I've known worse fools. Better ones too, come to that. Just none I'd trust with a lamp."

He went back down. The tea went cold in its cups. Outside, toward dawn, the storm began at last to spend itself, the wind dropping by degrees the way a fever breaks, and the sea's single word softened into something almost like speech, almost like forgiveness.

At five minutes past six, with the eastern sky going the color of pewter and then the color of a wound and then the color of morning, Noor opened the log book to the current page, took up the pen, and entered the night's record in the official hand: *Wind moderating. Lamp serviceable. Watch kept.*

Then, beneath it, in smaller letters, in handwriting that shook only a little:

*Also kept: a promise.*

Noor set down the pen and looked east, where the light was coming up over the water, and thought: *Let him come, then. Let Hale bring his notebook and his trade and his terrible courtesy. Let the ferry load at dawn whenever dawn comes. The boy stays, and I stay, and whatever the cost of staying is, I will pay it in full and sign for it.*

Far out on the gray water, small with distance, a shape was standing in toward the point — a launch, flying naval colors, making heavy weather of the leftover swell.

Hale was coming ashore, and he was early.
