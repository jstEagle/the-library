## 20. After the Bells

The ferry had no name that Callum Frazer could see, or if it had one it was painted over with grey, the way everything was painted over with grey now — ships and lorries and men's records and the whole four years of it, a coat of dull paint slapped over the wound of Europe. He stood at the rail with his hands in his coat pockets because his hands still hurt to look at, even bandaged as they were, even wrapped by Noor Underhill's own careful fingers in the last hour before the gangway came down.

Behind him, the boy slept.

Tam lay curled on a bench beneath the wheelhouse awning, wedged into the corner where the wind couldn't reach him, wearing Callum's spare jersey rolled twice at the cuffs and Noor's old scarf wound up past his chin. His face, in sleep, had gone soft in a way it never was awake. Awake, the boy watched everything the way gulls watch a gutting table — patient, unblinking, certain the world would eventually drop something.

The bells were still ringing. They'd been ringing since dawn, since the wireless room aboard had crackled out the news in a clerk's flat voice — *hostilities ceased, eleven o'clock, all firing will cease* — and then the whole deck had gone mad, men weeping openly, men singing songs with words too filthy for a child's ears, one old woman dancing alone with her arms around nothing, turning and turning like a leaf in wind. The bells of the mainland town across the water had caught the sound and thrown it back, church after church after church, until the air itself seemed to be made of ringing.

Callum did not feel what they felt. He kept waiting for it to arrive, the joy, and it would not come. What came instead was the shore, shrinking behind them, and on it a figure standing at the end of the pier that he could not stop looking at even now, even when the pier was only a pencil-line, even when it was only a smudge where the sea met the sky.

Noor Underhill. Standing straight as the lighthouse itself. Giving them away with both hands and keeping nothing back.

*You'll be the death of me,* Callum thought at the smudge, *and I don't mean it unkindly.*

He had spent four years learning how quickly a person could become a smudge. He had walked out of a dressing station near Ypres with another man's greatcoat over his shoulders and another man's name in his pocket, and he had learned that identity was not a thing you carried but a thing other people agreed to see, and that agreements could dissolve overnight. He had been Corporal Callum Frazer, 2nd Battalion, and then he had been nobody, walking south through fields that smelled of mud and mustard gas, and every mile the name had weighed less until he could barely remember what it was for.

And then he had come stumbling up a cliff path in a storm to a lighthouse where a keeper with borrowed eyes had fed him soup and asked no questions, and a boy had looked at him from behind a door and seen exactly what he was — deserter, coward, whatever word you liked — and said nothing about it, because the boy knew about hiding too.

Nobody here had agreed to see anybody. That was the strange mercy of Gullwrack Point: three people who had each buried a name, living together in a tower, pretending the pretence was invisible.

* * *

"You're staring at the water."

Tam stood beside him without seeming to have crossed the deck. The boy moved quietly; he always had. Callum suspected he'd learned it from whoever had taught him to run.

"I'm staring at the land," Callum said. "It's different."

"It's smaller." Tam put his elbows on the rail, which was too high for him, so that he stood on his toes inside his boots. "Everything looks smaller going away from it. Wren told me that once. She said grief is just distance you can't walk off."

Callum looked down at him. Nine years old and quoting his mother like scripture. "She sounds like she'd have done well in the trenches," he said, and immediately regretted it, but Tam only nodded seriously, as though this confirmed something he'd long believed about adults.

"Is he going to be punished?"

There it was. The question had been sitting between them since the gangway, unasked, growing heavier with every fathom of water. Callum had heard it coming the way you hear a shell coming — a whine in the air before anything lands.

"Punished for what?" he said, buying time.

"For lying. About his name. About me. The inspector man knew. You saw his face. He knew, and Mr. Underhill — is that even his name? — he just *told* him. Out loud. In front of everyone." Tam's voice climbed and he dragged it back down with visible effort, glancing along the deck at the celebrating passengers, none of whom were listening, all of whom were drunk on bells. "Why would anyone do that? Why would anyone say it out loud?"

Callum turned back to the sea. The smudge of the coast had gone. Just water now, grey-green, moving in long slow swells that lifted the bow and set it down like a hand rocking a cradle.

"Because some lies get so heavy," he said, "that carrying them costs more than putting them down. Even when everyone's watching when you do it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Callum agreed. "It's not."

* * *

They went below when the rain found them — a thin cold drizzle out of the north-east, more mist than water, the sort of weather that had followed Callum through France like a debt-collector. The cabin was low-ceilinged and smelled of coal smoke and wet wool, and it was crowded with families who had waited months for this crossing and would not sleep for talking. Tam wedged himself against the bulkhead and pulled the scarf up over his nose, and Callum sat beside him, and for a while they simply listened to the engine turning over beneath them like some vast patient animal.

The papers were inside Callum's jacket, against his chest. Two sets. Forged in a lighthouse kitchen over three nights by lamplight, by a man whose hands shook from old fear and then, later, from burns — forged anyway, carefully, beautifully, because it turned out Callum Frazer had a talent he'd never had occasion to use: he had worked six weeks in a record office in Rouen before the war took him properly, stamping and signing and dating other men's lives, and his hand remembered every trick. The ink was right. The stamps were convincing enough for a civilian manifest checked by a clerk who wanted his own supper more than he wanted the truth.

One set read *Thomas Frazer, aged nine, son of the bearer.* One set read *Callum Frazer, discharged, papers verified.*

Both names his. Both berths his, if he'd wanted them to be. And Noor Underhill had known it — had handed over the papers at the dock with a look that said *I know what I'm giving you, and I'm giving it freely* — and Callum had stood there holding two lives in an envelope while the man who made them stayed on the pier to face Inspector Hale alone.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Tam asked suddenly. "That night, in the kitchen. When I gave you the piece about the boat. My mother's boat. You already knew I was testing you, didn't you. You knew and you didn't say."

Callum considered lying. It was a habit of long standing, and comfortable, like an old boot. But the bells were still faintly audible even down here, carried through the hull in vibrations rather than sound, and it seemed wrong to lie on a day like this, of all days.

"I knew," he said. "I've known since the second week. You leave your boots pointing toward the door. Always. People who plan to run keep their boots pointed at the door. And you counted the tins in the cupboard when you thought nobody was watching. Twice a week. Same shelf." He paused. "I used to count the exits in every building I was in. Every barn, every church, every estaminet with a pretty girl pouring beer. Four years. Your body does what the war teaches it, whether you want it to or not. Yours learned early."

Tam absorbed this. Down the cabin someone began singing, softly, an older song than the filthy ones on deck — a harvest song, Callum thought, or a christening song, something with roots in it.

"What happens to him?" Tam asked again, quieter now. "Will he be punished, or forgotten? Those are the only two things that ever happen to people, Wren says. Punished or forgotten. She said it about my da."

Callum looked at the boy — at the sharp chin under the scarf, at the eyes that were unmistakably, unforgivably familiar. He'd seen that face before, years ago, in another life. A face at a dinner table inland, in a house full of shouting and warmth, a younger sibling sitting silent among louder people, watching him the way this boy watched everything. He had never told Noor that he recognized the family resemblance. Some knowledge is a kindness to withhold.

"He won't be forgotten," Callum said. "Not by me."

"But punished?"

"That depends," Callum said slowly, "on whether anyone speaks."

* * *

The pardon notice was pinned to the bulkhead beside the purser's office, a single sheet of typescript behind cracked glass, and Callum stood in front of it for a long time while Tam waited impatiently at his elbow.

*By order of the Admiralty, under the terms of the Armistice and subsequent instruments:* — the language went on in its bureaucratic way — *all desertions occurring prior to the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of November are hereby remitted. Persons affected shall present themselves at any naval office within ninety days for discharge of record. No prosecution shall follow.*

Ninety days. Ninety days to walk into a naval office and say *here I am, I ran,* and have a clerk stamp it dead. Four years of flinching at uniformed backs, of sleeping in ditches with one eye open, of hearing boots behind him on every lonely road — and it ended with a form and a stamp and a clerk who wouldn't even look up.

"They can't touch him either, then," Tam said, reading over his shoulder with the fierce speed of a child who'd taught himself letters young. "Mr. Underhill. There wasn't any war crime. He just lived under a wrong name. That's not deserting."

"It's false particulars," Callum said. "It's fraud against the Board of Trade, technically. Lightkeeping certificates, wages drawn under a name that isn't yours." He touched the glass over the notice. "But there's a difference between him and me, lad, and it's this: nobody's hunting him. Hale suspects. Suspecting isn't knowing, and knowing isn't proving, and right now Noor Underhill is standing on that pier giving Hale everything, voluntarily, which means whatever happens next happens in a room with a lamp and a statement and a signature — not in a cell."

"Then testify," Tam said.

Callum looked down at him.

"You know things," the boy said, with that terrible directness children have, the directness that sees the shape of a thing before it knows its name. "You know why he took the other name. You know about the brother. I don't know all of it yet — he'll tell me when he tells me, he promised — but you were there, weren't you? Before. Inland. You knew our family." Tam's voice dropped. "You knew *me.*"

"I knew a baby," Callum said. "Babies grow."

"Then testify for the grown-up version." The boy's jaw set, and it was such an adult gesture on such a small face that Callum had to look away, out the porthole, at the empty grey water. "If they try to punish him. If they put him somewhere. You go and stand in the room and say what you know. That's what Wren would do. That's what she did for my da, even though he didn't deserve it. She stood up in a courtroom full of people who hated us and she told the truth because somebody had to and nobody else would."

The engine turned. The rain ticked against the porthole glass. Somewhere above them the bells had finally stopped, and the silence they left behind was enormous.

"I spent four years," Callum said, "learning that the safest thing a man can do is be no witness to anything. See nothing. Sign nothing. Stand nowhere." He flexed his bandaged hands inside his pockets, feeling the burn-scars pull. "A man saved my light and gave me his berth and never once asked me the question that would've got me shot, and he did it all while being the biggest liar on that coast. So." He exhaled. "Yes. All right. If it comes to a room with a lamp and a statement — I'll stand in it. I'll sign my true name on paper for the first time since 1914 and let them do what they like with it. Pardoned men make poor prisoners and worse cowards."

Tam studied him for a long moment, the way he studied everything, cataloguing, deciding. Then he held out his hand — a small hand, chapped from salt wind, absurdly formal.

"It's a bargain, then," the boy said.

Callum took it. "It's a bargain."

* * *

Evening came down over the water like a lid. The ferry ran its darkening course north-east toward the port where they'd disembark, where there'd be a naval office, a clerk, a stamp, a bed in some requisitioned boarding-house — the first bed Callum had legitimately occupied in four years. Tam fell asleep again against his side somewhere past dusk, mid-sentence, mid-question, his head finding the hollow of Callum's shoulder as though it had always belonged there.

Callum sat very still so as not to wake him, and watched the porthole go from grey to black, and let himself think about the man on the pier.

He kept seeing it. Not the dramatic parts — the confession, the crowd, the inspector's face going careful and hungry at once. Those he'd expected. What he kept seeing was smaller: Noor crouching at the gangway to fix Tam's scarf one last time, fingers quick and precise, the same fingers that trimmed wicks and wound clockwork and had bandaged Callum's ruined hands without a single wasted motion. The fingers of a man who had spent twenty years maintaining a light for ships he never saw again. Who had kept faith, all that time, with strangers.

*I'll send word,* Noor had said at the dock, quiet under the bells. *However it goes. There's a fisherman runs out of Harrow Point Tuesdays. Ask for Maren.*

And Callum had said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a promise, and promises were the one currency he'd refused to carry through the war. But now, with the boy asleep against him and the pardon notice stamped into his memory like a brand, he found the promise forming itself anyway, unbidden, solid as ballast.

Tuesday. Maren. Word sent. And if word came back that Noor Underhill — whatever his true name was, and Callum had a guess, an old half-remembered syllable from a dinner table twenty years gone — if word came back that he'd been taken anywhere, charged with anything, then Callum Frazer would walk into whatever room they'd put him in and stand up in it, and he would not sit down until the truth had finished with him.

The boy stirred. "Are we nearly there?" he mumbled, not waking properly.

"Miles yet," Callum said. "Sleep."

"Tell me about him. When he was young." Tam's eyes stayed closed. "Before the light. Tell me one thing that's true."

Callum looked out at the black water, at the faint line of the coast where somewhere behind the dark a lighthouse was burning that no longer needed keeping, and he chose — deliberately, for the first time in four years — a truth instead of a silence.

"He had a sister," Callum said softly, "who worshipped him. And he broke her heart so thoroughly that she had to find him again by leaving her son on his doorstep in a storm. Whatever else happens Tuesday — whatever the clerks decide — that's the story, lad. Everything else is weather."

Above them, unseen, the ferry's running lights burned steady through the dark, and ahead, hours south, a cell or a desk or a lamp-lit logbook was waiting for the man who had given them both away — and Callum meant to find out which.
