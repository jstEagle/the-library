## 1. The Weight of Letters

The lamp had to be turned low before the shutters opened. That was the first rule of the foundry after dark — not written anywhere, never spoken aloud, but understood the way a body understands a bruise. Turn the lamp low, angle the shade toward the wall, and let the shutters stand open only as wide as a hand's breadth, so that anyone watching from the square below would see nothing but the ordinary darkness of a house where no one was awake.

Bram Winterbourne adjusted the shade now, counting the seconds it took her eyes to readjust. Four hundred pounds of type stood in cases against the far wall, sorted and boxed in her father's own order, and she could have found any letter among them blind. She had been doing this work since she was nine years old, back when she was still called by her true name and allowed to say it.

She did not think of that name at night. Thinking of it at night was dangerous. A name was a small thing, but names were what got people taken from their beds in Vellmarch lately, and so she kept hers folded away like cloth in a chest, taken out only when necessary, aired rarely, and never in company.

Outside, the army was drilling again.

She heard them before she saw anything — the slap of boots on cobbles in unison, the barked count of an officer whose voice carried up through the shutter gap with the clarity of a man who enjoyed being heard. Third night this week. They were drilling in the market square as though the square belonged to them, which, since the garrison arrived two months ago, was more or less the law of the matter. Two hundred men, perhaps, moving by lamplight in formations that threw long shadows across the fountain where the fishwives used to argue about herring prices.

Bram let herself look for exactly three breaths. No longer. Looking too long was how people got caught looking.

Then she closed the shutter to its hand's width and went back to the press.

* * *

The pamphlet was titled *On the Fair Price of Grain*, which was the kind of title that had saved more than one printer's neck in this country. It sounded like economics. It was meant to sound like economics. Only a man reading past the second page would find the passage about requisitions — how the army's "fair price" for wheat came to less than a third of what the grain cost to grow, how the millers of Vellmarch had been ordered to sell at gunpoint, how two hundred families in the river districts would eat nothing but turnip greens by midwinter if the harvest went south to feed regiments that had not asked anyone's permission to be fed.

The author was a schoolmaster in Kessel who would deny writing it if asked, and everyone knew he would deny it, and that was part of the arrangement. The words traveled. The words always traveled. It was the names that had to stay home.

Bram set the last line of the final page by feel, her fingers walking the type case the way her father's had walked it, quick and certain, plucking letters and seating them in the composing stick upside down and backward, mirror-writing that only made sense when the press kissed the paper. She was good at this. That was the bitter joke of her life: she was better at this than almost anyone alive, and she could tell no one, win no commission under her own name, sign no masterwork. The stamp on every piece the foundry produced read ALDOUS WINTERBOURNE & SON, and the son was a fiction she maintained the way other people maintained their hair or their teeth — daily, carefully, without thinking about the maintenance itself.

Her father had died eleven months ago of the congestion in his chest, in the bed upstairs, with his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting politely for something. He had died as Aldous Winterbourne, respected type cutter, widower, a craftsman whose matrices were known as far as the capital. And he had died knowing — because he'd made her swear it at the end, gripping her wrist with a strength that surprised them both — that his daughter would bury him as his son.

*"They'll close the foundry,"* he had said. *"A woman alone can't hold a trade license here. You know the statute."*

She knew the statute. She had helped him copy it out once, years ago, for a pamphlet on property law that nobody had dared distribute. Under the Trade Act, a licensed workshop passed to male heirs or reverted to the guild, and the guild answered to the magistrate, and the magistrate answered, these days, to the colonel.

So Bram Winterbourne, daughter, had gone into the ground with her father, and Bram Winterbourne, son — same name, conveniently, her father having chosen a name that served both purposes long before he ever needed it to — had emerged from the funeral wearing his coat cut down at the shoulders and a new stoop he practiced in front of the hall mirror until it stopped feeling like a costume.

That had been the hardest part, the first months. Not the binding. She'd bound herself at fifteen with linen wraps and learned to sleep in them; the body adapted, bodies always adapted, that was the whole tragedy of bodies. No — the hardest part was the voice. Her father's customers had known his son as a boy of fourteen with a cracked, uncertain voice, and then the boy had gone quiet for years, sent away to study, everyone said, and returned grown. A man could change in seven years. But a voice was a signature written in air, and Bram had spent whole evenings alone in the cellar practicing lower registers until her throat ached, learning to speak from the chest instead of the throat, to let consonants fall heavy, to laugh — God, laughing was the worst — in short, dry syllables that gave nothing away.

She was good at it now. That was the second bitter joke: she had become excellent at being no one.

The press took her weight and she leaned into it, pulling the bar, and the bed came around, and the sheet lifted off the type wet and black and perfect. Forty-one copies tonight. Forty-one copies of a dead schoolmaster's careful sentences, to go out tomorrow in flour sacks and coffin linings and the false bottoms of cheese wheels, into kitchens and taprooms and the pockets of men who worked the ferry landing.

She hung each sheet from the line above the stove, where they dried without wrinkling, and while they dried she cleaned the type, because type left inked overnight pitted and grayed, and good type was getting harder to come by. Foundries across the river provinces had gone over to war contracts — shell casings, buckles, the brass fittings of rifles. Letters, her father used to say, were becoming a luxury item. *Which is why we must spend them wisely.*

She missed him most at this hour. That was the truth of it, the truth under all the other truths. In daylight there was too much performance for grief to get a word in — the foundry had orders to fill, legitimate ones, wedding invitations and shop signs and the Vellmarch almanac, and customers came to the door and needed the son, needed the stoop and the low voice and the modest, apologetic competence of a young man who was plainly not his father's equal and never would be. She performed that lesser version of herself eight hours a day. It was only at night, alone with the lamp turned low and the army stomping around in the square below, that she could be what she actually was: the best letter cutter in three provinces, working anonymously, illegally, in service of a resistance she had joined without ever being formally asked.

Oskar had recruited her, if you could call it recruiting. Oskar Venn, printer, fat and quick-eyed and perpetually smelling of wine he claimed was medicinal. He had come to the foundry a year ago wanting a job of type cut — a seal, a mark, nothing traceable — and had watched her hands for ten minutes and then said, quietly, without looking up from the galley, *"Your father taught you well. Better than well."* And she had understood that he knew, and he had understood that she knew he knew, and neither of them had ever said another word about it, because in Vellmarch that year, discretion between conspirators was worth more than courage.

Courage got you arrested. Discretion got the pamphlets printed.

* * *

The sheets were drying. The drilling below had paused — she could hear the officer's voice going down the ranks, correcting someone, the particular rhythm of a man dressing down a recruit. She let herself listen while she wiped the type with the rag and turpentine, because listening was free and because there was a strange comfort in knowing exactly where danger was. Danger you could hear was danger you could measure. It was the silent kind that frightened her.

Two months since the garrison came. Colonel Hessler — nobody had seen him yet, he kept to the converted customs house, but his name was already on everything, stamped on the requisition notices and spoken in the tone people used for weather that might turn — had arrived with four companies and a stack of pre-printed orders, as though the occupation of Vellmarch had been planned down to the paper stock. Maybe it had. The town sat on the river where the valley narrowed, and the valley road was the artery between the capital and the western provinces, and whoever held Vellmarch held the throat of the country. Everyone in town understood this. It didn't make it easier to live inside the throat.

The notices had gone up on the church doors and the customshouse wall within a week of the garrison's arrival: curfew at dusk, assembly prohibited, printing of unlicensed material punishable by imprisonment, possession of such material punishable likewise, informing encouraged, informers protected. Bram had read the notice twice, standing in the crowd with her cap pulled low, wearing the son the way she wore everything, and had felt something she couldn't at first name. Not fear exactly. Fear was a visitor; it came and went. This was more like the sensation of standing on ice and hearing it begin to talk — that faint, crystalline creak beneath the feet that told you the season was ending whether you were ready or not.

The town talked back anyway. That was the thing the colonel hadn't counted on, or had counted on wrongly. Vellmarch was a printing town. It had been a printing town for a hundred and sixty years, since the Winterbournes' great-grandfathers had cut the first matrices on this street, and a town full of people who make letters does not easily accept silence. The pamphlets moved like water finding cracks. Every seizure seemed to teach the network something new about the seizures.

But the army learned too. That was the creak in the ice.

Below, the drill resumed — the slap of boots, the count. Bram checked the sheets, found them dry enough, and began folding. Quire folds, sharp crease with the bone folder, done fast because her hands knew the motion the way her father's hands had known the graver. Fold, crease, fold, stack. The pamphlets grew into a neat brick on the bench, forty-one copies of sedition dressed as arithmetic.

It was while she was wrapping them in oilcloth that she heard the knock.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, then a pause, then two more — the pattern Oskar used, the pattern they'd agreed on a year ago and never changed, because changing a signal was sometimes more dangerous than keeping it. Bram killed the lamp entirely and stood in the dark for the length of ten heartbeats, listening. The drilling continued below, indifferent. The knock came again: three, pause, two.

She opened the door on the chain first, the way he'd taught her, even though she'd have bet the foundry that no soldier alive could pick out Oskar Venn's silhouette from a shadow's. He slipped in smelling of rain and cheap brandy, and she relit the lamp behind the shielded shade, and they looked at each other the way they always did — quickly, thoroughly, checking for damage.

"You're late," she whispered.

"I'm worth waiting for." He was wringing water out of his hat brim, and there was something wrong with his face. She'd learned to read Oskar's face the way she read type — the small defects that meant the impression wouldn't come clean. Tonight there was a defect. "We have a problem."

"We always have a problem. That's why you pay me."

He didn't smile. Oskar always smiled. He smiled when the news was bad and smiled harder when it was worse, because smiling was his armor the way the stoop was hers. Tonight he stood there with the rain running off his coat onto her father's floorboards, and he did not smile, and Bram felt the ice creak again, louder, right beneath her feet.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'm working."

"Sit down, child." He glanced at the brick of pamphlets wrapped in oilcloth, and something crossed his face that she'd never seen there before — not fear for himself. Fear for her. "The grain pamphlet can wait one hour. What I have to tell you won't."

She pulled the stool from the bench and sat, and outside the window the boots went on drumming their indifferent rhythm into the cobbles, and Oskar Venn took off his wet hat and held it in both hands like a man in church, and told her about the ledger.

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned down and the drilling had finally stopped and the town lay silent under its new masters — Bram would remember that moment as the exact instant the old life ended. Not the requisition, which came after. Not the checkpoints, or the flight, or the fire. This: a fat printer holding his hat in a darkened foundry, saying, *"There's a list. Of the disappeared. All of them, every province, with dates and places and witnesses — your father helped hide it, and I think he hid it here, and I think the army is about to tear this street apart looking for it."*

But that was later. For now, the boots marched, the lamp burned low, and the forty-one pamphlets sat wrapped in oilcloth on the bench, patient as seeds, waiting for morning.
