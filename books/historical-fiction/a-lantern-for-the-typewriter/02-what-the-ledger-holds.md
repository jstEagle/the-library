## 2. What the Ledger Holds

The pamphlets had gone out by midnight, and Bram Winterbourne stood at the sink washing ink from her hands the way she did every night — slowly, thoroughly, watching the water run black and then grey and then clear, as if clarity were something that could be rinsed into existence. Outside, the frost had come early. It feathered the edges of the foundry's one window, and through the clear patch in the center she could see the square below, where a squad of soldiers was drilling by lantern light, their boots striking cobbles in unison like a slow, patient heartbeat.

*Left. Left. Left.*

She dried her hands on her apron and tried to count how many nights she had watched them from this window. A month? Six weeks? The drills had begun small — a dozen men, an officer with a whistle — and grown each week until the square seemed less like a town's heart than a garrison's courtyard. Her father would have noticed the pattern. Aldous Winterbourne noticed everything: the way type wore at its shoulders before it cracked, the way a customer's voice changed when he was about to ask for something he couldn't afford, the way governments rehearsed their violence in public squares long before they committed it anywhere else.

Her father had been dead eleven months now.

Bram banked the forge fire, hung her apron on its nail, and climbed the stairs to the flat above, where she kept two chairs at the table because a household with only one chair invited questions. She ate bread and hard cheese standing up, which was another habit of invisibility — a man alone might eat standing; a woman eating standing invited comment, and comment invited looking, and looking was death. She had learned the grammar of it so thoroughly that she sometimes caught herself performing masculinity even when no one watched, squaring her shoulders in empty rooms, letting her voice drop into its lower register as she read aloud to check the sound of it.

*You are Bram Winterbourne,* she reminded herself, the way other people said prayers. *Son of Aldous. Heir to the foundry. You have always been him.*

The knock came at half past one — three soft raps, then two, then one, the rhythm Oskar had invented himself and changed twice since autumn, because Oskar trusted nothing that stayed still, including his own signals.

She let him in.

Oskar Venn looked worse than he had a fortnight ago. He was a compact man built like a bale of compressed paper, with ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles that they looked like bruises, and tonight his eyes had the wet shine of a man who had been reading things he wished he hadn't. He carried a satchel against his chest the way a farmer carries a lamb in cold weather.

"You printed them," he said, not quite a question, scanning the room out of habit even though he'd helped design its hiding places.

"Four hundred. Wrapped in oilcloth under the floor of the drying room, minus the sixty already distributed." She poured him the dregs of the tea she'd made hours ago and warmed it over the spirit stove rather than relight anything. "The type is cleaned and redistributed across three cases. If Duvall's men search tomorrow, they'll find nothing but a tired foundry and a son who sleeps too little."

"Good." He drank without tasting it. He was staring past her at the wall where her father's portrait used to hang — she had taken it down months ago, not out of disrespect but because a face in a house is a witness, and witnesses could be interrogated. "Bram. I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything."

She sat down across from him. In the low light, with his coat still on and snow melting off his shoulders onto the chair back, he looked suddenly old — older than his forty years, older than the town itself.

"The ledger has moved."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped weight. Every printer in the resistance knew what *the ledger* meant, though fewer than five people alive knew where it slept: the master record of Vellmarch's disappeared, four hundred and some names — no, more now, the number grew monthly — each entry dated, witnessed, annotated with the last known sighting, the arresting unit where it could be determined, the family left behind. Oskar had spent three years building it, page by page, name by name, smuggling fragments between safe houses like a man moving shards of glass in his pockets.

"Moved where?" she asked.

"That's just it." He set down the cup. His hands weren't steady. "It was at the tannery. Has been since summer. Two nights ago I sent Toma to rotate it — standard procedure, we move it every six weeks whether there's reason or not, because the only safe hiding place is one nobody has finished finding. Toma never came back."

Bram felt the room tilt slightly, the way it did when the forge ran too hot. "Arrested?"

"Or worse. There was a sweep on Tanner's Row — you saw the wagons. Whatever happened, Toma didn't reach the drop point, and the ledger wasn't there when my second runner checked at dawn." He held up a hand before she could speak. "It *wasn't taken*. The second runner found the cache undisturbed but empty — the wrapping gone, the oilcloth folded neatly, which is not what soldiers do. Soldiers tear. Someone we trusted took it somewhere new. Someone who thought they were protecting it."

"And you don't know who."

"I know who *could* have known the rotation schedule." He counted on ink-stained fingers. "Me. Toma. And one other person, who set the original schedule and every rotation since. One person who insisted on knowing where the ledger slept at all times, who wouldn't delegate it, who argued with me for a year about it because I said knowledge should be shared among cells and he said—" Oskar stopped. His jaw worked.

"He said what?"

Oskar looked at her, and Bram understood, with a sensation like stepping into a hole she hadn't known was in the floor, exactly who he meant.

"My father," she said.

"Aldous designed the ledger's security. Not me. I collected the names — God knows I collected the names, I filled pages until my hand seized up — but your father built the system around them. The caches. The rotations. The folding conventions that tell a trained reader whether a document has been moved by friend or enemy." Oskar's voice dropped. "He said a secret held by many men is a fire in a hay barn. He kept the master key of it in his head and refused to write it down, and I hated him for it right up until the day the first arrests started, and then I thanked God for him nightly."

*My father.* Bram sat very still. In her mind she walked again through the cellar below the foundry — the racks of matrices, the casting bench, the coal bin, the smell of hot metal and machine oil that was the smell of her entire childhood. She thought of her father bent over his work late into the night, so often, so quietly, and she had assumed — she had *always assumed* — it was commissions. Private orders. The fussy custom work of a perfectionist craftsman.

How much else had he done down there behind a closed door while she slept?

"I didn't know," she said. It came out smaller than she intended.

"No one knew. That was the point of him." Oskar rubbed his face. "And now here is our situation, Bram, and I'll give it to you plain because you've earned plainness: the ledger is hidden somewhere in this town by someone following your father's system, and the only living person who fully understands that system is a dead man. I can find a cache. I can't necessarily *read* one. Your father built traps into it — misdirections. If the army finds it before we do, every name in it becomes a confession signed by the victim. If we can't find it at all, then it might as well be ash, and those people die twice."

The soldiers' boots outside had stopped. In the sudden quiet, Bram could hear the frost ticking against the window glass, the small sounds of a town pretending to sleep.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why now? You've managed this alone for three years."

Oskar was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he didn't quite meet her eyes, and she would remember that later — the exact quality of his evasion, the way his thumb worried the rim of the teacup — though she would not understand it until much later, when understanding cost more than either of them could pay.

"Because Hessler's requisition orders are being drafted. I've seen the paperwork pass through the clerk's office. Within days, maybe a week, the army will seal this town and seize every press, every paper stock, every record in Vellmarch." He finally looked up. "When they do, they won't just be hunting printers, Bram. They'll be hunting *paper*. And a ledger is paper. The best-hidden paper in the world is only hidden until a hundred men turn a town inside out looking for it."

"So we find it first."

"We find it first," he agreed. "But we can't find it without him. And him—" He gestured vaguely upward, toward nothing, toward the portrait that no longer hung on any wall. "Him we buried in March."

Bram rose and went to the window. Below, the soldiers had resumed their drill, and the boots struck the cobbles again — *left, left, left* — patient as erosion. She thought of her father's hands, which she knew better than almost any sight in the world: scarred at the fingertips from hot type, precise, unhurried, always closing over whatever mattered before she could see what it was. He had taught her to cut letters. He had taught her to read the grain of metal. He had taught her everything except the shape of the thing he was actually building, and she realized now, standing in the cold dark of the flat above the foundry, that this had not been an oversight. It had been a design. The same instinct that scattered type across three cases had kept his own daughter innocent — protected, or unburdened, or simply uninformed; she genuinely did not know which, and the not-knowing hurt worse than betrayal would have.

He had trusted her with silence instead of secrets. She had accepted it without ever asking why.

"Oskar," she said slowly, "when my father died, he left me the foundry. Everything in it. He was specific about it — specific in a way that surprised me at the time. 'Nothing gets sold, nothing gets melted down, not the oldest cases, not the broken machines in the cellar.' I thought it was sentiment. He wasn't a sentimental man, but grief makes you generous with the dead." She turned from the window. "What if it wasn't sentiment?"

Oskar went very still.

"What if the system isn't lost?" she said. "What if it's sitting in my cellar, waiting for somebody who knows how to look?"

The frost ticked against the glass. Down in the square, the boots kept their time.

Oskar set down his cup and stood, and for the first time that night he looked afraid in a way that had nothing to do with soldiers.

"Bram," he said quietly, "what exactly is in your cellar?"

* * *

They went down together with a single shaded lamp, Oskar insisting on darkness protocol even now, even between friends — or whatever they were to each other; she had never settled on the word. The stairs to the cellar were stone, worn concave by a century of feet, and the cold rose up to meet them like water. Bram unlocked the door at the bottom — two locks now, since autumn — and stood aside to let Oskar enter first, because it was his war, even if the cellar was hers.

The lamp light moved across the racks of matrices, the casting bench swept clean, the great disused press under its canvas shroud like a sleeping animal. Nothing had changed. Nothing ever changed down here, and yet Bram found herself seeing all of it newly, suspiciously, the way one rereads an old letter after learning the writer lied.

"There," Oskar said softly. "Start there."

*There* was the corner beyond the coal bin, where her father had kept the failures — cracked molds, mis-struck sorts, the typewriter cases and machine housings he bought cheap from the pawnbroker and cannibalized for brass fittings. Bram had sorted that corner a dozen times. She crossed to it now and knelt, lifting pieces into the lamplight one by one: a sewing machine base, a scale with a cracked pan, a wooden case stamped with the maker's mark of the Corvane Typewriter Company, its leather scuffed white at the corners, its brass latches green with age.

She had handled this case a hundred times. She had dusted it, shifted it, nearly sold it twice during the lean months after her father's death and been stopped both times by that same inexplicable instruction — *nothing gets sold, nothing gets melted down* — obeyed without understanding, like a child keeping a rule whose reason died with the rule-giver.

Now, under Oskar's gaze, she turned it over in her lap and felt it.

Not saw it. Not heard it. *Felt* it — the faint wrongness of weight, a heaviness toward the spine of the case that a lifetime of handling metal had taught her to register before her mind could name it. Her father had taught her that too: *the hand knows before the eye, Bram. Always trust the hand.*

"Oskar," she whispered. "Bring me the lamp."

He held it close. Together they examined the case's lining — cracked leatherette over board, ordinary, unremarkable — except along the back wall, where the stitching ran not in the factory's neat machine rows but in small, uneven, human stitches. Stitches made by a man working by lamplight with a sailmaker's needle, in a hurry, in secret, in his own cellar, while his daughter slept above him.

Neither of them spoke. The lamp flame stood perfectly still.

Bram found the seam's end tucked beneath the corner brace, and her fingers — her father's fingers, trained by his training — worked it loose the way she imagined he had worked it shut. The lining parted. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth folded in a convention she recognized from Oskar's descriptions, lay a bound book the size of a psalter, its cover plain black cloth gone soft with age and handling.

She didn't open it. Not yet. Her hands were shaking too badly to trust with it.

"It was here," she said. "All this time. Under my hands." A laugh escaped her, thin and wrong. "He hid it *here*, Oskar. In the one place in Vellmarch the army will seal and search and strip to the bricks within the week — and also the one place no stranger would ever think to look, because why would the resistance bury its most dangerous document in a loyal foundry run by a dead man's son? He hid it where only I would keep it safe. He trusted me to guard it without ever telling me it existed."

"Or," Oskar said, very quietly, taking the lamp from her numb hands and setting it on the casting bench, "he trusted you to find it when the time came. Those aren't the same thing, Bram. One is using you. The other is believing in you." He nodded at the book. "Open it."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The folds followed her father's convention — Oskar made a small sound when he saw it, confirmation and grief in one breath — and inside was the ledger itself, dense columns of names in her father's upright draftsman's hand, dates, units, last sightings, annotations in three colors of ink. Page after page after page. Four hundred souls in a psalter's skin.

Near the front, in the margin beside the first entry, her father had written a single line, small enough to require the lamp held close:

*A list is a lantern. Keep it lit.*

Bram read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving. Above them, faintly, through floors and frost, the soldiers' boots went on drilling in the empty square — left, left, left — patient as the coming requisition, patient as the dawn.

Oskar touched her shoulder. "We need to move it," he said. "Tonight."

Bram closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping child, and shook her head.

"No," she said. "He put it here for a reason. Before we carry it anywhere — before we touch another thing in this room — I want to know what else my father hid, and why he chose *me* to hold it."

Above them, in the dark of the square, a whistle blew, and the boots stopped.
