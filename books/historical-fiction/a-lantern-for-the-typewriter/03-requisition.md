## 3. Requisition

Bram heard the drums before the light.

They came up out of the dark like something rising through water — a low, doubled beat from the direction of the square, not the parade rhythm the garrison used for inspections but a slower pulse, deliberate as a heartbeat in an ear pressed to a chest. She had fallen asleep at the composing stone again, cheek against the cold iron of the chase, her fingers still black with ink to the second knuckle. For one confused moment she thought the drums were her own pulse, that she had died at her work and this was what dying sounded like.

Then the light came too.

It slid under the cellar door first, a thin blade of it, then climbed the stairs and laid itself across the floor of the shop above in long grey bars. Dawn. But wrong dawn — dawn with boots in it. She pushed herself upright, joints crackling like paper, and went to the window with the caution that had become so habitual it no longer felt like caution, only like breathing.

The square below was filling with soldiers.

Not drilling — she knew drilling the way she knew her own hands, had watched it every evening for three weeks from this same window while she set type by lamplight. This was different. The men moved in files but without the crispness of ceremony; they spread out across the cobbles like water finding its level, and they carried things. Hammers. Nails. Sheets of paper rolled into tubes. At the corners of the square, four of them were raising something on poles — she squinted, and her stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling in a sack.

Seals. Army seals, red wax the size of a man's fist, already stamped and waiting.

"They're closing the town," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it less true somehow and she needed it less true for another few minutes. Her voice in the empty shop was her father's voice — low, roughened by lead dust, pitched down years ago until it stopped being a performance and became simply how she spoke. *Aldous Winterbourne's son.* The son who never appeared in daylight. The son the neighbors had stopped asking after, because grief was a fine excuse for strangeness and everyone in Vellmarch had grief enough of their own this winter.

She dressed quickly: the padded coat that broadened her shoulders, the cap pulled low, the smudge of ink she pressed behind each ear without thinking, the way another woman might pinch color into her cheeks. Then she unlocked the shop door and stepped out into the last morning of her old life.

* * *

The cold was total. It had come in the night off the mountains and settled over Vellmarch like a lid, and the smoke from the chimneys rose straight up in columns so still they looked painted there. Frost furred the iron sign above her door — WINTERBOURNE & SON, TYPE FOUNDERS, the letters her father had cut himself forty years ago and gilded twice since — and Bram stood beneath it watching a sergeant move down the row of shops with a hammer and a fistful of nails, unhurried, almost gentle, the way a man is gentle with a task he has done so often it bores him.

At the baker's door he nailed up his sheet. At the chandler's. At the house where the Widow Kessel sold thread and buttons and, Bram knew, carried messages to Oskar's people sewn into hemmed cuffs. He did not read the papers aloud. He did not need to. Half the street had come out onto their steps already, drawn by the drums, standing in their nightclothes with shawls clutched at their throats, and the silence they made together was louder than any proclamation shouted from a cart.

The sergeant reached the foundry last, or perhaps first — perhaps hers was simply where he started, though she would never learn which — and he looked up at the sign, and at her, and touched his cap with two fingers in something that was almost courtesy.

"Proprietor?"

"I am."

"Aldous Winterbourne?"

"His son." The lie came out smooth as poured metal. Eleven months of practice. "My father is dead."

"My condolences." He said it the way men said it to her constantly now, without weight, a coin passed without counting. He turned to the door and held the paper flat against the wood, and Bram read it upside down over his shoulder, her eyes moving faster than his hammer.

*By order of Colonel Hessler, commanding, and pursuant to the Emergency Regulation of Public Order:* her eye snagged on the legal scaffolding, the clauses nested inside clauses like boxes inside boxes — *all printing presses, typesets, matrices, paper stock, ink, and records of production within the town of Vellmarch are hereby declared property of the Army of the Interior. All such materials are to be surrendered for inventory within three days. Any press operated, any page printed, any document concealed after this notice constitutes sedition against the state and will be tried by military tribunal.*

*Records.* Her father's word came back to her from the grave, or from wherever dead type cutters kept their words: *Everything is records, Bram. Every ledger, every list, every name set down in ink. Whoever holds the paper holds the past, and whoever holds the past—* he had never finished it, that last lesson, because he'd died between one sentence and the next, mid-argument about kerning, and she had finished it for him a hundred times since in the dark. *Whoever holds the past decides who was ever alive.*

The sergeant drove the last nail and stepped back to admire his work, and Bram saw that the paper covered, precisely, the place where a notice of her father's funeral had once hung, two winters gone. Someone in the army's clerical machinery had measured for that. Or maybe it was coincidence. Lately she could not tell coincidence from design, and suspected the difference no longer mattered.

"How many days," she said, "for the inventory?"

"Three. Officers will come door to door with manifests." He was already turning away, then paused, and glanced back at her with the frank appraisal of a man doing arithmetic he'd been ordered to do. "You're a type founder. You'll have a fair quantity."

"Some."

"You'll want everything counted proper. They're strict about shortages." He said it almost kindly, a warning wrapped in procedure, and walked on toward the tannery at the end of the row, hammer swinging.

Bram stood in front of her own sealed door for a long time. Behind her, the street murmured — the Widow Kessel weeping quietly into her shawl, the baker's boy asking his father what *sedition* meant, an old man spitting carefully into the gutter as though the frost itself were an officer. Above the rooftops, on the church tower, soldiers were rigging a signal lantern. The town was being closed like a hand closing around something it intended to crush.

And underneath the shock, underneath the cold crawling in her chest, something else was happening. Something she recognized only dimly at first, the way you recognize a smell from childhood before you can name it.

It was resolve. Arriving early. Hardening while the rest of her was still soft with sleep and dread.

* * *

She spent the morning doing the most dangerous ordinary thing available: working.

If the officers came today — and they might; the regulation said three days, but regulations were weather, they changed by the hour — she wanted them to find a foundry behaving exactly as a foundry should, mourning its requisition openly, nothing hidden, nothing hurried. So she opened the shop, swept the step, and began breaking down the previous night's pamphlet run into sorted piles of type, letter by letter back into their cases, her hands moving through the familiar litany — *a, b, c, the devil's alphabet,* her father had called it, *the only alphabet that talks back* — while her mind worked the problem underneath the work.

All presses. All type. All paper. All *records.*

Her livelihood, itemized and confiscated. And more than her livelihood — because a type foundry in a town on the edge of revolt was not merely a business. It was the resistance's throat. Every pamphlet she had printed these past months, every handbill and obituary and forbidden poem, had passed through these stones. If Hessler's men inventoried properly — and the sergeant's warning suggested they would — they would find the fresh-cut sorts she'd made for the pamphlet faces, the altered matrices, the second galley hidden behind the wall panel where her father had once stored his master patterns. They would find evidence. And evidence, in Vellmarch this winter, had a way of becoming gallows.

But it was the word *records* that kept returning, circling back like a bird she couldn't drive off. Records of production. What did the army want with records? Paper was paper; presses were weapons. But records—

Oskar's face came to her, unbidden: the printer's grey stubble, the way he'd leaned close two nights ago among the reeking vats of the dye-works and told her the ledger had been moved again, that its hiding place was known only to a dead man, and watched her face while he said it as though waiting for her to flinch. She hadn't flinched. She hadn't understood. She had gone home and printed pamphlets and slept against the composing stone like a child.

Now, sorting type in a condemned shop, she understood a little better. If the army was seizing records across the whole town, it wasn't scavenging. It was searching. Somewhere in Vellmarch was a document worth sealing a town shut over — a list, a ledger, names — and Hessler meant to find it the way you find a single nail in a barrel of them: by taking the whole barrel apart.

And her father had been dead eleven months, and had kept secrets she had never thought to ask about, and somewhere in this building or attached to it might be the reason soldiers were nailing proclamations to her door.

The thought arrived with physical force, a pressure behind the sternum. She set down the type stick very carefully, as though it might go off.

* * *

Midday brought the carts.

They came through the north gate in a line, escorted, and halted in the square, and the unloading began — not loading, Bram noted, watching from the doorway with her arms crossed against the cold, but *unloading*. Crates of army paper. A portable press in a slatted wooden case, military-grey, its brass fittings dull with grease. Ink in tins stenciled with the eagle-and-pike. Soldiers hauled it all into the old customs hall, and by early afternoon the windows of the customs hall glowed with lamplight and the sounds of a press running drifted out into the street — the rhythmic iron breathing she knew better than any lullaby.

They were going to print. Here. In her town, with her trade, on seized paper.

An officer stood in the customs hall doorway overseeing the work, and even at a distance Bram knew the silhouette: Colonel Hessler himself had come, which meant this was not routine confiscation. This was a statement. He was a spare, neat man with the kind of face that aged slowly because it had never expressed anything worth wrinkling for, and he stood with his hands clasped behind him surveying the square the way a farmer surveys a field before plowing. Once, briefly, his gaze crossed the street and passed over the foundry, over the sign, over the figure in the doorway — and moved on without stopping.

Bram had spent eleven months training herself to be overlooked, and she had never resented it more than in that moment. She wanted, violently, absurdly, to be seen — to stride into the square and tell him what she thought of his regulation, his seals, his grey press thieving the town's own voice and turning it against it. The wanting frightened her more than the soldiers did. That was how carelessness began: not with mistakes but with the itch to stop hiding.

She turned back inside and locked the door and stood in the dim shop among her father's letters, thousands upon thousands of them, every word ever cut in this room still here in miniature, waiting in their cases like a city asleep.

"All right," she said softly, to the letters, to the dead man whose craft she wore like armor. "Three days."

Three days before the officers came with their manifests and found whatever they found. Three days to decide what could be shown, what must be buried, what — if Oskar was right, if her father had left her something she'd never asked about — might need to be moved in the night through a town full of soldiers.

She took down the lamp, trimmed the wick, and went to the wall panel behind the pattern cabinet. Her fingers found the seam without hunting; she had opened it a thousand times. But tonight, before touching anything inside, she knelt and put her ear to the cold stone of the cellar beyond, half expecting to hear her father's voice still mid-sentence about kerning, still explaining that whoever holds the paper holds the past.

The cellar said nothing. Cellars are patient that way.

She swung the panel wide, lifted the lamp into the dark — and stopped, because there on the shelf where her father's master patterns should have been, the space stood empty. Dust everywhere else, thick as felt, undisturbed for months. But that shelf: clean. Recently clean. As if someone had taken the patterns out.

Or as if the patterns had never been the point of the hiding place at all — and whatever her father had kept there, he had trusted someone else to move it.

Someone dead, Oskar had said. Known only to a dead man.

Bram stood in the lamplight with her heart going hard and slow, and understood that the requisition nailed to her door was not the beginning of anything. It was the middle. It had begun a long time ago, in this cellar, in her father's silence — and tomorrow she would find Oskar among the dye-vats and ask him the question she should have asked the first night:

*What did my father hide, and where did it go?*
