## 4. The Hollow Case

The cellar door stuck the way it always had, and Bram shouldered it open with the same grunt her father used to make, as if the house itself required an apology before it would admit anyone below.

Lamplight found the familiar shapes: the racks of type cases stacked against the stone, the quenching trough gone dry and ringed with rust, the great iron press that had been Aldous Winterbourne's pride and was now, by order of Colonel Hessler's men, contraband. Dust hung in the beam of her lantern like a held breath. Three days since the requisition notices had been pasted to every door in Vellmarch, three days since she had watched a corporal with ink-stained fingers slap paper over her own threshold — *all presses, all matrices, all records* — and she still caught herself reaching for the lamp at midnight out of habit, out of the old hunger for work.

But there was no work now. There was only what remained.

She had come down for a practical reason. The army would inventory the cellar within the week; Duvall's men were already cataloguing the foundry floor above, chalking numbers on crates. If there was anything down here that could hang her — anything that spoke of the pamphlets, of Oskar, of the long nights of forbidden print — it had to go into the river or into the fire before the soldiers' boots came down these stairs. She told herself that was why she'd brought the lantern. She told herself this was tidying, not searching.

Her father had died believing she knew everything worth knowing about him. She had let him believe it.

She started at the far wall, where his personal effects had sat untouched for two years because neither grief nor courage had been equal to them. His coat, still smelling faintly of coal smoke and lead. His reading spectacles, one lens starred with a scratch from a flying shard of type metal. A tin of pipe tobacco he had never smoked because the cough got him first. And on the lowest shelf, shoved back against the cold stone as though he had wanted it forgotten —

the typewriter case.

It was a battered thing, leather over wood, its brass corners worn to bright crescents where his hands had gripped it for thirty years. He had carried it to every commission, every wealthy client's parlor, every demonstration of his craft. Inside it lived the machine itself: a Vellmarch-made typewriter of no particular distinction, the kind merchants bought their sons to learn lettering on. Bram remembered being small and watching him open it like a priest opening a reliquary, showing her how each key struck a steel arm bearing a single letter — *look how the letters fly to the paper, little bird, look how they answer.*

She lifted the case down. It was heavier than she remembered. Or perhaps memory had made it lighter, the way memory makes everything lighter, everything kinder.

Set it on the workbench. Turned the latches.

Inside, the typewriter sat exactly as he'd left it, keys yellowed, ribbon dried to a brittle black ribbon of itself. Beneath the carriage, folded into the paper roller as if the machine had simply stopped mid-sentence years ago, lay a sheet of blank paper. She drew it out with two fingers. Blank — except that when she tilted it toward the lantern, the ghost of an impression rose up: a date, a name half-formed, *Vellm*, nothing more. Her father's hands had rested here last.

"Bram," she said aloud to the empty cellar, using her own false name like a charm against weeping. "Don't be a fool."

She began to empty the case properly, because a soldier would, because thoroughness was survival now. The ribbon spools came free. The little brush. A screwdriver wrapped in cloth. Then she tipped the case on its side and shook it gently, and something inside did not rattle so much as shift — a soft, dense sliding, like sand in a sack, like pages.

She turned the case over. Ran her thumb along the bottom lining. It was leather over canvas, glued and stitched, and the stitching along one edge was wrong. Not wrong the way a cheap craftsman's work is wrong — wrong the way a careful man's deliberate work is wrong. The stitches were smaller there, tighter, doubled. Aldous Winterbourne had cut letters for forty years and sewn nothing in his life that Bram knew of, but he had known precision the way other men knew prayer, and someone had taught him this stitch or he had taught himself, late at night, alone, downstairs while his daughter slept above him dreaming innocent dreams.

Her heart began doing something slow and enormous behind her ribs.

She went to the wall rack for her father's awl — his good one, bone-handled — and knelt on the cold flags with the case between her knees like a child preparing a lamb for slaughter, and she picked the stitches out one by one. It took a long time. Her hands, which could cut a serif finer than any hand in three counties, shook so badly on the fourth stitch that she had to stop and press her palms flat against the stone floor until the shaking passed.

The lining peeled back.

Beneath it, sewn flat against the wooden shell of the case itself, wrapped in oilcloth and bound twice with waxed cord, was a book.

Not a pamphlet. Not a bundle of papers. A book, thick as a psalter, its cover darkened with handling, its spine cracked white from opening and opening again. She knew before she touched it what it was. Every soul in Vellmarch knew of it the way you know of a saint's relic — something spoken of quietly, in cellars, in the pauses after doors closed. The ledger. The master ledger. The book Oskar's people had kept and hidden and moved through the town like a kidney stone of pure dread, passing it from hiding place to hiding place, until the men who carried it started disappearing and the knowledge of its location narrowed and narrowed until —

until it rested, apparently, in the lining of a dead type cutter's typewriter case, six feet beneath the bedroom where his daughter slept.

"Father," she whispered. The word came out strange, half accusation, half awe.

She untied the cord. Her fingers remembered themselves then — the trembling stopped, replaced by the old workshop steadiness, the craftsman's calm that descended whenever her hands had real work. She unwrapped the oilcloth and opened the book across her knees.

Names. Columns of names, in a dozen different hands, some careful, some scrawled by lamplight by people who wrote fast because writing fast might keep them alive. Dates beside the names. Beside some, small annotations: *taken from the grain gate, Tuesday.* *Seen on the ferry, then not seen.* *Hanged at the barracks wall, denied burial.* Beside others, nothing at all — just a name and a date and then blankness, which was its own kind of entry, its own terrible verb tense.

She read names she knew. Frau Ilsa Brandt, who had sold her eggs at market and once given young Bram — given the girl Bram had been — a honey cake for no reason but that children liked honey cakes. Taken in October. The Petzold brothers, both of them, taken the same night, and their mother had stood in the square every morning for a month afterward holding a candle until the soldiers took the candle too. Tomas Lindqvist, the schoolmaster, whose handwriting she recognized now filling whole columns, neat and pedagogical even in terror, recording his neighbors the way he'd once recorded their children's lessons.

And there, in the margin of the third page, in a hand she knew better than her own — a hand she had watched shape letters across a composing stick for twenty years — her father's initials. *A.W.* Beside a name she did not recognize. Beside another she did. Beside dates, beside details, beside observations written in the compressed shorthand of a man who understood that ink was evidence and evidence must be economical.

Aldous Winterbourne had not merely hidden the ledger. He had fed it. He had been one of its keepers.

She sat back on her heels in the cold and the dust and felt the truth arrive in her body before her mind consented to it: all those nights he'd sent her to bed early. All those mornings she'd come down to find the lamp burned dry and him asleep at the bench with his head on his arms. She had thought he was working — cutting matrices, finishing commissions. He had been. But he had also been doing this. Writing down the disappeared in a cellar while the town above him learned to look through its missing neighbors as though emptiness were contagious.

He had never said a word. Not one word, not a hint, not a slip in two years of dying slowly and well. He had sewn this book into the case of the machine he loved best in the world and put it on a shelf in the dark and trusted — without asking, without telling, without burdening her with the weight of knowing while he lived — that if she ever needed it, she would find it. That she was the kind of person who would find it. That his daughter's hands, trained to precision, trained to notice a wrong stitch, would do what his dying hands could not.

Trusted her with hundreds of lives and never spent one evening of her peace to tell her so.

The grief that came up through her then was unlike the grief of his death, which had been a clean wound, a finished thing. This was older and rawer, a grief for all the conversations they had not had, for every supper where he'd asked after her day and she'd invented a harmless version of it and he had smiled and let her lie, carrying his secret alone because love, in her father's grammar, meant never making your child complicit. She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth and let the lantern light waver, and for a long time the only sound in the cellar was her breathing and the tick of cooling stone.

Then she wiped her face with her sleeve, because crying was a luxury with a shelf life, and read.

She read for an hour. She lost track of the lantern oil. The ledger was organized by season, by roundups, by the grim arithmetic of Hessler's occupation — and it went back further than the occupation, back to the first quiet disappearances that the town had agreed not to discuss, back to names that predated even Oskar's network. Whoever had begun this book had begun it early, with the foresight of someone who had watched other towns forget and refused. In the back pages, in fresher ink, entries continued nearly to the present year. Nearly. The last entry was dated five weeks before her father's death. After that, blank pages waiting, patient as graves.

Which meant the keeping had stopped. Which meant whoever inherited the duty had either fled, or been taken, or —

Oskar. Oskar had told her, ten days ago in the reek of the hidden press, his voice pitched low under the sound of the army drilling in the square: *the ledger's been moved again, and only a dead man knows where.* He had looked at her strangely when he said it. She had thought the strangeness was fear. Now she understood it had been something closer to hope — Oskar, who trusted no one, had sent her home to her father's cellar with those words like a key thrown into deep water, betting that the daughter would know what the son never could have known.

Or — colder thought, arriving uninvited — Oskar hadn't bet at all. Oskar had known. Had known where it slept all along and kept it as his last card, and had played it now only because the requisition had changed the game.

That was a question for daylight, for Oskar's face, for whatever remained of trust between them. Down here in the dark there was only the book, and the book did not care who had wanted it found. The book only insisted, page after page, column after column, *we existed, we had names, we are owed.*

She heard boots above her — muffled, distant, crossing the foundry floor where the chalk numbers multiplied daily. Her whole body went still, the way it went still when she cut type, everything unnecessary falling away. The boots faded. A door closed somewhere in the upper rooms. The town above went on performing its new obedience.

Bram looked down at the ledger lying open across her knees, and then at the hollowed case beside it, its lining gaping, its secret out.

The army wanted presses, paper, records. *All records.* Somewhere in Hessler's requisition orders, in Elias Rehn's careful clerk's hand — she had seen the man once at the town office, pale and precise, signing his name to other people's ruin — the word *records* included books like this. If the ledger stayed in Vellmarch it would burn, and with it every name in every column, and the burning would succeed where the hangings had failed: the town would truly forget, cleanly, officially, with documentation.

She wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth. She did not resew the lining — there was no time, and no point; the case was empty now anyway, just a battered box carrying a useless machine. Instead she bound the cord tight, and then, after a moment, took up her father's pencil from the bench and opened the ledger's final blank page.

She thought about what to write. Her hand hovered. Everything she might set down felt too large for graphite, too true for the space of a line.

At last she wrote only: *Found. Kept. — B.*

Then she closed the book, blew out the lantern, and climbed the stairs into the grey morning, holding her father's typewriter case against her chest like a sleeping child, knowing that from this moment forward she was not a woman carrying contraband but a woman carrying testimony — and that testimony, unlike type, unlike pamphlets, unlike the clever invisibility she had worn for two years, could not be melted down and recast. It could only be delivered or die with her.

Outside, in the square, the soldiers were drilling again. And at the corner by the baker's, watching the foundry windows with the patient attention of a man matching ink samples in his head, stood Sergeant Duvall.
