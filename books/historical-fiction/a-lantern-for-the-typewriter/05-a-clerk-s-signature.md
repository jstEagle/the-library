## 5. A Clerk's Signature

The requisition had taken Elias Rehn four hours and eleven minutes to process, and he knew this because the army kept time in everything now, even theft. Four hours and eleven minutes of his pen moving down columns, converting a living town into inventory: presses, platen machines, fonts of type, reams of paper, the Winterbourne Foundry and its contents, all entered in a hand so neat it looked like an apology.

He sat in the requisition office above the old customs house with the shutters half-closed against the morning glare off the river, and he told himself what he always told himself: that paper did not kill. That he moved names from one column to another and other men moved other things, and between his ink and any actual suffering stood the whole length of Colonel Hessler's chain of command, every link of it forged by someone else.

It was a comfortable arithmetic. He had been practicing it for six years.

On the table before him lay the seizure manifest for the foundry — the last item processed before the seals went on the doors. Type, assorted, 214 cases. Press, hand-lever, one. Furniture, chases, quoins, composing sticks. Ink, fresh, quantity irregular. He had written *fresh* without thinking about it, because the sergeant conducting the inventory had said it with a frown, and Elias had recorded the frown as a fact.

Fresh ink. In a foundry whose owner was eleven months dead.

He turned the page. The inventory clerk had stamped each case of type with the foundry's mark where the mark appeared — a small device pressed into the wooden side of every case, as typefounders had done since before anyone in this room was born. Aldous Winterbourne's stamp was a lantern. A little lantern, cut with absurd delicacy, its flame rendered in three strokes, sitting above the letters *A.W.* Elias had seen it yesterday when the cases were carried past his desk, and something in him had snagged on it like a coat catching on a nail, and he had not known why until this morning, alone, with no one to perform indifference for.

Because he had seen that lantern before. Not on type cases. On paper.

Three years ago, before the purges began in earnest, before his own signature had learned to travel light across terrible documents, Elias had worked the records annex in the provincial capital. One afternoon a clerk senior to him — a soft man named Brandt who wept easily and signed anything — had handed him a folder and told him to log its contents into evidence and then forget it. Inside were pamphlets. Seditious pamphlets, the kind that circulated hand to hand and were worth a beating if found in your pocket. But these had been seized not from agitators but from a print shop in Vellmarch, and among them was a single sheet that was not seditious at all. It was an obituary. A printed obituary for a ferrywoman's husband, set in beautiful type, the name rendered so cleanly it seemed less printed than carved. And beneath it, small as a whisper, the lantern stamp.

Elias had logged it and forgotten it, the way he'd been told, and had thought about it perhaps four hundred times since.

Now he sat looking at the lantern again, multiplied across two hundred and fourteen wooden cases stacked in the courtyard below, and the arithmetic he practiced began to fail him in a new place. Because everyone in Vellmarch knew what happened to the town's dead. They did not get obituaries. They got silence. The registry office burned its records quarterly now; the churchyard had stopped adding markers; men and women were taken from their houses and became, administratively, *relocated*, and relocation was a word with no opposite, no return address, no grave.

And yet someone in that foundry had been printing names. Recently. In secret. Someone had been doing, in a cellar by lamplight, the exact thing the state existed now to prevent: remembering.

Elias closed the manifest and sat very still.

Below him, through the floorboards, came the sound of soldiers stacking the confiscated type into a wagon. Each case knocked against the next with a hollow wooden clatter, two hundred and fourteen small coffins of someone else's craft, bound for the smelter, where lead became bullets, which was the army's whole theology in a sentence. Letters into ammunition. Names into noise.

He should have felt nothing. Men in his position learned to feel nothing the way other men learned to swim — badly, but enough. Instead he felt the specific vertigo of a man who has walked the same corridor for years and suddenly notices the floor is thinner than he thought, that below it there is a drop, and the drop has been there all along.

*Who will die if it burns.*

That was the phrase that arrived, unbidden, and would not leave. He didn't even know what *it* was. But he knew how Hessler's administration worked, and he knew what a hidden press meant, and he knew — with the sick certainty of a man who has read ten thousand files and understands their grammar — that wherever there was a secret press printing obituaries, there was a list. There was always a list. The families kept them. The printers kept them. Somewhere in Vellmarch, in someone's cellar or floor or coffin, there was a ledger of the disappeared, every name the regime had tried to erase, kept alive in ink by people who understood that ink was the only country left to them.

And he had just requisitioned the press that made it possible. His signature. His neat, apologetic hand.

Elias opened his desk drawer and took out the file he was not authorized to take out: the master index of Vellmarch district records, the book that told him which files existed and where they slept. He ran his finger down to *W*. Winterbourne, Aldous. Deceased. And beneath it, in the cross-reference column, a notation he had never noticed before, or had noticed and declined to see: *See also: Annex 7, Confiscations, 3rd quarter, year prior to death. See also: Registry irregularities.*

Annex 7 was two floors down and one corridor over, in the basement where files went to be forgotten, which in this army meant to be safe. Elias looked at the clock. He had forty minutes before his afternoon queue. It was, he told himself, entirely ordinary for a processing clerk to verify cross-references before finalizing a manifest. Clerks verified things. Verification was practically piety.

He took the master key from the hook where it hung, feeling its weight like a decision.

* * *

The basement smelled of cold stone and damp glue. Rows of shelving ran back into the dark, and the gas lamp at the end of the row gave just enough light to read by and not enough to feel brave by. Elias found Annex 7, third quarter, and pulled the box, and knew within a moment that something was wrong with it, because it was too light.

Confiscation boxes held paper. Paper was heavy with grief and guilt in equal measure. This box held almost nothing: a few receipts, an empty envelope, and a card — an index card — bearing the lantern stamp and a single typed line:

*Received in full. Disposition: retained by donor.*

Retained by donor. Which meant that Aldous Winterbourne, in the last quarter of his life, had walked into a government annex with a box of documents, surrendered some of them officially, and kept the rest — legally, with a receipt, in the manner of a man who wanted very much for the army's own paperwork to prove that nothing else existed. Elias stared at the card. It was the most careful thing he had ever seen. A dead printer had used the bureaucracy itself as a hiding place, had buried his secrets under the one blanket no soldier would think to look beneath: a filed receipt.

Donor. Not *owner*. Donor — as if the documents were a gift. As if they were being kept *for* somebody.

Elias put the box back exactly as he had found it, aligning the dust shadow on the shelf, because he had learned long ago that in this building the dust remembered more faithfully than the clerks.

Then he did the thing he would later understand had divided his life into before and after. He copied the card's contents into his pocket notebook, in his own hand, in his own shorthand — the small treachery of a bureaucrat archiving against the archive — and he wrote beneath it, though he could not have said why, the words *who else knows*.

He climbed the stairs. The daylight at the top seemed different, thinner, like light through dirty glass.

* * *

Back at his desk, the afternoon queue waited: twelve requisitions to finalize, two demolition assessments, a payroll dispute. Ordinary work. Work that did not kill anyone today, probably. He signed the first three papers before he let himself stop.

Sergeant Duvall came in while he was staring at the fourth.

Duvall had the gift of appearing without seeming to arrive, a quality Elias associated with cats and informants. He was a compact man with a sergeant's permanent posture and eyes that processed rooms the way Elias processed files — completely, and without mercy. He set a sample envelope on Elias's desk.

"Ink," Duvall said. "From the Winterbourne premises. I want it logged as evidence rather than stock. There's a discrepancy."

"A discrepancy."

"The inventory says fresh ink, quantity irregular." Duvall's voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud from inside your skull. "Fresh means recent use. Recent use means a working press after the owner's death. A working press means a worker." He paused. "The heir hasn't presented himself for the inheritance interview. Standard notice went out nine days ago."

Elias felt the corridor floor give way again, gently, professionally. "Perhaps grief keeps him indoors."

"Grief," Duvall said, "keeps people indoors for a week. Nine days is something else." He tapped the envelope once. "Log it. And Rehn — when the inheritance interview happens, I'll conduct it myself. I find family members talk more freely to someone who isn't writing everything down."

He left. Elias sat with the envelope in front of him and understood that he was now holding two pieces of a puzzle that, fitted together, would hang somebody. Fresh ink plus an absent heir equaled a hunt, and Duvall was already hunting, and the only question was whether the quarry would be found by the man with the sample envelope or whether — 

Whether what? Elias examined himself with the same cold thoroughness he brought to a column of figures. Whether you intend to warn them. Whether you, Elias Rehn, who signed the requisition, who converted two hundred and fourteen cases of lantern-stamped letters into smelter fodder with a stroke of your apologetic pen, are contemplating treason over an index card in a basement.

His hand was steady when he picked up the pen. That frightened him more than trembling would have.

He logged the ink as evidence. Then, in the disposition column, where protocol demanded he write *secure*, he wrote instead — in handwriting so slightly altered that only a clerk of clerks would catch it — *secure, pending verification of chain of custody*. Chain of custody. Three words that meant the sample could not be acted upon until its handling was certified, and certification required a second officer, and the second officer assigned to such matters was currently on leave, and leave extensions required form 44-C, and Elias was the man who processed form 44-C.

It was a small delay. Days, perhaps a week. It was also, he recognized, the first lie he had ever told with his work instead of despite it, and it fit his hand better than any truth ever had.

* * *

Evening came down over Vellmarch the way it always did, gilding the river and darkening the square where the drills continued past sundown now, boots striking cobbles in rhythms designed to remind everyone whose town this was. Elias walked home the long way, past the sealed foundry. The army seal on its door was bright yellow paper, and beside it, nailed up that morning, the requisition order with his signature at the bottom, and he stopped and read his own name as though it belonged to a stranger.

*Aldous Winterbourne had come to the annex in person,* he thought. *An old man carrying a box, surrendering part of it to make the rest invisible. Donor. He was giving it to someone. Keeping it for someone. Who inherits a dead man's silences?*

The heir. The son who had never appeared in daylight, never presented himself, never answered a standard notice. The son whom nobody in Vellmarch's taverns could describe beyond *the foundry boy, keeps to himself, cuts type like his father did.* Elias had heard that phrase twice today, from two different informants, and both times the speaker had hesitated a half-second before *boy*, the way people hesitate when a description doesn't quite sit right in the mouth.

Somewhere in that sealed building, or somewhere beyond it, a person was keeping a ledger of the disappeared, printing names the state had ordered forgotten, and the army was closing in with ink samples and inheritance interviews, and the only man positioned to slow the machinery was the man who had oiled it.

Elias took out his pocket notebook, standing right there under the streetlamp like any citizen consulting his accounts, and added a line beneath *who else knows*:

*The son knows. Find him first. Decide afterward what kind of man you are.*

He closed the notebook and looked up, and across the square, at the corner by the baker's shuttered stall, he saw a figure watching the foundry seal — slight, hooded despite the mild evening, still in the way that only guilt or vigilance makes a person still. The figure saw him seeing and turned away, unhurried, deliberate, a departure rehearsed like everything else about it.

Elias did not follow. He wrote the time in his notebook instead, and the corner, and the color of the coat, because that was the only language he had ever been fluent in. Then he went home, and did not sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn he took out a fresh requisition form and, in the space marked *processing clerk*, began practicing a signature that was not quite his own —

because he had finally understood what the lantern stamp had been telling him for three years: that in Vellmarch, the only way to survive a signature was to learn to forge one.
