## 6. Three Checkpoints

Oskar arrived after midnight, the way he always arrived, as though the dark were a door he had learned to open without sound. Bram heard him on the stairs before she saw him — not his boots, which were soft-soled and silent, but the particular hesitation of a man pausing on each landing to listen for the listening of others.

She blew out the lamp over the composing stone but left the one in the back room burning, low, behind the curtain of hanging type trays. That was their signal. A light meant *come*. No light meant *stay away until the bells*, and once — only once — no light had meant *run*.

He came through the curtain smelling of coal smoke and river water, his coat still dripping at the hem. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a printer's forearms and a face that looked like it had been set from worn type: all the sharp edges knocked off years ago. He set a wrapped bundle on the workbench and unwrapped it with the care of a man handling wet paper, which was, in a sense, what it was.

"Maps," he said. "Drawn from memory. Don't ask me where I got the distances."

"I wouldn't insult either of us."

"Good." He spread them out and weighted the corners with quoins. "Sit down, Bram. This will take a while, and you'll want to interrupt, and I'd rather you heard all of it before you start telling me why it can't be done."

She sat on her father's stool — the one with the leather seat worn to the shape of Aldous Winterbourne's bones, which now fit hers almost exactly. She did not let herself think about why that was. The ledger lay hidden below them, sewn into the lining of a typewriter case, and for three days she had been unable to walk across the cellar floor without feeling its weight through the boards, the way you feel a buried pipe, a dead thing under the house.

"The border press office is at Sellenbrück," Oskar said. "You know that much."

"Everyone knows that much."

"Everyone knows the name. Nobody knows the road anymore, because nobody crosses. That's our advantage. It's also your death sentence, because the roads are watched by men who are bored, and bored soldiers search things." He tapped the first map with one thick finger. "Three checkpoints between here and there. Three masters, three temperaments, three ways to die. I want you to understand each of them separately before I show you how they connect, because if you think of this as one journey you will make one mistake, and one mistake at any of the three ends everything — not just for you."

"For the names," Bram said.

"For the names." He said it flatly, without ceremony, which was how Oskar said anything true.

* * *

"The grain gate first." He turned the map toward her. The eastern edge of Vellmarch, where the town spilled out onto the flats and the army had thrown up timber barricades across the old cart road. He had drawn the gate itself in careful cross-hatching, and beside it, small figures: two sentries, a sergeant, a wagon queue. "The gate is army, but it isn't Hessler's army — not really. It's garrison troops. Local levies, mostly boys from upriver farms who joined because there was bread in it. Their master is routine. Routine is the best master you could hope for, because routine doesn't think. It counts wagons, it checks manifests, it waves through what looks ordinary."

"And me?"

"You'll look ordinary. That's the whole art of the grain gate — you go through amid grain. There are confiscation convoys leaving every morning now, requisitioned wheat going out to feed the garrison further east. Carts, mules, dust, stink. Nobody searches a widow's cart in a convoy of forty, because searching slows the line, and slowing the line gets the sergeant shouted at, and getting shouted at is worse than contraband, out there." He glanced up. "Do you have a widow yet?"

"I'm working on her."

"Work fast. She has to be perfect by Thursday, because the convoy leaves at dawn Thursday and not again until the following week, and I don't like your chances of sitting on that typewriter case for eight more days with Duvall sniffing around ink stains." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "The danger at the grain gate isn't the search. It's the joke. Soldiers joke. A woman hauling a broken typewriter through a wheat convoy is funny, and funny things get remembered, and remembered things get described, and descriptions travel faster than carts. You want to be dull. Duller than dust. Can you be dull?"

Bram thought of ten years of walking through her own town as someone else's son, of lowering her voice in doorways, of learning to laugh at the wrong pitch so no one would look twice. "I've been dull my whole life."

"Then the grain gate may even bore you." He drew a line eastward, down off the flats to the blue scribble of the river. "* * *

"Now the ferry. And here I'll tell you plainly: the ferry is the one I can't buy you past."

Bram waited.

"It's not army at all. It's municipal — or it was, before the requisition swallowed everything else. The crossing at Marlow's Bend is run by ferrymen who've held those licenses since before either of us was born, and they answer to the harbor master, and the harbor master answers to whoever paid him last. Right now that's the army, loosely. But the men on the water are their own country. They know everyone who boards, and worse, they remember everyone who ever boarded." He leaned back. "Your father crossed that river four hundred times. I'd wager the ferryman knew his face better than mine."

"Marta," Bram said quietly.

Oskar's eyebrows went up. "You already know her."

"She used to come to the foundry. For the obituaries." Bram kept her voice level. "During the fever years. When the registry office stopped printing the deaths, my father printed them anyway, small runs, one sheet, names and dates. Marta carried them across the river to the villages on the far bank so families would know. She never paid. He never asked her to."

Oskar was quiet a moment, looking at her with something that might have been envy. "Then you're richer than I am. Debts like that don't appear on any ledger, and they're worth more than anything in yours." He tapped the river on the map. "If Marta will take you, take the ferry. If she won't — if she's frightened, if there's a new man watching the dock — then you have a problem, because the river checkpoint downstream is the second of the three, and it belongs to the worst master of all."

"The army proper."

"Hessler's own. Regulars. Officers who rotate through every few weeks so they don't grow roots, which means they're suspicious of everything precisely because they know nothing. They don't count wagons; they open them. They don't check papers; they interrogate. The commander there — the word I have is a Captain Ferreol — made his reputation in the southern provinces, and the way he made it was by finding things other men missed." Oskar's finger rested on the blue ink as though he could hold the river still. "But here is the thing about Ferreol's checkpoint: it processes manifests. Cargo. Passengers with papers. If Marta puts you on her boat as working crew — a hand, a niece, somebody's quiet daughter helping with the crossing — you never touch Ferreol's desk at all. Boats that work the bend go through on the ferryman's stamp. One signature, years old, worth more than any forgery."

"And if the storm comes up," Bram said, "or a patrol decides to board mid-river—"

"Then you're in the bilge with the bilgewater, praying." He met her eyes. "I won't lie to you about that part. The river is the middle of the plan, and middles are where plans die. The gate is luck and boredom. The pass is distance and weather. The river is other people's courage, and we can't control whose."

Bram looked at the map a long moment. The blue scribble seemed wider than she remembered the actual river being. Everything on paper was smaller than the world; that was the first lesson of type, that you could fit a whole confession into a space the size of your palm, and it would weigh nothing and cost everything.

* * *

"The pass last, then," Oskar said, and his voice changed — flattened, the way a man's voice flattens when he stops selling and starts confessing. "The mountain checkpoint at the Kessel Pass. Third master. And I'll say it now so you can hate me properly: this one may kill you regardless of how well you've done the first two."

"Tell me."

"The pass is held jointly — foreign troops on the far side, ours on this side, a stone building halfway up where both sides drink bad coffee and pretend the border runs through the middle of the room. Our side answers directly to the Ministry, which means Hessler, which means the men there are chosen, not assigned. They're not bored boys and they're not rotating officers. They are men who were picked because they notice things." He traced the switchback road upward, hairpin after hairpin drawn in tight, patient arcs. "They check everything. Papers twice, seals against a reference book, faces against memory. They have photographs now — do you understand? Not sketches. Photographs. Of printers, of couriers, of anyone the Ministry thinks might try to carry paper over that line."

"They'd have mine?"

"Not yours." He said it carefully, and she understood the care. "They'd have Bram Winterbourne's. The son. Which means—" He stopped.

"Which means the son has to exist right up until the pass," Bram finished for him, "and then stop existing entirely."

"That's the shape of it. At the gate you're a widow. On the river you're a boat-hand. At the pass you must be neither — you must be someone the photographs have never touched, with papers so clean they insult the checker's intelligence, because the clever forgeries are the ones they look for. Your father's seal work could fool God Himself, I believe that. But a perfect seal on an unknown name is its own kind of flag. The pass wants documents so ordinary they're beneath suspicion, and making ordinary documents is harder than making beautiful ones. Ask any printer."

Bram looked at the switchbacks climbing the page. Somewhere above them, snow already. Somewhere above that, a stone room with two flags and bad coffee and men who noticed things.

"Three checkpoints," she said. "A gate run by boredom, a river run by memory, a pass run by attention. And I have to be three different people, and none of them can resemble each other too closely, and none of them can resemble me."

"Yes."

"And what happens when I get through? The press office at Sellenbrück takes the ledger, prints the names, and the pages come back over the mountains and none of this matters anymore — the checkpoints, the widows, the bilgewater. All of it becomes nothing, because you can't requisition a thousand copies scattered in a thousand coat pockets."

Something moved across Oskar's face then, quick as a shadow over type metal, and was gone before she could read it. Later — much later — she would come back to that shadow and understand it. Now she filed it away with all the other things she did not let herself think about.

"That's the idea," Oskar said.

"It's simple," Bram said.

"It's simple," he agreed.

"And fatal."

"Almost certainly." He began folding the maps along their creases, precise as a compositor locking a chase. "I want you to hear the arithmetic, Bram, because I refuse to send anyone anywhere under a fog. Of the couriers who've tried the full route since the requisition — and there have been six — two turned back, one vanished at the pass, and three went through. Better than half. Those are good odds, for this work. But the three who made it weren't carrying anything the Ministry wanted. You'll be carrying the single most dangerous object between the mountains and the sea, and the day Duvall understands what's missing from that cellar, every man at every checkpoint will have your description memorized before the next bell rings."

"Then I go before he understands."

"He already suspects the ink. He suspects the son who doesn't see daylight." Oskar tied the bundle. "What he doesn't suspect yet is that the son is a fiction. The moment he suspects that, he stops hunting a smuggler and starts hunting a woman, and there aren't many women traveling alone out of Vellmarch these days, and fewer carrying typewriters."

"So I stop being a woman too."

Oskar looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight his worn-type face seemed older than it had an hour ago, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "That's the next lesson," he said finally. "And it's the hardest one I have to teach, and I can't teach it. Nobody can. You have to learn to forget your own hands, Bram. Your walk, your voice, the way you hold a cup, the way you look at a soldier's belt buckle instead of his face — everything the body knows about being yourself, you have to put away somewhere it can't leak out under pressure. Because pressure is coming. Pressure is the only certain thing on that road."

Bram stood and went to the window, easing the curtain back a finger's width. Below, the square lay empty except for the sentry at the corner, his breath ghosting in the lamplight, his rifle held in the posture of a man who had stood there so long the rifle had become part of his arm. Beyond him, the dark bulk of the requisitioned granary. Beyond that, east, the invisible flats where the grain gate waited, and the river beyond it, and the pass beyond that, stacked up like plates of glass she would have to walk across one after another, each one cracking a little under her weight.

"My father hid that ledger where I would find it," she said, not turning around. "Sewn into a case I've carried past my own hands a hundred times. He trusted me to finish something he never explained."

"Dead men trust us with terrible things," Oskar said. "It's the one advantage they have over the living. They don't have to watch us carry them."

"When does the convoy leave?"

"Dawn Thursday. Four days."

"Then I have four days to build a widow." She let the curtain fall. "Teach me how she walks."

Oskar shook his head slowly. "Not me. I can draw you maps and buy you stamps, but I can't teach erasure — I'm the wrong man for it; I've spent thirty years putting myself into print." He pulled his collar up and became, in the space of a breath, just a heavy tradesman leaving late from a friend's shop. "There's a woman on Tanner's Row. Widow herself, twice over. They say she can teach anyone to disappear. They also say she charges in secrets rather than coin, and I don't know what yours will cost you."

"Tanner's Row," Bram repeated.

"Number nine. Go tomorrow, in daylight, as the son — the son can visit a widow's house without scandal. And Bram." He paused at the curtain, one hand on the type trays, and for a moment he looked as though he wanted to say something large and could not afford it. "Whatever she teaches you — however good you get at forgetting — keep one thing written down somewhere inside yourself. One true sentence. Because the ones who vanish completely, they don't come back. Not across the pass. Not home."

Then he was gone, down the stairs and into the dark, soft as falling snow, and Bram stood alone in the lamplight with three folded maps and four days, and beneath her feet, through the floorboards, the weight of every name in Vellmarch pressing up against the cellar ceiling like a tide waiting for a door.
