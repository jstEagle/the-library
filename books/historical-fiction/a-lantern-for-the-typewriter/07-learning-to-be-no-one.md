## 7. Learning to Be No One

The mirror in the foundry office had been her mother's, and it was a liar. It caught the lamplight at an angle that softened everything, so that the face looking back at Bram Winterbourne seemed younger than she was, smoother, with none of the soot that lived in the creases around her eyes from years of leaning over the type cases. She had chosen this mirror deliberately. A liar's glass for a liar's work.

She stood before it in the dark hours of the third night after Oskar drew the route on the back of a grain invoice, and she began to kill herself.

Not all at once. That was the mistake amateurs made — they thought disguise was addition: a shawl, a stoop, a different name said in a different voice. Her father had taught her better, though he hadn't known he was teaching it. *Cut away until the letter holds nothing but what it must,* he'd say, guiding her graver through a piece of type metal. *Every gram you leave on is a gram the press has to fight.* So: subtraction. She would cut herself away until what remained was small enough to pass through three checkpoints without snagging.

The name first. She said it aloud to the empty office.

"Grethe Halvard."

It tasted wrong, the way new lead tastes wrong before it's been handled. Grethe. A widow of thirty-four — old enough to be weathered, young enough to have been married only six years before the fever took her husband. Oskar had built the identity from a real woman, a cousin of a cousin who had actually died in the spring fevers in Kesselmark, two provinces east, where no soldier in Vellmarch would know to check a parish book. The dead lent themselves generously to such work. They never objected.

"Grethe," she said again, and made her mouth soften around it. Not *Bram*, which she clipped short as a nail head. *Grethe* wanted a breath before it, a small country gentleness.

She practiced until midnight, then past it, saying the name into the dark between strikes of the clock in the square below, and somewhere around the hundredth repetition something strange happened, which was that the name stopped tasting wrong. That frightened her more than anything the army could have done. She wrote it down in her notebook — *remember that it stops being strange* — and then crossed it out, because a notebook could be seized too, and there were things even paper shouldn't know.

* * *

Walking came next, and walking was harder than names.

A widow hauling her dead husband's typewriter across half a province did not walk like Bram Winterbourne walked. Bram walked like a man who worked standing: weight forward, shoulders squared against the pull of the compositor's stick, steps quick and flat-heeled because the foundry floor punished any softness. Bram's walk said *I am going somewhere and I will arrive*. Grethe's walk had to say nothing at all.

Oskar had given her the frame of it, standing in the smoke-stink of the resistance cellar with his hands describing the shape of a tired woman. *Widows don't hurry. Hurrying means you expect to be somewhere, and being expected anywhere is a kind of importance. You are not important. You are a woman taking furniture to relatives because she can't afford to keep a room empty.* He'd demonstrated — a brief, absurd, unforgettable shuffle, shoulders drawn in, chin dipped, one hand ghosting toward the small of his back — and the other printers had laughed, and Oskar had let them laugh, and Bram had watched him over the top of their heads and seen that he wasn't performing comedy at all. He was performing survival, and he was good at it, and she had wondered then — briefly, and with a coldness she didn't examine — how many times before he had needed to be someone else.

So she practiced in the long gallery above the casting floor, where the windows faced the river and the light came up gray and honest at dawn. She tied a bundle of rags into the shape of a typewriter case — the weight mattered; the real case with its hollowed lining and its cargo of the dead weighed perhaps nine pounds more than it looked like it should, and her shoulders would have to learn to carry that lie without complaint — and she slung the strap across her chest the way a woman would who had grown used to its pull, not the way a thief carries stolen goods, tight and high and guilty.

Low. Loose. Let the case hang.

Her body fought her. That was the thing no one told you about becoming no one: your own muscles were loyalists. Every time she let her shoulders drop into the widow's apologetic curve, some trained part of her hoisted them back up, proud, visible, *Bram*. She walked the length of the gallery and back, forty paces each way, until the floorboards' complaints became a rhythm she could hide inside. Forty paces as Grethe. Turn. Forty paces as Bram, quick and hard, just to feel the difference. Then forty more as Grethe, deeper now, the difference carved cleaner by contrast, the way her father cut a counterpunch by cutting its opposite first.

By the second dawn her calves ached from walking slowly. It was the strangest soreness she had ever earned — pain from restraint, from the labor of being less — and she stood at the window watching the river fog burn off and thought: *this is what Father meant about letters. The hardest cuts are the ones that take away.*

* * *

Weeping on command was worse.

Oskar had been blunt about it. *They will not search a weeping woman as thoroughly as a calm one. Tears make soldiers stupid and embarrassed, and embarrassment makes them wave you through. But it cannot be theater. Duvall's men have seen theater. It has to be grief that frightens them.*

She had laughed at him. *Frightens them?*

*Grief that frightens them,* he repeated, and would not explain, and she understood later, alone, that he meant this: the tears had to come from somewhere true, or they would sit wrong on her face the way a forged seal sits wrong on a document — accurate in every line and yet somehow screaming *false* to anyone who had ever seen the real thing.

So on the fourth night she sat down at her father's desk, in his chair, which still held the shape of him the way a type case holds the shape of its sorts, and she opened the drawer where she kept the things she could not look at often. His last letter, unfinished, the pen laid across it mid-sentence as if he had merely stepped out to check a pour and would return. The little brass gauge he'd used to measure counters, worn bright on one edge from forty years of his thumb. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth, the single page she allowed herself rarely: the list, in his hand, of the men from the print guilds who had been taken in the first purges, seven years ago, when she was seventeen and already wearing his waistcoats and answering to his name. Nine names. Two of them had taught her to set type. One of them, Henrik Voss, had held the ladder for her the first time she climbed to fix the sign, and had called out *mind the letters, little cutter,* and had been gone within the year, and no grave, no record, nothing but that list in her father's careful hand.

She read the names. All of them. She let herself remember Henrik's voice, and old Marta's ferry bell, and the way her father had stopped humming after the purges and never started again, and she waited.

Nothing came. Her eyes stayed dry and stubborn, traitors in the other direction now, loyal to composure when she needed surrender. She tried anger instead — the requisition notices nailed to every door in town, her own type stamped ARMY PROPERTY in someone else's ink — and got heat in her throat but no tears; anger dried her further. She tried fear, and fear only sharpened her, sat her straighter, made her more Bram than ever.

It was the hands that broke her, in the end. She had been practicing forgetting them — that was the last discipline, the strangest one — holding them in her lap and trying not to know them. Because Bram Winterbourne's hands were known. They were scarred across both thumbs where hot metal had spat, and the left forefinger carried a permanent gray stain in the nail bed from decades — hers and her father's together, indistinguishable — of handling lead. Any soldier who had ever shaken the hand of a printer would know those hands for what they were. So she had to stop knowing them: hold them loose and open in her lap like a woman's hands, soft-palmed, unmarked, hands that had done laundry and bread and mending, nothing else.

And looking down at them in the lamplight — at the scars she was trying not to see, the stain she was trying to un-own — she thought of her father's hands doing exactly this same exercise somewhere in this same room, folding himself away layer by layer into a man who could carry a ledger full of names out of a burning city someday, and the grief arrived all at once, whole, unbidden, enormous.

She wept the way she imagined Grethe would: badly, helplessly, with her whole body, the ugly crying that has no audience and therefore no shame. She wept for her father and Henrik Voss and the sign she might never climb again, and she let it run until her face was swollen and her nose streamed, and then — this was the discipline, this was the craft — while the tears were still wet on her cheeks, she stood, took up the rag-weighted case, and walked the gallery.

Forty paces. Weeping. Slow. Low.

Because that was the truth of it: at the checkpoint, if the moment came, she would not get to choose between grief and walking. She would have to do both at once, with soldiers watching, and do them convincingly enough to live.

She walked until the crying ran dry on its own, which took a long time, and then she discovered the trick of it — the trick she wrote nowhere, told no one, kept in the locked room behind her eyes where her father's secrets lived. The trick was not to summon the grief. The trick was to keep a door open to it, always, and simply decide, moment to moment, whether to stand in the doorway. Grief was not a performance. It was a country she already lived in. All she had to do was stop pretending she lived anywhere else.

That was the night she understood why Oskar had said *frightens them.* A woman weeping like that frightened soldiers because it reminded them that everyone they had ever taken had someone who could weep like that. It was the one weapon the powerless carried that the powerful had no defense against, and it cost everything, every time, and it never missed.

* * *

On the fifth day she practiced forgetting her own history, and this was where the work turned genuinely strange.

She sat in the office with a slate and chalk — chalk, not pen, because chalk wiped clean — and interrogated herself aloud like a clerk deposing a witness. Where was Grethe Halvard born?

*Kesselmark.*

Where does she live now?

*Vellmarch. No —* chalk squeaked, erased — *she lived in Vellmarch. She is leaving Vellmarch. A widow cannot afford the town anymore. She goes east to her husband's people, the Halvards, outside Tarnow, who will take her in because she brings the typewriter, which was his pride, though it barely works, and she cannot sell it because selling it would be selling him.*

Why the relatives and not stay?

*Because staying means remembering, and remembering costs money she doesn't have.* Bram heard the sentence come out of her own mouth and paused, struck by it. It was a good answer. It was a true-shaped lie. Somewhere inside it, her actual life was folded up small, like a letter sealed inside another letter.

She drilled the husband. Aldric Halvard, a shipping clerk, dead of the lung fever fourteen months ago, gentle with figures, bad with people, owner of a typewriter he'd bought secondhand in better years and repaired obsessively though it never worked right. She invented details and then, crucially, she *forgot* them on purpose — practiced letting questions land where she had no prepared answer, because Oskar had warned her: *the dangerous moment is never the question you expected. It's the idle one. What did he do? How long married? Did you have children?* No children. That answer had to come without hesitation and without bitterness, and bitterness was the hard part, because Grethe should ache a little there, and ache was easy for Bram to supply, and supplying too much was its own tell.

By afternoon she could answer twenty questions in Grethe's voice — higher, slower, with a Kesselmark lilt she stole from memory of a wool merchant who used to visit the foundry — without a single pause longer than a natural breath.

Then Sergeant Duvall's patrol went past the foundry windows on their evening circuit, boots striking cobbles in that deliberate rhythm the army used to remind the town it was occupied, and Bram, sitting in the shadows with her slate, heard herself think, in Grethe's soft accent: *don't look up, they never trouble women with carts,* and caught herself, cold all over, realizing she hadn't translated. The thought had simply arrived in the other woman's grammar, unbidden, the way a word arrives in a language you've dreamed in.

She was losing the borders between herself and the invention. She sat very still and checked, the way her father checked a freshly cast sort against the master pattern — running her thumb along the edges of who she was. Bram Winterbourne, daughter of Aldous, cutter of letters, keeper of a ledger sewn into a typewriter case buried under the coal cellar floor. Still there. Still distinct. But thinner at the edges than she had been five days ago, worn smooth in places, like type that had printed ten thousand impressions and begun to lose its serifs.

*That's the point,* she told the dark office. *That's the entire point.*

But her father's voice, or her memory of it, answered from the chair behind her: *every impression wears the letter, girl. Cut deep enough and it survives the wearing. Cut shallow and it vanishes.*

She spent the rest of that night not rehearsing. She sat at the type cases and cut letters — real ones, honest work, a job for a chandler's shop sign that she'd promised before the requisition froze everything — and she cut them deep, and she let her hands remember themselves fully, scarred and stained and sure, because if she was going to spend tomorrow being no one, tonight she would be someone, completely, one last time in a while.

* * *

On the sixth evening, everything assembled.

Shawl — her mother's, dyed dull brown. Dress — borrowed from Widow Anschel two streets over, who owed the foundry three years of printing and asked no questions beyond one look at Bram's face, which had apparently answered them. Boots, cracked, half a size too large, stuffed with wool at the toe. Hair bound up and dusted with ash-gray powder until the young widow Grethe seemed older, tireder, a woman the fever had marked. The typewriter itself — the battered case, hollowed, lined, loaded — packed with a few of her father's shirts on top of the oilcloth-wrapped ledger so the weight sat honestly, and a genuine broken typewriter, rusted and worthless, bolted into the lid above the false bottom so that any casual opening revealed exactly the useless machine the story promised. Her father would have appreciated the engineering of it: a machine that contained a machine, evidence nested inside decoy.

She put it all on in the office, piece by piece, watching the liar's mirror take Bram apart.

The last piece was the name. She stood in the gray light with her hand on the door latch and said it once, quietly, the way her father used to test a finished letter against his thumbnail before releasing it to the case.

"Grethe Halvard."

It fit now. That was the terrible part. It fit like her own skin fit, and she knew that by the time she reached the grain gate it would fit better, and by the border it might fit so well she'd have to claw her way back out of it — and that was tomorrow's problem, next week's problem, a problem that existed only if she lived.

She picked up the case. The strap settled across her chest into the groove five days of walking had worn there, and her shoulders dropped into the widow's curve without being asked, and her chin dipped, and her pace slowed, and the woman who descended the foundry stairs and slipped out through the yard gate into the blue dusk was almost no one at all.

Almost.

She had planned to practice the route to the grain gate only — walk it once as Grethe, see the checkpoint's rhythms from the outside, learn the sentries' shift change — and she was halfway down Tanner's Row, anonymous among the evening market stragglers, when she saw the lanterns.

Soldiers at her own corner. Soldiers at the mouth of Printers' Lane. A wagon backed up to the foundry district with its tailgate down, and Sergeant Duvall — she knew him even in shadow, by the way he held his lantern high and patient, a man who liked to watch things — standing at the foundry door with a sheet of paper in his hand, reading it by lamplight, reading it twice.

Fresh ink, they'd want to know about. Fresh ink where no work should be done. Her stomach went cold and steady, the way it went before a pour. The chandler's sign. The letters she'd cut last night to remember herself by — still drying in the case upstairs, still smelling of her, still bearing the deep, unmistakable bite of Winterbourne steel.

Behind her, unseen, the woman who was almost no one began, very quietly, to rehearse being afraid.
