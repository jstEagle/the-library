## 9. Elias Asks a Question

The requisition office had been a bakery, once. You could still smell it under the army's smell of boot leather and lamp oil — yeast and warm stone, baked into the walls the way grief gets baked into a town. Corporal Voss had hung the requisition ledger on a nail where the price list used to be, and every morning Elias Rehn sat beneath it at a table scarred by flour knives and listened to Vellmarch lie to him.

He was good at hearing lies. That was the trouble with him, his superiors had said, in the tone men use when they mean to flatter and condemn in the same breath. Rehn hears everything. It had made him valuable in the provincial archives, where he'd spent six years cross-indexing grain shipments against harvest reports until three magistrates went to prison on his arithmetic alone. It made him useless as a soldier and dangerous as a clerk, which was why Colonel Hessler had brought him along to Vellmarch — a man who could find anything in paper was a man you wanted finding things for you rather than against you.

So they gave him the town's records to hunt.

"Missing," Voss said, tapping the inventory list with one thick finger. "That's what Hessler wants understood. Not destroyed, not hidden — missing. The parish registers from Saint Aldhelm's, gone. The guild rolls from the cooper's hall, gone. Half the notary files from the courthouse cellar, gone. Somebody emptied this town of its memory before we ever crossed the bridge."

"Somebody always does," Elias said. He said it mildly, because mildness was a tool he kept sharpened. "Show me what's left."

What was left was enough to work with. Elias spent two days among the surviving papers, and the absence spoke to him the way a hole in a coat speaks — not by what remained but by the shape of what didn't. Whole years cut out of the parish registers, cleanly, with a razor: 1841 through 1846, the famine years, the years when men had starved in ditches while the granary ships sailed past them downriver. Whoever had taken the pages hadn't torn them in panic. They had cut them with care, the way a type cutter lifts a finished letter from its matrix.

*Type cutter.* The thought arrived uninvited and sat down across from him like a guest he hadn't asked for.

He pushed it away and went into the streets.

* * *

He interviewed eleven people the first day and learned almost nothing, except that Vellmarch lied the way other towns breathed — constantly, involuntarily, without shame. The ferrywoman Marta Skold told him she couldn't recall whether her husband had ever owned a press (her hands were stained with printer's ink to the second knuckle; she saw him notice and did not trouble to hide it, which was either innocence or insolence so deep it had curdled into art). The schoolmaster wept about his dead wife for twenty minutes and answered nothing at all. A boy of perhaps fifteen swore on his mother's grave that he had never seen a pamphlet in his life, then spat after Elias turned his back, and Elias heard it land.

They all knew why he was asking. The requisition orders posted on every door had been explicit: *all presses, paper, type, and records are property of the Army of the Interior.* And everyone in Vellmarch understood what the army wanted with records. Records were how you found names. Names were how you filled the lists that Colonel Hessler reviewed each evening over cold meat, tapping his ring against the tabletop.

Elias had written those requisition orders. His signature was on them — neat, upright, the handwriting of a man taught penmanship by a father who believed a hand revealed a soul. He had signed without reading past the third clause. Or rather: he had read all of it, and signed anyway, because refusing would have cost him his post and signing had cost him only something he'd assumed he could live without.

He was beginning to learn the exact weight of that something now, walking Vellmarch's crooked streets in a uniform that made children stop playing and go quiet.

On the second afternoon he came to Winterbourne's type foundry.

* * *

It stood on Foundry Lane like a held breath. From outside it looked shut — shutters drawn, no smoke from the forge chimney, dust on the doorstep in a way that suggested careful, deliberate neglect. But the requisition inventory listed it as operating, and inventories rarely lied; people lied to protect their inventions, but paper told the truth about itself if you knew how to listen.

He knocked. Waited. Knocked again, longer.

Footsteps above — quick, then deliberately slowed. A pause long enough for someone to arrange a face. Then the door opened on a young man with ink to his elbows and lamplight still in his eyes, though the sun stood high over the lane.

"Bram Winterbourne," the young man said. "You'll be wanting the inventory. I've got it ready. Everything's accounted for."

Everything accounted for, offered before any question was asked. Elias noted it, filed it, let it pass. "Corporal Rehn. Army of the Interior. I'm conducting interviews regarding missing municipal records." He showed his papers, watched the young man read them with more attention than a foundryman needed to give a clerk's commission. "May I come in?"

"I suppose the army doesn't wait on doorsteps."

"The army waits wherever waiting is useful." Elias stepped inside.

The foundry swallowed him. It was dimmer than it should have been at midday, every shutter latched, the great casting furnace cold but not yet cold enough — there was warmth standing in the room, the animal warmth of metal worked recently. Racks of matrices climbed the walls to the rafters, thousands of little brass letters sleeping in their rows, and the smell was of hot lead and machine oil and, underneath it, faintly, of fresh ink.

Fresh ink. In a foundry that was closed.

"You've stopped production," Elias said, moving slowly along the racks.

"Since the requisition. As required." Bram Winterbourne stood by the workbench with his arms folded, and Elias noticed the way he stood — weight even, chin level, eyes never quite settling on Elias's face but never quite leaving it either. A man performing calm the way an actor performs grief: accurately, and from the outside.

"And before the requisition?"

"Pamphlets. Bills of sale. Obituaries." A pause, half a heartbeat too long. "The usual trade."

"Obituaries," Elias repeated. "There must have been many, this last year."

"There are always many." Something moved behind the young man's eyes — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Grief, maybe, worn smooth by handling. "People die. We print their names. That's most of what a country town needs from a foundry, when you get down to it. Names, and the dates under them."

Elias walked to the composing stone and looked at it without touching. Scrubbed clean, but the wood was darkened in a rectangle the size of a broadsheet page, darkened by oil from hundreds of pages laid and locked. He looked at the quads and furniture in their cases, counted the spaces where sorts were missing — whole runs of certain letters depleted. E, N, R, S. The letters you'd need for words like *requisition*, *resistance*, *remember*.

He had interviewed eleven liars in two days. None of them had frightened him. This one frightened him, and he took a moment to understand why, and understood: it was because Bram Winterbourne wasn't lying about the things that mattered. The obituaries were real. The names were real. Only the shape of the life around them was false — a shell built carefully around something true, the way a decoy duck is built around nothing at all.

"Your father," Elias said. "Aldous Winterbourne. The inventory lists him as master of the works."

"My father is dead. Eleven months now."

"So the record says. And you took over."

"Somebody had to."

"Of course." Elias let the silence do its work. Silence was the best question he owned; it made people reach for things to fill it. Bram Winterbourne reached for a rag and began wiping a bench that was already clean. "Tell me about the parish registers," Elias said at last. "Saint Aldhelm's. Pages removed from the famine years. You'd have printed memorial cards for some of those families. Perhaps you remember who ordered them."

"Why would I remember?"

"Because you're the sort of man who remembers." Elias turned and looked at him directly. "You have your father's hands. I've seen them in the guild rolls — Aldous Winterbourne, master cutter, admitted 1819. The guild photographed nothing in those days, but they described. 'Hands notable for steadiness.' Yours are steady now. Steadier than a grieving son's ought to be, standing in his father's shop talking to a man who can have him arrested before supper."

For the first time, something flickered and nearly broke the surface. Elias watched it happen and watched it be mastered, and thought: *there. There is the person. Under the son.*

"I don't know what you want me to say," Bram said quietly.

"I want you to answer questions precisely. Precision is my trade." He drew out his notebook, and as he opened it he asked — lightly, the way a man asks about weather — "One thing puzzles me. The requisition order requires all working presses within the walls to surrender their output records for the past twelve months. Output records, Mr. Winterbourne. Every job, dated, initialed. Where are yours?"

"They were lost," Bram said. "When my father died. Papers got scattered. I'm a type man, not a record keeper."

"Scattered where?"

"Around."

And there it was. Elias felt it land in the room between them, soft as a dropped stitch: *around*. Not lost, not burned, not stolen — *around*, which was the word a man chose when he knew exactly where and could not bring himself to name the place. Elias wrote nothing down. He looked up instead, and Bram Winterbourne was looking back at him, and for the length of four heartbeats neither of them pretended anymore.

It was a strange communion. Elias had known it once before, years ago, in an archive corridor, facing another clerk who had falsified a shipping manifest to keep grain from reaching a garrison that would use it to choke a striking district. They had recognized each other in the space of a glance — two men doing wrong for reasons the paper could not record — and had parted without a word, and the other man had later been shot against the wall of his own warehouse. Recognition, Elias had learned, was worth nothing. It changed nothing. It only meant you knew exactly whom you were betraying.

"You should understand something," Elias said, and heard his own voice drop below the register the room's corners might carry upward. "I am going to write in my report that the Winterbourne foundry shows no evidence of irregular activity. I am going to write that the son is a dull tradesman with poor memory and less education. That is what my report will say regardless of what happens in this room in the next five minutes. What happens in this room determines only whether I write it believing it."

Bram had gone very still. The rag had stopped moving.

"Why?" Bram asked. One syllable, stripped bare.

Because I signed the orders, Elias did not say. Because somewhere in this town there is a ledger with names in it, and I know what happens to towns whose names get taken, because I have processed the paperwork of three of them already, and the paperwork always comes back across my desk eventually, stamped and filed, and I have started dreaming about the stamps. Because my father taught me that a hand reveals a soul, and mine has been revealing one for six years that I cannot bear to look at.

"Because I ask precise questions," he said instead, "and I dislike receiving imprecise answers from people who are worse liars than they think they are. If you were a good liar, Mr. Winterbourne, I'd have arrested you by now and slept well. As it is—" He closed the notebook. "As it is, I'm going to walk out of here, and you're going to remain a dull tradesman, and we are both going to hope that Sergeant Duvall is half as clever as I suspect he is, rather than twice. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you."

"One more thing." Elias paused at the door, hand on the latch, and asked the question he had carried in since he'd first seen the depleted letter cases — the one question too precise, too knowing, the kind of question that could only come from a man who had already guessed. He asked it because he could not stop himself, and he knew, even as it left his mouth, that he would regret it.

"The typewriter case in your cellar," he said. "Is it your father's?"

The silence afterward lasted longer than any silence he had ever measured. Outside, a patrol went by on the cobbles, boots keeping time. Somewhere a woman was calling a child home to supper. And in the dim foundry, Bram Winterbourne stood with his father's steady hands hanging at his sides and said, in a voice that had finally stopped pretending:

"What typewriter case?"

Elias nodded slowly, as if the answer satisfied him. Both of them understood it satisfied nothing.

"Good day, Mr. Winterbourne," he said, and stepped out into the light.

* * *

He walked the length of Foundry Lane without looking back, which cost him more than the climb up the mountain pass would cost him later, though he didn't know that yet. At the corner he stopped, leaned against the wall of the cooper's hall, and opened his notebook, and wrote, in his father's careful upright hand:

*Winterbourne foundry: no irregularity. Son a dull tradesman, memory poor, education less. Recommend no further inquiry.*

Then, beneath it, smaller, in the shorthand he used only for himself, the truth that the report existed to bury:

*The case matters. Find out why before Duvall finds it first.*

He stood there a while, listening to the town breathe its lies around him. Down the lane, a shutter moved — just slightly, at the foundry's upper window, the way a curtain moves when someone steps back from having watched you go. Elias did not turn his head. He closed the notebook, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where he kept everything he could not file, and went to write his report.

By nightfall Sergeant Duvall had read it. By midnight Duvall had sent a man to watch the foundry's back entrance, and had begun, in his methodical way, a list of his own.
