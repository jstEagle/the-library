## 10. Oskar's Price

Oskar Vey had learned to measure his life in the sounds a press made.

A healthy press sang — the platen kissing the type, the paper breathing against it, the treadle keeping time like a patient heart. A dying press rattled. A frightened press went silent. And a press that had been stopped by soldiers made a particular sound when they pried it apart: a wet, reluctant tearing, like a man being woken from a dream he was not permitted to finish.

He had heard that sound four times now. Once in the capital, twice in the river towns south of Vellmarch, and once here, two winters ago, when they took Petrik's shop on Tanner's Row and dumped the type into the canal. He could still see the letters going under — hundreds of them, thousands, every name and grievance the town had ever set down, sinking in silver shoals through the green water while the soldiers stood on the bank and smoked.

He had stood on that bank too. That was the part no one knew. Not standing as a prisoner. Standing as a witness who had been invited to watch, because watching was part of the price.

Now, in the back room of the chandler's shop where he slept among barrels of tallow, Oskar sat with a candle stub between his knees and tried to write the letter he would never send. The tallow smell got into everything — his coat, his hair, his dreams. In the capital they had kept him in a room that smelled of lye. He had decided, somewhere in those eleven days, that if he ever came out of it alive he would never again complain about any smell, any cold, any hunger that did not come with a man asking him questions on the other side of it.

The letter was to himself, which was the only kind of honesty left available to him. *You traded fourteen names for one. You have spent six years pretending the arithmetic was forced. It was not. You could have taken the cell. You could have taken whatever they do in the cells. You chose the names.*

He burned the letter over the candle flame and watched the words curl black, because burning them was also part of the ritual, and ritual was all the penance he could afford.

* * *

Bram Winterbourne came to him at dusk the next evening, moving through the chandler's yard the way she moved everywhere — quickly, quietly, hood up, a figure shaped like an apology for taking up space. Even now, knowing what he knew about the foundry, about the lamplight that burned past midnight behind shuttered windows, Oskar found the disguise complete in a way that unsettled him. There was no Aldous's daughter in the set of those shoulders. There was only the son who did not exist, wearing his father's dead name like a coat cut for a taller man.

"You look terrible," Bram said, by way of greeting.

"I sleep next to fat," Oskar said. "It seeps into you." He tipped his head toward the back room. "Come in before someone sees you talking to nothing."

Inside, among the barrels, Bram pulled back her hood and became, for a moment, someone else again — not the son, not quite the daughter either, but the tired person underneath both, twenty-six years old with ink stains at the knuckles that no amount of pumice ever fully removed. Oskar had watched her father scrub at those same stains thirty years ago, sitting across a table much like this one, arguing about the price of paper.

"Tell me the route is ready," Bram said.

"The route is ready."

"Then tell me something else, because you've been avoiding me for four days, and Oskar Vey has never once in his life avoided a conversation unless the conversation was going to cost him something."

He should have lied. He was good at lying; it had kept him alive through three purges and one arrest, and it would keep him alive through whatever came after Hessler sealed the town. But Bram was looking at him with Aldous's eyes — the old man had passed them down whole, unaltered, along with the hands — and there was a limit to how long a man could lie into those eyes before the lie started costing more than the truth.

So Oskar sat down on a barrel of tallow and told her.

"There's a purge I haven't talked about," he said. "Eleven years ago. Before your father died. Before the ledger, even — before anyone thought a ledger was worth making." He turned the candle stub in his fingers. "In the capital. They swept every print shop on the east bank in one night. Forty-one of us in custody inside a week. I was twenty-four."

"And?"

"And they didn't want us all," Oskar said. "That's the thing nobody understands about purges. They're not exterminations. They're harvests. They want a few names to prosecute so the newspapers have something to print, and they want the rest to go home and be grateful and quiet forever. So on the fourth day, a captain came to my cell and put a piece of paper on the cot and said: fourteen names. Fourteen people still printing, still organizing, still passing documents. Give us fourteen and you walk out tomorrow."

Bram said nothing. The tallow barrels ticked as they cooled.

"I told myself I'd give them names they already had," Oskar went on. "Dead men. Men already fled across the border. I sat there all night trying to construct a list of ghosts." His laugh came out wrong, a dry click. "But they check. Of course they check. A ghost on the list is worth nothing, and they'd know I was playing games, and then I'd be worth nothing too. So I gave them fourteen living names. Fourteen people whose faces I knew. Two of them were hanged. One was Petrik — you remember Petrik's shop, the type in the canal? No. You wouldn't. You'd have been fifteen. They let me stand on the bank and watch that one. I think it was meant as a courtesy."

"They let you watch," Bram repeated. Her voice was very flat, which Oskar knew from years of reading printers' proofs was the flattest and therefore most dangerous tone a voice could take. "And the others. The resistance. Did they know?"

"Not then. They knew within the year. Word travels. I came south, changed my name twice, started over small. Your father—" He stopped. Started again. "Your father knew. Aldous figured it out before anyone. He never asked me directly. He just stopped leaving me alone with anything important, and he never once said why, and he kept working with me anyway. For nine years. I've thought about that longer than I've thought about the fourteen. I don't understand it. I'm not sure I want to."

"What does this have to do with the ledger?"

"Everything." Oskar leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and made himself hold her eyes, because the rest would be cowardice otherwise. "A ledger of the disappeared isn't just names of victims. To make it mean anything — to make it evidence instead of grief — it has to show the machinery. Who signed what. Who informed. Who was released when everyone else was taken." He tapped his own chest once. "Page sixty-one, Bram. My confession. Written out in full, in my own hand, dated, signed, witnessed by your father and Marta of the ferry. I gave it to him four years ago because I believed — God help me, I believed — that a record that spares no one is the only record that can't be called a lie. I wanted my fourteen in it. I wanted them printed. I wanted to stand in a courtroom someday and hear a clerk read my name off the same page as theirs."

"And now?"

"Now Hessler holds the town," Oskar said, "and the ledger sits in a typewriter case in your cellar, and every day it stays hidden it gets heavier, and I lie awake doing arithmetic." He spread his hands. "Here's the arithmetic. If it reaches the foreign press office, it prints. If it prints, my page prints. My confession, my signature, my fourteen names — including the fact that I named them, which makes me not a victim of this regime but one of its instruments. Do you know what happens to informers when a regime falls, Bram? It isn't courts. Courts are slow and merciful and mostly fictional. What happens is a crowd, and a lamp post, and somebody shouting that this man sold Petrik, and nobody asking whether the man who's shouting sold someone worse."

"So you want it destroyed."

The words came out of her without heat, and somehow that was worse than heat. Oskar nodded.

"Burn it. Melt it down. Drop the case in the canal with everything sewn in its lining. I'll help you carry it. I'll stand on the bank and watch it sink, and this time I'll deserve to watch." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, the crack, the visible seam in him. "One book, Bram. One fire. And I get to stop being page sixty-one."

* * *

They argued until the candle burned down to a coin of wax and Oskar had to light another from the stove, and the argument circled the same ground so many times that the ground began to wear smooth, like a stair trodden by pilgrims.

"It's not yours to burn," Bram said, for the fourth time. "Three hundred and forty names, Oskar. Wives who don't know if they're widows. Mothers writing letters to sons who were taken off the street two years ago. You want to unprint them so you can sleep."

"I want them *printed*," Oskar shot back. "Don't you dare dress this up as my cowardice alone. Think it through. Think it all the way through, since you're the one who'll be carrying it. Three checkpoints. An army requisitioned the entire town to find exactly this document. Duvall is already sniffing at your foundry — yes, I know about the ink, don't make that face, I hear things. You're going to walk a ledger of every name the army made disappear through three checkpoints guarded by the men who made them disappear, and if they catch you, they don't just hang you. They hang the ledger. Publicly. With commentary. Every name read aloud as a traitor's roster, every widow on that list reclassified as the family of a convicted enemy. You won't deliver those people to memory. You'll deliver them to the firing squad a second time."

"And if we burn it," Bram said, "they stay disappeared. Officially. Forever. The registry burns, the graves go unmarked, and in twenty years some clerk writes 'no records survive' and that becomes the history." She stood up, and in the low room among the tallow she seemed suddenly much larger than the son who did not exist, larger than the daughter, some third thing entirely. "Your page is sixty-one. There are three hundred and thirty-nine other pages. You're asking me to drown them so you don't have to read yours."

"Yes," Oskar said simply. "I am. That's what I'm telling you. That's the whole ugly confession, and you can add it to the ledger if you like — *Oskar Vey, in the year of the requisition, begged a young woman to destroy the only honest book in Vellmarch because his name was in it.* Put it on page sixty-two. At least then I'll be consistent."

Bram looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, sharp, unwilling — and the laugh broke something open in the room, some pressure that had been building since she'd walked in.

"You're impossible," she said. "You're a coward and an honest man in the same body, and I don't know how you fit."

"Neither do I. It's crowded in here." He rubbed his face. "Bram. Listen to me. I'm not going to steal it. I'm not going to sell it — I know you've wondered, these last four days, and I'd have wondered too. I'm telling you to your face because it's the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit: I will not betray this. But I'm asking you, before you commit to carrying it, to sit down and read the whole thing. Cover to cover. Including page sixty-one. Because right now you think you're carrying a lantern. I need you to know exactly how much of it is ash before you decide what it's worth."

"Why?" she said. "Why does it matter to you what I know?"

"Because if you carry it," Oskar said, "you carry me. Page sixty-one walks beside you through every checkpoint. And I need to know that when you reach the border — if you reach it — you'll have read my name in your father's hand and decided I was worth the walking." He looked away, at the wall of barrels, at nothing. "Your father decided. Nine years, he decided yes, every single morning, without ever saying a word to me about it. I never earned an explanation. Maybe I'm owed one less than nothing. But you — you're about to risk your life for a book with my rot in the middle of it, and I'd at least like you to do it with your eyes open."

Silence settled over the chandler's back room, thick as the smell of tallow. Somewhere out in the yard a dog barked twice and thought better of it. From the direction of the square came the faint rhythmic crunch of boots — the night drill, Hessler's men, marching in circles around a town they already owned.

Bram picked up her hood, then set it down again without putting it on.

"All right," she said. "I'll read it. All of it. Page sixty-one included." She held up a finger before relief could move anywhere near his face. "But hear my terms, Oskar, because I'm only going to say this once. I will read it, and then I will decide. Not you. Not your fear, not your lamp post, not your arithmetic. Me. And if I decide it goes to the presses — with your confession in it, your signature, your fourteen names, all of it — then you will help me plan the route with everything you have, and you will not sabotage it, and you will stand wherever I tell you to stand when the pages come off the press, and you will *watch*. The way they made you watch the canal. Except this time it will be your own name coming up out of the water, clean."

Oskar stared at her. Somewhere beneath the terror, beneath the six years of managed silence, something in him that he had assumed was dead turned over in its sleep.

"That's a cruel bargain," he said.

"It's the only one on the table."

He thought about the cell in the capital. Eleven days. Lye-smell and questions. He thought about Petrik's type sinking silver through the green water, and the courtesy of being allowed to watch. He thought about Aldous Winterbourne saying nothing, for nine years, saying nothing every morning, which had been the loudest thing anyone had ever said to him.

"All right," Oskar said. "Read it. Decide." He almost smiled, and the almost hurt more than a real one would have. "But do it soon. Because there's something else, and you're not going to like the timing."

Bram went still. "What else?"

"Duvall requested the foundry's production records today," Oskar said. "Officially, it's routine — inventory for the requisition. Unofficially, my contact at the quartermaster's office says he's asking specific questions. Ink yields. Hours worked. Whether anyone's seen the son." He met her eyes. "The grain gate is our best way out, and the best window closes in three days. Whatever you're going to read, Bram — read it tonight."
