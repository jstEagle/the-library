## 12. Duvall Closes In

Sergeant Duvall had a theory about ink.

Most men in the occupation thought of ink as nothing — a wetness, a stain, something that dried and was forgotten. Duvall had spent eleven years in the army's records bureau before Hessler's requisition gave him a town to administer, and eleven years among files taught him that ink was the most honest thing in the world. It could lie on the page, but it could not lie about itself. Every batch carried its signature: its mineral weight, its bite into paper, its color under lamplight when you tilted the sheet just so. Two documents printed from the same press were brothers. They might wear different names, but they shared a face, and Duvall had learned to see faces.

So when Colonel Hessler's adjutant dropped the stack of confiscated pamphlets on his desk with the bored instruction to *catalogue these*, Duvall did not catalogue them. He interrogated them.

He worked at night, in the requisitioned schoolhouse that now served as the garrison's administrative office, because the light there was good — north-facing windows, no glare — and because at night he could lay out forty sheets of seditious print across six desks without some lieutenant asking why a paperwork man cared so much about paper. He worked by lamp and by loupe, an old jeweler's glass he'd bought off a pawnbroker in the capital for less than the cost of his boots, and he read the pamphlets the way other men read faces at checkpoints: not for what they said, but for what they couldn't hide.

*The Vellmarch Voice*, they called themselves. No imprint, no address, which was itself a confession — legal presses stamped their names like proud fathers. The pamphlets were well-set, too. Not amateur work. The letters sat square on their baselines, the spacing breathed evenly, the ink pulled clean from the forme without the ghosting you got from tired rollers or a hurried hand. Whoever printed these knew type the way a farrier knows horses.

Duvall tilted a sheet toward the lamp. The black held a faint warmth, a brown undertone at the edges of each stroke where the ink had spread a hair's breadth into the fiber. He made a small note. He turned to the second sheet, printed weeks earlier by the look of it, and found the same brown undertone, the same spread, the same everything.

Same press. Same ink batch. Same hands.

He began a ledger of his own, separate from the official one — a habit the records bureau had beaten into him and the war had never quite knocked loose. *Sheet one: 14 October. Sheet two: undated, water damage lower left corner. Sheet three...* And beside each entry, the measurements. Width of the em-quads used between paragraphs. Depth of impression, judged by how far the ink had bitten through to be felt with a fingernail. The particular slanted flaw in one lowercase *r* that appeared on every sheet — a piece of type with a chipped shoulder, still in use, meaning the printer either couldn't afford to replace it or didn't care to, and both possibilities told Duvall something.

By the third night he had what he wanted. Not a name. A place.

The chipped *r* was the key. A printer who set forty pages of incendiary prose would use hundreds of *r*s, and the chip appeared in exactly one position every time — the same physical piece of metal, drawn again and again from the same case, in the same job case, in the same room. Duvall had spent two evenings with the town's property rolls, cross-referencing the addresses of licensed print shops against the seizure lists Hessler's men had already compiled, and the licensed shops were all accounted for. Their type was locked in army storehouses. Which meant the *Voice* came from somewhere unlicensed, somewhere old, somewhere with its own stock of type — and there was precisely one such establishment left in Vellmarch.

Winterbourne Type Foundry. Foundry Lane, by the tannery cut.

Duvall sat back and looked at the name for a long time.

Everyone in the administration knew of Winterbourne, the way everyone knew of a landmark — the elder Winterbourne had cut type for half the official printers in the province, back when there had been such a thing as official printers with anything like independence. Aldous Winterbourne had been dead, what, two years? Three? Duvall checked the death registry himself, walking down to the town hall through streets gone quiet and watchful since the requisition. Dead three years, recorded properly, witnessed properly. And the foundry passed to —

He stopped at the clerk's desk, where Elias Rehn was stamping inventory manifests with the mechanical diligence of a man trying to disappear into his own paperwork.

"The Winterbourne file," Duvall said. "Requisition inventory. I want to see what was taken."

Rehn's stamp paused — only a heartbeat, but Duvall noticed heartbeats; noticing them was his trade. "Foundry inventory," Rehn said, and pulled the folder without being asked twice. "Type stock, molds, the press — no press actually, just casting equipment. Paper. Ink." He laid it open and turned it around. "Ink," he repeated, and something flickered behind his eyes that Duvall chose, for the moment, not to name.

"Who processed this?"

"I did." Rehn's voice was level. Too level. "Standard walk-through. The son showed us the premises."

"The son." Duvall looked at the inventory. Signed at the bottom in a careful hand: *Bram Winterbourne, heir and proprietor.* The signature was confident, unhesitating — no tremor of a frightened boy. But then, boys who ran foundries weren't frightened of clerks. "Where is this son now?"

"Running the business, I assume. Someone has to feed the furnaces even if the type is confiscated." Rehn closed the folder. "Why the interest, Sergeant? The pamphlet detail is below your—"

"My detail is whatever the colonel says it is, and the colonel says the *Voice* is mine." Duvall took the folder. Rehn let it go a fraction too easily, and Duvall logged that too, in the private ledger behind his eyes: *the clerk is glad to be rid of it.*

*

He went to the foundry the next morning, alone, out of uniform coat, wearing the plain gray wool of a civilian clerk. It was an old trick and it still worked: people told a soldier what they feared and a civilian what they assumed. He wanted assumptions.

Foundry Lane smelled of tallow and the tannery's acid breath. The building itself leaned over the cut like an old man listening to water, its windows fogged with furnace heat, its sign — *ALDOUS WINTERBOURNE & SON* — freshly painted, the letters too fine for a sign-painter's hand, cut-looking somehow even in paint. Duvall stood across the lane for ten minutes and watched.

Smoke rose from the chimney. That was the first thing. A type foundry whose type had been confiscated had no reason to keep a furnace hot enough to make smoke — melting metal for what? Casting what? Unless the furnace ran for another trade entirely, or unless the smoke was meant to say *nothing to see here, ordinary work, an honest chimney*. Either answer interested him.

The second thing took longer. He waited through the morning, through a delivery boy leaving bread, through a woman from the tannery office crossing to knock and speak briefly at the door. And then, near midday, the door opened and a young man stepped out to barter with a rag-and-bone cart — tall, broad-shouldered, hat low, gloves on despite the mild day, moving with a certain deliberate heaviness, the way a man walks when he has practiced walking as someone else.

Duvall had seen that walk before. In the capital, in the purges, on the occasions when the bureau's files caught up with people who had spent years being no one. It was a specific craft, that walk. It had a grammar. And like all grammar, it could be read by anyone who knew the language.

The young man — Winterbourne's heir, presumably — argued cheerfully with the rag-man about brass weights, laughed once, went inside. The laugh bothered Duvall more than anything else. It was pitched wrong. Half a tone too high. A man laughing in a language he'd learned from books.

He crossed the lane and tried the door. Locked. He knocked, and after a while the door opened on a chain, and one eye looked out at him — dark, quick, assessing, gone before he could read more than its alertness.

"Foundry's shut," said a voice, also pitched carefully. "Army took the type. You'll want the requisition office."

"Just a question about my father's watch," Duvall said mildly. "Aldous Winterbourne repaired watches, didn't he? I have his mark inside an old case."

"He didn't repair watches." The eye didn't blink. "Good day."

The door shut. Duvall stood on the step and smiled at it, and wrote nothing down, because he had already written it all down inside himself: *the heir does not know whether his father repaired watches. The heir hesitated a quarter-second too long. The heir wears gloves indoors-outdoors and walks like a borrowed coat.*

He spent the afternoon in the town hall archives, and this was where the hunt acquired its teeth. Birth registries. School rolls. Militia exemption papers. He pulled every document that had ever required Bram Winterbourne's signature or description, and he laid them out under the archive's dusty lamp and compared them the way he compared inks.

There were four signatures spanning nineteen years. The earliest, a school enrollment at age seven, was a child's careful scrawl. The next, at fourteen, apprentice papers. Then a gap — and here was the interesting part — then nothing until the requisition form, signed three years later at age twenty-two. Between fourteen and twenty-two, Bram Winterbourne had signed nothing. Had appeared nowhere. No militia registration, though the law required it at sixteen. No guild dues. No tax roll appearance. No witness marks, no debts, no marriage banns. Eight years of legal invisibility, ending precisely — Duvall checked the date against the registry — eight days after Aldous Winterbourne's death.

People did not vanish for eight years and reappear with a mature hand and steady nerves. People *changed* for eight years. And whatever changed, changed behind that door on Foundry Lane, behind that furnace smoke, in a household that had taught its child to walk like a borrowed coat.

Duvall copied the four signatures onto a single sheet, aligned them, and studied the letterforms. The schoolboy scrawl and the requisition signature shared a skeleton — the same peculiar loop on the *B*, the same forward lean — separated by years, but recognizably kin. Except. He held the requisition signature to the lamp. The pressure was wrong. Even strokes, top to bottom, no natural variation, the way a person writes when they are drawing a signature rather than signing one. When they are cutting it, almost. Rendering it.

Like type, he thought. Like someone who thought of handwriting as letterforms to be manufactured rather than motion to be spilled.

He sat back in the creaking archive chair and let the whole shape of it assemble in his mind, the way he let ink evidence assemble: not forcing, just tilting the page toward the light until the truth showed its undertone. The Winterbourne heir was a fiction — perhaps a half-fiction, perhaps something stranger — maintained for years, polished daily, walked and laughed and signed into existence by someone very skilled at being no one. And someone that skilled was exactly the kind of someone who could run a clandestine press for months under a garrison's nose.

He was not certain. Certainty was for fanatics and he'd seen what fanatics cost the bureau. But suspicion, documented suspicion, layered and dated and initialed — that was currency the army accepted everywhere.

That evening he wrote the request.

*To Colonel Hessler, commanding, Vellmarch garrison. From Sgt. T. Duvall, intelligence and records. Subject: authorship of seditious material ("The Vellmarch Voice").*

He laid it out in his best bureau hand, each fact in its place like type in a stick. The ink analysis matching the pamphlets to a press using pre-confiscation Winterbourne-foundry type, identifiable by the chipped lowercase *r*. The furnace activity at a foundry stripped of lawful work. The heir's documentary silence from ages fourteen to twenty-two. The anomalies of bearing observed on the premises. He did not write *the heir is a woman*. He had no proof of that and knew better than to spend a guess where a fact was needed; the guess could come later, if the facts obliged. He wrote instead: *the identity of "Bram Winterbourne" appears substantially constructed and merits detention and formal examination.*

He signed it, blotted it, weighed it in his hand a moment. Eleven years of filing other men's orders, and here at last was one going up the ladder with his own name at the foot of it. He felt none of the pride he'd imagined feeling. What he felt was colder and more useful: the particular clarity of a man who has matched two inks and cannot afterward pretend they came from different bottles.

He sealed it. In the morning he would deliver it, and the machinery would begin — warrants, soldiers, a door broken on Foundry Lane, questions asked of a young man with gloved hands. Duvall banked the office stove, put on his coat, and paused at the window. Below, the town lay dark except for one street where a light still burned past curfew hours: Foundry Lane, a thin orange glow behind fogged glass, someone working late over something they should not have had.

*They're printing tonight,* he thought. *Which means tomorrow may be too late.*

He took the request back out of his coat, and beneath the signature added one line, initialing it:

*Request immediate search. Delay risks destruction of evidence.*

Then he folded it away, turned down the lamp, and went to find the officer of the watch, because some hunts do not wait for morning — and because somewhere across the dark town, behind a furnace-lit window, the printer of the *Voice* was at that moment setting type with hands that Duvall was now certain he could describe, stroke by stroke, to any man who asked.

What Duvall could not yet describe — what no document in the archive contained — was the face beneath the borrowed coat. But he had requested the authority to find out, and Colonel Hessler, who needed a traitor the way a fire needs air, would sign it by noon.

By noon tomorrow, then, Foundry Lane. By noon, the Winterbourne heir.

Unless the heir read the wind the way fugitives always eventually did — and was already gone.
