## 13. The Ferryman's Debt

The river came first, before the town, before the names, before Marta herself. Her father had said it like a prayer and a complaint in the same breath: *the river was here when they laid the first stone of Vellmarch, and it will be here when they carry the last one out.* Forty years on the water had taught her the truth of both halves. The river did not care who governed its banks. It only asked to be read — the color of the current, the lean of the reeds, the way gulls sat facing into wind or away from it, each a sentence in a grammar older than any colonel's commission.

Marta Voss had been reading that grammar since she was seven years old, standing at her father's elbow with a pole too heavy for her arms, and she had been reading it alone for eleven years now, since the stroke took old Henrik mid-crossing and left his daughter to bring the ferry in by feel while he died politely in the pilothouse, apologizing with his eyes. She was sixty-one. Her hands were rope. Her back was an argument she lost every morning and won every evening, and she intended to go on winning it until the river itself told her to put down the pole.

She knew every face that rode her deck. That was not sentiment; it was bookkeeping. A ferryman who could not match faces to fares went broke, and worse, went blind. She kept her ledger where other women kept their prayers: folded into the muscle memory of her hands, in the callus on her thumb that counted coins without looking.

And so she noticed, three days after the army sealed the town, that the morning traffic had thinned to almost nothing, and that what remained of it had gone quiet in a way she distrusted deeply. Fear had a smell. It came off people like steam off wet wool. The soldiers at the new checkpoint upriver smelled of nothing at all, which frightened her more.

* * *

She saw the Winterbourne boy on the fourth day.

He came down the towpath just past gray dawn, when the mist still lay on the water like unspooled cotton, and even from forty yards Marta knew something was wrong, because Bram Winterbourne never walked like that. The boy — young man now, though the town still called him *the boy* behind his back, the son who never appeared in daylight, the son nobody seemed able to describe twice the same way — normally moved with the particular economy of a craftsman, shoulders square, hands loose, a man who knew exactly what his body was worth and carried it accordingly. This morning he walked like a man carrying a piano on his back. He kept glancing at the case slung under his arm — battered leather, brass corners green with age — as though it might open and speak.

Marta leaned on her pole and watched him come, and let the mist do its work, and thought about obituaries.

That was the debt. Everyone in Vellmarch owed somebody something, but most debts were measured in coin, and coin was the cheapest thing on the river. Hers was measured in print. Eleven years ago, when Henrik died, there had been no money for a stone — the ferry barely paid for itself those seasons, the harvest had failed twice running, and the mason wanted prices that would have made a bishop blush. Marta had stood in the foundry doorway, hat literally in hand, humiliated down to the marrow, asking Aldous Winterbourne whether a man could be remembered on credit.

Aldous had looked at her over his spectacles for a long moment. Then he'd said, "Sit down, Marta. Tell me about him."

She'd talked for two hours. About Henrik poling through ice so thick it sang. About how he'd once ferried a pregnant woman across in a hailstorm with the whole boat groaning like a damned soul, and delivered the child's name himself because the mother was in no state — little Ansel, born on the water, first of the river-born in a generation. About how he'd taught her to read the current before he'd taught her letters, saying letters were optional but the river was not.

Aldous had cut the type himself. Set it himself. Pulled twelve copies on paper he refused to charge for, and the notice ran in the town gazette under a small black border, and Marta had framed one copy and nailed it inside the pilothouse where the rain couldn't reach, and it hung there still, the ink faded to the brown of old tea: *HENRIK VOSS, FERRYMAN OF VELLMARCH, WHO NEVER ONCE CHARGED A MOURNER.*

The last line had been Aldous's addition. She hadn't said it. He'd known.

And then, because Aldous Winterbourne was the kind of man who did things thoroughly or not at all, he had done the same for half the town. Obituaries on credit. Notices for the drowned and the destitute and the disgraced, set in type and run for nothing, or nearly nothing, or for payment in eggs and firewood and, once, a live goose that terrorized the foundry for a week. People had called it eccentricity. Marta had understood it differently, even then. A type cutter spent his life making letters that belonged to everyone and no one. The obituaries were how he made the letters belong to someone. Every name he set was a name he refused to let the river take without a receipt.

Now the boy was coming toward her boat with a case under his arm and fear steaming off him like wet wool, and Marta Voss did arithmetic the way she always did: quickly, and against her own interest.

* * *

"Morning crossing," Bram said, when he reached the gangplank. His voice cracked in the middle of it, and he covered by coughing.

"It's the middle of the morning and the crossing is closed." Marta didn't move from beside the pole. "Army order. Posted on my own wheelhouse door, which I'll note they nailed up without asking me, like everything else they've done this week."

"I can pay."

"You can pay." She looked him over properly now. The boy was thinner than he'd been in autumn. There was ink — actual printer's ink, black as sin — worked into the creases of his knuckles, the kind of staining that doesn't wash out, the kind you only get pulling press after press after press, hour after hour, in a room with the shutters closed. She filed that away with everything else. "Since when does a type cutter's son have army money?"

"Family savings."

"Your family saved money the way I save rainwater. In a bucket with holes." She let that sit. Downriver, faintly, a patrol boat's engine muttered — the army had requisitioned two of the pleasure launches and mounted a gun on one, which was blasphemy of a very specific kind. "Where's the typewriter going, then? Because I know what's in that case. My brother-in-law hauled one up from the capital for his clerk work. They all look alike, that make. Green corners. You're taking your dead father's typewriter somewhere in a hurry, at dawn, on a closed crossing, and you're bad at pretending otherwise."

Bram went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, Marta thought — not the stillness of innocence caught out, but the stillness of a person deciding, in real time, how much truth a situation could hold.

"Marta Voss," Bram said carefully, "do you remember what my father charged you for Henrik's notice?"

And there it was. The debt, named aloud, held out between them like a lantern.

Marta spat over the side, an old habit, less contempt than punctuation. "I remember," she said. "I've remembered eleven years, every time it rains and I see that paper curling in the frame."

"He never sent the bill."

"There wasn't a bill."

"No," Bram said. "There wasn't."

They looked at each other across the gap of the gangplank, and Marta felt the strange vertigo of watching a debt come due in the wrong currency. Coin she could refuse. Print she could not. Somewhere in a half-ruined foundry on the hill there was a drawer full of type Aldous had cut with his own hands, and every letter of it had been a promise that the people of Vellmarch would not pass through this world unrecorded. If the army took that foundry — and the army took everything now, that was the whole of the new law, posted on every door — then the promises burned with it.

And whatever was in that case, the boy believed it was worth the risk of asking a woman he barely knew to commit a crime.

"Get aboard," Marta said.

* * *

She poled them off the bank with the practiced economy of four decades, and only when the current had them properly — when turning back would cost more than continuing — did she jerk her chin toward the hatch amidships. "Below. There's a space under the ballast boards, aft of the water cask. It smells like bilge and dead fish, because it is bilge and dead fish, and you'll lie in it and you won't move, and if the boards above you creak you'll stop breathing until they don't."

"How long have I got to decide—"

"That wasn't an offer, boy. That was an instruction. Get below."

He hesitated one second longer — and Marta watched him weigh it, watched the calculation move behind his eyes, and thought: *he's not afraid of being caught, he's afraid of being seen,* which was such an odd fear for a fugitive that she turned it over twice before stowing it with the rest. Then he swung himself down through the hatch with the case hugged to his chest like a child, and she heard the ballast boards shift, and settle, and go quiet.

Good. At least he could follow instructions. Half the men in this town couldn't follow a recipe.

She poled on. The mist was burning off, which she disliked — mist was a curtain, sun was a window — but the current was with them and the patrol boat's engine had fallen silent, probably idling at the bend where the channel narrowed. She had maybe twenty minutes of honest river between here and the landing stage on the far shore, and she intended to use them thinking.

Because here was the thing Marta Voss had not said aloud to anyone, including the river, including the framed obituary, including God: she had watched this town's memory being dismantled plank by plank for three years, long before the army came. First the gazette's foreign pages stopped arriving. Then the gazette itself shrank, and shrank, until it was four pages of grain prices and a column of announcements about patriotic festivals. Then the announcements changed too. Names began to disappear from them — not the living names, the dead ones. Men who'd drowned. Women who'd died of fever. Ordinary deaths, ordinary griefs, and yet their notices simply ceased to appear, and when Marta had asked the gazette's last honest typesetter why, he'd looked at her with the flat gray face of a man already halfway gone and said, "Lists get cross-checked now, Marta. Dead men have relatives. Relatives have opinions."

After that she had started keeping her own record. Nothing grand — a school exercise book, bought for two coppers, in which she wrote down, in her rope-handed scrawl, every passenger who crossed and every name she heard spoken of in the past tense. Ansel, born on the water. The Kessling twins, taken by the fever winter. Old Frau Adler, who'd ridden her ferry every market day for thirty years and once paid her fare in buttons. Three years of it. Two hundred names. It lived wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of her water cask, which was either the safest place in Vellmarch or the most obvious one, depending on how well you knew ferrymen.

So when the boy came down the towpath carrying a case full of somebody's records, Marta had recognized a colleague before she'd recognized a fugitive. Whatever else the world was becoming, it was apparently still producing people who wrote names down. That was worth a bilge hole. That was worth considerably more than a bilge hole.

* * *

The trouble came at the narrows, as trouble does.

Not the patrol boat — the engine stayed silent, mercifully, and she slipped the narrows with the current doing half her work. The trouble was human, and it was waiting at the far landing stage: a corporal, young, pink-cheeked, with a requisition clipboard and the terrible earnestness of a man who has recently been given minor authority and is determined to die defending it.

"Ferry traffic's restricted," he announced, before her bow even touched. "Manifest required. Passengers declared."

Marta shipped her pole and regarded him with the expression she used on drunks and inspectors, which was the same expression. "Corporal. I have crossed this river since before your mother met your father. This morning I am carrying six sacks of flour for the miller's widow, one crate of chickens, and myself. Would you like me to declare the chickens individually? They have names. It'll take some time."

The corporal flushed. But he didn't lower the clipboard, and his eyes went past her to the deck, cataloguing, and Marta felt the moment stretch thin as wire. Six sacks of flour. One crate of chickens. And beneath the deck, under the ballast boards, a boy holding his breath in the dark with a dead man's secrets against his chest.

"Regulations say—" the corporal began.

"Regulations also say the flour gets to the widow Bremmer today, because her husband's in the army jail for arguing about a fence post, and if her children eat another day of boiled nettles she'll come down this bank and tell you about it at length, corporal, at *length*, and I will stand here and enjoy it." She stepped forward onto the planks, close enough that he had to choose between stepping back and standing his ground against a woman who smelled of tar and sixty winters. He chose back. "Look at me. Look at this boat. Does this look like a vessel of strategic significance to Colonel Hessler's war?"

Something flickered across the young face then — not defeat exactly, but recognition, the look of a country boy remembering that he had grown up around women like this, that armies were temporary, that *this* was permanent. His eyes dropped to the clipboard. He made a mark.

"Flour, chickens, one ferryman," he recited. "Go on, then. Before Sergeant Duvall's men come down the road. They're stopping everyone today."

"They're stopping everyone every day," Marta said. "That's what makes it a habit instead of a policy."

But the words landed somewhere in her chest and rang wrong, because *Duvall* was a name she'd been hearing more often lately, always attached to the word *foundry*, and she watched the corporal walk away up the road and felt the cold come off the river in a new key. They were stopping everyone. Which meant that somewhere behind them, in the town under the hill, someone had started counting who was missing.

She cast off. The current took the boat. Below her feet, faint as a heartbeat, she heard the ballast boards creak once, and go still.

* * *

Mid-river, she spoke to him through the hatch, low, without looking down, the way you speak to the river.

"Listen. There are two landings on this bank. The public one, where I just was, and a private one half a mile south where the reeds grow tall enough to hide a church. I'm putting in there, not the stage. When we ground, you stay below until I've walked the bank and come back and knocked twice. Not once. Twice. Once means soldiers. Twice means me."

Silence below. Then, muffled: "Thank you, Marta."

"Don't thank me. Thanking me implies I've decided what I think about all this, and I haven't." She poled through a slack stretch, jaw working. "My father used to say the river keeps every secret ever whispered on it, it just charges interest. So understand me, boy — whatever's in that case, whoever's chasing it, I'm not in your war. I'm a ferryman. I move things from one bank to the other. That's the whole of my politics."

Another silence, longer. Then Bram's voice came up through the boards, quiet and level:

"My father never charged you for Henrik's notice. Or for Frau Adler's. Or for the Kessling twins. Or for forty others I found in his ledger — his real ledger, the one with the debts marked *paid in remembrance*. Do you know what he wrote next to your name, Marta?"

Marta poled. The reeds slid past. "Tell me."

"*Paid in full, and I owe her.*"

The pole stopped moving. Just for a breath — half a stroke, the boat yawing gently before her hands corrected without her permission, hands that knew the river better than her heart did. She stared at the far bank, at the reeds rising gold-green out of the haze, and felt eleven years of a framed obituary rearrange themselves into something larger and heavier and warmer than grief.

"Aldous Winterbourne," she said finally, to the river, to the boy below, to the dead man directly, "was a sentimental old fraud, and I hope wherever he is, they're making him set type."

She brought the boat in among the reeds soft as a leaf settling, grounded her on the mud, and walked the bank alone in the brightening morning, listening for boots, hearing only birds and the small industrious sounds of the river eating its breakfast. When she came back she knocked twice on the hull, and the hatch opened, and Bram Winterbourne climbed up into daylight blinking like something exhumed — pale, ink-stained, gripping the case, alive.

"South along the towpath," Marta said, keeping her voice practical because practical was all she had left. "Two hours' walk puts you past the army's sightlines. After that I can't help you, and I don't want to know where you're going, except—" She stopped. Looked at him straight. "Except that you'll need to cross water again before this is finished. Everyone does. The river's the only road the army doesn't control, because they're fools, and because controlling a river is like arresting weather."

"And if I need you again?"

"Then you'll find me," Marta said, "because I'm always here. That's the entire point of me." She thrust a bundle at him — bread, hard cheese, a flask, wrapped in sacking. "And boy. Whatever name you're traveling under this week—" she watched him flinch, and was not sorry, "—make sure it's a name somebody would print an obituary for. That's the test. My father knew it. A name is only worth carrying if someone would refuse to let it sink."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Something passed over the young face — gratitude, yes, but underneath it something harder and stranger, almost like resolve being forged in real time — and then he shouldered the case and stepped off onto the towpath, and was gone into the reeds, walking south, walking like a man carrying a piano, walking like a man carrying a hundred names.

Marta Voss stood in the shallows a while longer, hands on the pole, watching the empty path.

Then she went back aboard, pried up the floorboard behind the wheelhouse, unwrapped the oilcloth, and opened her school exercise book to the first blank page. She dipped her pencil. For three years she had written down only the dead.

Today, for the first time, she wrote down the living: *Crossed the river southbound, dawn, one passenger, carrying names. Owes me nothing. I owe the father.*

She was blotting the page when she heard it — upriver, around the bend, the patrol boat's engine starting at last, and beneath it, fainter, the sound she had been dreading all morning without knowing it: voices on the towpath behind her, many boots, moving fast, and a sergeant's voice carrying flat across the water, calling for every crossing, every ferryman, every boat to be accounted for by nightfall.
