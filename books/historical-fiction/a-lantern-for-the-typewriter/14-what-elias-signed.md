## 14. What Elias Signed

The requisition office smelled of cold tea and wet wool, and by the third hour of the morning Elias Rehn had stopped noticing either. That was the trouble with this work — it trained you not to notice. A man could sign away a town's livelihood in triplicate and feel nothing but the ache in his writing hand.

He sat at his desk in the corner of the customs house, beneath a window that looked out on the drill square where Colonel Hessler's men wheeled and countermarched in the grey light. The square had been a market once. He remembered it from his first posting, seven years ago, when Vellmarch was only a name on a rotation list and he was a young clerk with clean cuffs who thought paperwork was the peaceful end of a soldier's trade. Paper, he had believed then, kept men from killing each other. It seemed an almost tender idea now, like believing that naming a disease cured it.

The file in front of him was thick with carbon copies, each one pressed through his own typewriter — a military Remington, serial-stamped, its keys worn shiny on E, R, S, T, the letters that made up most of what the army ever said to anyone. *Requisition Order 44-C. Foundry of Aldous Winterbourne, type-cutters, Wheelwright's Lane.* His signature sat at the bottom of every page in its small, disciplined hand: *E. Rehn, Clerk-Adjutant.* Three years of signing had made the letters automatic, a signature like a lock turning.

He hadn't meant to pull the file. That was the thing he would have to be honest about later, if there was a later in which honesty mattered. He had told himself he was simply cross-referencing the seized type against inventory — standard procedure, nothing more. Sergeant Duvall had brought in two trays of sorts recovered from the Winterbourne foundry, and one of them carried the foundry stamp ground into the shoulder of every letter: a lantern over a serif, tiny as a louse, unmistakable if you knew to look. Elias knew to look. He had processed the original registration of that stamp himself, four years ago, back when registering a type foundry was still a courtesy rather than a confession.

So he had opened the file. And now he could not seem to close it.

*Foundry of Aldous Winterbourne.* Aldous Winterbourne had been dead eight months. Elias had seen the death notice pass through the office — one line among forty, no cause listed, which usually meant something no one wanted investigated. The son had signed the continuation papers. Bram Winterbourne. The handwriting had been cramped and careful, a young man's hand trying very hard to look older than it was. Elias had approved the transfer without reading past the second line. Why would he? A foundry was a foundry. Letters were letters. The army wanted presses and paper and records, and it did not particularly care whose name was on the door when they took them.

Except that now, reading the annexes he had never been required to read, he learned what the army cared about very much indeed.

Annex Four. *All customer ledgers, account books, and correspondence registers to be surrendered intact.* Intact. Not destroyed — the word *intact* appeared three times, underlined twice, once in a hand that was not his own. He traced the underline with his fingertip and felt the groove the pen had cut into the paper, as though the order itself had been anxious.

He knew why a ledger would matter. Everyone in the office knew why a ledger would matter; they simply did not say so above a whisper, and not even that near the windows. For two years the disappearances had been happening — not executions, never executions, the word used was always *transfers* or *resettlements* or, in the earliest and most honest documents, *removals*. Men taken from their beds. Women taken from the ferry queue. A boy taken from the churchyard while burying his grandmother. And in a river town like Vellmarch, where nearly everything moved by water and paper, someone somewhere had written down every name. Someone always did. Grief was a bureaucratic force; it generated records the way storms generated runoff.

If such a ledger existed, Annex Four had ordered it confiscated. And Elias Rehn had typed the annex, copied it, stamped it, and signed it, and then gone home to his lodgings above the chandler's shop and slept soundly, because the file had been only words, and words were his trade, and he had long ago taught himself that his trade was neutral.

He turned the page. There was an inventory addendum, initialled by Duvall: *One (1) master ledger, contents unverified, location of recovery pending.* Pending. Duvall had been looking for it for eleven days. There were interrogation summaries stapled behind it — Elias made himself read them, all of them, because he understood that reading them was a kind of penance and he had no better penance available. A printer named Oskar Veyl, questioned and released. A ferrywoman named Marta Sorel, questioned and released. Questions about a hollow case. About a typewriter case, of all things. The army's suspicion had narrowed, without knowing it, to a battered piece of luggage in a cellar on Wheelwright's Lane, and Elias sat very still in the cold office and felt the floor tilt under him.

Because he had walked past that cellar. He had inspected the Winterbourne property personally on the second day of the requisition — it was procedure for registered industrial sites, a formality, ten minutes of ticking boxes while the son hovered in the doorway with lamp-oil stains on his fingers and would not quite meet anyone's eyes. Elias had stood at the top of the cellar stairs, felt the cold breathing up out of the dark, ticked the box marked *lower storage: cleared*, and left. Cleared. He had cleared a room he had not entered, because entering it would have taken another twenty minutes and the tea in the mess went cold at noon, and because some part of him — he admitted this now, alone, with the file open like a wound — some part of him had not wanted to find anything. Finding something meant doing something. Doing something meant being somebody. And Clerk-Adjutant Elias Rehn had spent six years perfecting the art of being nobody: the man who typed orders but did not write them, who signed but did not decide, who was present at everything and responsible for nothing.

It had worked beautifully. It had worked so well that he had condemned a foundry, armed a hunt for a book of names, and cleared a cellar, all without ever once feeling his own hands move.

* * *

At midday he walked out. Not far — just to Wheelwright's Lane, telling himself it was a routing errand, verifying delivery addresses for the confiscated type. The lie was smooth. He was good at lies; that was practically the job description.

The lane was narrower than he remembered, cobbled, running down toward the river smell. The foundry stood with its shutters nailed and the army seal pasted across the door in yellow paper already going soft with damp. Across the lintel, hand-painted in faded gold flaking like old bread, ran the foundry's motto. Elias had passed it a hundred times without reading it. Now he read it: *EVERY LETTER A NAME.*

He stood in the street like a fool and read it again. *Every letter a name.* A type foundry was a factory of names; that was all it was. Every sort Aldous Winterbourne had ever cut had existed so that some person — a bride, a debtor, a dead man in a parish register — could be set down in ink and known. And Elias had signed the paper that nailed those shutters shut.

A woman was scrubbing steps two doors down. She watched him with the flat, unblinking patience of townspeople watching soldiers, and Elias became suddenly conscious of his uniform, of how it fit him, of how it had always fit him, like a skin he'd mistaken for himself.

"You're from the customs house," she said. Not a question.

"I'm checking delivery addresses."

"Nobody delivers anything anymore." She wrung her cloth. "Winterbourne's boy came by this morning. Stood where you're standing. Looked at the door a long time."

Elias said nothing. There was a question sitting in his chest — *which direction did he go, did he carry anything, was he alone* — and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that asking it would be the first honest act of his career, and that he could not afford it. Not here. Not yet. He touched his cap to the woman and walked back up the lane the way he'd come, and behind him he felt her gaze the whole way, patient as water.

That afternoon he requested the transfer.

He composed it carefully, on the machine with the worn keys, choosing each sentence the way a sapper chooses footholds. He could not request the river checkpoint outright; outright requests invited questions, and questions invited Duvall, who had begun lately to look at Elias the way a man looks at a clock he suspects of losing time. So he wrote instead that the river manifests were understaffed since the storm losses, that a clerk with customs experience was wasted on internal inventory, that he offered himself for redeployment to Ferry Point effective immediately. He cited efficiency. He cited his familiarity with cargo documentation. He did not cite shame, or a yellow-sealed door on Wheelwright's Lane, or the memory of standing at the top of cellar stairs he'd been too cowardly to descend. None of that belonged in an army document. All of it belonged underneath one, the way a river belongs under ice.

Captain Brandt approved it within the hour, relieved to lose a clerk who asked for less desirable postings and asked for nothing else. "River duty," Brandt said, stamping the order. "Quiet work. Ferries, manifests, weather complaints. You'll be bored inside a week."

"Yes, sir," Elias said. Boredom sounded like mercy. Boredom sounded like a place where a battered typewriter case might come downriver without being noticed, or might be noticed by exactly one man who had finally decided what side of his own signature he stood on.

He packed that evening by lamplight, taking almost nothing: a spare shirt, his personal typewriter ribbon, and — after a long moment — the carbon copy of Requisition Order 44-C, folded small and put in his breast pocket, over his heart, where it lay like a stone. He told himself he took it as evidence. He was learning to tell himself things.

Below the window, in the dark, the drill square was silent at last, and beyond it the river moved, carrying its cargo of night toward the border, and somewhere along that water, he was certain of it now, certain in the way one is certain of weather coming — somewhere on that road or that river was the Winterbourne heir with a case full of names, walking straight toward the checkpoint where Elias Rehn would sit tomorrow with a stamp in his hand and, for the first time in six years, a decision of his own to make.
