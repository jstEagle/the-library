## 17. Terms

Elias Rehn had followed her for two miles before he understood why he was following.

It was not duty. Duty had been left behind on the dock at the river checkpoint, along with the stamp he'd pressed to her manifest without searching, along with his career, which now sat in a drawer in Vellmarch like a coat someone else might come back for. It was not fear of Duvall, though Duvall would come — Duvall always came. And it was not, whatever he told himself in the first mile, curiosity about the ledger itself. He knew what was in it. He had spent eleven years knowing what was in it, the way a man knows what is under the floorboards of his own house: not by looking, but by walking carefully around that part of the room.

He followed her because she walked like a liar.

That was the thing about Bram Winterbourne — or whoever she was; the manifest said a widow named Ansa Kell, hauling her dead husband's typewriter to relatives in the hill country, and the manifest said it because Elias had written it there himself with a straight face while her hands shook inside her sleeves — that she had learned to be invisible so thoroughly that invisibility had become visible to anyone trained to see it. She walked too evenly. Grief did not walk evenly. Grief stumbled, stopped, forgot where it was going. A widow with a heavy case walked in fits and starts, wept at hedgerows, sat down on stones. This woman walked the way a soldier walks away from a position he has been ordered to abandon: fast, level, eyes forward, counting.

Elias counted too. Two miles of hedgerow, then the road narrowed where an old landslide had eaten the shoulder, and the pines came down close enough to make a corridor, and there was a flat rock beside the way that a man could sit on without looking like he was waiting.

He sat on it.

She saw him from thirty paces. He watched her see him — watched the calculation run behind her face like water behind ice, quick and total and then deliberately frozen over. She did not stop. She adjusted nothing. That was the tell, of course: an innocent woman would have adjusted something, slowed, clutched the case tighter, glanced back down the road toward help. She kept walking exactly as before, as though he were furniture, and only her hands changed — they went still against the case's handle, the way a person goes still when they are deciding how much noise a fight will make.

"Ansa Kell," Elias said, when she drew level with him.

She stopped after all. One stride past him, then a half-turn, wary, performing reluctance. "Do I know you?"

"No," he said. "You know my handwriting, though."

He took the folded manifest from inside his coat and held it out, unfolded, so she could read the stamp, the signature, the date — yesterday's date, yesterday's lie, both of them his. Her eyes went to the paper and then to his face, and what crossed her expression then was not fear. It was something colder and more careful, the look of a type cutter examining a letter that has been cast slightly wrong: measuring the flaw, deciding whether to melt it down or use it anyway.

"You stamped me through," she said.

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I recognized the case."

The wind moved through the pines above them with a sound like paper turning. She looked down at the battered typewriter case under its burlap, at the brass corners worn to shine, at the strap her father — Aldous Winterbourne's — hands had stitched and restitched, and Elias watched her decide, moment by moment, how much to admit. He decided to save her the trouble. There were things a man carried that were heavier if he had to set them down alone.

"Winterbourne Foundry," he said. "The stamp on the seized type. I processed the requisition myself, so I know the mark better than most men alive — three weeks ago I sat in an office cataloguing four hundred pounds of your letters, and every one of them had your father's punch-mark in the shoulder of the stem. A little crescent, like a moon lying down." He folded the manifest again, slowly. "And I knew the case because I'd seen it once before, fourteen years ago, in a room where a man was signing papers he didn't want to sign. Your father brought it. He set it on the requisition clerk's desk and he said, *Read this first.*"

Bram had gone very still. Not the stillness of guilt — the stillness of someone hearing a lock turn in a door they believed was bricked over.

"And did you read it?" she asked.

"I signed it instead." He heard his own voice go flat, the way voices do when they carry their own verdict. "I was twenty-three. The colonel wanted the order countersigned by noon and my supper was getting cold, and Aldous Winterbourne stood in my office with a typewriter case full of God knows what and asked me to read it first, and I told him the requisition was standard procedure and pushed it across the desk for his acknowledgment. He wrote his name. Then he looked at me — not angry. That was the worst of it. He looked at me the way you look at a boy who hasn't grown into his own shadow yet. And he picked up his case and carried it home, and I ate my supper, and eleven years later the army burned the foundry's records and every name in them went into a ledger that nobody official will ever admit exists."

* * *

They walked. He hadn't suggested it and neither had she, but sitting felt dangerous on an open road and standing felt like a duel, so they fell into motion together, ten feet apart, the case between them on her side like a third party to the conversation. The pines thinned. Below the road the river they had crossed that morning showed silver between trees, small already, already behind them.

"I'm not taking it from you," Elias said. "Understand that first, because everything else I say will sound like it."

"Everything else you say *does* sound like it."

"Good. Then we're being honest." He kept his eyes on the road. "If I wanted the ledger destroyed or delivered to Hessler, I'd have searched the case on the dock and shot you in the customs shed, and no one at the ferry would have said a word, because everyone at that ferry understands how the army handles widows. Instead I stamped you through and deserted my post within the hour, which means that as of this morning I am a fugitive with a name, a face, a service number, and a signature on approximately nine hundred documents that the provisional government — or whatever rises out of this — will one day want explained."

She glanced at him sideways. "So you're running."

"I'm *delivering*," he said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the entire subject of what I came here to say." He stopped, and after two strides she stopped too, and for the first time they faced each other properly in the open daylight, which neither of them seemed to enjoy. "Your father's ledger. I know what's sewn into that lining. Not the names themselves — I've never seen them. But I know the categories, because I built some of them. Detained. Transferred. Accounted for. Those aren't army phrases, Frau Kell — or whatever I should call you. They're clerks' phrases. Somebody wrote them, somebody filed them, somebody signed them, and the ledger your father kept wasn't just a list of the disappeared. It was a list of who made them disappear. Every entry cross-referenced. He was cutting type by day and building a prosecution by night, and he tried to hand it to the one man in the requisition office young enough to still be ashamed, and that man signed the paper instead."

The silence went on long enough that a cart could have passed through it. None did. The road belonged to them and to the crows working the far field.

"My name is in that book," Elias said. "Not as a victim. As an instrument. Page after page, probably — I signed transfers for two hundred and forty-one people over eleven years, and I never once read past the summary line, because reading past the line would have meant knowing, and knowing would have meant choosing, and choosing would have cost me—" He gestured at himself, at the road, at the whole gray apparatus of his life. "*This*. A warm office. A pension accruing. The respect of men like Sergeant Duvall, which turns out to be worth exactly what you'd expect." His voice caught on Duvall's name and he hated that it did, and covered it with a cough, and saw that she had noticed the cover, because she noticed everything, this woman who had spent her whole life pretending not to see and seeing all of it.

"So when I say I want that book delivered," he finished, "I am not offering to help you out of virtue. I'm telling you I need it more than you do. You want it printed so the dead get their names back. I want it printed so the living get theirs *taken*. Mine included. I want my signature read aloud in a courtroom with my own ears listening. That is not redemption. Redemption is a word for people who still have time to be good. What I want is *accounting*, and accounting requires the book, and the book is currently being carried by a woman whose forged papers I stamped through a checkpoint, which makes us, whether either of us likes it, accomplices."

* * *

She sat down on a milestone then — abruptly, the way people do when their legs make the decision for them — and pulled bread from her pack and broke it and held half out to him without looking up, and he understood it was not generosity but procedure: a body needs fuel, and the argument would be long, and she was the kind of person who prepared for long arguments the way sappers prepare ground.

He took the bread. It was dense and dark and tasted of the ferry, of Marta's galley, though he didn't know that yet.

"If your name's in it," she said, chewing slowly, watching the road south, "why didn't you burn the office records yourself? Eleven years. You had keys."

"Because burning records protects the men who wrote them. Destroyed evidence convicts no one. It just makes the crime unwitnessable." He turned the bread crust in his fingers. "I used to tell myself I was keeping the files safe for a future court. Some nights I even believed it. Most nights I knew I was a coward with excellent filing habits."

A sound escaped her — brief, involuntary, gone almost before it arrived. It might once have been a laugh. "Excellent filing habits," she repeated. "My father would have liked you. He used to say the revolution would be won not by gunmen but by whoever kept the receipts."

"He was right. Gunmen die and the receipts live." Elias crouched to her level, forearms on knees, and made himself meet her eyes, because the next part deserved eye contact the way a sentence deserves its verb. "But here's my price, and I'll state it plainly so you can refuse it plainly. When that book reaches the press office and the editor asks how these documents were obtained — and he will ask, because editors ask, because unverified lists are libel and verified lists are war — I want my testimony attached. Full statement. My name, my rank, my signature, my account of the requisition and the two hundred and forty-one transfer orders and the day Aldous Winterbourne stood in my office and I failed him. Printed. Under my name. In the same sheets as the victims."

"That's it?" She sounded genuinely suspicious, and he couldn't blame her; he'd spent eleven years being precisely as trustworthy as his uniform required and no further.

"That's it. No money. No escape arrangement. No promise you'll speak well of me afterward. Just — ink. My name in ink next to what I did, in my own words, unverifiable by anyone except me, which is the point." He stood, knees cracking, thirty-six years old and feeling older than the milestone. "You've spent your life being invisible, Frau Winterbourne. I've spent mine being legible. Every document I ever touched has my name on it in beautiful, damning, permanent script. Do you know what that's like? To be *known* only for paperwork? To have your whole self reduced to a signature that means *yes, proceed, account for them*?" He shook his head. "I don't want to be erased. Erasure is what they do. I want the opposite of erasure. I want to be seen clearly, once, at full weight, and let history do what it likes with the sight."

She was quiet for a long time. Somewhere below, the river ran on without opinions.

"You understand," she said at last, "that if we're caught, your testimony dies with you and mine dies with me, and the book burns or gets buried, and everyone in it stays disappeared forever. That's what we're carrying. Not paper. Everyone's last chance."

"I understand it better than you do. I've read the disposal orders."

"Then you understand why I can't trust you on charm and shared guilt alone." She rose, brushing crumbs from her coat, and looked at him with the frank appraisal she must usually have hidden behind the widow's downcast eyes, and Elias thought: *there she is. The real face. Worth the whole walk.* "Terms, then. Since you want terms. One: you don't touch the case. Not to carry it, not to inspect it, not to 'help' with it. If it leaves my hands it's because I'm dead."

"Agreed."

"Two: when we reach the press office, I testify first. Alone. Whatever you add, you add after, in writing, reviewed by me. I'm not walking into a foreign embassy chained to a confessed instrument of the requisition."

"*Confessed*—"

"Three," she continued, relentless, "you tell me everything. Everything you signed, everything you remember, dates and numbers and names of officers, before we're within a hundred miles of that border. If your testimony is going to hang beside the victims' names, it's going to be complete, and complete means I've checked it against the ledger, and checking it means I read every page with you sitting there answering questions. You don't get to hand over a tidy statement and walk away clean. You're going to spend the road to the pass remembering out loud, to me, in detail, and I am going to write it down in my father's shorthand, and if one number disagrees with the ledger, we stop and resolve it. That's the price of your name in print. You buy it with the truth, all of it, at the pace I set."

Elias stared at her. Something in his chest that had been clenched since the dock — since the requisition, since the supper gone cold eleven years ago — loosened by one degree, which was the most it had loosened in a decade. She wasn't punishing him. She was doing what her father had done in his office: setting a paper in front of him and saying *read this first*. Except this time there was no counter-signature to hide behind. This time the reading would take days and the ink would be his own.

"It's going to take the whole journey," he said. "Two hundred and forty-one entries. Some of them I'll have to reconstruct from memory — I didn't keep copies, I wasn't stupid."

"You kept *something*. Men like you always keep something. A scratch record. Dates in a prayer book. You'll produce it tonight." She shouldered the strap of the typewriter case, and the crescent moon of her father's punch-mark might as well have been stamped on her forehead for all the family she showed. "We walk until dark, then find shelter off the road — north-facing slope, no farmhouse, farmhouse keeps dogs and dogs bring soldiers. You'll start reciting at the fire."

"And if my memory contradicts the ledger?"

"Then one of us is wrong," Bram Winterbourne said, "and we find out which, because the press office won't print a book with errors in it, and Hessler's lawyers will feast on a single wrong date. Accuracy is our ammunition. Sloppiness is theirs." She started walking. Over her shoulder, without softening: "Four: you eat last and sleep second watch. I don't care what you were in the army. On this road you're cargo insurance."

He almost smiled. It surprised him — the muscles had gone rusty. "Cargo insurance," he echoed, and fell into step ten feet behind her, the agreed distance, and began, silently, to organize the first memory. The first transfer order was dated the ninth of March, eleven years ago, a boy of nineteen from the tannery district, transferred for questioning and accounted for within the month, and Elias Rehn had signed it between breakfast and the morning mail, and he had remembered the date ever since because it was also his mother's birthday, and he had never been able to separate the two again.

Behind them, far down the valley, a thread of smoke stood up from the direction of the river checkpoint — thin, deliberate, the kind of signal smoke that meant *pursuit authorized*. Neither of them said anything about it. But Elias saw Bram lengthen her stride, and he matched it, and the ledger rode between them like a silent third signer, waiting for the fire, waiting for the dark, waiting for two liars to begin, at last, telling one truth.
