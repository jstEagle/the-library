## 18. The Town That Forgot

They came down out of the hills at dusk, when the light went the color of old pewter and the road below them emptied of everything but wind. Elias had been silent for most of the afternoon — the particular silence he wore like a coat on days when his conscience was heavy in the pockets — and it was he who stopped first at the crest above the valley and said, quietly, "There."

Bram looked.

A village lay in the shallow bowl of the land: forty roofs, maybe fifty, a church with a squat bell tower, smoke rising thin and gray from a handful of chimneys. It should have been an ordinary sight, the kind of place she'd passed a hundred times without thought. But something was wrong with it, and it took her a long moment — the kind of long moment her father had taught her to give a piece of type before judging it — to understand what.

There were no signs.

Every village she had ever walked through wore its names openly. The smithy had its sign, the inn had its sign, the bakery, the mill, the tavern with its painted boar or barrel or sun. Doors carried the marks of their households; fences bore the chalked tallies of debts and favors; wells were labeled with the names of those who'd dug them. Names were how a place breathed.

This village had none. No signs hung anywhere. The doors were bare wood, unpainted, unlabeled. Even the church — she squinted against the failing light — even the churchyard gate stood blank.

"Kessel," Elias said. "That's what this one is called. Or was called. I processed the requisition for this district eighteen months ago." He said it the way a man confesses to a debt he cannot pay, staring down at the roofs as if expecting them to accuse him. "It was supposed to be administrative. Seizure of presses, records, registries. Standard order." He paused. "I signed the second order too. The one about *records*."

"What does that mean — records?"

He didn't answer. He shifted the typewriter case higher on his shoulder — the case that held the ledger, that held every name the army wanted dead twice, once in the flesh and once in ink — and started down toward the village, and Bram followed because there was nowhere else to follow.

* * *

The inn had no name either, only a hanging bracket from which the sign had been unscrewed so recently that four bright rectangles showed where the mounting plate had shaded the wood. Inside, the common room was warm and half-full, and the silence that fell when they entered was not the silence of strangers arriving. It was the silence of people who had learned that attention was dangerous.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with flour to the elbows. She looked at them, looked at their travel-worn coats and the bulky case on Elias's shoulder, and said, "Room?"

"Two rooms," Elias said. "And whatever's hot."

She named a price that was honest by the standards of honest places, took the coin without counting it — which told Bram more than any speech could have — and led them up stairs worn hollow in the centers. On the landing, Bram paused by a small window and looked out over the churchyard, and what she saw there kept her at the glass until the cold came through the seams.

No headstones.

The churchyard was full of mounds. She could see them clearly even in the last gray light — rows of them, some fresh, some settled and grassed over, dozens upon dozens, far too many for a village of fifty roofs even in a hard year. But there was not one stone among them. Not one cross, not one marker, not so much as a stick with a name whittled into it. Just earth, ranked and anonymous, like a field planted with something no one would admit to harvesting.

Below, in the yard, a woman crossed between two sheds carrying firewood, moving quickly, eyes down, the way people walk past checkpoints. Bram watched her go and felt the ledger's weight through the floorboards, through the whole house, as if it were pressing down on the foundations.

* * *

At supper, in the corner of the common room farthest from the door, Bram learned the shape of what had happened here, though no one told her directly. It arrived in pieces, the way truth arrives in frightened places.

An old man sat near the hearth mending a harness, and a boy of perhaps ten sat across from him doing nothing, which is itself a kind of work in a frightened house. When the boy asked a question — Bram caught only the tail of it, something about a grandfather — the old man's hands stopped moving. He didn't look up. He said, very softly, "We don't say that name," and went back to his stitching, and the boy looked at the floor, and neither of them spoke again for an hour.

Bram set down her spoon.

Elias followed her gaze. "Don't," he murmured. "Whatever you're going to do, don't."

"I'm not going to do anything."

"You're going to do something. You have the same look your father must have had when a letter was cast wrong and everyone around him pretended it wasn't."

She turned back to her bowl. "How did you know my father?"

"I read his paperwork. That's all anyone is, to me — paperwork. That was the job." He tore bread without appetite. "Names, dates, signatures. I could tell you the weight of a man by the thickness of his file."

"And now you're carrying the opposite of a file."

"Now I'm carrying the opposite of everything I ever filed." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and she understood that this was the only way he could carry it at all.

Later, when the room had thinned and the innkeeper came to bank the fire, Bram tried her. "Your church registry," she said gently. "My grandmother kept ours. I wondered—"

The woman's rag stilled on the stove iron. For a moment her face did something complicated — grief and fury and fear all trying to occupy the same space — and then it smoothed into nothing, into the practiced blankness Bram recognized from her own mirror, the face of someone who has made invisibility a discipline.

"There's no registry," the innkeeper said. "There never was one. Ask anyone."

"But—"

"Ask. Anyone." She set the poker down with a small, deliberate click. "You're travelers passing through. In the morning you'll be gone. And whatever you think you saw in that churchyard, you saw a meadow." She straightened. "The ones who gave the orders are gone north, mostly. Promoted. But the ones who *enforce* the orders live here still. Among us. They drink at the table by the window on Saturdays. So there is no registry. There never was one. God keep you, and mind your own parish."

She left. Bram sat looking at the fire and understood that she had just been warned more thoroughly than any soldier could have managed.

* * *

She couldn't sleep. Near midnight she rose, took the lamp, and went down to the churchyard, and Elias — who slept badly and lightly, who woke at every sound the way guilty men do — came after her without being asked, carrying the typewriter case, because he would not let it out of arm's reach even for the length of a graveyard walk.

The moon was three days shy of full and gave enough light to make the mounds legible. Rows of them. Bram counted as she walked, and lost count past sixty, and stopped counting because the number had stopped meaning anything.

At the far row she knelt beside one grave — a recent one, the earth still raw-edged — and found what she had already known she would find. Not a stone. A marker of another kind: a single printed page, folded once, weighted under a river stone gone soft with weather. She lifted it with fingers that shook and opened it in the lamplight.

It was a hymnal page. Torn from a psalter, the words of some comfort long since confiscated along with every other book bearing names. Someone had written on the blank reverse, in pencil, in a hand pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — a hand writing fast, in secret, terrified:

*MARTA KESSEL-ANNA, b. 1801, d. 1849. Wife of Jorun. Mother of five. Kept bees.*

Nothing else. No surname beyond the village-name. No date of burial. No priest's attestation. Just the barest skeleton of a life, smuggled onto stolen paper, buried where the army wouldn't think to dig because the army believed it had already abolished memory here.

Bram knelt in the frost with the little paper open in her hands and wept — not elegantly, not on command, the way she had rehearsed weeping for the checkpoints, but ugly and silent and helpless, the tears of someone shown her own future.

Because she could see it. That was the horror, the precise and surgical horror of it: she could see Vellmarch from here. She could see the foundry sign unscrewed, leaving four bright rectangles in the paint. She could see her neighbors' doors stripped of their household marks. She could see the parish register of St. Aldhelm's burning in a courtyard while a clerk checked items off a requisition list she had seen the format of, the format Elias himself had stamped. She could see the graves behind the tannery growing new mounds with no stones, hymnal pages under river rocks, children learning not to say their grandfathers' names.

The ledger in the case on Elias's shoulder held the antidote to exactly this, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread. All this — the erased village, the nameless dead, the boy forbidden his own grandfather — all of it had been accomplished with nothing heavier than signatures. Paper against paper. Ink against ink. And the side that kept its records won, because the other side had been made to lose even its own handwriting.

"They burned it," Elias said behind her. She hadn't heard him kneel. "The registry. I know because I filed the certificate of destruction. One line item. *Parish register, Kessel district: one volume, burned, witnessed.*" His voice was very quiet. "I remember thinking it was efficient. Eighteen months ago, sitting at my desk, I remember thinking, *well, at least they've finished this district,* the way you'd think about a warehouse cleared of inventory." He looked at the rows of mounds. "Inventory."

"Elias—"

"My name is in your ledger." He said it without drama, the way he seemed to say everything that mattered. "Not as a victim. As an instrument. Somewhere in those pages there's a line with my signature authorizing a transport, and somewhere downwind of that line there are names that ended at the end of that transport. I'm not asking you to forgive that. I'm telling you why I can't sleep in villages like this one." He rose, and held out his hand for the hymnal page, and she gave it to him, and he read it twice by the lamp and then handed it back with enormous care, as if it were a reliquary. "Put it back exactly as it was. Whoever tends these comes in the dark, and if we disturb the ground they'll know strangers were here, and strangers asking questions get reported."

She replaced the stone, smoothed the frost, and stood. For a moment neither of them moved. The church tower rose black against the sky, its bell presumably confiscated — bells got confiscated too, she'd heard; bronze was war material, and besides, a bell calls people together, and gathering was now illegal in groups larger than five.

"Her name was Marta," Bram said. "Marta Kessel-Anna. She kept bees."

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"Marta Kessel-Anna. She kept bees."

"That's why it has to reach the press." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and colder, and harder, and she welcomed all three. "Not for the court. Not even for justice. Because somewhere in this country tonight there are a hundred villages becoming this one, and the only weapon that works against erasure is a name spoken by a stranger. If we print them — if thousands of sheets come off a foreign press with these names on them, scattered like seed, carried by ferrymen and farmers and deserters — then every hymnal page under every rock gets its echo. Then the army's one-line destruction certificates stop being the last word." She looked at the dark mass of the case. "That's what my father was buying when he hid it. Not safety. *Persistence.*"

Elias was silent a long moment. "You sound like him," he said finally. "The paperwork. Your father's file — I told you I read it. There was a note in his hand on the last page, an odd thing for a type founder to write. It said: *A letter survives its word. A word survives its speaker. Cut accordingly.*" He picked up the case. "Come inside. Frost is coming down hard, and tomorrow we climb."

* * *

But sleep would not come for her, and near dawn she heard the boy.

Through the floor she heard the front door open below — soft, furtive — and then small feet on the stairs, and then the creak of the corridor boards outside her room, pausing. She lay still, breath shallow, hand finding the little knife she'd taken to sleeping beside.

A slip of paper came under the door.

She waited a full hundred heartbeats before she rose and retrieved it. Pencil, pressed hard, a child's hand but a careful one, written fast in the blue hour:

*you looked at the graves. nobody looks at the graves. gran says if you are who i think you are, take the shepherd road west of the church, not the pass road — soldiers came tuesday asking after a woman dressed as a man, and there is a paper nailed to the mill with her face drawn on it.*

Bram stood in the freezing room holding the note, dawn graying the window, and heard in her memory the innkeeper's voice — *mind your own parish* — and understood that she had been wrong about one thing. This village had not forgotten. It remembered perfectly. It simply kept its memory the way the graves kept theirs: buried, guarded, passed hand to hand in the dark.

Somewhere down the western slope, nailed to a mill door, her face was already drawn on paper.

She dressed, and went to wake Elias.
