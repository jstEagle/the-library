## 19. Duvall's Warrant

The authorization came through on the fourth day, in an envelope sealed with Hessler's personal crest — a black eagle clutching a bundle of wheat stalks, as if the bird were harvesting the country rather than ruling it.

Duvall read it standing at his desk in the requisitioned customs house, because he had learned that if he sat down with such documents they seemed to grow heavier, and if he read them standing they could almost be mistaken for someone else's orders. Almost. The ink did not care about posture. *Sergeant Duvall is hereby empowered to apprehend, and where resistance is offered or flight attempted to destroy evidence, to shoot on sight, any person implicated in the theft of military records.* Signed. Countersigned. Stamped twice, once in red, which was the color they used for things that were meant to be obeyed rather than understood.

*Shoot on sight.*

He read it three times. The first time he read it as a soldier, and it was simple: a warrant, a tool, a widening of the leash. The second time he read it as the man who had written the supporting memorandum, four pages of careful inference — ink analysis, witness statements regarding nocturnal light at the Winterbourne foundry, the curious absence of any neighbor who had seen the heir's face since the funeral — and it was still simple, simpler than he'd intended, because somewhere between his pen and the Colonel's desk his suspicion had hardened into permission to kill.

The third time he read it, he read only one line, the line his own clerk had transcribed from Duvall's dictated description of the fugitive:

*Subject is believed to be Bram Winterbourne, aged twenty-six, son of the deceased type-cutter Aldous Winterbourne. Subject may attempt passage disguised as a woman.*

He stood very still, the way he had once stood still in the archives at Corvenne when he'd discovered that a file he himself had stamped had been altered above his signature. That stillness had not saved him then. It would not save him now.

Because the description was wrong. Not false — that was the devil of it — but wrong in the way a key is wrong for a lock it was cut to fit. His informants, the neighbors who spoke in the low voices of people denouncing each other for bread, had told him what they believed: that the Winterbourne son was slight, fine-boned, soft-voiced; that the son had never married though women of the parish had tried; that the son walked oddly, held his arms close, moved like someone perpetually making herself smaller. And Duvall, assembling the portrait, had done the arithmetic aloud in front of two lieutenants: *the son is a fiction; the fugitive is the daughter; she has been her father's ghost for years.*

He had said it as deduction. It had gone into the record as exposure.

Now every gate, ferry landing, chapel door, and crossroads within thirty miles of Vellmarch would carry a placard with that sentence on it, printed in army type — army type, some of it very likely cut at the foundry whose owner he was hunting — and every peasant, priest, smuggler, and child who could read would know that the woman with the typewriter case was worth a hundred crowns dead or alive, and that shooting first was not merely permitted but encouraged.

She had survived this long by being no one. He had just made her everyone.

Duvall set the warrant down and looked out the window at the square, where a work gang was loading confiscated type cases onto a wagon under guard. The letters rattled like teeth. Somewhere in those crates was the alphabet of a town, sorted and boxed and hauled off to be melted or warehoused, and nobody in authority saw anything in that but paper value. He had been a records man for eleven years. He had always believed that files were neutral — that ink recorded, and men acted, and the distance between the two was called duty. Lately the distance had collapsed entirely. Now the ink acted and the men merely recorded, and he was one of the men.

" Sergeant?" His junior clerk hovered at the door, holding the placards fresh from the press. "Distribution?"

"All of them." His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Grain gate, ferry, both river landings, the pass road, every church board between here and the frontier. Read them aloud in the villages — half these people can't read, so make sure they hear it."

"Hear what part, sir? All of it?"

Duvall looked at the topmost placard. The printer had set the reward figure large and the physical description small, in the cramped column beneath, where the detail about the disguise sat in nine-point type like a confession tucked into a hymnal. Most people would hear only the number. But the ones who mattered — the ferrymen, the innkeepers, the women who noticed other women's hands — would hear all of it.

"All of it," he said. "That's the point of a description."

The clerk saluted and left. Duvall remained at the window until the wagons rolled out, and only then allowed himself to think the thought he had been refusing since dawn: *she cannot pass now. Any checkpoint, any village, any pair of eyes. I have taken her disguise away with a signature.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, the second one, the one he kept locked below the waterline: *good. Then it will be over quickly, and no one will have to choose.*

He examined that second thought the way he examined suspect documents — tilted to the light, checking for forgery. Was it true? Partly. A quick end at some river checkpoint was cleaner than the alternative: the Colonel's tribunal, the foundry demolished with the family accounted for, the ledger burned unread in a courtyard while clerks logged its destruction in triplicate. If she were taken alive, that was what happened. He knew because he had drafted the procedure.

So yes — part of him wanted her caught. And another part, the part that had spent three nights rereading the requisition manifest and finding his own name attached to the seizure of a dead man's letters, wanted her across the border so badly he could taste iron.

Both parts had signed the same warrant. That was the army for you. It let you be two men at once and billed you for the privilege.

* * *

The placards went up faster than he expected. By evening of the next day, reports came back: grain gate posted, ferry landing posted, the mountain villages posted, one churchwarden in Lowen had refused to nail it to his board and had been compelled by escort. In Lowen the warden had asked, politely, whether the reward applied if the subject surrendered voluntarily, and the escort sergeant had said the reward applied either way, and the warden had said, still polite, that in that case he would pray for the subject, and there was nothing in regulations against praying.

Duvall noted it in the file. He had begun keeping a private file, separate from the official one — a habit he told himself was method and knew was something else. Into it went anomalies: the churchwarden's prayer; the ferrywoman Marta Voss, sixty-one, who had answered questions about river traffic with the serene completeness of a woman reciting scripture and volunteered nothing that wasn't asked; the fact that among the seized papers from the foundry, three drawers of type were missing outright, as if removed by someone with strong arms and a knowledge of what to take.

And into it, that night, went the newest anomaly. A patrol had stopped a widow on the river road two days ago — before the placards, before the description — hauling a typewriter case toward the border. They had let her through after a joke about widows and their junk. The report reached Duvall's desk late, misfiled under grain transport, and when he read it he felt the particular cold of a man realizing the fish was already past the net when the net was woven.

Two days. Two days of lead time she had, and he had spent them drafting memoranda.

But — he checked the dates again, because hope required precision — the ferry crossing was not the border. Beyond the river lay forty miles of farmland, the closed pass, the third checkpoint at Stonefall, and the press offices beyond. She was inside the country still. Inside meant catchable. The warrant said so in red ink.

He spread his maps. The river route led east to the lowland towns, but the lowlands were flooded with patrols chasing deserters. The direct route ran through Stonefall, and Stonefall's commander had already signaled that word of a woman fugitive had reached him and that he would appreciate clarification of jurisdiction. Everyone wanted jurisdiction. Nobody wanted responsibility.

Where would you go, Winterbourne?

He asked it aloud to the empty office, and the empty office, being made of requisitioned furniture and other people's paperwork, declined to answer. So he answered it himself, as he imagined her: not the lowlands, too many ears; not straight to Stonefall, too obvious. The chapel country. The high scrubland where deserters camped and priests buried people without reporting the burials. The places where the army's writ ran thin and memory ran thick. He drew a circle on the map around the chapel ruins above the Vell valley, and looking at the circle he felt less like a hunter than a man reading someone else's letter and understanding it completely.

Which was, he admitted, the whole trouble. He understood her. He had spent weeks inside her circumstances — the foundry, the ink, the dead father's trade, the necessity of vanishing into a borrowed name — and understanding was a door that opened from both sides. Every hour he spent predicting her made him more able to predict what he would have done, and what he would have done was exactly what she was doing.

He closed the map. On his desk the warrant lay face-up, and the eagle stared at the ceiling with its wheat harvest.

* * *

On the sixth day, a courier brought him a prisoner.

Not her — the courier made that clear before Duvall even rose, almost apologetic, as if failing to deliver the correct quarry were a breach of etiquette. The prisoner was a ferry-hand from Marta Voss's boat, a boy of nineteen caught passing silver to a deserter's mother, and he was brought in because he had been heard asking questions. Questions about whether the reward notice was true. Whether it was really a hundred crowns. Whether it was paid for information as well as capture.

Duvall interviewed him alone, without the usual witnesses, which was irregular, and irregularity was a currency he had begun spending freely.

"What's your name?"

"Tomas, sir."

"You work the ferry."

"Yes, sir."

"For Marta Voss. How long?"

"Four years, sir."

"And she treats you well."

The boy blinked. He had expected a different interview — the standard one, about silver and deserters — and Duvall watched him recalibrate. "She pays fair. She taught me the river."

"She knew Aldous Winterbourne."

It wasn't a question, and the boy didn't treat it as one. "Everyone on the landing knew him. He printed the obituaries. When the fever came through, he did them free. Marta's husband — she keeps the broadsheet framed in the wheelhouse. Printed when there wasn't paper to spare. He gave her his last sheet of good stock, she says."

Printed obituaries. Free. Duvall wrote nothing down, which was itself becoming a habit — the important things he now kept only where warrants couldn't reach. "Tomas. If a woman came to your ferry with a typewriter case, and Marta Voss put her below deck, what would that be worth?"

The boy looked at him for a long moment, and Duvall saw the calculation happen behind his eyes — not the calculation of fear, but of loyalty, weighed against a hundred crowns, weighed against whatever the boy had seen in four years on a boat owned by a woman who kept her dead husband's obituary framed beside the tiller.

"I wouldn't know, sir," Tomas said. "I mind the engine."

"You'd know if you'd seen it."

"I mind the engine, sir. There's a lot of noise down there. A man hears nothing but his own machinery."

Duvall dismissed him. Through the barred window he watched the boy go, unhurried, whistling, and understood that he had just witnessed the exact thing the ledger was written to prevent and the exact thing that would keep it moving: a town deciding, name by name, whose side its silence belonged to.

He returned to his desk and took out the private file. Under Marta Voss's name he added the boy's account of the obituaries, and under that, after some deliberation, he wrote a single line in pencil, the pencil being for things not yet true:

*The town is hiding her in plain hearing.*

Then he sat back, and the full shape of his position assembled itself around him like a cell. He had obtained the warrant. The warrant had posted her description to every crossroads, including the truth of her disguise — the one protection she had left, spent now, spent by him. If she was taken, she would hang or face the tribunal, and the ledger would burn, and his own name would stay safely inside it, an instrument, unindicted, invisible — the very condition she had fled, the condition he suddenly could not bear.

If she escaped, he had failed to stop her, and the failure would be legible in his file in handwriting the Colonel knew intimately.

There was a third path. He had been circling it for days the way a man circles a frozen pond, testing. It required him to leave this office. To request transfer to the river checkpoint at Grayling Bend — a lateral move, unambitious, explainable as thoroughness: *the fugitive's most probable route, sir; I request to process manifests personally.* No one would question diligence. Diligence was the one vice the army rewarded.

And at Grayling Bend, when the manifests came through, he would either do his duty or he wouldn't, and whichever he chose, he would finally know which man had been signing his name all along.

Duvall pulled a transfer form from the drawer, filled in the header, and stopped at the reason line. Regulations required a stated purpose. He thought of the boy whistling on the stairs, of the churchwarden praying over a nailed-up placard, of the framed obituary beside the tiller, of a woman somewhere in the chapel country carrying her father's alphabet toward the border while every wall in the province whispered what she was.

In the reason line, in his precise clerk's hand, he wrote: *Fugitive's route assessed via river crossing. Request personal oversight of manifest inspection.*

Every word true. The army had never yet required him to write the whole truth, only enough of it to hold a stamp.

He signed it. As he blotted the page, a runner arrived with the evening dispatches, and the top sheet carried Stonefall's reply to his query about jurisdiction: *Commander acknowledges. Pass remains open. Advise: unusual traffic of strangers on chapel road, incl. armed men in undress uniform. Will detain and question all parties fitting circulated description.*

All parties. Including, Duvall noted, the armed men — meaning the deserters, meaning the lawless country she would have to cross, meaning that between her and the border there now stood not only his warrant but every desperate man with a rifle and a grudge who could read a reward notice.

He looked at the map again, at his penciled circle above the Vell valley, and made the mistake of speaking to the empty room once more.

"Hold on," he said — two words, addressed to no one, to a fugitive, to a ledger, to his own better self, he couldn't have said which — and then he took his transfer order and went to find the duty officer, before the version of him that had signed the warrant could wake up and countermand the version that was trying to outrun it.
