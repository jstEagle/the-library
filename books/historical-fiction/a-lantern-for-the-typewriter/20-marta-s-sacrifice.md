## 20. Marta's Sacrifice

The river woke Marta before the soldiers did.

Forty years of dawn crossings had tuned her body to the water the way a tuner tunes a piano — she felt the Vell's moods in her wrists before her eyes were open, felt the cold coming down off the mountains two days early, felt ice forming in the reeds like a rumor spreading. So when boots came up the ferry landing in the blue hour, four pairs and then six, she knew it before she heard them, the way you know hail is coming by the sudden hush of birds.

She lay in her cot above the boathouse and listened, and did not move, and thought: *Three days. I gave them three days.*

It had been eleven since the girl came off the river soaked to the ribs, hauling that battered typewriter case like it was a child pulled from drowning. Eleven nights since Marta had looked at the Winterbourne jaw on her — Aldous's jaw, unmistakable, the same square stubbornness she'd carved into memory thirty years ago when he'd stood at her rail with his hat off and asked her to carry a coffin-sized crate upriver and never ask what was in it — and known exactly what she was holding in her hands.

*You owe me nothing,* the girl had said, dripping on the deck planks, and Marta had almost laughed. Almost. Because the debt ran the other way, and had run for years, and the girl didn't even know its weight.

Now the boots stopped below her window, and a voice said, in the flat administrative tone that had replaced all other tones in Vellmarch: "Marta Kessel, licensed ferrywoman of the Vell crossing. By order of Colonel Hessler, commanding, you are detained for questioning regarding the transport of contraband persons."

Contraband *persons.* As if the girl were bales of wheat, crates of type, something with a weight in kilograms and a line in a ledger. Well. That was the whole war in three words, wasn't it. People as freight.

Marta got up, put on her good coat — the one with the brass buttons she kept for funerals and inspections — and went down the stairs slowly, because there was no point hurrying toward a thing like this.

* * *

They had taken over the boathouse the way soldiers took everything: completely, without imagination. Her passenger manifests were spread across the ticket desk, weighted with bayonets. A lieutenant with a face like an unpaid bill sat behind the desk in her own chair, and a sergeant stood at either shoulder, and through the open door she could see two more going through her boat, prying at floorboards with the delicacy of men who had been told there was treasure and believed it.

"Marta Kessel." The lieutenant said her name like he was testing whether it rang true. "You are recorded as having made an unscheduled crossing on the night of the eleventh."

"Every crossing is unscheduled," Marta said. "The river doesn't keep appointments."

"The manifest for the eleventh shows one passenger. A merchant named Corvus, traveling alone. Yet the ferry log—" he tapped a page without looking at it, "—records ballast shifted to port. Extra weight. You took on cargo that night that you did not declare."

"Cargo," Marta said. "I take on cargo every crossing. Sacks, barrels, coffins. The river carries whatever grief people pay me for."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him decide she was a stupid old woman — which was fine, stupid old women had survived four regimes by being underestimated, and if this was the hour she spent that coin, so be it. "Where is the typewriter case?"

And there it was, laid out plain. They knew about the case. Which meant they knew more than she wished, which meant somewhere behind this lieutenant stood a man doing arithmetic, a man who added ink samples to manifests and carried the sum forward. She'd heard the name Duvall whispered at the market like weather coming. A sergeant who counted things. Counters were worse than zealots; zealots could be drunk or flattered, but counters only slept when the columns balanced.

"I sold my last typewriter case in spring," Marta said. "To a tinker. For lamp oil money. Ask your quartermaster — half this town has sold its furniture for lamp oil since your requisition."

"You were seen." He leaned forward. "A fisherman, upstream. He saw a young man board your ferry that night who never disembarked on the far bank. Where did he go?"

Marta folded her hands. She had ferried brides and bodies, deserters and brides again, and in forty years she had learned the single most important discipline of the river: *the current takes what it takes, and you do not chase it.* A lie needed a current too. Give a lie still water and it sank under its own weight.

"A young man," she said. "On the eleventh? Let me think. There was a surveyor's assistant, going up to the mill camps. He got off at the willow bend and walked the towpath. I can show you where."

"He never arrived at the mill camps."

"Then he changed his mind, or drowned, or found a girl along the towpath. Young men do all three. I am not responsible for what legs do after they leave my deck."

The lieutenant's mouth thinned. Behind her, out the window, she watched the sun come up over the water and thought, absurdly, of how the light hit the river at this exact hour, gold laid over grey, and how she would not be seeing it from her deck tomorrow, or perhaps ever again, and how strange it was that the mind chose such moments to inventory beauty.

"Mrs. Kessel. Marta Kessel." He said it gently now, which was worse. "You are sixty-one years old. Your husband drowned twenty years ago. You have no children. No one will grieve you but this river. Is that worth it? Whatever debt you imagine you're paying — is it worth the rest of your life?"

And here was the moment, and she had known it was coming since the boots on the landing, and she had rehearsed nothing because rehearsing was lying ahead of time and she wanted no lies between herself and this.

"My husband drowned," Marta said, "carrying a boy out of the flood of '39 who wasn't his son. Nobody thanked him. The boy grew up, moved away, forgot our name entirely. I used to be bitter about that. Then I understood: that's just how saving works. It goes downstream and doesn't send word back." She unfolded her hands and set them flat on the desk. "You want to know if it's worth it. Ask me instead whether a woman can spend forty years learning to hold a boat steady in bad water and then, when the worst water comes, tie it off and walk away. I can't. It isn't in my hands anymore than yours. Now charge me or don't, Lieutenant, but stop wasting my morning. I have ropes that need drying."

* * *

They took her not to the town jail but to the requisitioned granary, which the army had converted with bureaucratic efficiency into a holding pen: rows of cells built of new planks and salvaged church ironwork, each cell holding eight to twelve souls, the air thick with the smell of straw and fear and unwashed wool. They put her in with women mostly — market wives, two schoolteachers, a girl of maybe sixteen who hadn't stopped shaking since Marta arrived.

By noon the whole cell block knew why Marta Kessel was there. News traveled through granary walls faster than through open streets; shame traveled slower, and courage traveled fastest of all, passed mouth to ear in the dark like a coal.

"The widow," the girl whispered, huddled against her side. "They say you hid the Winterbourne ghost."

"They say wrong," Marta said. "I ferried a paying customer. What he was, is between him and God and whoever's chasing him."

"But it's true. The son. They say the son is the printer, the pamphlets—"

"The son is dead," said one of the schoolteachers, sharply. "Aldous Winterbourne's son died of lung fever, everyone knows it. There is no son. Sit down, Ilse."

But Ilse leaned closer and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "My brother saw the ghost. On the towpath, the night of the storm. He says the ghost walked like a woman."

Marta said nothing. Outside the bars, a guard turned his collar up against the draft and pretended not to hear, which was its own kind of information — the walls themselves were tired of the army, the walls wanted the war over, the walls remembered whose grain this building had held.

That first night, they took her for questioning again. Different room this time, a lamplit office smelling of paper and mildew, and the lieutenant gone, replaced by a captain with soft hands and hard eyes who poured two glasses of plum brandy and pushed one across the desk.

"Mrs. Kessel. Let us be adults." He drank. "We know the stowaway was the Winterbourne heir. We know the heir is moving toward the border with an item of military interest. We know you assisted. None of this is in question, so let us skip past the part where you insult both of us by denying it." He smiled. "What we don't know is the route. And here is the beautiful thing about you, Mrs. Kessel: you don't need to tell us. You need only fail to deny. I'll say a name — the grain gate — and you'll breathe differently or you won't. The mountain pass — breathe differently or you won't. Old women are honest with their lungs even when they lie with their mouths. Brandy helps. Drink."

So that was the shape of it. Not interrogation but seismography, reading tremors. Marta picked up the brandy and held it, warming her palms, buying seconds, thinking fast the way she thought in white water — not cleverly, but truly, which on a river amounts to the same thing.

Because here was what the captain didn't know: she *didn't* know the route. The girl hadn't told her. Oskar's plan had stayed shut even from her, and thank God for it, thank God for the girl's caution and Oskar's paranoia, because it meant Marta's lungs had nothing to betray. Every name he spoke would land on her like weather on stone.

But a body can't help flinching at some names. So she gave her flinches away freely, scattered them like seed corn. At *grain gate*, she breathed. At *river ferry*, she gripped the glass. At *mountain pass*, she closed her eyes as if in prayer. Let him read a map of everywhere and trust none of it.

The captain set down his pencil at last, dissatisfied, a man whose instrument had returned static. "You're a harder read than most, Mrs. Kessel."

"I've been reading rivers longer than you've been reading faces," she said. "Rivers cheat too."

He sent her back to the cell. In the dark, Ilse crept close. "Did they hurt you?"

"No."

"What did they want?"

"To know where a letter is going," Marta said. "And I couldn't have told them if I'd wanted to, child, because nobody tells the ferry anything. That's the whole trade. People hand you their secrets at the gangplank and step aboard lighter, and you carry the weight so they don't have to. Forty years of it." She lay back against the wall. "Go to sleep. Sleep is also resistance. Nobody ever told you that, but it's true."

* * *

On the second day they tried hunger, which was a mistake, because Marta had fasted every Lent since she was nine and regarded an empty stomach as an old acquaintance. On the second night they tried the opposite: a sergeant with a kind face and a story about a mother in the hills, about how he only wanted to go home, about how one name spoken quietly would let everyone — her, him, the girl, the whole valley — stop bleeding. He wept a little. It was well done. She had seen better acting at the autumn fair, but not much.

"Son," she said, when he finished, "if I had a name to sell, I'd have sold it to the lieutenant with the brandy, who offered more than tears. But I'll tell you something worth your trip. My husband drowned pulling a stranger's boy out of the flood. Never knew if the boy lived. Some nights I hope he did. Some nights I hope he grew up and forgot us, because forgetting is what being saved feels like from the inside." She touched the sergeant's sleeve. "Whatever name you're hunting — someone pulled it out of the water once too. Remember that when you find them."

He left without looking back. She heard him stop outside the cell door, stand a long moment, and walk on.

On the third morning, they brought the papers.

The captain himself this time, no brandy, two guards, a document on a clipboard. "Last offer, and then I stop offering. Confess to aiding the transport of a fugitive, sign, name any accomplices, and you serve five years' labor. Refuse, and under the emergency statutes I am authorized to treat silence as conspiracy, and conspiracy, in wartime, carries—" he let the sentence die, elegantly, letting her finish it herself.

"Carries what it carries," Marta said. "Everything carries what it carries. Boats. Rivers. Regimes." She looked at the paper without reaching for it. "Captain, I'm going to teach you something about ferries, since you're standing in my town. When a boat takes on water, the green passengers panic and start throwing things overboard — trunks, cargo, sometimes each other. But the ferryman knows: you don't throw anything over. You bail, and you steer, and you hold, because everything on that boat belongs to somebody, and the day you start throwing lives overboard to save the boat, you haven't saved the boat. You've become the flood." She met his eyes. "Sign your own papers. Leave mine blank."

Something flickered in the captain's face — not mercy, nothing so useful, but recognition, the look of a man meeting a fixed object he had assumed was soft. He clicked his pen shut.

"Three days we've spent on you," he said. "Three days the trail has gone cold while we interrogated a ferrywoman. Do you understand what you've bought with your stubbornness, Mrs. Kessel? Nothing. Whoever you protected is already miles from anywhere you could name, and we'll catch them anyway, and you'll have starved and frozen in a plank cell for a gesture." He gathered his papers. "Was it worth three days?"

*Three days.* The words went through her like the cold coming off the mountains, and she kept her face still, and only after the cell door shut did she let herself understand what she'd done, sitting down slowly on the straw with her hands in her lap.

Three days. She had given them three days, and she hadn't even known she was doing it — except that some part of her had known, the part that read water, the part that had watched the girl haul that case up the gangplank and thought: *she needs a head start, and head starts are measured in days, and I have days in me, I have a few more days in me yet.*

Eleven days ago, Bram Winterbourne — though Marta hadn't known the name then, only the jaw, only Aldous looking at her across the gunwale thirty years back saying *Marta, there may come a time when someone comes to you carrying something heavier than they look able to bear, and I ask you now, in advance, to bear it with them* — had stepped onto her deck. And Marta had owed that man one crossing, unasked, unexplained, a debt she'd carried so long she'd stopped calling it a debt and started calling it ballast. Now the ballast was paid, and paid dear, and the strange thing, the thing she would try to explain to Ilse in the dark and fail, was that she felt *lighter*. Lighter and colder, like a boat after unloading, riding high and easy on black water.

"Worth it?" she said aloud to the empty cell, answering the captain across the distance. "Ask me in the morning. Everything's worth it in the morning. Even mornings in here."

Ilse pressed against her side that night, and the schoolteacher pretended to sleep and listened, and somewhere beyond the granary walls the river went on running, taking everything downstream, sending no word back.

* * *

On the fourth day they stopped asking questions, which was the most frightening development yet, because armies only stopped asking when they started deciding. Marta was transferred at dusk to a wagon bound up-valley — "processing," the guards called it, that wonderful word that meant whatever the colonel needed it to mean that week. As the wagon rolled through the gate she caught one glimpse of the river between warehouses, one sliver of grey water going about its work, and lifted her cuffed hands as far as the chain allowed, a small wave, ferryman to ferryman, *you take it from here.*

In the wagon bench beside her, a boy of nineteen, arrested for hoarding print — actual printed pages, poems, hidden in a mattress — whispered sideways: "They say there's a woman soldier hunting the Winterbourne ghost. Sergeant Duvall. He posted a notice in the square this morning."

"Noticed it myself," lied Marta, who had noticed nothing but the inside of a cell.

"It described 'him,'" the boy said. "The heir. Height, build, the jaw. And then one more line, added in different ink, later, like an afterthought." He swallowed. "It said: *the fugitive is a woman.*"

Marta closed her eyes. The wagon lurched over the bridge stones, and beneath her, faint through the floorboards, she could feel the river — still running, still carrying, still keeping every secret ever handed to it at the gangplank — flowing north toward the mountains, toward the pass, toward wherever a girl with her father's jaw was walking even now with three days bought in blood and straw.

*Hold steady,* Marta told her silently, the old blessing, the one she'd murmured over ten thousand crossings. *Bad water ends. Hold the tiller and hold.*

Then the wagon turned up-valley, and the river went out of sight, and Marta Kessel rode on toward whatever they processed next, light as an unloaded boat, and colder, and free.
