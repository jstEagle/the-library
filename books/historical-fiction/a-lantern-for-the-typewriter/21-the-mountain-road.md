## 21. The Mountain Road

The road left the valley at dawn, and by midmorning Bram had stopped counting how many times she had wanted to stop.

It was not a road so much as an argument between the mountain and whoever had first tried to cross it — a track of loose shale and frozen mud that switchbacked up through pines gone black with cold, narrowing until two people could not walk abreast and had to fall into single file, the typewriter case slung across her back like a coffin too small for its occupant. The strap had worn a groove in her shoulder by the second hour. By the third she had stopped feeling it, which frightened her more than the pain had.

Elias walked ahead. He walked the way he did everything — precisely, without flourish, his army boots placed on the shale as though each step were an entry in a ledger that would later be audited. He had rolled the cuffs of his clerk's coat and bound the hem with cord to keep it from snagging, and somewhere in the last day the coat had stopped looking like a uniform and started looking like a poor man's traveling clothes, which was perhaps the only honest thing he owned.

"You're favoring your left knee," he said, without turning around.

"I'm not."

"Your gait changed a hundred meters back. If you're hurt, tell me now. Above the treeline there's nowhere to hide from weather or patrol."

Bram adjusted the strap and said nothing, because he was right, because he had been right about the ferry manifests and the grain gate's blind spot and the exact hour the pass sentry changed watch, and being known by him was still a sensation she had not learned to carry. It sat strangely against her ribs, this being read by a man whose profession was reading.

The trees thinned as they climbed. Below them Vellmarch had already vanished behind the shoulder of the mountain — no smoke, no bells, nothing to show that a town of nine thousand souls lay folded in the valley like a letter no one dared open. She thought of Oskar in whatever cell or safehouse held him now, thought of Marta below deck in some army holding pen, refusing to speak, spending her silence three days at a time. Every step upward was bought with someone else's coin. She climbed anyway. There was no other direction in which the ledger could be spent.

At noon they reached a shelf of flat stone where a shrine had once stood — just the base of it now, the saint long since toppled, the niche empty except for snow. Elias called a halt there, and they ate hard bread and cheese gone waxy with cold, and neither spoke for a while, because speaking cost breath and breath cost something they were both hoarding.

It was Elias who broke the quiet, in the end, and what he said surprised her.

"My father was a cooper," he said, staring down the valley at the white haze where the river ran. "Barrels. Good ones. He could make a cask hold wine for ten years without a seam weeping." He turned the bread over in his hands without eating it. "I signed my first requisition order when I was nineteen years old. Do you know what it was?"

Bram shook her head.

"A barrel. One wine barrel, requisitioned for officers' mess at the garrison at Hohlwald. My father's own work — I recognized the brand on the head before I finished reading the form." A short, airless laugh. "He never found out. Or he found out and never said. He died that winter, and I told myself the two things weren't connected, because they weren't, because one barrel is nothing, is *nothing*—" His hand closed around the bread until it crumbled. "That's how it starts. That's the whole machinery in miniature. You sign for a barrel, and five years later you sign for presses, and the signature looks exactly the same on the page. Same hand. Same ink."

Bram watched him. She had spent weeks cataloguing this man — the clerk who processed the requisition of her father's life's work, the coward who stamped papers through at the river checkpoint rather than search them, the deserter-in-place who followed her off the dock because his name was written in the ledger same as hers, an instrument rather than a victim. She had built a version of him out of those facts and kept it between them like a drawn blade. Now, on the mountain, the blade seemed suddenly a crude thing to have made.

"The first order you ever signed," she said. "You remember the number?"

"Of course I remember the number."

She almost smiled. "So do I remember every job number my father ever cut. The pamphlet run the night the soldiers drilled in the square — job 4471. The obituaries Marta ferried across the river for nothing but memory — 3902 through 3960, fifty-eight names." She looked at the empty shrine niche. "We're the same kind of fool, Elias Rehn. We think if we can hold the numbers, we're holding something."

"Are we not?"

"I don't know yet. Ask me at the border."

* * *

They slept that night in a shepherd's hut with half a roof, and Bram learned that Elias Rehn could not sleep unless he counted things.

She woke once in the black hours and heard him murmuring under his breath — not prayers; figures. Barrels requisitioned. Men transported. Forms filed. He was reciting his own ledger, she realized, the private one no archive held, and she lay still in the dark and listened to him count himself toward morning.

In the grey light before dawn she repaid him in kind, because that was the economy they had fallen into without either of them naming it: histories traded across small fires, confession for confession, debt for debt.

"My father didn't want me in the foundry," she said. They were walking again, the pines giving way at last to bare slope and sky. "Aldous Winterbourne wanted a son who'd inherit the forge and a daughter married off to a chandler in the low towns. He got neither. When the fever took my mother and then came for me and left me alive but with lungs full of holes, he stopped arguing with God and started teaching me the graver." Her breath smoked as she talked, climbing. "I was eleven. My hands were too small for the punches, so he made me a set scaled down, and I cut my first letter sitting on a beer crate — a lowercase e, Times face. He kept it in his pocket until the day he died. I found it after. In the same pocket as his tobacco tin, which tells you what he thought of it."

"And the disguise?" Elias asked carefully. "When did the son appear?"

"When the conscription notices did." The word tasted like iron filings. "Third year of the war. Every male of age in Vellmarch, name by name off the parish rolls. Aldous stood in the square and listened to his son's name read out — a son who existed only on paper, because he'd registered the foundry heir as male from the start, to keep the trade license clean — and he went home and looked at me across the workbench and said, 'Well. Bram has always been thin. We'll keep his shoulders down.'" She heard her own voice go flat and strange. "He sewed the binding myself. Taught me to bind. Taught me to lower my voice by breathing wrong, which ruined my singing forever, which I minded more at fourteen than I have ever minded since."

"And your name? Your true one?"

"No." She stopped on the trail and faced him. Wind pulled at her cropped hair. "That one isn't for the mountain. That one I'll spend where it buys something — at the press office, in front of witnesses, or not at all. You'll have it when it costs me everything to say it. Not before."

Elias held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man initialing a clause he understood perfectly.

"That's fair," he said. "I wouldn't trust anyone who gave it away cheap."

* * *

The storm found them on the third day, and by the fourth they were climbing inside a cloud.

The world contracted to a circle of ten meters: grey vapor, black rock, the case's weight shifting on her shoulders with each switchback. Elias navigated by counting paces and compass bearings he checked obsessively, muttering figures again — the same arithmetic, but now pointed forward instead of back, toward survival instead of sin. Somewhere above them, invisible through the murk, waited the third checkpoint and its commander, who by now had been told — Duvall saw to everything, Duvall missed nothing — that a woman dressed as a man was moving toward the pass with stolen army property.

They sheltered the fourth night in the lee of a boulder field, backs against stone, sharing a single blanket because Bram's had soaked through crossing a melt-swollen stream. Cold like this made pretense impossible; huddled hip to hip for warmth, she felt less exposed than she had in months. Bodies in extremity are honest bodies.

"Elias." His name in the dark, barely voiced.

"Mm."

"Why did you really request transfer to the river checkpoint? The truth. Not the ledger answer."

Silence, long enough that she thought he'd refuse. Then, quietly: "Because I processed the requisition of the Winterbourne foundry. Because when I walked the seized floor and saw the type laid out in trays — your father's type, the stamps matched the files — I recognized the punchcuts. The lowercase e." A breath. "There was a specimen book in the archive. Foundry marks, faces, dates. Your father's hand was in that book since before I was born. And I had signed the paper that made it all contraband, and I stood in that beautiful dead workshop knowing that within a month the type would be melted for shot and the ledgers burned, and I thought: I am the exact weight on the scale here. Nothing tips without me." His voice roughened. "I requested the river checkpoint because the river was where things could still slip through. Where *I* could let them. I told myself it was strategy. It was begging."

Bram stared into the dark, at nothing, at everything.

"He'd have liked you," she said at last. "My father. He believed a person was a piece of type — that you couldn't change the character once cast, only decide where in the line you set it." She pulled the blanket higher. "You cast yourself badly young. You're choosing your place in the line now. He'd have said that's all any of us get."

In the dark beside her, Elias Rehn made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else entirely, and said, "Go to sleep, Winterbourne. Four thousand paces to the tree line tomorrow."

But sleep came slowly, and when it came, Bram dreamed of the forge — of lamplight, of a small steel e held in a large scarred palm, of her father saying *every letter cut is a name refused to oblivion*, and beneath the dream, beneath the wind, she heard something that did not belong to the dream at all: far off down the valley, carried impossibly on the storm, the sound of dogs.

* * *

They crossed the tree line at first light and stopped as if struck.

Below them, spread out along the road they had climbed for four days, moved a column of soldiers — infantry, mules, supply carts, the pennant of Colonel Hessler's own regiment snapping red in the wind. Not pursuing them. Worse than pursuing them: *reinforcing*. Whatever awaited at the pass checkpoint was about to be doubled in men, doubled in eyes, doubled in rifles.

Elias lowered the spyglass he'd taken from a dead patrolman's pack a week ago and handed it to her without a word.

Through the lens Bram watched the column crawl, and picked out, riding near its head on a grey horse, a figure she knew by posture alone even at this distance: upright, patient, thorough. Sergeant Duvall rode toward the pass like a man going to collect a debt.

"Well," Bram said, handing back the glass. Her voice was steady. Inside, something was already running the numbers — sentries, sight lines, the forged seal in her breast pocket, the gallows price of forgery discovered. "Then we'd best be someone else by nightfall."

Elias was already unrolling the oilcloth from around their remaining supplies, already reaching for the blank paper and her father's travel kit of punches. "How good are you at cutting a seal," he asked, "when your hands are frozen?"
