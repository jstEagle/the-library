## 22. Ambush at the Chapel

The chapel came out of the fog like a thing remembered rather than seen.

Bram had been walking since before dawn with her hand flat against the typewriter case, as if touch alone could keep its contents safe. The mountain road had narrowed through the morning into a shelf of stone and pine root, and Elias walked ahead of her in his army coat with the collar up, moving with the careful economy of a man who no longer trusted his own body to carry him. He had been shot at twice on the river. He limped now without admitting it.

"There," he said, and stopped.

It was a small church, or what remained of one — a squat stone nave with a roof half fallen, a bell tower like a broken tooth. No cross on the gable. Someone had pried it off, or it had never been there. The door hung open on darkness.

"Shrine road," Elias said. "Pilgrims used to stop here on the way to the pass." He said *used to* the way he said everything lately, with the past tense doing heavy lifting.

"We could go around."

"We could. And lose four hours and arrive at the pass after dark, in weather that's already turning." He looked up. The clouds sat low and gray, heavy with the first suggestion of snow. "Or we shelter for an hour, eat something, and walk the last stretch with our legs under us."

Bram looked at the dark doorway. She thought of Marta below deck on the ferry, hands folded, saying nothing while soldiers stamped overhead. She thought of the deserter's warning back at the river checkpoint: *the mountains are full of men who took off the uniform and kept the rifle.* Elias had told her the same, more carefully, somewhere past the second village of burned registries.

"They'll be armed," she said.

"Yes."

"And we have a typewriter and your service pistol you barely know how to use."

"I know exactly how little I know how to use it," Elias said. "Which is why I'd rather meet whoever's in there sitting down than standing up and looking like something worth shooting."

They went in.

The chapel smelled of cold ash and pigeon droppings. The roof over the altar had collapsed inward, letting down a shaft of gray light that fell across broken pews stacked like driftwood. Bram's eyes adjusted slowly. There was a fire pit in the center aisle, ringed with stones, still warm. Tin cups. A blanket nailed over the empty window where a saint might once have stood.

"Occupied," she whispered.

"Was," Elias said. "An hour ago, maybe less—"

"Put your hands where I can see them."

The voice came from behind the ruined altar, and then there were three of them, stepping out of shadow with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times. Two carried army rifles, old pattern, well maintained. The third held a woodcutter's axe with both hands, casually, the way a man holds a tool he has grown tired of but not afraid of.

They were young. That was the first obscenity of it. The tallest couldn't have been twenty-five. All three wore fragments of uniform — a tunic here, trousers there — mixed with civilian wool, as though they had each decided separately how much soldier they were willing to remain.

Elias raised his hands. Bram did the same, keeping her left on the typewriter case's handle.

"Easy," Elias said. "We're not patrol."

"No," said the tall one. His eyes went to Elias's coat, to the cut of it, the buttons. "You're worse. You're staff." He spat into the ash. "Clerk."

The word landed like a slap. Bram felt Elias flinch — not much, a millimeter of the shoulders, but she had spent three days learning to read him the way she once learned to read type, letter by letter, flaw by flaw.

"My name is Rehn," Elias said steadily. "Second requisition bureau, Vellmarch district. And yes. I processed orders. And I'm walking away from all of it, which is more than I can say for anyone still wearing the whole uniform."

"Bold," said the one with the axe. He was older, maybe thirty-five, with a beard gone gray at the chin and a scar through one eyebrow. "Talking. Men who talk usually have nothing worth taking. Search them, Piotr."

The tall one — Piotr — came forward and did it efficiently, with the impersonal thoroughness of a man frisking sacks of grain. He found Elias's pistol and pocketed it. He found the bread and cheese from the last village and set them aside without ceremony. Then his hands reached the typewriter case, and Bram's whole body went rigid around it.

"It's just a machine," she said. Her widow's voice came out cracked and true. "My husband's. Worthless. I'm taking it to his brother beyond the pass because he asked me to and I promised, God help me, I promised him—"

"Open it."

"You can't mean to—" She let herself shake. It was not entirely an act; some part of her was shaking anyway. "It's all I have of him. Take the bread. Take my rings, take the coat off my back, but—"

"*Open it,*" Piotr said again, and this time there was something underneath the hardness — not cruelty, but a kind of hunger, and Bram recognized it with a lurch. It was curiosity. These men had lost everything worth owning. Curiosity was the only appetite left that could be fed.

She knelt and opened the case.

The typewriter sat inside, black and ordinary, its keys dulled with disuse. For one long second Bram allowed herself to believe that would be enough — a dead man's machine, junk, exactly the story she had rehearsed on a hundred miles of road.

Then the older one with the axe crossed the aisle in three strides, lifted the typewriter out with one hand, and turned the case upside down.

Nothing fell. But the lining of the lid bulged wrong, and everyone saw it, and the silence in the ruined chapel became total. Even the pigeons in the tower seemed to hold their breath.

"Cut it," the older man said quietly.

"Don't—" Bram started.

He didn't cut it. He worked the stitching open with the point of his knife the way a surgeon opens a wound, careful, almost respectful, and the ledger slid out into the light of the broken roof — canvas-bound, swollen with pages, Aldous Winterbourne's cramped hand on the flyleaf and hundreds of names marching beneath it in columns.

The axe-man went very still.

"Piotr," he said. "Come look at this."

* * *

They built the fire up. It seemed important to them suddenly, absurdly — three deserters crouching over flames in a dead church while the light died outside, feeding sticks into a pit in the middle of a nave. They made Bram and Elias sit on a pew with their backs to the wall, within reach, within sight. Nobody had returned Elias's pistol. Nobody had touched the food.

The older man read.

He read the way illiterate men sometimes read — slowly, with his lips moving, tracing each name with a blunt finger as if the finger could carry the meaning up to his eyes. Piotr leaned over his shoulder. The third one, a boy of perhaps nineteen with a bandaged hand, sat apart, hugging his knees, watching.

"Vasyl Kremen," the older man read aloud. "Corporal. Disappeared, eleventh of March, Vellmarch garrison stockade. Reported by his mother, Anna Kremen, weaver's lane." He stopped. He read it again. Then he said, to nobody, to the ash, "That's Jozef Kremen's brother. Jozef Kremen who slept two bunks down from me at Fort Drava for two years and cried when his mother's letters came because he couldn't read them himself."

Nobody answered. He turned the page.

"Tomasz Brel. Ferryman's son. Taken from the fish market, ninth of April. No charge recorded. Body released to family on condition of immediate burial, no viewing permitted." His finger moved down the column. "Mira Soltesz. Schoolteacher. Mira Soltesz, Christ, my sister had her for letters. My sister can read because of this woman and she's in here between a cooper and a boy of fourteen."

"How do you know it's true?" Piotr said, but his voice had changed; it wasn't a challenge anymore, it was a plea. He wanted it not to be true, and he wanted it confirmed, and both wants were breaking him open at once.

"The handwriting changes," the older man said. "Look. Different inks. Different years. Somebody's been keeping this a long time, page by page, whenever it could be kept." He looked up, and for the first time he looked directly at Bram — not at the widow, not at the case, but at her, with eyes the color of wet slate. "Who wrote this?"

"A type cutter," Bram said. There was no longer any point in the widow's voice; whatever story she told next would have to stand beside these names. "In Vellmarch. He copied every record he could reach. Arrest lists, requisition manifests, parish registers before they were burned. Names, dates, what was done. Where the bodies went, when bodies went anywhere at all."

"And you're carrying it over the pass."

"To a foreign press office. So it gets printed. So every name comes back down the mountain on paper that can't be requisitioned." She swallowed. "So their mothers finally learn which lie they were told."

The old man closed the ledger. He held it against his chest with both arms, the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"What's your name?" Bram asked him. It was a risk. She asked it anyway.

A long pause. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the tower a pigeon shifted.

"Havel," he said at last. "Sergeant Havel, once. Fourth Rifles. Before I was a thief on a shrine road." He laughed, short and joyless. "We heard stories about this book. In the camps, before the fourth of us ran. Men said the cutter of Vellmarch was writing everything down, and we called it a ghost story to make ourselves feel less alone. Like thinking someone is keeping a candle lit for you in a window somewhere." His thumb moved along the ledger's spine. "And it's real. And it was walking up our road in a typewriter case."

"We don't want trouble with you," Elias said carefully.

"You don't want trouble with anyone," Havel said. "That's your whole condition, clerk. That's what your whole species is — men who arrange trouble for others so they never have to stand inside it themselves." But he said it without heat, almost wearily, and then he did something none of them expected. He opened the ledger again to the first page, and began, deliberately, to read the names aloud. Not to Bram. Not to Elias. To the chapel. To the broken roof and the gray light and the two boys who had followed him into the trees.

"Vasyl Kremen. Tomasz Brel. Mira Soltesz..."

The boy with the bandaged hand began to cry without noise, tears sliding down a face that made no expression at all, and Piotr put a hand on the back of his neck like a brother.

When Havel finished the first page he closed the book and looked at Elias.

"You signed requisitions."

"Some," Elias said. "I signed the one that seized the Winterbourne foundry. I signed dozens of paper transfers that moved men from garrison cells to places I was ordered not to record. I told myself I was only ink. That the hand that moves the pen isn't the hand that pulls the trigger." He met Havel's stare. "Then I watched what my ink did, and I requested transfer to a river checkpoint so I could start stamping people *through* instead of *away*, and now I'm here, robbed at gunpoint in a ruin, and I find I'm grateful to be robbed by men reading names instead of men burning them. Judge me however you like. I've already filed the verdict against myself. It's in that book too, if your cutter was thorough. Look under instruments of requisition, Vellmarch district. My signature will be there. I want it printed beside theirs. That's part of why I'm walking this road."

Havel stared at him for a long time. Then, incredibly, he laughed — one bark of it, echoing off the stones.

"A clerk who wants his own name in the indictment." He shook his head. "I've shot at better men for saying stupider things."

* * *

They shared the bread. It seemed the natural conclusion of everything, though Bram could not have said how they arrived at it — the despoiled pilgrims and their robbers, seated among the broken pews, passing a loaf and a hard cheese while the fire sank to embers. Havel refused to give back the pistol. "Road tax," he said, and almost smiled. He gave back the bread.

The boy with the bandaged hand — his name was Lukas, and he spoke perhaps thirty words in an hour, most of them to the fire — sat down next to Bram at some point and looked at the ledger where it lay closed between Havel's boots.

"My brother's in there," he said. "I checked. While you were talking." His bandaged hand opened and closed. "Page forty. Stefan Lukasz Veyra. I ran because they told me he'd deserted and been shot, and I believed them, and then I found out there was never any record at all. No trial. No grave. Just gone, and me carrying the shame of a desertion that never happened." He looked at her. "Is it really going to be printed? All of it?"

"That's the entire purpose of my life," Bram said. "Everything else is detail."

Lukas nodded slowly, absorbing this the way stone absorbs rain.

It was Piotr, unexpectedly, who raised the practical question. "How many are you?" he asked, nodding at the ledger. "Names."

"Thousands," Bram said. "My father counted eleven hundred and forty the last time he showed me, and he was still copying."

"Eleven hundred and forty," Havel repeated. He turned it over in his mouth like a coin of foreign mint. "There are maybe nine hundred men between the garrisons and the pass who've thrown down their rifles and don't know yet what they're for. Deserted, straggled, been thrown out. Half of them think they're the only ones. Half the rest think the war will swallow them and no one will ever write down that they existed." He tapped the ledger with one boot toe. "But somebody wrote down that other people existed. Somebody kept the count when the counting was forbidden." He looked up, and Bram saw the decision arrive in his face the way she had once seen type set — letter by letter, irreversible. "That's an army, clerk. Or the seed of one. Not the colonel's kind. The other kind. The kind that fights with a list."

"You're suggesting what, exactly?" Elias said. "That we raise a rebellion on the mountain road?"

"I'm suggesting that a book like this doesn't get across a border alone. And that the men guarding the passes answer to the same officers who emptied those pages, and that there are ways through those lines that no widow and no clerk will ever be shown." Havel leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight moving on his scarred face. "I'm suggesting you've been lucky for three days and luck is not a route. I know the shepherd tracks above the chapel. I know which sentries drink. I know the commander at the third checkpoint takes his bribe in tobacco and his orders in writing, and hates the writing." He glanced at Piotr and Lukas. Piotr shrugged with his whole body — *you're the sergeant*. Lukas said nothing, but he moved closer to the fire, closer to them, which was answer enough.

"You want to come with us," Bram said.

"I want those names printed more than I want anything I've wanted since I took off the uniform," Havel said. "And I want them printed by someone who'll live long enough to see it, which you won't manage alone. So yes." He picked up the ledger and held it out to her, both hands, formally, the way colors are surrendered. "I'm giving this back. Understand what that costs a man who owns nothing else. And understand what I expect to get for it — not payment. A place in the column. When the presses run, I want to stand where I can watch eleven hundred and forty names come off the drum, and I want Lukas to find his brother's page and keep it."

Bram took the ledger. Its weight was different now — heavier, or rather distributed differently, carried by more hands than hers. She thought of her father cutting letters by lamplight, believing no one would ever read what he assembled except history, if history survived. History was sitting in a ruined chapel eating stolen bread, and it had a scar through its eyebrow and a brother on page forty.

"Then we move tonight," she said. "Before the weather closes."

Havel was already shaking his head. "Tonight we sleep. All of us. You've got a day of climbing ahead and you'll do it on legs like wet rope, and I won't guide corpses." He nodded toward the tower stairs. "There's straw in the vestry. We keep watches." Then he paused, and his slate eyes went past Bram's shoulder to the north, where the fog was thickening against the broken windows, and his voice dropped into something flat and certain.

"And tomorrow we climb fast. Because I've been up this road six weeks, and I know every smuggler's sign from here to the pass, and today, coming down through the trees, I saw fresh chalk marks on the boundary stones." He let that sit in the cold air. "Army marks. Route markers. Somebody's been through recently, somebody official, posting the mountain. Which means word of a fugitive has reached the pass ahead of you." He fed the last stick into the embers, and the fire flared, throwing all their shadows huge and trembling up the ruined walls.

"So whatever you three were running from," Havel said, "it's waiting for you at the top."
