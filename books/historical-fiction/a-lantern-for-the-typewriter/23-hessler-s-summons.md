## 23. Hessler's Summons

The requisition of a town, Colonel Hessler had learned in nineteen years of service, was a matter of arithmetic before it was ever a matter of violence. A town was a column of figures: so many mouths, so many rifles it might hide, so many presses that could turn grievance into paper and paper into fire. Vellmarch's figures had never balanced to his satisfaction. The town produced more resentment per capita than any garrison on the river, and it did so quietly, which was the worst kind of production there was.

He sat in the commandant's residence that the army had taken over in the first week — a merchant's house with good light and bad acoustics, where every word spoken in the study carried up through the floorboards like water through cloth — and he read Sergeant Duvall's report for the third time.

Duvall wrote well. That was unusual in a sergeant and Hessler noted it the way he noted all anomalies: without warmth, as data. The report ran eleven pages, cross-referenced, with ink samples sealed in wax at the back and a diagram of the Winterbourne type foundry showing the cellar stair, the press room, the position of the lamp that burned past midnight. The central finding occupied a single underlined sentence near the top:

*The heir Aldous Winterbourne, registered as Bram, son of the deceased cutter, does not exist as described. The person working the foundry by night is a woman.*

Hessler set the pages down and looked out the window at the square. Rain again. It had rained for nine days and the river had come up brown and patient against the ferry landing, and the soldiers drilling in the square had begun to look like men who were drowning slowly in a place where nothing could drown them. He understood their mood. Occupation was a kind of standing water. It bred things.

The problem, as always, was not whether the finding was true. He had no reason to doubt Duvall; the man was meticulous to the point of obsession, and obsession made honest witnesses if you kept it pointed outward. The problem was what the finding was *for*. A fact, unattached to an action, was dead weight. An empire did not run on facts. It ran on the uses facts could be put to, and the uses had to be chosen with care, because a wrong use cost more than ignorance ever had.

He rang for his adjutant.

"Bring me the file on the pacification schedule," he said. "And the casualty projections from the last district review. And coffee that has been made this week."

The adjutant — a pale lieutenant named Kessel who had the gift of appearing already regretful — brought all three within ten minutes, including, remarkably, drinkable coffee. Hessler worked while he waited, because waiting was for other men. He spread the documents in the lamplight and did what he always did: reduced the situation to its numbers.

Vellmarch: population four thousand two hundred at last census, probably less now; the requisition had driven off perhaps three hundred souls across the border or into the hills. Garrison: one hundred eighty effective. Sympathizers: unknowable, but the pamphlets told him something. In six months the army had intercepted forty-one separate printings of seditious material from inside the walls. Forty-one printings meant paper, ink, type, labor, and distribution — an entire invisible industry running beneath the town's respectable surface like a river under ice. They had seized presses, yes. Eleven of them. And still the pamphlets came, thinner now, cruder, printed on salvaged paper by smaller machines, which meant the industry had not died but *dispersed*, and dispersed industries were the ones you could not kill with a single blow.

What you needed, then, was not another seizure. You needed a story.

This was the lesson of the last district review, written in the dry language of casualty projections: in towns where the army had punished broadly, resistance had gone underground and multiplied; in towns where the army had punished *specifically* — one name, one face, one crime narrated clearly enough that everyone could agree on its shape — the population had done the work themselves, informing on neighbors, policing grief, keeping their heads down through sheer social pressure. People did not fear the state. People feared each other, and the state's genius lay in arranging introductions.

For that you needed a traitor. Not a martyr — martyrs multiplied. A traitor. Someone whose betrayal could be made legible, whose crime could be explained at a market stall in one breath. Someone who had profited while others suffered.

Hessler picked up Duvall's report again and read the middle section, the part about the foundry.

*The Winterbourne establishment operated openly as a licensed cutter of type throughout the period of sedition. Its output of matrices and sorts cannot be accounted for by its commercial orders. The inference is unavoidable: the foundry supplied the illegal presses. The heir — if such a person exists — has concealed this activity behind the fiction of a dead father's legitimate trade. I further note that the requisition inventory lists the foundry's stock as incomplete against its own ledgers, indicating removal of materials prior to seizure.*

There it was. Hessler felt something that was not quite pleasure — he had trained himself out of pleasure years ago, considering it a security risk — but a kind of professional satisfaction, the click of a mechanism seating properly. A type foundry. A business everyone knew, a family everyone liked, a dead man whose name was spoken with respect in half the houses on the river road. If the Winterbournes had armed the pamphleteers, then the pamphlets were not anonymous grievance after all; they were a *product*, manufactured by people the town had trusted, sold to it disguised as truth. The story almost told itself.

Almost. There were gaps, and Hessler was too old a hand to paper over gaps. He took out a pencil and wrote questions in the margin, small and precise:

*Where is the heir now? Why has no arrest been effected? What is the evidentiary chain between ink sample and press? Who else knew?*

The last question mattered most. A traitor story only worked if the traitor had accomplices, because accomplices gave the population someone to inform on. One criminal was a curiosity. A network was an invitation.

He summoned Kessel again.

"Send for Sergeant Duvall."

* * *

Duvall arrived damp, having crossed the square at a pace that suggested he considered rain a personal affront. He stood at attention before the desk with his cap under his arm and water dripping off his collar onto the good carpet, and Hessler let him stand a moment longer than necessary, not from cruelty but from habit. Men talked in silences. They filled pauses the way water filled holes.

"You've requested authority to detain the Winterbourne heir," Hessler said. "Twice."

"Yes, Colonel."

"The second request was more detailed than the first. Why?"

"Because the evidence improved." Duvall's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man walking on river ice. "Fresh ink in the press room dated after the requisition. Type matched to three pamphlet fragments by punch identification. And the matter of identity. The heir registered as male is not male. I've confirmed it twice, independently."

"Confirmed how?"

"Observation, sir. Over time." Something moved at the edge of Duvall's face, quickly suppressed — not sympathy exactly, Hessler thought. Recognition, maybe. The discomfort of a man who had watched someone perform a self for years and could no longer quite believe performances were lies. "The point is the deception. If the foundry's heir will falsify himself for months, he'll falsify anything. Papers. Inventories. Names."

Hessler turned a page without looking at it. "You want to arrest this person."

"I want the foundry secured and the person detained. Yes, sir."

"And then?"

"Then interrogation. Then—" Duvall paused.

"Then a trial," Hessler finished for him. "A public one. Very well. Now tell me what happens next, Sergeant, in the town. Take your time. Think like the town, not like the army."

Duvall thought. Hessler watched him do it — watched the sergeant's eyes go somewhere above the colonel's shoulder, watching the town arrange itself in his mind the way a man watches weather. When Duvall spoke again his voice had lost some of its certainty.

"There'd be anger. The family is respected. The father especially. Half the obituaries in this town these five years came off his presses — proper ones, paid for, when no one else would print the names." Duvall stopped. "Sir, I know what the theory says. But I've walked that street. If we hang the foundry's heir on pamphlet fragments, the town won't believe it. They'll believe we invented it. They'll believe it *harder* because they want to."

"And if we demolish the building instead? Publish the evidence, tear it down stone by stone, and account for the family in the process?"

Something flickered across Duvall's face then — fast, gone — that Hessler catalogued without naming. The sergeant had spent weeks hunting this woman through ink samples and midnight lamps. There is a particular intimacy in a hunt. Hessler had seen it before: pursuers who arrived at their quarry's door feeling strangely bereaved.

"Demolition doesn't require belief," Hessler said, answering his own question. "It requires only sight. A hanging makes a martyr you can argue about. A ruin makes a fact you walk around every day. The town sees the foundry down and understands that the price of harboring printers is measured in roof beams. No argument survives a roof beam, Sergeant. Argument requires shelter."

"It also requires," Duvall said slowly, "that the family be dealt with first. Or the ruin becomes a shrine."

"Which brings us to the phrase I dislike most in our profession." Hessler drew the requisition schedule toward him and uncapped his pen. "*Accounted for.*"

He wrote as he spoke, in the neat, joyless hand that had signed away granaries and school registers and, once, a hospital wing.

"The heir is to be apprehended. Alive if practical — a trial is useful even a closed one, for the record — but alive is a preference, not a requirement. Any associates encountered in the act are subject to field judgment. The father is dead; the file closes itself. What matters is the record shows the family was given due process, that due process found guilt, and that consequence followed. Whether the process was due is a question for historians, and historians are not stationed in Vellmarch."

Duvall said nothing. Rain worked at the window. Down in the square, faintly, a drill sergeant's voice rose and fell like a bird that had learned only one song.

"You have reservations," Hessler said. It was not a question.

"I have observations, sir."

"Give me one."

Duvall chose his words the way a man crosses a flooded street, testing each stone. "The heir left the town. Before I could effect detention. With a typewriter case, according to the ferry manifests, and a widow's papers that fooled Marta Voss, who has known the family thirty years. The case went through my own grain gate under burlap, through the river checkpoint — stamped by Clerk Rehn, who has since transferred and will be spoken to — and onto the mountain road. Whoever this person is, Colonel, they're not hiding anymore. They're moving. Toward the pass. Toward the foreign press office, if they have any sense, and everything about this individual suggests sense."

"So the theory travels."

"The theory travels. And the description travels with it. I've drafted it." Duvall produced a folded paper and laid it on the desk. "I've included the sex of the fugitive. I understand the sensitivity, sir, but a description that omits it is a description that fails, and a failed description means the ledger — if there is a ledger, and I believe there is — reaches the border."

Hessler unfolded the paper. The draft was precise, almost loving in its detail: height, build, the way the fugitive walked, the calluses described in terms a checkpoint man could feel for in a handshake. *Disguised as male. Carrying or forwarding a typewriter case of unusual weight.* At the bottom, the physical description of the face, rendered plainly: a face, Hessler supposed, that could belong to anyone, which was the trouble with faces.

"You realize," Hessler said, reading it twice, "what you have just done. The moment this description posts, the disguise dies. And a woman traveling alone on the mountain road with a price on her head—"

"—is a woman who stops being able to hide," Duvall said. "Yes, sir. That's the intent."

"It's also a woman the countryside may decide to shelter. Out of pity, if nothing else. Pity is more dangerous than politics. Politics changes with the season. Pity breeds."

"Then we post it everywhere at once," Duvall said, "and move faster than the breeding."

Hessler looked at him for a long moment. The sergeant was wet, tired, and certain, and Hessler had commanded enough men to know that certainty of this texture — earned, incremental, built out of ink samples and sleepless nights — was the most reliable material an officer ever got to work with. He approved the description. He signed the detention order. Then, at the bottom of the demolition authorization, he paused, pen hovering.

One thing still bothered him. Not morally — he had long ago arranged his conscience the way a quartermaster arranges stores, by category and access, with the heavy items locked below and rarely inventoried. What bothered him was structural. A story required completeness. The traitor story had a hole in it the size of a typewriter case.

"If the foundry armed the pamphleteers," he said, "where are the pamphleteers' files? Every printing operation keeps records — costs, contacts, distributions. Requisition seized eleven presses and not one page of organization papers. Either the records don't exist, or they were moved. And if they were moved, Sergeant, they were moved by someone who knew the requisition was coming."

Duvall went very still.

"You're suggesting the clerk."

"I'm suggesting," Hessler said, "that Elias Rehn processed the requisition, had access to every inventory and every timetable, transferred himself to the river checkpoint the same week the Winterbourne heir passed through it, stamped that heir through without search, and then abandoned his post to follow her up the mountain road. I am suggesting that when your theory and my arithmetic meet, they produce the same sum from different directions." He signed. "Rehn is now a fugitive in aid. Add him to the description. Two sets of boots on one road are easier to track than one."

"They'll reach the pass in days," Duvall said. "If the snow hasn't closed it."

"The snow is doing our work for us. A closed pass funnels everything through the third checkpoint. Nothing crosses the mountains except through a door we hold." Hessler capped his pen and stood, and the meeting was over in the way his meetings ended, abruptly, leaving men holding more than they'd expected to carry. "Sergeant. When you take the foundry down — and you will take it down personally, today — leave the sign hanging."

Duvall blinked. "Sir?"

"The sign. Winterbourne & Son, Cutters of Type. Leave it on the rubble. Let the town read it every morning on their way to market. A name on a ruin accuses better than any proclamation. Proclamations get torn down. Ruins don't." Hessler gathered his papers into their folder, squared their edges. "And send a wire to the pass commander. Full text of both descriptions. Tell him the woman cuts type — that she can forge anything short of a soul. Tell him to trust seals less than hands, and hands less than hesitations. A forger hesitates over her own work. It's the one flaw in the craft. Pride."

"Yes, Colonel."

"One more thing." Hessler moved to the window. Below, the drill sergeant was marching his wet men in circles through the puddles, teaching them to keep formation in weather, which was the entire art of occupation boiled down to a morning exercise. "When the town asks why — and it will ask why, at market, in church, over fences — the answer is the pamphlets. Not the ledger. Never mention a ledger. A ledger implies names worth protecting, and names worth protecting imply a resistance worth joining. The official record states that the foundry supplied seditious presses, that the family fled justice, and that the army responded with proportionate severity. Say it exactly that way. Proportionate. The word does half the work."

Duvall saluted and went. Hessler heard his boots on the stairs, unhurried, a man descending into weather with a job inside him.

Alone, the colonel stood at the window a while longer. Across the square, smoke rose thin and gray from the bakery chimney — the one enterprise in Vellmarch the requisition had spared, because armies learn early never to touch the bread. A woman came out of the baker's door with a shawl over her head and stood a moment in the rain looking toward the river, and Hessler found himself wondering, idly, whether she was looking at the water or thinking of someone on the far side of it, and then he stopped wondering, because wonder was an expense the schedule did not carry.

By evening it was done. The wire had gone to the pass. The descriptions were printed on the army press in three hundred copies, and soldiers pasted them to doors and gateposts and the ferry office wall, beside the requisition orders they were rapidly replacing, so that the town's doors became a running correspondence between the state and its enemies. By full dark, Sergeant Duvall's detail reached the foundry with axes and lanterns, and the sound of it — methodical, rhythmic, oddly like printing, strike after strike after strike — carried across the water to the hills, where anyone listening could count the blows and understand that a name was being demolished in Vellmarch, and that the army believed the name was alone.

The army was wrong. Three days' ride east, in a village with no names on its doors, the name had company: a disgraced clerk who slept badly, a deserter who kept watch, and a woman cutting letters by candlelight with a steadiness that would have unsettled the colonel deeply, had he seen it — because it was the steadiness of a craftsman, and craftsmen, whatever else may be said of them, always finish what they cut.

The wire reached the pass commander at dawn. He read it twice, looked out at the snow-choked defile, and gave the order that closed the third checkpoint to all traffic, civilian and military alike — which meant that when Bram Winterbourne came down out of the hills, she would find not a gate, but a wall, and behind the wall, waiting, a sergeant who had learned her face by lamplight.
