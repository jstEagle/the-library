## 24. The Pass Closed

The snow began in the afternoon of the second day on the mountain road, and by morning it had erased the world.

Bram woke to a silence so complete it felt like deafness. She lay in the lee of a shepherd's hut — a dry-stone shelter no bigger than a coffin stood upright, its roof half caved, its doorway facing away from the wind — and for a long moment she could not remember where the sky ended and the ground began. Everything was white. The pines were white. The boulders were white. The narrow track they had followed up from the chapel was a rumor beneath a foot of fresh fall, and somewhere beyond it, hidden now behind curtains of blowing snow, stood the pass.

She crawled out into air that cut her throat like a blade. Behind her, inside the hut, Elias stirred among the blankets, coughing the wet rattle that had lived in his chest since the river. In the corner, wrapped in his army coat with the buttons turned, the deserter slept like a man who had learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing to last.

"Corvin," Bram said. "Get up. I want eyes on the pass before the light's fully come."

The deserter opened one eye. He was perhaps twenty-three, with a farm boy's broad hands and a face already lined by things no twenty-three-year-old face should carry. He'd told them once, around the chapel fire, that he had deserted after being ordered to burn a granary with the family locked inside the threshing floor. He said it without drama, the way men say terrible things when the terrible thing has become ordinary. His name wasn't Corvin — he'd admitted that much — but he answered to it, and names, Bram had learned, were things you could put on and take off like coats.

He rose without complaint and climbed the rise above the hut, moving carefully, keeping below the ridgeline out of habit even here, where there was nothing to see them but snow and God, if either cared to look.

Elias came to the doorway, hunched in a blanket, his breath smoking. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that we should have crossed three days ago." She didn't soften it. She had stopped softening things for Elias somewhere on the mountain road, somewhere between her father's forge and his first signed order, somewhere in the long gray hours when they had traded histories the way starving men trade bread. "If the drifts have closed the high track, there's only the checkpoint road."

"The checkpoint road is the checkpoint."

"Yes."

They looked at each other in the white morning. Between them, unspoken, sat everything the road had taught them: that they trusted each other more than either had ever planned to; that this trust was built on a shared crime — his stamp, her forgery, both of them liars who had agreed to tell one truth together; and that the truth was sewn into the lining of a battered typewriter case which Bram now carried the way other people carried children, or wounds.

Below the rise, Corvin whistled — two notes, low. *Come see. And bring your stomach.*

* * *

From the ridge, through a tear in the weather, they saw it.

The pass was a notch in the mountains like the gap under a door, and through that gap ran the only road: a shelf of stone cut into the mountainside, wide enough for a cart, dropping away on the far side into the border country where the foreign press office waited with its typesetters and its editors and its cowardly fear of war. At the near mouth of the notch stood the third checkpoint — a blockhouse of raw timber, a palisade of sharpened stakes, a barrier beam across the road black against the snow.

And it was not a small thing anymore.

When Oskar had mapped this route in Vellmarch, months ago, in another life, he had described the third checkpoint as "a toll house with delusions" — two sentries, a stove, a commander who took bribes in brandy. What stood in the notch now was a fortress. Sod roofs on new dugouts. A flag snapping in the wind. Sentries walking the palisade in pairs. A wire fence running from the cliff face down to the drop, strung with something that caught the light and glittered — Bram needed no glass to know it was barbed. Wagons stood queued on the approach road, their drivers huddled at their horses' heads, waiting in a line that did not move.

"They've garrisoned it," Corvin said flatly. He passed Bram a battered spyglass — taken, she suspected, from a dead officer whose boots he also wore. "That's not a winter posting. That's a statement."

She put the glass to her eye and swept the checkpoint slowly, learning it the way she had once learned letterforms: patiently, stroke by stroke, until the whole was legible. Timber blockhouse with a signal tower on its roof — heliograph shutters, meaning they talked to the valley stations by mirror, meaning word traveled faster than any rider. A stable. A woodpile the size of a cottage — they meant to stay through the thaw. At the barrier, soldiers were turning back a family with a handcart, and Bram watched a woman argue, watched a soldier shove her, watched the family turn around and start the long walk down. No one argued twice.

Then the glass found the man on the tower, standing at the rail with his collar up, looking north — looking back down the road toward Vellmarch, toward them — with the particular patience of a man who is waiting for someone specific.

She held the glass steady and studied him. Heavyset. Gray at the temples. An officer's coat but no insignia of rank that she could resolve at this distance, which itself meant something; Hessler's officers wore their rank like jewelry. This man dressed like a functionary. Like a clerk grown fat and careful.

"I can't read his face," she said. "But he isn't watching the traffic. He's watching the road behind us."

Elias took the glass. He was quiet a long time. When he lowered it, his breath came out slow and deliberate, the way he breathed when he was choosing words instead of gasping for them.

"That's Commander Voss," he said.

"You know him?"

"Everyone who ever processed paperwork in the Fourth Army knows Voss. He ran the records annex at Hessler's headquarters for six years. He was the man who decided which reports went up and which reports went into the stove." Elias handed the glass back, and Bram noticed his fingers, and how carefully they did not shake. "He was reassigned to the frontier last month. Nobody gets reassigned to the frontier in November unless they're being rewarded or buried, and Voss was neither. Unless—"

"Unless someone wanted him exactly here," Bram finished.

"Unless the requisition reached all the way up the mountain." Elias coughed, pressed his fist to his chest, and went on. "Bram. Think about what Duvall knows. He knows a fugitive left Vellmarch carrying documents. He knows the ferry crossing. Marta wouldn't talk — but the harbor watch saw what Marta wouldn't say. He knows the deserter joined the fugitive on the mountain road, because the chapel ambush was reported by the farmers who found the deserters' camp. Every report funnels upward, and every report lands eventually on a desk. And if the desk it landed on was Voss's—"

"—then Voss knows what's coming through his pass."

"He knows more than that." Elias's voice dropped, though there was no one within a mile to hear it but the wind. "Duvall's warrant described a woman. If that description has traveled — if it's been read aloud at every garrison mess between here and the capital — then Voss isn't waiting for a courier. He's waiting for *you*. For the widow with the typewriter. For the son who never appears in daylight. For—" He stopped.

"For Bram Winterbourne," she said.

The name hung in the cold air between them. It was strange, hearing it spoken aloud on a mountainside. In Vellmarch she had been the son of Aldous Winterbourne for nine years, and before that she had been nobody, a girl in the back rooms of the foundry sweeping up lead dust, learning the weight of letters before she learned her own. On the road she had been the Widow Kessel, a fiction with a forged spine. But her true name — the one cut into the ledger's final page in her own hand, beside the names of the dead — she had spoken it exactly twice since leaving home. Once to Elias, in the dark, in chapter upon chapter of confession. Once to herself, in the barn, reading the ledger cover to cover with a wound in her side, adding names in the margin until dawn.

Now the name was traveling ahead of her through the snow, faster than she could walk, wearing her body's description like stolen clothes.

* * *

They retreated below the ridgeline and took stock in the hut, the way armies take stock, with maps drawn in the frost of the stone floor and hope strictly rationed.

"The high track," Corvin said, tracing it with a stick. "It comes over the shoulder of the mountain here — no checkpoint, because no cart can go that way. In summer it's a goatherd's path. In winter—"

"In winter it's a grave with scenery," Elias said.

"It might be open yet. Two days of climbing, one night above the treeline. If the storm hasn't filled the col—"

"And if it has?" Bram asked. "We climb onto the shoulder of a mountain in a whiteout with a sick man and one rope, and either we freeze or we turn around and come back to this same spot having spent our strength." She looked at Elias — at the gray under his skin, the way he braced his arm against the wall when he thought no one counted. The river had nearly drowned him. The mountain was finishing the job slowly. "I won't spend him on a maybe."

"I can be spent," Elias said quietly. "That's rather the point of me, these days."

"You'll be spent *at the presses*," Bram said, with more sharpness than she intended, "or not at all. Your signature goes beside those names, Rehn. You made me promise nothing — I made *you*. Don't die on a goat path out of politeness."

Corvin watched them with the expression of a man watching two people argue over which of them loves the other less. Then he tapped the frost-map again. "So. The high track is out. The low road runs through the checkpoint. What else is there? There's always something else. Rivers, mineshafts, smugglers' stairs."

"There's nothing else," Elias said. "I've read the survey maps. This range is a wall with one door in it, and the door has a lock, a lamp, and Voss standing in front of it. That's why the requisition works, Bram. That's why the whole machine works. It doesn't need to catch everyone. It just needs to close every door but one, and stand in the one."

Bram knelt at the frost-map and looked at the shape of it. One road. One barrier. One man with her name in his pocket. Somewhere behind them, days down the mountain, Duvall was climbing with a warrant and a rifle and the knowledge that the Winterbourne heir was a woman — and the knowledge, too, of a village with no names on its doors, the memory that had made his gun barrel hesitate at the chapel road. She did not know which Duvall would arrive first: the hunter or the doubter. She suspected she would meet both, and that they would wear the same coat.

"We can't go over, we can't go around," she said slowly. "So we go through."

Corvin laughed — one short bark, without humor. "Through Voss's garrison. With barbed wire and a heliograph. With your description nailed to his door, likely as not."

"Not my description," Bram said. "Not anymore."

She looked at her hands. They were ruined hands by any gentle standard — nicked and calloused, the right forefinger flattened at the pad from years of dressing type, a scar across the left palm where a graver had slipped when she was fourteen and learning to cut a lowercase *g*. Her father had bandaged it and said, *A cutter's scars are her signature, little bird; learn to sign neatly.* She had spent nine years hiding those hands — binding them, roughening her voice, teaching them to move like a boy's, then like a widow's. Learning to be no one. Practicing forgetting her own hands.

But her father had left her another lesson, older than the hiding one, and it had been waiting in the cellar all along, sewn into a typewriter case: that letters could be made. That identity was type. That any seal, any signature, any self could be cut — given steel, given lamplight, given a night.

"Elias," she said. "Your records annex. You processed travel warrants for the frontier districts. You've seen hundreds."

"Thousands."

"What does a military transit pass for the pass district actually look like? Not the forgery — the real thing. The paper, the stamps, the countersigns. The order of the ink."

He stared at her. Then understanding moved across his face like weather, and with it something that was almost grief. "You're talking about cutting a pass. A genuine-grade forgery. At the third checkpoint, in front of Voss himself." His voice went flat. "Forged papers at a frontier post means the rope, Bram. Not a cell. The rope, on the spot, as an example to the queue."

"I know what it means."

"If they examine the seal under glass—"

"They won't, if the seal is perfect. And the seal will be perfect, because I will make it perfect, because I am my father's daughter and I have been cutting letters since I was nine years old." She heard her own voice ringing off the stones, too loud, too certain, and made herself lower it. "It's the only door. So we make ourselves a key."

Silence in the hut. The wind worried the broken roof. Outside, the snow went on falling with the indifference of snow, filling tracks, erasing roads, doing the regime's work for free.

Corvin broke it. "What do you need?"

"Light. Time. Materials." Bram counted on her scarred fingers. "Paper of the right stock — Elias, what weight do they use for transit warrants?"

"Linen-faced. Cream. There's a watermark — a crowned gate, left of center."

"Watermark I can simulate with wax sizing and pressure, well enough for lamplight inspection. Paper we can strip from something — a ledger page, a good letter. Stamps." She closed her eyes, seeing the checkpoint tower again, the fat patient man on the rail. "Voss's own counterstamp I can't copy blind. Which means someone has to get me an impression of it."

"How does anyone get an impression of a checkpoint commander's stamp?" Corvin asked.

"Someone sells the garrison something," Bram said. "Firewood. Cheese. A knife-sharpening service. Someone gets inside the palisade, gets into or near the blockhouse, and presses paper against whatever's lying on that desk — a warrant awaiting stamp, an order book, anything. It's done in every occupied town in the country; the resistance used to buy impressions of the police prefect's seal for thirty crowns." She paused. "Oskar used to buy them."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. They all heard it land. Oskar, who had traded names to survive one purge. Oskar, who had sold the route to save himself, and whose betrayal was still lodged between Bram and Elias like shrapnel no surgeon dared probe. Oskar, who had wanted the ledger burned, and who was presumably still in Vellmarch, still breathing, still making his arrangements with whatever authority would keep him alive.

"We don't speak to Oskar," Elias said. "Ever. Whatever he's sold by now, buying from him is buying from Duvall."

"I'm not buying from Oskar." Bram kept her voice level. "I'm telling you the method exists. Corvin — you were a soldier four months ago. You know how garrisons think. You know what a bored sentry buys."

Corvin rubbed his jaw. "There'll be a cook, and the cook will want salt pork, and the sentries will want tobacco, and somebody in the queue of wagons down there will be trading all three." He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it — the rope for her if caught forging, the firing squad for him if caught aiding, the whole arithmetic of the thing — and then, as at the chapel, when he had read his brothers' names aloud from the ledger and chosen mercy outside the law, he shrugged. "I'll go down tonight. The storm gives cover. Farmers are stuck in that wagon line; a man selling a knife-sharpening and a bit of gossip won't be remarkable."

"And the impression?"

"If God is kind and Voss is sloppy, I press paper to his blotter and walk out. If God is otherwise engaged—" He grinned, and it was a terrible young grin. "Then you'll have to make do with the counterstamp of the duty sergeant, and pray the duty sergeant stamps everything worth stamping anyway. They usually do. Armies run on tired men rubber-stamping things."

* * *

They waited out the daylight hours because movement in snow is visible for a mile, and waiting is its own kind of torture.

Bram spent it preparing. She stripped a page from the blank endpapers of her father's old pocket almanac — cream linen-faced stock, close enough to warrant paper to pass at lamplight — and ironed it flat between two stones warmed at the fire. She ground charcoal fine and mixed it with tallow for a working black. She took the small graver from her kit — the one tool she had never surrendered, the one piece of her father's trade she carried everywhere, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, which it was — and tested its edge on her thumbnail.

Then, because there was nothing else to do but wait, and because waiting is when the mind turns on its owner, she let herself think about what waited on the other side of the pass.

The foreign press office. The editor who had already refused once, in her imagination, a hundred times — a soft-handed man wringing his soft hands, saying *unverified*, saying *provocation*, saying *war*. And past him, the presses. Past the presses, the sheets coming off damp, bearing names — her neighbors, her father's marginalia, Elias's signature promised beside the instruments' column, Marta's name if Marta died refusing to speak, and Marta would die refusing to speak, Bram knew it the way you know weather. All of it waiting on one thing: getting a case full of dead men's names past one fat careful man on a tower.

Every road leads through recognition, she thought. That was the whole design of the machine. Not walls — recognition. Because walls you could climb, but recognition lived inside people, and you couldn't cut a new person overnight.

Or could you?

Her father's voice again, from the years of lamplight: *Every letter is a decision, little bird. The serifs are where the truth lives. Anyone can cut a stem; the serif tells you whether the cutter was hurried, honest, afraid.* She had thought he was talking about type.

She thought about Duvall's hesitation at the chapel road, the half-second where his rifle had wavered — the village with no names living behind his eyes. She thought about the deserter asleep in the corner, wearing a dead man's coat. She thought about Elias, who had signed orders and was spending his dying strength unsigning them. Everyone in this story, she realized, had at some point stood in front of a door and decided whether to be the lock or the key.

Even Voss, maybe. Especially Voss. Records men knew what paper could do. Records men feared it most.

Toward dusk, Elias sat up, feverish but lucid, and worked beside her by the last gray light, dictating the anatomy of a transit warrant from six years of memory: *District stamp upper left. Counterstamp center-right, overlapping the text — always overlapping, Voss was fanatical about overlap, said clean stamps were forgeries. Serial number in violet ink, and the violet batch changed quarterly, and God help us if it changed this week. Route written in the commander's own hand, never typed, because commanders' hands were on file at every post.* He recited it like liturgy, coughing between verses, and Bram committed it all to the place behind her eyes where letterforms lived.

"You understand," Elias said at last, quietly, watching her scribe practice strokes on scrap, "that if this fails, they won't question you. They'll hang you from the barrier beam and leave you there for the queue to see. And the ledger burns with you, or worse, it sits in an evidence room until some clerk like I used to be feeds it to a stove."

"Yes."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something he'd known all along and had only dreaded hearing aloud. "Then cut it perfectly," he said. "For your father. For all of them. Cut it so that Voss looks at it and sees not a document but a decision — his decision, made easy."

Outside, the light failed. The snow thickened. Down at the notch, invisible now behind the weather, the checkpoint lamps would be lighting one by one, and the barrier beam would stay down, and Commander Voss would stand his tower and wait for a woman whose name the wind was already carrying.

Corvin checked the knife at his belt, pulled his hood up, and slipped out into the dark to buy an empire's seal with a sharpening stone and a lie.

Bram set her graver to steel by lantern-light and began to cut.

The first letter was a *V*.
