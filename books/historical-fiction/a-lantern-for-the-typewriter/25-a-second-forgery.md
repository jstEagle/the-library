## 25. A Second Forgery

The village of Hrenov had one candlemaker, and Bram bought him out.

She carried the candles back to the loft above the cooper's shed in two armfuls, forty of them, enough light to work by if she never slept. Elias watched her climb the ladder with her hood up and her jaw set and said nothing until she was through the trapdoor. Then he said, "You can't mean to cut it here."

"I mean to cut it nowhere else."

"The pass commander will have seen genuine papers every day of his career. He'll know the weight of the stock. The watermark—"

"Then I'll give him something heavier than his own," Bram said, and began unpacking the satchel.

It had taken her three days to assemble what she needed, and she had done it the way her father had taught her to do everything: slowly, invisibly, one harmless errand at a time. The brass from a broken hinge, filed soft. Beeswax and tallow for the mold. A sheet of good rag paper bought as wrapping from the grocer, who had used it around salt fish and apologized for the smell — but the fibers were long and even, and under her thumbnail they sang the way paper ought to sing. A stick of sealing wax pried from an abandoned house two lanes over, its owner's name still legible on the doorplate. And ink: she had made ink from oak gall and iron sulfate gathered on the mountain road, boiled it in the cooper's copper pot while he drank schnitz downstairs, and strained it through linen until it ran black and clean as a winter stream.

Now she laid it all out on the floorboards like a surgeon laying out instruments, and lit the first candle, and sat down cross-legged with the graver her father had carried across two borders in a boot heel.

Elias lowered himself onto a crate opposite her. He had not slept either; there was a gray shadow under each eye, and his hands, which had been so precise with manifests and stamps, hung between his knees like tools left out in the rain. "Tell me what you need me to do."

"Nothing yet. Watch, and tell me if I'm lying."

"To you?"

"To myself. When I finish this, I have to believe it's true. If I flinch anywhere, you'll see it before I do."

He nodded once and did not speak again for a long time.

* * *

Her father's graver had a handle worn into the shape of his grip, a hollow where his thumb had pressed ten thousand times. Bram's thumb did not fit the hollow. For the first hour that seemed like an omen. For the second hour she stopped believing in omens and started cutting steel.

The problem was not skill. She had been cutting letters since she was seven years old, standing on a crate at her father's bench, learning that a serif is not decoration but structure, that the difference between a B and an R is a single decision made at the shoulder of the stem, that every letter is a small argument about weight and the eye wins arguments by being given less to forgive. She could cut a face of type blind, had done it once when the lamp guttered out during a rush order and her father had said, keep going, your hands know more than your eyes ever will.

The problem was that identity papers were not type. They were a whole world compressed into a page: the crest embossed in the corner, the guilloche pattern of fine interlocking lines no counterfeiter could draw because no one drew them — they were engraved on plates guarded in a ministry office in the capital. The border commanders knew this. That was the whole architecture of trust: not that the papers were hard to copy, but that copying them required access no fugitive would ever have.

So Bram did not copy them. She invented.

She took the genuine pass she'd carried since Vellmarch — the widow's paper, the one that had died at the ferry when Duvall posted her description — and studied how it was put together rather than what it said. The crest was struck, not printed: a die pressed into dampened paper, leaving a relief you could feel with a fingertip. The signature line carried two signatures, a commander's and a countersigning clerk's, written in different inks at different pressures. The date was stamped with movable metal characters, slightly crooked, because the clerks were hurried and no one corrected them.

Crooked. She almost laughed. All those years of cutting letters true and level, and the state's own hand trembled.

She filed the hinge-brass flat, then built the die in reverse, mirror-wise, working from memory of the provincial seal: the double-headed eagle — no, not eagle, the river warden's knot, Vellmarch's province had abandoned the eagle thirty years ago and half its own officers still used the old dies, which meant the new papers bore the knot and the old ones the eagle, and a checkpoint commander deep in the mountains might see either and be none the wiser. She chose the knot. Newer. More plausible for papers issued this month.

Cutting the knot into brass took four hours and both candles burning at once. Her father's voice was in her hands the whole time: *don't chase the line, let the tool find it; if you force steel you get steel's anger, if you guide it you get its patience.* Twice the graver skipped and gouged a furrow she had to burnish out with the back of a spoon. Both times she set the tool down, walked to the window, breathed the cold mountain air until her lungs ached, and came back.

Elias broke silence only once, near midnight. "Who is she?"

Bram didn't look up. "A woman named Ansa Kolver. Widow of a wheelwright from Drast, which is far enough away that no one at the pass has met anyone from Drast. Traveling to her sister in the valley beyond the border. She's forty-one. Tired. Unremarkable." She paused. "She has my hands."

"That's the part that worries me."

"It should worry everyone. It's the only true thing about her."

* * *

Tomas came up the ladder a little past two o'clock, moving quietly the way deserters move, and stood at the edge of the candlelight holding bread and hard cheese wrapped in cloth. He looked at the spread of tools, the brass die half-finished, the sheets of paper soaking in a tray of water to soften their grain, and his face went carefully blank.

"You're making papers," he said.

"I'm making a person," Bram said. "The papers come with her."

"In Vellmarch they hang you for that."

"In Vellmarch they hang you for printing obituaries," Elias said from his crate. "The tariff has lost meaning."

Tomas set the food down and crouched to look closer, and after a moment he pointed at the die with a finger rough as tree bark. "The knot's tail. On the real ones, the tail crosses under the second loop. My brother carried papers five years; I saw them often enough."

Bram looked at what she'd cut. The tail crossed over.

She stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I held them while he read them out to me. Names." His voice went flat. "He read names off the back pages sometimes. Said if anything happened to him I should know who else was marked." He shrugged, as if he'd said nothing. "Under. The tail goes under."

Bram burned down the crossing and recut it in twenty minutes. It was the strangest gift of the whole night: her forgery corrected by a deserter's memory of his dead brother's papers. She thought, *this is how the ledger works too. Everyone carries a piece of it. No one carries the whole.*

When Tomas climbed back down to take his watch, Elias spoke without opening his eyes. "You're faster than I expected."

"My father said speed is just accuracy that isn't afraid."

"Was he always right?"

"No," Bram said. "But he was always specific when he was wrong. That's nearly the same thing."

* * *

The paper came out of the tray damp and limp, and she pressed it between boards weighted with the cooper's anvils — he had lent her everything in the shed and asked nothing, which meant Marta's arrest had reached even here, which meant the roads behind them were closing one debt at a time.

While the paper pressed, she cut the date stamps. Movable characters, tiny, six-point faces, each one a separate punch: A-N-S-A K-O-L-V-E-R, and beneath it the town, the purpose of travel, the issuing office. She cut them the way she'd cut pamphlet type a hundred nights in the foundry below the drilling square, except that now each letter was evidence against her, each stroke a rope. She found, somewhere in the third hour, that the fear had changed shape entirely. In Vellmarch the fear had been cold and general, fog. This fear was exact: *this letter, this curve, this decision.* Fear with edges. She discovered she worked better inside it. It kept her honest the way a good press keeps a forme honest — everything locked tight, nothing drifting.

Near dawn the die was ready. She warmed the sealing wax, pressed the brass into it, and pulled her first impression of the warden's knot.

It was wrong. Too clean. No die in any ministry office produced lines that crisp; real seals were tired, slightly softened by ten thousand strikes into wax. So she took a horsehair brush and abraded the die's face, deliberately, gently, aging it by minutes and hours, until the impression it gave had the faint blur of official use. Forgery, she realized, was not the art of making things perfect. It was the art of making things imperfectly in exactly the way the truth was imperfect.

"You're smiling," Elias said.

"I'm committing a hanging offense," Bram said. "I may as well enjoy the craftsmanship."

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, the paper was dry and stiff again and ready for its layers. She printed the body text first, using her tiny punches locked into a makeshift forme wedged between two stones — the crookedness of the alignment she achieved honestly, by hand, and then improved dishonestly by nudging one line askew. Then the guilloche: impossible to engrave, so she faked its logic instead, ruling dozens of hairline arcs in faded violet ink with a compass needle and a steady breath, close enough to the pattern that at arm's length the eye surrendered. At arm's length. Everything about the document lived at arm's length. A commander would hold it at arm's length. Commanders were tired, and cold, and wanted the line to move.

Then the seal, struck into wax pooled on the corner, the brass die pressed down for a count of five, lifted clean.

Then the signatures. This was the part she dreaded. She was a cutter of letters, not a writer of them, and handwriting betrayed the hand that forged it the way a witness betrays a stranger — in details no one plans. But her father had kept a drawer of specimen signatures, collected over decades for engraving commissions: banknotes, certificates, proclamations. Two of those specimens were army officers' hands, copied years ago from commission documents sent to the foundry for commemorative plates. Colonel-grade signatures. She had brought the drawer's most useful page.

She practiced the commander's hand forty times on scrap, learning its rhythm — a man who wrote fast and slanted and finished his final letter with an impatient downward flick, a man who signed things all day and hated every minute. By the fortieth attempt her wrist ached and the signature had stopped being drawing and become habit. She signed the paper once, in iron-gall ink mixed slightly browner than the body text, because older ink browned.

Last, the countersigning clerk — smaller, neater, a different pressure, written after the first ink had dried so the strokes lay atop rather than beside.

She held the finished paper up to the gray light coming through the loft's one window.

A woman looked back at her from the page. Ansa Kolver, widow, of Drast. Permission to travel to the valley settlements, issued under authority of the mountain command, sealed, signed, countersigned, dated nine days ago in a town whose name Bram had chosen because its stamp office had burned in the autumn and its records were ash — unverifiable, uncheckable, a ghost town vouching for a ghost.

"She's beautiful," Elias said quietly.

"She's a lie with excellent posture."

"All the best lies are. I spent six years filing them." He took the paper from her with a care that surprised her, turning it toward the light, tilting it so the wax caught a dull gleam. His thumb hovered over the knot. "My hands signed the requisition that emptied your cellar's town. Now yours have signed a woman into existence. Between us we've invented more people than the army has disappeared." He handed it back. "I don't say that to wound you. I say it because I've decided to stop pretending the ledger is only about other men's names."

Bram looked at him. In the candlelight his face had lost the clerk's smoothness entirely; underneath was something rawer and younger and more frightened than the man who had stamped her through the river checkpoint.

"When we deliver it," she said, "your name goes in. You asked for that."

"I asked for that."

"Then learn to write it clearly. They'll set your line from your testimony, not mine."

He almost smiled. "I'm told I have a precise hand."

* * *

She slept two hours, woke, and destroyed everything.

This was the discipline her father had drilled hardest: *the proof of the crime must not survive the crime.* The practice sheets went into the stove first, then the failed impressions, then the tray water dumped out the window into the frost. The brass die she could not bear to smash — a day and a night of her father's craft lived in it — but she wrapped it in oilcloth and buried it beneath the floorboard by the chimney, noting the board's grain in her memory the way she noted everything now, hoarding details against forgetting.

Only the finished paper remained, and the specimen page, and the graver, which went back into her boot heel where her father had carried it across two borders before her.

She dressed Ansa Kolver's clothes — widow's black, mended badly on purpose, a shawl that smelled of woodsmoke — and braided her hair in the plain style of a woman who had stopped expecting to be looked at. In the small clouded mirror nailed to the loft wall, a stranger adjusted her own collar.

"Bram," Elias said from the ladder. He rarely used the false name anymore; hearing it now was like hearing an old song in a language she'd begun to forget. "If the commander turns the paper sideways. If he asks one question about Drast. If Duvall is already there."

"Then Ansa Kolver dies at the pass," Bram said, "and I decide what happens next."

"And if they hang you for the forgery itself?"

She picked up the typewriter case and felt, through the battered leather, the ledger's weight settle against her hip — hundreds of names, her father's secret, Elias's signature waiting to join them.

"Then they'll hang a woman who existed for exactly one night," she said, "and never know she was the best work I ever cut."

Below, in the yard, Tomas appeared at the gate of the cooper's shed, walking fast, and the look on his face stopped Bram's heart before he said a word. He had a piece of paper in his fist — not theirs, not hers, a scrap torn from something larger, its edges ragged.

"There was a rider," he said, "through the village an hour before dawn. Heading for the pass. He dropped this in the mud at the turn, and I went back for it after first light." He held it out. "It's army dispatch. And it's got a name on it I know."

Bram took the scrap. The ink had smeared but not fatally. She read it once, and the cold that moved through her had nothing to do with the mountain morning.

It was a routing order — and among the names of officers summoned to the third checkpoint, written in a clerk's careful hand she recognized with sick certainty, was the name Oskar Veyl, printer, of Vellmarch. Traveling under escort. Destination: the pass.
