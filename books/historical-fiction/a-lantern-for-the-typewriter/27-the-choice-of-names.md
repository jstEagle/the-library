## 27. The Choice of Names

The fire was small — small enough to cup in two hands, small enough to smother with a boot if the light drew eyes on the slope below. Bram fed it a single branch and watched the resin catch, and for a long while neither she nor Elias spoke. The mountain stood around them like a held breath. Somewhere above the tree line, wind worked at the rocks with patient fingers, sorting scree from snow.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath an overhang where a shepherd had once stacked stones into a rude shelter, and now Bram sat against those stones with the typewriter case across her knees, her palms flat on its cracked leather, feeling through the hide the shape of what slept inside. The ledger. Two hundred and eleven names in her father's careful hand, and the pages he'd sewn around them like a mother sewing a coat against winter.

Elias sat opposite, his requisition orders spread on his thigh though there was no light left to read them by. He read them anyway, or pretended to. He had been pretending to read them since the ferry. It was how he prayed, she suspected — not to any god but to the paper itself, as if penance could be performed by rereading one's own signature until it wore smooth as a river stone.

"The pass opens at first light," he said finally. "If the weather holds."

"If Oskar hasn't sold us."

"He's already sold us." Elias said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "We're climbing toward a man who knows we're coming. The only question left is what we carry when we arrive."

Bram looked at him across the small fire. In its light his face had the quality of something excavated — all the clerkly softness worn away since the river, leaving planes and hollows and a jaw set like a man chewing on a stone he couldn't swallow. She had known him for eleven days. She had trusted him for four. And tomorrow one of them would probably die at the third checkpoint, carrying the other's truth in their pockets.

It seemed suddenly unbearable that he should die knowing everything about her except the thing that mattered.

"Elias."

He looked up.

"I want to tell you my name."

* * *

She watched the words land. Watched him go very still, the way men do when they understand that whatever comes next cannot be taken back — that the air between them is about to be permanently rearranged.

"Your name," he repeated carefully. "You're Bram Winterbourne. Son of Aldous Winterbourne, type founder of Vellmarch. I stamped you through the river checkpoint under that name myself." He touched his breast pocket, where the manifest copy lived. "If that's not—"

"It's not my name." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "My father named me Brenna. After his mother. The woman who taught him to cut his first letter when he was six years old — a lowercase e, because she said it was the only letter that looked like it was listening."

The fire popped. Below them, far down the dark slope, a night bird called once and thought better of it.

"Brenna Winterbourne," Elias said slowly, tasting it. He didn't laugh. He didn't recoil. He looked at her the way he'd looked at the foundry stamp on the seized type back in Vellmarch — with the particular attention of a man who has spent his life reading documents and has just found a forgery so good it hurts. "How long?"

"Since I was nineteen. Seven years."

"And your father — did he—" He stopped. Started again. "Was it his idea?"

"No." And here was the strange thing: saying it aloud didn't wound the way she'd expected. It felt more like setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying, her arms still curved around the absent weight. "That's the part no one ever guesses. Everyone assumes some dying father pressed his disguise onto his daughter like a last wish. But my father never asked it of me. He would never have asked. What he did was worse, or better — I've never decided. He built me a door and left it unlocked, and then he died and let me choose whether to walk through it."

* * *

The fire needed another branch. Bram rose to get one, partly because the fire needed it and partly because it is easier to confess standing up, with your hands busy, your body given something honest to do.

"My father cut type for thirty years," she said, crouching at the edge of the firelight, snapping dead branches from a fallen pine. "Punches, matrices, the whole craft. He could cut a letter smaller than a grain of barley and it would still hold its voice after ten thousand impressions. That was his religion, if he had one — that a letter properly cut outlives the hand that cut it. Outlives the language, maybe." She carried the branches back and knelt, feeding them in. "But he had another skill he kept quieter. He could cut anything. Not just letters. Seals. Signatures. The little crossed lines on a treasury stamp. When the border provinces started requiring papers, half the merchants between here and the coast came to him secretly, and he charged them nothing, because he said papers were just type that walked around wearing clothes."

Elias made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "A counterfeiter with a conscience."

"A craftsman with opinions," she corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that he never took money. He did it the way some men give directions to lost travelers. It offended him that a piece of paper could decide whether a person ate."

She watched the flame climb the fresh branches. The resin hissed and spat little stars.

"When the purges started — the first ones, before Hessler, when it was just disappearances and denials — people began coming to the foundry at night. Not for papers. For names. They wanted their sons' names cut in type, so they could print memorial sheets the army couldn't confiscate fast enough. And my father cut every name himself. Free. Hundreds of them. I used to watch him work after closing — this old man bent over his graver under the lamp, cutting letters for the dead, and each one perfect, because he believed the dead deserved better work than the living got."

Her throat tightened. She let it.

"I was nineteen and I wanted to help. That's how it began. Not heroism. A girl who wanted to help her father hold a burin steady. He taught me the punches, the justification, how to dress a matrix. And then one night he put a finished identity card on the bench in front of me — mine, mine exactly, except the name read Bram. Son of Aldous. And he said—" Her voice caught, and she pressed through it. "He said, 'I'm not asking you to become him. I'm telling you the door exists, and someday the town may need someone who can walk through it. Whether you open it is yours.'"

"What happened to him?" Elias asked quietly. Though she thought he already knew; everyone in Vellmarch knew in the way you know the shape of a scar on your own body.

"They came for the memorial sheets in the autumn of the year before last. Duvall's predecessor led the raid. My father met them at the door alone and told them the type was his and the names were his and if they wanted to arrest someone they could arrest a seventy-one-year-old man who would die in their prison inside a month and make them look like murderers of old men. Which is precisely what happened." She turned a branch with her boot. "They released the body to me. To the son. The sergeant handing over the death certificate never once looked at my face. He saw a young man grieving his father, and grief was the one thing I wasn't performing."

Silence, except for the fire.

"So I buried my father twice," Bram said. "Once in the ground at Saint Ferrel's, and once in the name. Because the day after the funeral, I opened the foundry as Bram Winterbourne, son of Aldous, and I bound my chest and lowered my voice and learned to walk like a man carries weight in his shoulders instead of his hips, and I have been him ever since. Seven years of being a ghost wearing my father's clothes." She looked up at Elias. "And the worst of it — the part I've never said to anyone — is that I got good at it. I liked being invisible. It was a craft, same as cutting type. You can do anything, anything at all, if nobody sees you doing it."

"That's not the worst of it," Elias said.

She blinked at him.

"The worst of it," he said, "is that you were right. It works. Until the night it doesn't, and then you discover you've spent seven years building a self that can't survive daylight." His mouth twisted. "I would know. I built mine out of signatures instead."

* * *

The fire settled. Elias fed it the last of the branches, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register men use for confessions they've rehearsed too many times.

"You should know why I asked for the river transfer. Before you hand me your name like it costs you something — know what mine cost." He pulled the requisition orders from his pocket, unfolded them, laid them flat on his knee even in the dark. "I signed the seizure order for your foundry. My handwriting. I processed the file, I entered the stamp numbers, I flagged the press as priority contraband. And I did it knowing — knowing — what the Winterbourne press had been printing, because I'd seen the memorial sheets three years earlier when they came through the records office as evidence. I held one in my hands. A sheet with eleven names on it. I remember thinking the typography was beautiful." He laughed, short and terrible. "I remember admiring the type."

"You were following orders," Bram said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and heard him hear it.

"Yes. That's the whole horror of it, isn't it? It's such a small sentence for such a large grave." He folded the orders along their old creases. "When Duvall matched the ink to your foundry, I pulled your file that same night and read my own signature at the bottom of the condemnation order, and I understood that I was going to be one of two kinds of man: the kind whose name appears in ledgers like the one you're carrying, or the kind who helps carry it. There's no third kind. I looked for one for three days. It doesn't exist."

Bram was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "There might be a fourth kind. But it's expensive."

"How expensive?"

"It costs your name." She reached for the typewriter case and lifted it onto her knees, and began, deliberately, working the brass clasps. "Tomorrow we walk into a checkpoint where a commander has been told to look for a woman fugitive. Every plan we have assumes I stay Bram — the widow's brother, the son, the shadow. But I've been thinking about what you said at the chapel, when the deserters read those names aloud. How Kaderin stood up straighter when he heard his brother's name spoken by a stranger. Like the name itself was a ration he'd been living without."

The clasps gave. She raised the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the leather lining, the ledger waited. Beside it, nested in straw, lay her father's travel kit: the graver rolls, the punches in their felt slots, a cake of seal wax, the small steel dies. And beneath these, flat as a promise, a blank identity card stock and the second-best loupe.

"I can forge us both through that checkpoint," she said. "I told you that. New papers, new seals, new lives — the shepherd cousins from the valley, it would hold for an hour, maybe two. But here's what forging teaches you that nothing else does: every false name is a debt. Someone pays it eventually. Marta is paying mine in a cell right now. Oskar paid his in advance, in other people's names. And tomorrow, if we slip through as ghosts, we slip through owing — and the ledger arrives at the press office attached to two liars, and the editor asks the question he'll ask, the only question that matters: who are you, and why should the world believe you?" She looked at Elias across the fire. "And I am so tired, Elias. I am so tired of having an answer ready for that question that isn't true."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I've been choosing all week without admitting it. Choosing invisibility over and over because it was safer, because it was habit, because being no one is a kind of armor. And it almost killed Marta, and it will kill the deserter tomorrow, and it will not save the ledger, because evidence without a witness is just paper." She lifted the ledger out of the case and held it against her chest, both arms, the way you hold a sleeping child or a shield. "So tonight I'm making the choice properly. On purpose. With my eyes open."

She opened the ledger to the flyleaf. Her father's hand marched across it in columns — not names yet, but instructions, dates, a cipher key. And beneath the last line of his writing, space. Blank space, sized for one entry.

"There's room here," she said, "for one more name before the record closes. My father left it. I used to think it was an accident of layout." Her thumb traced the blank margin. "It wasn't. Nothing he ever set was an accident. He left room for whoever finished the work."

Elias leaned forward. The firelight moved on his face. "What name goes there?"

"Brenna Winterbourne. Daughter of Aldous. Type cutter. Witness." She said it aloud and felt the syllables leave her body like heat leaving a cooling iron — irreversible, shaping the air behind them. "Not hidden in the text. Not smuggled in the binding. Written on the first page, in my own hand, above my father's notes, so the first thing any editor or officer or hangman reads is who is testifying and that she is not ashamed of it."

The wind shifted above the tree line. Somewhere down-slope, a branch cracked — deer, probably, or nothing, or a patrol. Neither of them moved.

"You understand what that means," Elias said. Not a warning. A check, the way a man checks a knot before trusting it with his weight. "The moment your true name touches that page, you stop being a fugitive and become a defendant. If we're taken, you hang as Brenna Winterbourne, forger and traitor, with a signed confession sewn into your own luggage. No ambiguity. No mistaken identity to hide behind. Duvall won't have to prove anything. You'll have proven it yourself."

"Yes."

"And if we reach the press office, you testify publicly. Under oath. Your face next to your name next to two hundred and eleven others."

"That's rather the point."

"Why?" And he asked it gently, genuinely — the clerk's question, the auditor's question, the question behind every document he'd ever stamped. "Why trade armor for a target? What does visibility buy that seven years of perfect invisibility couldn't?"

Bram looked down at the ledger in her lap, at her father's cipher key, at the blank margin waiting like an open door.

"Because I watched a village with no names on its doors," she said. "I stood in that churchyard where they burned the registry, and I understood that erasure isn't violence done to bodies. Bodies they can kill anywhere. Erasure is violence done to the fact that you existed — and it doesn't require hatred, only paperwork, only silence, only everyone agreeing quietly to forget. My father spent his whole life cutting letters because he believed a letter is a refusal. Every character on that press is a small stubborn *no* spoken against oblivion. And I have spent seven years being a letter that refuses to be read." She lifted her head. "I'm done. Tomorrow, whichever way it goes, I walk through that checkpoint as myself. If they take me, let the record show exactly who they took. If I make it through, let the testimony stand on a real name. Either way, no more debts. No more ghosts. The armor goes in the fire."

She held the ledger out slightly, an offering and a dare.

"Elias Rehn. Will you witness it? Your signature beside mine — instrument and witness, both of us, on the record. Because you're right that there are only two kinds of men. But there's a third kind of document." She smiled, and it cost her something and freed something in the same motion. "The kind where everyone tells the truth."

For a long moment Elias didn't move. Then he took off his coat, folded it into a cushion, and laid the requisition orders on it — his signature facing up, catching the firelight. He took out his pen, uncapped it, and held it a moment like a man holding something hot.

"Rehn," he said. "Elias Tomas Rehn. Clerk, Third Bureau, complicit in one hundred and six seizures, accessory to God knows what else, and — as of tonight — witness for the defense." He wrote his name in the margin below the space she'd indicated, small and precise, the best handwriting of his life. Then he passed her the pen.

Brenna Winterbourne took it. And in the ledger her father had built to outlast a war, by the light of a fire small enough to smother with a boot, she wrote her own name — slowly, carefully, each letter a door opening — and beneath it, in the space her father had left:

*I was never Bram. I was always his daughter. This is my testimony.*

* * *

They banked the fire low and took watches in turns. Bram took the second watch, and sat with the ledger closed against her chest, listening to Elias breathe and the mountain grind its slow teeth overhead, and she found that her hands, for the first time in seven years, were entirely still.

Near dawn, gray light finding the eastern rocks, Elias stirred and looked at her across the ashes.

"One problem," he said. "If you walk through that checkpoint as Brenna Winterbourne, the forged papers are worthless. Worse than worthless — they're evidence. We can't carry them."

"No," she agreed. "We can't."

She looked at the typewriter case, at the false seals and cut dies and the two sets of beautiful, damning papers her father's kit had produced overnight. Then she looked east, where the pass waited with its checkpoint, its commander, its word of a woman fugitive — and, if Oskar's letter told the truth, a certain Sergeant Duvall already walking the line.

"But we can carry something better," she said. "Tonight I'm going to cut one more thing. Not a forgery."

"What, then?"

"A broadsheet. One page. The first hundred names — set by hand, printed on the foundry proof press we can't bring, or written out fair if we must. So that when they search me at the checkpoint, what they find isn't a lie I'm trying to pass." She tightened the straps on the case, and the buckles spoke softly in the cold. "It's the truth, in type, addressed to them. Let Duvall read his own orders in the morning paper."

Elias stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — quiet, disbelieving, the laugh of a man watching the rules of a game change in front of him.

"They'll shoot you."

"They might. But they'll have to do it looking at me." She shouldered the case and turned toward the pass, where the sky was going the color of new-cut metal. "Come on, Elias. We've got a name to deliver — and I intend to be there when it lands."
