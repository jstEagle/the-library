## 28. Night Before the Pass

The shepherd's hut stood at the elbow of a frozen stream, half-swallowed by pine, its roof sagging under snow that had been falling since dusk in fat, unhurried flakes. Inside, the three of them had built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and around that fire they arranged themselves the way soldiers do — backs to stone, faces to the dark, each man watching a different quarter of the night out of habit rather than necessity.

Bram fed the fire a stick at a time. She had learned from her father that a flame could be made honest or dishonest depending on how you fed it: a blaze for defiance, a whisper of coals for survival. Tonight she fed it honestly. Tomorrow it would not matter what the light showed.

Elias sat across from her with his back against the wall, his requisition ledger — his own copy, the clerk's duplicate he had carried out of the army like a stolen organ — open on his knees. He was not reading it. He had read it so many times the pages had gone soft as cloth. He was holding it the way a man holds a mirror when he has finally decided to look.

The deserter, whose name was Toma Vey and who had been an army cook before he had been anything else, was sharpening a knife on a whetstone with the patience of someone who believed that repetition was its own form of prayer. *Shhk. Shhk.* The sound filled the hut the way a clock fills a room.

"Tell me again," Toma said without looking up, "how many sentries."

"Twelve at the gatehouse," Elias said. "Four on the road below. Two patrols walking the tree line, changing every two hours. The commander keeps a lantern lit all night because he's afraid of the dark, which tells you something useful about him."

"It tells me he's human," Bram said.

"It tells me he wastes oil," Elias said, and almost smiled.

* * *

They had burned the letters after supper.

It had been Toma's idea, or perhaps no one's idea and simply something that happened, the way things happen on last nights. Elias had carried letters since the ferry — papers from his old life folded into his coat, orders he should have destroyed months ago, a letter from a sister in the capital written in a hand so careful it looked printed. Bram had carried her father's last letter, the one she had found tucked inside the typewriter case beside the ledger, four lines in Aldous Winterbourne's cramped cutter's script:

*If you are reading this, you found the names. Cut them well. A letter that survives is worth more than a man who hides.*

And there were others — a scrap of Oskar's handwriting, kept as evidence against him; a page torn from a hymnal; the forged identity papers for the widow hauling her husband's typewriter, now useless, now dangerous, the ink still smelling faintly of Bram's cellar.

They burned them one at a time in the small stove, and each letter gave off a different light. The army paper flared blue-white and died fast. Her father's letter burned slow and gold, and Bram watched the words go — *cut them well* — and felt nothing tear, only something settle, the way a foundation settles in winter.

"You're supposed to feel worse than this," Toma said, watching the ash.

"No," Elias said quietly. "You're supposed to feel lighter. That's the trick of it. Everything heavy we've been carrying, we were carrying for them." He nodded toward the border, toward Vellmarch, toward the town where every door bore a requisition order in his own neat clerk's hand. "Tonight we put it down."

But he did not burn his copy of the requisition ledger. He fed the fire everything else and then closed that book and set it aside, and when Bram looked at him he said, without being asked, "Mine burns at the press office. With my name beside it. Not before."

* * *

Bram took the first watch.

She sat in the doorway with the case across her knees — the battered typewriter case, hollowed by her father's hands, the ledger sewn into its lining like a heart inside a ribcage — and she listened to the snow come down through the pines. It made a sound she knew from childhood, from winters in the foundry loft: a sound like pages turning somewhere just out of sight.

Her father had taught her to cut letters by lamplight, his big hands over hers on the graver, saying: *Feel the resistance. Every metal has a place where it wants to give. Find that place and the letter cuts itself. Force it and you get a wound, not a letter.*

She thought about that now, watching the fire. All winter she had been forcing. Forcing the walk of a widow, the voice of a son, the stillness of a fugitive. Tomorrow there would be no more finding the place where the metal wanted to give. Tomorrow she would walk up to twelve rifles and present a lie stamped with her father's skill, and if the seal wavered under the commander's eye for one second too long —

No. She made herself stop. That was forcing. Instead she did what her father would have done: she found the place that wanted to give.

It was this: she was not afraid of dying at the pass. She had discovered this sometime on the mountain road, somewhere between the village with no names on its doors and Marta's empty boat, and the discovery had frightened her more than death itself until she understood it. She was not afraid because she had already done the hardest thing. She had told Elias her true name. She had spoken it aloud on the cold road — *Branna Winterbourne* — and the sky had not fallen, and the man beside her had not laughed or recoiled, and the name had fit her like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Everything after that was only cutting. And she cut well.

Toma relieved her at midnight. He sat down heavily beside the door, knife across his thighs, and said, "Sleep. You'll want your hands steady tomorrow."

"My hands are always steady."

"I know," he said. "I watched you forge those papers. Steadiest hands I ever saw outside a surgeon." He paused. "That's why they'll give me the diversion instead of you. In case you were going to argue about it."

She hadn't been. But she looked at him now — the broad, scarred hands, the cook's calm, the deserter's rope-burn scar circling one wrist where he had slipped his own hanging — and she said, "Why volunteer?"

Toma turned the knife over once, twice, studying the edge in the firelight.

"When they caught us reading the ledger at the chapel," he said, "you didn't run. They had us dead to rights, and you stood there while I read my brother's name out loud like a fool, and you didn't run." He shrugged, a big slow movement like a bear shifting in its sleep. "I've been running my whole life. From the conscription, from the purge, from my own company. One night of not running seems fair payment."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"It's the only kind they offer up here." He smiled without much humor. "Go sleep, Branna Winterbourne. A man can carry a false name up a mountain. But a true one — that deserves rest."

* * *

Elias could not sleep, so he wrote.

He had no proper paper left; the letters were ash. So he took the flyleaf of his duplicate ledger and a stub of pencil and drafted, in the small unvarying hand that had signed away a town, the statement he intended to make at the press office. He had been drafting it in his head since the ferry. Now he got it down:

*I, Elias Rehn, clerk fourth class, Army of the Interior, do swear that between the months of —*

He stopped. Between the months of what? Between the month he learned to write beautifully and the month he learned what his writing did. His whole commission lived in that gap.

He tried again. *I processed the requisition of the town of Vellmarch. I sealed the records. I inventoried the presses. I did not burn anything myself. I only told other men what to burn, and they burned it, and this is the difference between a clerk and an arsonist, and it is no difference at all.*

Better. True enough to hurt. He read it over twice and found himself listening for the sound it would make coming off a press — because that was the strange thing he had learned on this road, the thing he could never explain to Hessler or Duvall or any man still wearing the uniform: that ink was a voice. That a line of type, once set, spoke forever, in rooms you would never enter, to people unborn. He had spent ten years believing his signatures disappeared into files. They had not. They had been speaking the whole time. He had simply never listened.

Across the hut, Bram slept sitting up with her head against the wall and the case held against her chest like a child, and even asleep her arms were locked around it. He understood that. There had been a time — the whole of his life until eight days ago — when he would have said she was guarding evidence. He knew better now. She was guarding people. Three hundred and eleven of them, plus one name she had added herself in a hand that shook only slightly: *Elias Rehn, clerk fourth class. Signed the orders. Delivered himself.*

She had shown him the entry without comment, the way you show a man his own grave and let him decide whether it's an insult or a gift. He had decided. He was still deciding. He suspected he would be deciding for the rest of his life, however long the press office and the patrol bullets allowed that to be.

He finished the statement, tore the leaf free, folded it into his breast pocket over his heart, and went to take the middle watch from Toma, because a man who cannot sleep might as well stand guard over the thing he is ashamed of.

* * *

In the hour before dawn, with the fire down to red eyes in the ash and snow still sifting through a gap in the roof, Toma woke them both.

Not with alarm. He simply crouched by the embers and said, in the flat tone cooks use for announcing that the soup is ready, "I want to say my piece while you're both awake, because if I wait for morning I'll find a reason to be brave quietly, and quiet bravery never gets argued with."

Bram sat up. Elias struck a match and touched it to a fresh stick, and the small flame stood up between them like a witness.

"The gatehouse has a kitchen yard on the east side," Toma said. "Cook smoke, woodpile, a grease trap they never clean. I know it because every garrison kitchen in this country is the same kitchen. At first light they render fat and stoke the ovens, and there's a window into the storeroom, and the storeroom connects to the guard corridor." He laid it out with his hands, drawing lines in the air the way he must once have drawn knives across a chopping block. "I go in through the woodpile before the relief watch comes down. I start a fire in their flour store. Not a little one."

"They'll shoot you," Bram said.

"They'll try. I'm quick in kitchens and slow everywhere else, and a kitchen is exactly where I'll be." He met her eyes, and his face in the firelight was older than his years and entirely untroubled. "A burning storehouse pulls every sentry off the road for ten minutes. Ten minutes is enough. You two walk up the road in the smoke confusion, papers out, heads down, and by the time they count heads you're through the gate and I'm either gone over the wall or I'm not."

"And if they catch you," Elias said, "they'll hang you as a deserter. Slowly."

"They were always going to catch me eventually, Clerk. I deserted in spring. It's nearly winter. I've had a good run of it." Toma picked up his knife, considered it, and then — deliberately, ceremonially, the way a man lays down a tool at the end of a job — drove it into the tabletop and left it standing there. "Besides. My brother's name is in that book of yours. Anton Vey. Disappeared, March, the grain riots." His voice did not break; it simply went very level, the way water goes level before it goes over the edge. "You're carrying him to the printers. Someone ought to clear the road for him. It might as well be family."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Outside, the snow went on turning its invisible pages.

Then Bram reached into the case's lining, drew out the ledger itself — she had not meant to open it tonight, but some things demand it — and turned to the March pages, and found the name, and read it aloud into the firelit dark: *"Anton Vey, miller's laborer, taken from the grain square, no record of release."* She looked up. "When it's printed, it will be in ten thousand hands. Every farmhouse between here and the capital."

Toma nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a load set on solid ground.

"Well then," he said. "Best get some sleep, both of you. We've a fire to start at first light, and I intend to be thorough."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the snow stopped, the pines hung white and silent above them like a congregation. Bram packed the case, checked the forged papers one final time — the seal perfect, the ink aged with tea and smoke, her father's craft living in every stroke — and stood at the doorway looking down at the pass road, where far below, tiny with distance, the gatehouse lantern was still burning in the commander's window. Afraid of the dark. Human. Wasteful of oil.

Elias buttoned his coat over his folded statement. Toma rolled the last of the bread into a cloth and handed half to each of them without ceremony, a cook to the end.

They started down through the trees toward the road, three sets of prints in the fresh snow, and behind them the hut stood empty with the knife still standing upright in the table, pointing at nothing, waiting for morning.

It was Bram who saw it first — a smudge of movement far down the valley, on the road they would have taken yesterday, before the snow closed the high trail: riders. Six of them, maybe eight, moving unhurried toward the gatehouse, and at the head of them a figure on a gray horse who sat the saddle with a stiffness Bram would have known anywhere, would have known in any light, at any distance, the way you know the handwriting of a man who has signed your ruin.

Sergeant Duvall had reached the pass before them.

Toma followed her gaze, then glanced at the eastern sky, gauging the light, the hour, the time remaining before the kitchen fires were stoked. He began, calmly, to roll up his sleeves.

"Not ten minutes," he said. "We'll need twenty."
