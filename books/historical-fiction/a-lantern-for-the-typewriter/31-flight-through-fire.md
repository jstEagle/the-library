## 31. Flight Through Fire

The world was noise and orange light.

Bram did not remember deciding to run. One moment she was pressed flat against the guardhouse wall while the diversion tore itself apart — the deserter's smoke pots blooming along the eastern barricade, shouts folding into shots, Duvall's voice cracking somewhere in the confusion like a whip finding no target — and the next her legs were carrying her, the typewriter case strapped across her back like a hump of iron, Elias's hand locked around her wrist, hauling her into the treeline as though she were a child being dragged from a flooded river.

Behind them, the checkpoint burned.

She looked back once. She would spend the rest of her life wishing she had not, and the rest of her life being glad she had.

The oil stores had caught. Flames climbed the wooden watchtower in a single unbroken sheet, so that for one impossible second the tower stood whole and burning at once, a column of fire where a man with a rifle should have been. Sparks wheeled upward into the dark like startled birds. Soldiers ran beneath it, small and black against the glare, some fighting the fire, some fighting each other's panic, none yet looking toward the trees.

None except the ones who would. It was only a matter of seconds. She knew it the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop lands — in the teeth, in the blood.

"Left," Elias gasped. "The game trail. Stay off the ridge line."

They went left. Bram's boot found the trail by luck rather than sight; the forest here was old pine, the canopy thick enough to swallow the firelight whole, and within twenty paces she was running blind, one hand on the case strap, the other slapping against trunks she could not see. Pine needles muffled their footsteps but not their breathing. Her breath came in ragged saw-strokes. The case bounced against her spine with every stride, and through the wood she could feel the ledger shifting, hundreds of names jostling against each other like passengers on a rough road.

*Hold together,* she thought at it. *Not one page. Not one name.*

A shot cracked through the trees — high, wild, more warning than aim — and then another, and then the particular ugly rhythm of a rifle fired by someone running, which meant the soldiers had found the trailhead, which meant they had minutes, perhaps less.

"Toma," Bram said. The word came out between breaths, half prayer, half question.

"He knew what he bought us," Elias said. "Don't spend it standing still."

But she could not help spending it anyway, a coin at a time, in the currency of memory. The deserter — Vasko Toma, three weeks with them, who had read his brother's name aloud from the ledger in a ruined chapel and wept without shame — had volunteered for the diversion with the calm of a man settling an account. *You carry the names,* he'd said. *I'll give them something louder to look at.* He had taken the last of the smoke pots, the pistol with four rounds, and Bram's spare coat, because a figure seen fleeing east in a familiar coat was worth ten minutes of any sergeant's attention.

Ten minutes. She counted them now with her heartbeat.

*One.* The firelight vanished entirely behind them, swallowed by the pines, and they ran by feel alone, down into a fold of ground where a stream muttered over stones.

*Two.* A flare went up somewhere south — white light bleeding green through the branches — and the shouting reorganized itself, became purposeful, became a hunt with a shape to it.

*Three.* Elias stumbled, caught himself on her shoulder, swore. His breathing was worse than hers; the clerk's body, built for desks and ledgers, paying its debts all at once.

*Four.* Dogs. Not yet — but someone would think of dogs by morning, and morning was three hours away.

*Five.*

At five they stopped hearing shots from the checkpoint, and Bram understood, in the wordless way grief understands things, what the silence meant.

She stopped running. She couldn't help it. Her body simply quit, the way a clock quits, all at once and forever, and she stood in the dark with her hands on her knees and the case pressing cold against her back and let the truth arrive.

Elias doubled back two steps and gripped her arm hard enough to bruise. "Not here."

"He's dead." Her voice sounded foreign to her, scraped raw. "We can't even hear—"

"That is why we keep moving. If they're not shooting, they've stopped needing to shoot, which means either he's done his work—" Elias's voice caught, and for one strange moment she heard the guilt underneath it, the clerk who understood exactly what arithmetic he was part of, "—or they're coming after us quietly. Both reasons are the same reason. Walk."

They walked. Then the walk became a jog, and the jog became the run again, because fear is patient but not infinitely so, and somewhere behind them in the black lattice of the forest men with lanterns were moving through the trees in a line, the way Hessler's men did everything — in formation, by the book, without malice and without mercy.

* * *

They ran until the stars wheeled.

Bram measured time afterward only in fragments. A fence crossed at a dead run, wire catching her trouser leg and tearing it to the knee. A meadow silvered with frost, crossed low and fast, the case held flat against her belly like a child carried through gunfire. A farm road where they lay in a drainage ditch while hooves passed — a patrol, unhurried, lanterns swinging — close enough that she could smell the horses and hear a soldier complain about the cold, about the fools who'd burned a perfectly good checkpoint, about wanting his bed. She lay with her cheek in frozen mud and felt the man's boredom pass over her like weather, and when the hoofbeats faded she discovered she was shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and Elias put his hand on the case between her shoulders, not on her — on the ledger — as if to steady them both through it.

"It's still there," he said. "Every hour I'll tell you it's still there, and you won't have to ask."

"You don't have to—"

"I signed orders," Elias said quietly. "Let me do this instead."

So they went on, and every hour, in his dry clerk's voice, out of the darkness: *Still there.* And every hour she hated how much it helped.

Dawn found them in a ravine where two hillsides leaned together like conspirators, choked with bramble and birch scrub, deep enough that the light arrived gray and broken. Bram sat with her back against a stone and finally allowed herself to take stock of her own body, the way her father had taught her to take stock of a press — what's worn, what's broken, what will hold until the run is finished. Her left ankle was swollen and would need binding. Her palms were shredded from bramble. There was blood on her sleeve that she was fairly sure was Toma's, transferred from a handshake three days ago, and she could not make herself brush it off.

The case she unbuckled with hands that would not quite obey. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining her father had stitched with his own clever hands, the ledger lay untouched. Dry. Whole. Every page.

She touched it the way you touch a sleeping child — barely, with two fingers, confirming breath.

"We lost him so this could be boring," Elias said. He was sitting a few feet away, elbows on knees, staring at nothing. His coat was torn at the shoulder. In the gray light he looked older by years than he had at the checkpoint, and thinner, as if the night had rendered him down to essentials. "Paperwork. That's what we bought with a man's life. A book arriving at an office."

"Don't."

"I'm not diminishing it. I'm telling you what it weighs." He looked at her then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "If we treat it as anything less than a price paid in full, we'll start believing it was cheap."

Bram said nothing. She thought of Toma reading names in the chapel, his finger moving down the columns his brother's death had made him a stranger to. *János Toma, miller's son, taken in the spring.* He had closed the ledger gently, like closing a door on a sickroom. And then he had asked for the smoke pots.

Survival, she thought, feels like theft. You run, and someone else pays the toll. She had been stealing since the first pamphlet — stealing her father's name, stealing Oskar's courage, stealing Marta's freedom, and now Toma's life, all of it stacked up behind her like unpaid debts, and the terrible thing, the thing she could not say aloud, was that she would steal again. Tonight. Tomorrow. All the way to the presses. Because the alternative was letting the theft be for nothing, and that was the one sin she could not commit.

"Elias." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "When we get there. When it's printed. His name goes in too."

"The ledger has provisions for that?"

"The ledger has blank pages at the back. My father left them blank on purpose. He said—" she heard Aldous Winterbourne's voice in her own, the foundry cadence, patient and exact, "—he said a record that can't grow is just a grave. Names go in as long as the ink holds out."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he stamped documents — final, precise. "Then we make sure the ink holds out."

* * *

They moved again at full light, slower now, off the trails, following the ravine north by Elias's reckoning. He had memorized the route the way clerks memorize routes — from manifests, freight schedules, the timber roads marked on requisition maps. It was, Bram thought, its own kind of literacy. Her father had read metal; she read type; Elias read paper, and paper, it turned out, remembered everything.

By midday they heard the dogs.

Far off at first — a thin belling sound threading through the wind, easy to mistake for birdsong if you wanted desperately to mistake it. Neither of them pretended. They simply looked at each other, and Bram rebuckled the case tight against her spine, and they began to move faster, which was the wrong instinct and the only one available.

"They'll lose the scent at water," Elias said between breaths. "There's a river — the Vell, the same one that runs past the town — it bends northeast below the pass. If we can reach it—"

"How far?"

"A day. Perhaps two, the way we're traveling." He didn't say *the way you're limping*, and she was grateful.

An hour later the dogs sounded closer, and Bram made the decision that would haunt her in the good way and the bad way for the rest of her life: they split the difference between hiding and speed, and took the open slope.

It was madness. Open ground, winter-brown grass, a fugitive with a case on her back visible for a mile. But the ravine was becoming a box canyon ahead, and the dogs were behind, and between the two evils she chose the one you could see coming. They ran the slope in full view of the sky, exposed, absurd, two dark figures stitching downhill through the brown, and no shot came, and no shout, and when they dropped into the tree cover of the far side Bram realized her heart was going like a trip-hammer and her ankle had gone from pain to a bright white signal beyond pain.

She did not stop. Stopping had a price too, and she had decided, somewhere in the burning checkpoint's shadow, that she would pay in flesh before she paid in pages.

They reached the Vell at dusk.

It was narrower here than the broad gray river she'd ferried with Marta, running fast and shallow over stones, loud enough to cover conversation and scent enough to break a trail. They waded it — the cold went through boots and skin and settled in the bone — climbed the far bank, walked a quarter mile in the streambed itself where the water would kill their tracks, and only then, in the lee of a fallen birch, did Bram sit down and admit she could go no farther that day.

Elias built no fire. He bound her ankle with strips of his shirt and a splint of birchwood, his fingers quick and impersonal, a clerk filing a damaged document. Above them the sky was doing something extravagant — clouds breaking around a rising moon, bands of gold and slate — and neither of them watched it. Beauty was a country neither could afford entry to tonight.

"Do you hear that?" Bram said suddenly.

Elias froze. Then, distantly, carried down the river on the wind: bells. Church bells. Not alarm — the slow, measured tolling of evening service, some village upstream keeping its ordinary hours, lighting its ordinary lamps, saying its ordinary prayers for its ordinary living and dead.

Bram listened until her eyes burned. Somewhere behind them a man had died so this could continue. Somewhere ahead, across a line on a map drawn by men who had never cut a letter in their lives, a printing office waited with its indifferent machinery. Between the two stretched a river, a pass, a checkpoint where a commander already knew a woman was coming, and a sergeant who had looked her in the face and hesitated — and whose hesitation would not survive a second meeting.

"Still there?" Elias asked.

She had her hand on the case. Through the wood, through the oilcloth, through the lining her father had sewn, she could feel it: the weight of every name, János Toma among them now in spirit, waiting for its blank pages.

"Still there," she said.

They slept in shifts, and in the gray hour before dawn Bram woke to Elias's hand on her shoulder and his voice, low and stripped of everything but fact:

"Smoke. Ahead. A lot of it — and it's not ours."

She rose, ankle screaming, and looked north through the trees to where the mountains stood up black against the fading stars. On their flank, small and steady, a fire burned that no farmer had lit.

The pass was watching for them.
