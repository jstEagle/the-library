## 32. The Border Stone

Elias Rehn had spent eleven years learning to count things that could not be counted back.

Grain requisitioned: so many tons. Presses seized: so many frames. Names entered into the master registry of Vellmarch's disappeared: he had never once allowed himself to total that column, though his hand had copied figures from it into three separate reports, each one signed in a small, disciplined script that had once been praised by a captain who was now dead and would not have to answer for anything.

Now, on the last morning of his life, with the border stone in sight — a squat granite pillar no taller than a fence post, whitewashed at its crown, standing in a field of frost-killed clover like a tooth knocked loose from some enormous jaw — Elias found himself counting again.

Two hundred paces to the stone.

Four hundred more beyond it, across the stream, to the low slate roof of the foreign press office, where a flag hung limp in the still air and smoke rose thin and gray from a chimney that meant, at least, that someone was awake inside.

One case, carried by Bram Winterbourne, slung under her arm the way she had carried it through two checkpoints and a storm and a mountain road: heavy, unremarkable, holding four hundred names sewn into canvas lining.

And behind them — this was the number he had been refusing all morning, the way he had refused the column for eleven years — pursuit. Not certain pursuit. Possible pursuit. The word *possible* had kept him walking since the chapel, since Oskar's letter had told them everything, since the deserter Tomaz had fallen in the treeline at the pass with a soldier's bullet in him and Bram's hand pressed over the wound as if pressure could argue with death.

*Possible* was what Elias had written on requisition orders too. *Possible resistance activity.* *Possible unauthorized printing.* Men had gone into trucks on the strength of his *possible*. He knew precisely how much a soft word weighed when it was set down in ink by a clerk who wanted to go home at the end of the day.

So he did not say *possible* to Bram. He said nothing, and he counted.

"You're limping," she said. She did not look at him when she said it; she kept her eyes moving across the field, the hedgerow, the stream, the roofline beyond. She had learned that from him, or he from her, or they had both learned it from the same long winter — the habit of watching everything except the thing you were afraid of.

"I've been limping since the ferry," he said. "It hasn't improved my character."

"It's gotten worse since last night."

"Everything gets worse since last night. That's what last night is for." He shifted the weight off his left ankle, which had turned badly on the scree two days ago and had been filing complaints ever since. "How is the shoulder?"

"Bruised." She rolled it once, deliberately, showing him it worked. It was not entirely true and they both knew it. The bullet at the checkpoint had gone through the meat of it, high, and she had bound it herself with strips of Tomaz's shirt while Elias held the lantern and discovered that his hands shook worse than hers. "I can carry the case."

"You will carry the case. I'll carry myself, which is the lighter burden, if only barely."

She almost smiled. In the flat white light coming up over the mountains, her face was thinner than it had been in the foundry, sharper, the jaw he had once tried to read across an interview table now stripped down to its architecture. He thought: I have watched this person pretend to be a widow, a smuggler, a man, a stranger, and underneath all of it there has always been someone so stubbornly, catastrophically honest that she burned her own papers rather than lie one more hour. He thought: I have never in my life been that brave, and I am about to find out whether I can be it for two hundred paces.

The field lay open before them. No cover worth the name — the hedgerow ran along the stream, but between the hedgerow and the border stone was open ground, and beyond the stone more open ground, and somewhere behind them, down the mountain road where the burning checkpoint still sent up its column of smoke, a patrol that Duvall had posted against exactly this possibility.

They crossed the stream first, boots in the freezing water, Bram steadying him without being asked, her good arm under his elbow with a matter-of-factness that made the touch easier to bear than any tenderness would have. On the far bank the frost-clover crackled under their feet.

"Elias." She said his name quietly, and he understood that she was about to say something real, because she saved his name for real things. "If we're separated—"

"We won't be separated."

"If we are." Her voice stayed level. "The case opens at the left seam. My father's stitching — you pull the thread and the whole lining comes free. There's a page at the front, in his handwriting, explaining what the book is. After the names there's a section of orders — requisition orders, transport manifests. Signatures." She glanced at him then, briefly. "Some of them are yours."

"I know which ones are mine."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway, because I want you to hear me say that it doesn't change—" She stopped. Started again. "It doesn't change what I think of you. That's all. I wanted it said out loud, in daylight, before we get to that roof."

He looked at her. The whitewashed stone stood ahead of them, absurdly small, and beyond it the flag, and beyond the flag the rest of a life he had assumed, until six weeks ago, he would live out in ledgers and quarterly reports and a small apartment where nobody visited twice.

"Bram," he said. "When we get inside, they'll ask how the documents were obtained. They'll ask for provenance. A foreign office can't print rumor; they'll need testimony, corroboration, a chain of custody. You'll give your statement. And when they ask who else can verify the contents — who handled the requisitions, whose signature appears on the transport orders for the eastern convoys —"

"Yes," she said.

"—you should tell them there's an army clerk named Elias Rehn who can confirm every figure, every date, every convoy number, from memory, because he copied them three times and hated himself thoroughly each time."

"That's not how I'd put it."

"No," he said. "You'd be kinder. Don't be kinder. Kindness makes testimony sound unreliable."

They walked. Ninety paces. Sixty.

The field was so quiet he could hear the water in the stream behind them and, very faintly, carried down the wind off the mountain, the sound he had been counting instead of naming: hooves. Or wheels. Something mechanical and rhythmic, far back along the road, that might have been a farm cart going to market on the far side of the ridge and might not have been.

Bram heard it too. He saw her head tilt, saw her hand tighten on the case strap.

"Faster," she said.

"Faster draws attention."

"So does standing still." But she matched his pace, adjusting to his ankle without comment, and he hated the limp then with a pure and concentrated hatred, hated his own body for being slow at the one moment in his entire careful, cowardly, calculating life when speed would have been worth anything he owned.

Thirty paces from the stone.

The sound resolved. Wheels, and behind the wheels the particular jingle of harness that no farm cart in Vellmarch had used since the army confiscated the good horses. Elias did not turn around. Turning around was a luxury for people who intended to keep walking afterward.

"There's a ditch," Bram said, "left side, before the stone—"

"They've seen us already. If they'd wanted to take us quietly they'd have waited till the trees." He said it calmly and believed it, and the believing was strange, like reading a report about someone else's death. "They want us running. Running men can be shot lawfully. Standing men require paperwork."

"Then let's make them do paperwork." She stopped walking. Turned. Set the typewriter case down in the clover with both hands, carefully, the way her father must have set type, and faced the road.

Elias looked at her and felt something he had no clerical vocabulary for. Eleven years of forms and he could not find the box to tick.

"Bram Winterbourne," he said. "Pick up the case."

"Not yet." Behind them, down the field, the patrol came around the hedgerow's end — four riders and a mounted sergeant, blue-gray coats, carbines. Far. Two hundred meters, maybe more. Enough time to run. Not enough to run and win. "Tell me something true first. We may not get another chance and I'm tired of carrying things unsaid."

The riders came on. Elias counted them the way he counted everything, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

"The first order I ever signed," he said, "was a requisition for the Aldous Winterbourne foundry. Paper stock, ink, movable type, and one master ledger recovered from premises, disposition pending. I wrote *disposition pending* because I didn't want to write what happened to things with that disposition. That was the whole of my rebellion — a phrase. Four hundred people went into trucks and I softened a phrase, and I told myself for eleven years that the softening was mercy." His voice did not shake, which surprised him. "It wasn't mercy. It was cowardice wearing mercy's coat. And then I met a woman who burned her own name rather than let a book burn, and I understood that I had never once, in my entire life, chosen the expensive thing."

"Elias—"

"Let me finish, I've rehearsed this." The hoofbeats were louder now; he could see the lead rider's breath in the cold. "I don't regret the transfer to the river checkpoint, or the stamp I gave you, or any of it. If they shoot me here, I want it on record — and you're the only record left — that Elias Rehn, clerk second class, chose this. Nobody forced me onto this road. I walked onto it. That's the first thing I've ever done that wasn't assigned to me, and it's the only thing I've done right, and I need you to know it wasn't your doing. You gave me the map. I chose the direction."

"Then walk the last thirty paces," she said, and her voice finally cracked, just once, like ice on a pond. "Walk them with me and choose it again on the other side. That's how choosing works. It doesn't stop at the border stone."

For a moment — one full second, he counted it — he almost obeyed her. Then the lead rider raised his carbine, unhurried, at rest but raised, and shouted something across the field that the wind took most of, except the word *halt*, which travels well in every language.

"They'll fire warning shots first," Elias said. "Regulation. Then they'll fire at the runner, not the stander. Bram." He took the case from the ground and pushed it into her arms. "Run."

"No."

"*Run.*" He stepped away from her, toward the road, toward the riders, putting his body between her and the carbines the way a man steps between a dog and a child — instinctively, stupidly, without any plan for what happens after the dog arrives. "You're the faster. You've been the faster since the ferry. The case goes to that roof or none of this — Tomaz, Marta, the village with no names, none of it — means anything. Go. Now."

She looked at him. Thirty paces from freedom, with soldiers coming and her shoulder torn and her father's life's work in her arms, she looked at him with an expression he would carry wherever he was going, whatever that turned out to be: fury and gratitude braided into one rope, pulling opposite directions.

"Print your signature beside the names," he said. "That's my price. That's what I want. Not forgiveness — ink. Put Elias Rehn on the same page as them. Let them try me forever."

Then he turned and ran — limped, staggered, ran — *toward* the riders, shouting, waving his arms, giving them a target that was not her, and behind him he heard her boots strike the frozen clover, fast and getting faster, and he thought: *that sound. Let that be the last sound. Better than a stamp on a form. Better than anything I ever wrote.*

The first shot went wide, as regulations demanded.

The second did not.

* * *

Pain arrived late, politely, after the fact of it — a blow like a door slammed against his back, and then the ground coming up, and then the sky above the clover, enormously blue, indifferent. He had read casualty reports. He had filed casualty reports. None of them had mentioned the cold of the ground, or how loud your own breathing becomes, or that the first coherent thought is always administrative: *the report on this will be inaccurate.*

Boots. Voices. Someone rolling him over with a boot toe, careless as handling freight. Above him, the sergeant's face, young under the cap, frowning down with the particular annoyance of a man whose morning has developed complications.

"On the ground, sir?" the sergeant called across the field. "There's a woman — she's making for the building—"

Elias turned his head. Through a grayness that was beginning to gather at the edges of the world like fog through a broken window, he could see Bram running — running beautifully, terribly, the case clutched to her chest, her braid come loose and flying behind her like something cut free. Shots cracked. Dirt jumped near her feet. She did not falter. Of course she did not falter. She had never once, in all the weeks he had known her, done the easy thing at the necessary moment, and she was not going to begin at the border.

"She's unarmed," Elias said. The words cost him; each one came up from somewhere deep, like hauling a bucket from a well that kept refilling with blood. "Sergeant. Look at me. Clerk second class, requisition bureau — you know my face, you've seen it on the manifests. I order you—" A cough took him, wet and awful. "I order you to note in your report that I was armed. That I resisted. Anything. Write anything you like about me. Just write that the woman was following my orders. Give her my authority. It's the only use I ever had for it."

The sergeant looked down at him for a long moment. Somewhere across the field another shot, and another, and then — Elias's heart seized on it like a drowning man seizing driftwood — the sharp triple-note of a whistle from the press office roof, and answering shouts in a foreign accent, and the riders pulling up, wheeling, cursing, because the building stood on the other side of a line and everyone in Vellmarch's army knew what happened to careers that crossed lines.

Bram reached the stream. Bram crossed the stream. Bram stumbled on the far bank, rose, and did not look back, and Elias loved her with his whole ruined heart for not looking back, because looking back would have wasted the distance she had paid so much to gain, and she was not a woman who wasted anything.

The gray fog was coming faster now. The sergeant's face swam above him, saying words — *hold on, hold on, medic, someone get the medic* — the reflexive liturgy men recite over the dying because the alternative is silence.

"Don't bother," Elias said. It came out mostly air. He found that his hand had crawled across the clover, searching, and closed on nothing, and he laughed, or made the shape of a laugh. "No paper. Of course. I die without a form. Fitting. Poetic. Someone should file it."

The sergeant knelt. Young face, frightened eyes. "Sir, we can—"

"My pocket. Left coat pocket." The fog was nearly total now, but there was one thing left, one entry unfinished, and Elias Rehn had never in his life left an entry unfinished. "Notebook. Give it to the woman who comes back for the body. She will come back. Tell her—" Breathe. Count. One, two, three, the way he had taught himself to survive inspections, interrogations, the long nights of copying names he wished he could unknow. "Tell her the figures in the back are the eastern convoys. Dates, numbers, destinations. Everything the ledger needs to be believed. She'll understand. Tell her I finished the column."

The boy's hands, fumbling at his coat. The notebook leaving his pocket — good, good, that mattered, that was the one dispatch that had to get through. The cold was in his chest now, vast and quiet, and beneath it, impossibly, warmth: the memory of a stamp coming down on a manifest, his own hand doing, at last, the one thing his whole life had pointed toward without his knowing.

"Her name," he said. "In your report. Write her name."

"Sir?"

"Her true name. Bram Winterbourne. Daughter of Aldous. Type cutter." The fog closed like a ledger closing. "She was never no one. Write it down. Write it down. Write—"

* * *

What follows, the sergeant's report recorded accurately, because the boy was new enough that the machinery had not yet taught him otherwise:

At 0640 hours, pursuing a fugitive female and an unidentified male accomplice near the border marker at Closter Field, this patrol encountered the accomplice advancing on their position in a threatening manner. The accomplice was shot attempting to flee. The female escaped across the border into the grounds of the foreign press office, where jurisdiction ends. The accomplice was subsequently identified as Elias Rehn, clerk second class, requisition bureau, listed absent without leave since [date]. Before expiring, the deceased spoke repeatedly, asking that certain statements be recorded.

This patrol records them: that he acted alone and on his own authority; that the woman followed his orders; that her name is Bram Winterbourne; and that he asked — these were his last words, and this patrol does not know what they mean — *put it in ink.*

* * *

The fog, when it lifted, was not fog. It was a ceiling, whitewashed, with a hairline crack running through it like a fault line through territory.

Elias lay still and listened to himself breathe, which was the strangest work he had ever done. Breath came. Pain came, enormous, everywhere, and beneath the pain a smaller, cleaner fact repeated itself like a stamp coming down: *alive. alive. alive.*

A face leaned over him. Not the sergeant. Older, bearded, smelling of pipe smoke and machine oil, in a shirt with ink stains up both forearms.

"He's asking in Vellmarchish," the face said, to someone out of view. "Something about paper. He keeps saying *paper*." Hands adjusted a blanket over him, competent and gentle. "Well. Whoever he is, he's in our house now, and the colonel's patrols don't cross our fence. Rest, friend. You're past the stone. Whatever you were running from, it stops at the stone."

Past the stone.

Elias turned his head, an expedition that took everything he had. Through a window, in the gray afternoon light, he could see the border marker standing in the empty field, and beyond it the stream, and beyond that the mountains they had crossed, smoking faintly where the pass still burned.

And on the bench outside the window, in the cold, guarded by no one because no one imagined she needed guarding, sat Bram Winterbourne — shoulder bandaged properly now, hands wrapped around a mug of something steaming — with the typewriter case open across her knees and her father's ledger spread upon the press-room table beside her, and a composing stick in her hand.

Even from the window, even through pain and distance, Elias could see what she was setting: metal letters gleaming in rows, gathered one by one into the stick with those quick, sure fingers, and among them, unmistakable, the tall strokes and firm serifs of a name.

Not hers.

His.

She looked up then, through the glass, and their eyes met across the border stone and the whole length of everything that had been lost to bring them here, and Bram Winterbourne raised the composing stick slightly — a salute, a promise, a sentence begun — and turned back to her work.

The presses, Elias understood, were already warm.

Somewhere below, iron began to turn.
