## 34. The Press Office Refuses

The press office of the *Courier de la Frontière* occupied the ground floor of a former customs house on the far side of the border stone, and it smelled of ink and cabbage and the particular dampness of buildings that had changed hands too many times to belong to anyone. Bram stood in the doorway with Elias's blood still tacky under her fingernails — she had not been able to wash all of it off at the barn, and had stopped trying — and understood, in the way one understands a locked door before touching the handle, that she was about to be turned away.

The man behind the desk wore spectacles mended with wire and a waistcoat that had once been fine. He looked up from a galley proof, saw her — a woman, gaunt as a fence post, carrying a typewriter case that left rust-colored smears where her fingers gripped it — and his face did the arithmetic of risk before he even asked her name.

"Closed," he said. "Office hours are nine to four."

"It's ten past nine," Bram said.

"Then you've missed yesterday's four o'clock." He went back to his proof.

She did not leave. She had walked eleven miles on a leg that no longer reported pain so much as a distant, administrative objection, past fields where the other country began in increments — fences painted differently, a church spire with a different kind of cross, children shouting a different language in a farmyard — carrying forty pounds of names on her shoulder. She set the case down on the floor with both hands because her arms were done pretending, and the sound it made against the boards was heavier than a typewriter case should sound, and the editor heard it too. He glanced up again.

"You're hurt," he said. It wasn't concern. It was an inventory.

"I'm from Vellmarch," she said. "I have documents."

"Everyone who comes through that door has documents." He took off the wire-mended spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and she saw then how tired he was, tired in the marrow, the tiredness of a man who printed other people's catastrophes for a living and had learned that most of them were lies wearing good coats. "Madame. Listen to me, because I'll only say this once and I'd rather not say it twice. Last month I ran a story about massacres in the valley towns. Eyewitness testimony. Photographs, even — photographs. Two weeks later three separate men came through that door with photographs proving the opposite. All three were lying. One of them was lying *for* us, if you understand me, which is worse. My government does not want a war over your river town. My readers do not want a war over anything. And I will not put my name — my *name* — under paper that some colonel across the border can burn tomorrow and call my corpse an accident. Do you follow me?"

"I followed you at *massacres*," Bram said.

He flinched. Good, some animal part of her noted. He should flinch.

"So you refuse," she said.

"I decline," he said, "to be reckless."

"It isn't recklessness if you know what it costs."

"Then tell me what it costs." He leaned back, and the chair complained under him, and for a moment he looked less like a gatekeeper and more like a man standing in front of his own locked door wondering when someone would finally break it down. "Go on. You have a ledger, I assume — everyone has ledgers now, they breed in cellars. Tell me why yours is different. Convince me. You have until my typesetter arrives, and he is never late, so make it good."

* * *

She told him about the foundry first, because it was the only door into everything else.

"My father was Aldous Winterbourne. He cut type by hand for thirty-one years. If you have ever seen a Vellmarch broadsheet — and you have, they come over the border wrapped in fish shipments — you have seen his letters without knowing them. The W has a foot like a little boat. He said a letter should stand up on its own or it deserved to fall." Her voice did something strange on *father* and she let it do it; she was past the country of controlling things. "The army requisitioned the town in the spring. Colonel Hessler. There's a clerk named Elias Rehn who signed the requisition orders — his signature is on the seizure of every press, every roll of paper, every record in Vellmarch, including the master ledger of the disappeared. Do you know what that ledger is? Every person taken by Hessler's men since the occupation began. Names, dates, last known addresses, the charges invented for them. Some entries have a line after the name. Some lines just stop. The stopped ones are the ones nobody came home from."

The editor had gone still in a way she recognized: the stillness of a man listening against his own interest.

"The ledger was hidden in my cellar. In a typewriter case. My father sewed it into the lining. He died before he could tell me — or before he chose to tell me; I've stopped trying to decide which. I carried it out of Vellmarch past three checkpoints. A ferrywoman hid me below deck and was arrested for it; her name is Marta Ostrow and she owes nothing to anyone but a dead type cutter. An army deserter named Corvin died covering my escape from the mountain pass two nights ago. And Elias Rehn—" She stopped. Her hand went to the case handle, unconsciously, and came away red. "Elias Rehn was shot by a pursuing patrol within sight of your border stone. Yesterday morning. He was a requisition clerk. His name is in the ledger — not as a victim. As an instrument. He made me promise to print his signature beside the names, because he said—" and here her voice finally broke, cleanly, like type snapping under the press, "—he said complicity redeemed in ink was more than he deserved and exactly what the record required."

Silence. Somewhere above them a floorboard creaked; the typesetter arriving, maybe, or the building settling.

The editor took his spectacles off again. Put them back on. Took them off.

"Ledgers can be forged," he said quietly. Not cruelly. Almost gently, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "A woman who cuts type by hand — your own account — could forge anything. Names, dates, a dead clerk's confession. I believe you're telling me the truth, madame. That is precisely the problem. Belief is not verification. If I print this and it is false, or even unfalsifiable, I hand Hessler the rope to hang every exile paper on the frontier. He'd frame the page. He'd print *our* retraction in *his* gazette with our masthead copied beneath it. One unverifiable ledger buys him a decade of 'the foreign presses invent their martyrs.' Do you see what I'm telling you? Your truth, unproven, is worth less than nothing. It is ammunition for the other side."

Bram stood very still. Outside the window a cart went by, ordinary as bread, and she hated it.

"What would verify it?" she asked. "Tell me the standard. I'll meet it."

"Independent corroboration. A second source inside Vellmarch. Official records released by the military government itself—" He laughed, short and bitter. "You see the trap. Only Hessler can confirm Hessler's crimes, and Hessler will die old and decorated first."

"Then there is nothing I can carry that you'll print."

"There is nothing anyone can carry." He spread his hands. "That is the century we live in, madame. Paper proves nothing anymore. Everyone learned to forge. Everyone learned to doubt. We have perfected disbelief until it protects exactly the people it was invented to expose."

And Bram felt something settle in her — not despair; she had spent her whole life being told what she was not, and there was a familiar weight to it, but underneath the weight something else was moving, something with edges. Her father's voice, absurdly, in the foundry dark: *a letter should stand up on its own or it deserves to fall.* She looked down at her hands. Ink in the creases. Blood in the knuckles. Forty years of hiding folded into two hands, and every hour of it suddenly, luminously useless — because the editor was right. Paper proved nothing. Forged papers had carried her past three checkpoints and forged papers had condemned her town and the two arts were identical and everyone knew it, everyone at every checkpoint knew that paper was a costume, and that was exactly why the costume had worked, and exactly why it would fail here, at the one desk in the world where she needed to be believed.

There was one thing left that could not be forged.

It could only be spent.

* * *

"Then don't print the ledger," Bram said.

The editor looked up.

"Not yet. Not alone. Print *me*." She heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used at the composing stone, reading type backward by lamplight. "Sworn testimony. Under my true name, my true history, witnessed and dated and published in full. Not 'sources say.' Not 'documents obtained.' Me. Aldous Winterbourne's daughter, who has lived in Vellmarch her whole life, who cut half the pamphlets the resistance printed, who carried the ledger out herself and can describe its contents entry by entry — and here is the part you cannot buy or forge, monsieur: I am describing crimes I can be hanged for admitting. Forgery at the checkpoints means hanging. Printing resistance pamphlets means hanging. I am standing in your office confessing to capital offenses in front of a witness, in front of *you*, and asking you to publish the confession. Hessler can call a document false. He cannot call me false while I am alive and testifying, and if he kills me for it, the testimony becomes the one thing he can never burn — because you will already have printed it under a name, a face, a trade, a street address, a father's grave. A name is verifiable, monsieur. People remember names. People remember *me*. Ask any refugee on this side of the stone whether Winterbourne had a son or a daughter. Half of Vellmarch bought type from my father and half of them suspected the truth about his son for years. Suspicion is memory, and memory is corroboration, and you said yourself corroboration is what you need. Here it is. Standing up on its own. Or it deserves to fall."

The room was quiet enough that she could hear the galley proof drying on his desk, the faint smell of solvent rising off it like a tide going out.

"That is the bravest thing," the editor said slowly, "and the most foolish, and they are usually the same thing. Do you understand what you're offering? Once your name is in print beside these accusations, there is no border for you anymore. No going back to any life. Hessler's warrant follows names across borders better than armies do. You would be spending—" he gestured at her, at the whole battered inventory of her, "—all of this. Permanently."

"I spent eleven years learning to be no one," Bram said. "It kept me alive and it kept everyone else invisible too. Every pamphlet I printed unsigned. Every name I set in type that I couldn't sign. That's what he counted on, monsieur — Hessler. He doesn't fear documents. Documents can be seized. He fears *witnesses*, because a witness is a document that walks and talks and multiplies. So yes. I understand what I'm offering. It's the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit."

The editor sat with that. Then he pulled a sheet toward himself, uncapped a pen, and wrote three lines without showing her, and turned the sheet around.

It read: *If I print this, I print it complete. Testimony, ledger contents as appendix, the clerk's signature, the ferrywoman's name, the deserter's name. All of it or none. And I want your statement sworn before a notary this afternoon, and I want your permission to photograph you holding the case.*

"All of it," Bram said. "Elias would insist on the signature. He'd probably insist on the exact spelling."

"And Oskar?" the editor said.

She blinked. "What?"

"Your account mentioned the resistance printer who moved the ledger. Oskar. If he sold the route — and you told me he did, an hour ago, in a sentence you probably regret — his name goes in too. As collaborator. As the reason Marta Ostrow is in a cell and Corvin is in a ditch." The editor's eyes behind the wire-mended spectacles were not unkind, but they were absolutely clear. "Complete means complete, madame. Even the parts that cost you."

Bram thought of Oskar's face when he'd admitted it, the terrible relief of a man setting down something heavy and shameful. Thought of her own hands, forging papers, forging a son, forging invisibility year after year — and how thin the line was between surviving a purge and serving it. *Even allies have reasons to want silence.* She had written that thought herself, in her own head, in Oskar's chapter, and it applied to her too, to the daughter who'd hidden inside her father's name.

"Complete means complete," she agreed. "Print Oskar. And print that I forgive him — no. Don't print forgiveness. Print the facts. Let the court do the rest. There's going to be a court, monsieur. There has to be."

The editor almost smiled. "There is always a court afterward. It's getting the evidence there alive that defeats everyone." He stood, joints cracking, a man rising from a long siege. "The notary is two streets over. He owes me money, which makes him punctual. Then we come back here and you sit at that desk and you give me your statement word by word, and I warn you now — I will interrupt you. I will ask what the weather was doing. What color the sergeant's coat was. How many steps from the cellar stairs to the door. Because detail, madame, is the one thing liars run out of and witnesses never do. Can you give me detail?"

Bram looked at the window, where the foreign sun lay on the cobbles of a street where no soldier had ever posted a requisition order. She thought of lamplight on brass type. Of the bilge water rising around her ankles. Of Elias's blood cooling between the wheel ruts, and his hand closing on her wrist with the last strength of a man paying a debt.

"I can give you the smell of the ink," she said. "I can give you the sound the ledger made when I cut the lining open. I can give you my father's foot on the W."

"Then we begin," the editor said, and held out his hand — and paused, mid-gesture, struck by a thought that visibly rearranged his face. "One more thing. Your name. Your true name, for the record. Say it for me now. I want to hear how it sounds in your mouth, the first time in eleven years."

Bram opened her mouth.

Outside, far off down the boulevard, a church bell began to strike the hour — and beneath it, thinner and wrong, came another bell, from the direction of the border stone, ringing fast and repeated, the alarm pattern, the pattern every border town drilled into its children.

The editor's head turned toward the window. "That's not the hour," he said. "That's the crossing alarm. Someone's come over the stone."

Bram was already reaching for the case.
