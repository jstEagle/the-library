## 35. Testimony

The press office smelled of ink and cold tea, and it was the most honest room Bram had ever entered.

She had expected marble, or something like it — the foreign consulate she'd imagined from Oskar's stories was all brass fittings and men who spoke in lowered voices about treaties. Instead the office of *Der Grenzbote* occupied two rooms above a bakery on the border town's only cobbled street, and the editor, a heavyset man named Vogel whose shirtsleeves were permanently grayed at the cuffs, received her at a desk buried under galleys of type.

He had refused her twice already. Once yesterday evening, when she had come in still bleeding through the bandage on her forearm and laid the ledger on his desk like a relic; once again this morning, when she returned having slept four hours in the barn behind the tannery and washed herself in a horse trough until her hands stopped shaking.

"Documents," Vogel had said both times, tapping the ledger's cover without opening it, "can be forged. I have seen forged ledgers before. I have seen forged diaries, forged orders, forged photographs of massacres that never happened and photographs of massacres that did happen, presented as something else entirely. My government will not go to war over paper."

Now, on the third day, he looked up from his desk as she came in and did not tap the ledger. He looked tired, and older than he had seemed, and Bram understood that he had spent two nights reading what she had carried.

"You read it," she said.

"I read it." He closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child. "Four hundred and eleven names in the main register. Cross-references to requisition numbers. Dates of detention. Dates — " he paused, and his hand flattened against the cover — "dates of disposition, where the army recorded one. Your father indexed them by trade. Blacksmiths together. Teachers together. There is an entire page of names under the heading *Printers.*"

"My father believed a man's trade told you why they took him," Bram said. "A blacksmith can make weapons. A teacher can make arguments. A printer — " her voice caught, and she let it, because she had decided somewhere in the barn last night that she would not perform composure anymore — "a printer makes memory."

Vogel sat back. Through the window behind him she could see the border stone itself, a squat granite marker in the middle of the street where the cobbles changed color, and beyond it the road climbing toward Vellmarch's mountains. Somewhere up there was a pass with soldiers on it, a burned checkpoint, a dead deserter whose name she did not know yet but would learn, and a grave she had not been able to dig deep enough.

"Miss Winterbourne." Vogel folded his hands. "I believe you. That is not the same as being able to print you. If I publish this and my ministry disavows it, the paper dies, I die professionally, and your town's names die with us — because after we are silenced, no one will dare reprint what the *Grenzbote* was destroyed for printing. You see the arithmetic. A false document costs me everything for nothing. An unverifiable true document costs me everything for less than nothing."

"I've thought about nothing else for two days," Bram said. "While I sat in that barn sewing my arm back together, I thought about it. And I kept arriving at the same place." She set her palm flat on the ledger, beside his hand. "You said documents can be forged. You're right. My father taught me to forge them — seals, signatures, whole identities cut in steel by lamplight. I could have made you ten ledgers as convincing as this one. So the ledger will never be enough, not for you, not for any editor, not for any court that comes after. Paper proves nothing anymore. The army saw to that when it taught an entire country to lie in print."

"Then what remains?"

"A person," Bram said. "One person, standing in front of you, who cannot be forged. Who signs her own name and lets you do whatever verification you want — write to Vellmarch, send someone across the pass when it opens, dig up the church registry that wasn't burned, interview the ferryman Marta Kessel, who hid me below deck and is probably in a cell right now for it. One witness who swears, under penalty of whatever law you invoke, that every page of this book is what it claims to be. You wanted verification no one can fake. Here I am. I'm the only thing in this entire affair that can't be counterfeited."

The bakery smell drifted up through the floorboards — bread, ordinary and warm — and Vogel was silent so long that Bram heard the presses downstairs shift into their rhythm, the great iron heartbeat of the building.

"You understand what you're offering," he said at last. "Testimony is not anonymity. The moment your name appears in our columns attached to this ledger, every officer in Colonel Hessler's command knows exactly who took it and where it went. The warrant on your head becomes a verdict. Even if the regime falls — *when,* if there is any justice — there will be men who never accept that fall, and they will know your face and your name and your trade."

"My name has been a forgery for six years," Bram said. "I would like to spend the real one on something."

* * *

They did it properly, because Vogel insisted on properness the way other men insisted on breathing.

A clerk — a young woman with ink-stained fingers named Adele, who had been typing dispatches in the corner throughout their negotiations and who now set down her work and asked, quietly, if she might be the one to take the statement — brought the sworn-affidavit forms used for customs disputes and inheritance claims, the most mundane legal paper in the building. Bram found something almost funny in that: her father's life's secret work, six years of disguise, three checkpoints, two dead men and one maimed deserter, all funneled at last onto a form designed for quarrels over grain weights.

She gave her name first, and it came out of her mouth strange and enormous, a word she had not spoken aloud since her father's funeral.

"Bramilde Winterbourne. Daughter of Aldous Winterbourne, type founder, of Foundry Lane, Vellmarch. Born in the foundry house above the workshop. Cut my first letter at seven years old — a lowercase e, badly done; my father kept it in his apron pocket for years anyway."

Adele typed it. Bram watched each word appear on the sheet and felt them land in her chest like stitches going in.

She told it in order, because order was the discipline her father had beaten into her: *a story told out of sequence is a story someone can take apart.* The pamphlets at night while soldiers drilled in the square. Oskar's warning that the ledger's hiding place died with its hider. The requisition posted on the foundry door, sealing her livelihood into contraband overnight. The hollow typewriter case, the lining slit with a surgeon's patience, the ledger sewn inside by hands she had watched cut type her whole childhood — her father trusting her with the truth without ever asking her to carry it.

Then the road. Sergeant Duvall's suspicion and the fresh ink in the foundry. Elias Rehn, the clerk who had signed the requisition and then unmade himself to help her carry it past his own signature. The grain gate, the storm on the water, Marta Kessel's debt of obituaries. The village with no names on its doors and no graves in its churchyard — she made Adele read that passage back twice, to be certain it was exact. The ambush at the chapel, deserters reading their brothers' names aloud from the ledger like a litany. The pass, the forged papers, the fire, the flight.

And the border stone. She told them about Elias Rehn stepping between her and the patrol's rifles with the case still slung over her shoulder, and how he had gripped her wrist hard enough to leave marks she could show them, and what he had said.

"He said: *print my signature beside the names. Not as a victim. As an instrument. Let them see what a pen does before a gun ever fires.*" Bram's voice held. She had rehearsed nothing, but grief, she was learning, had its own discipline. "He processed the requisition orders. His name is in that ledger among the functionaries, because my father recorded instruments alongside victims — that was the ledger's honesty. Elias knew it. He wanted it printed anyway. He said a man should be accountable for what his signature did, even — *especially* — if he spends the rest of his life trying to unsign it."

Adele's typewriter keys rang through the small room. When Bram finished, near dusk, the statement ran nine pages.

Vogel read every line. Then he had Adele read it aloud while he followed on the typescript, correcting two dates and one spelling. Then he pushed the affidavit across the desk along with a pen.

"Sign here. And here, acknowledging that this testimony is given freely, for publication, under your true name."

Bram signed. Her handwriting — her real handwriting, the one reserved for the foundry's account books, the one no soldier had ever seen — looked small and strange beneath all those typed words. Like a seed planted under a field of stones.

"There's one more thing," she said.

Vogel looked up.

"You'll set the ledger in type. Four hundred and eleven names, plus the functionaries, plus my father's annotations. But I want one more entry. Added at the end, in the main register, dated yesterday." She tapped the ledger's final blank column. "*Rehn, Elias. Army clerk. Died at the border stone carrying this record to print.*"

"He isn't among the disappeared," Vogel said carefully. "The register is for — "

"He is now," Bram said. "Your army made him disappear too. They just haven't filed the paperwork."

* * *

They set her name first.

It was her condition and Vogel did not argue, perhaps because he understood what she understood: that the ledger's authority would live or die on whether the woman who delivered it was willing to stand inside the printed record rather than beside it. So at dawn, in the composing room below the editorial offices, Bram Winterbourne stood at a case of type for the first time since leaving Vellmarch, and her hands remembered everything.

The foreign type was not her father's type. The letterforms were broader, the serifs blunter, cut for a language with different appetites. But a composing stick balances the same in any country, and spacing is spacing everywhere, and when she began setting her own name — B-R-A-M-I-L-D-E — her fingers moved with a fluency that made the pressmen stop their work and watch.

"Quads," she murmured, taking them without looking. "Thin space after the M. The D wants a hair more shoulder-room or it'll collide at pressure." She locked the line, lifted it to the light, and read it upside down and backward as she had read every line of her life: *BRAMILDE WINTERBOURNE, TYPE FOUNDER'S DAUGHTER, OF VELLMARCH.*

Her father had taught her that a compositor's first duty was to read everything mirrored — that a person comfortable seeing her own name reversed was a person who understood that truth sometimes arrived looking wrong-side-out.

She set the headline next, working from Adele's clean typescript while the newsroom argued around her about placement and the ministry's likely reaction and whether the consul would telegraph a protest before noon. Bram tuned none of it out, exactly — she had learned on the mountain road, trading histories with Elias in the blue cold, that you could carry a thing fully and still hear the world — but the argument passed over her like weather. She set the ledger's introduction in her father's spirit if not his steel: *These four hundred and eleven names were recorded by Aldous Winterbourne, citizen of Vellmarch, who hid this register inside a typewriter case beneath his workshop, believing that someday someone would carry it to light. That someone is his daughter. What follows was taken down under oath.*

Then the names themselves. Adele typed the register copy overnight; two compositors worked it in shifts; Bram proofed every galley against the original, name by name, four hundred and eleven times, her lips moving silently through the trades and the dates of disappearance. Blacksmiths. Teachers. Printers — the whole page of them, her father's colleagues, men whose foundries and shops had been seized in the first weeks and whose owners had followed the equipment into army custody. She reached the page at midnight and stood over it so long that the night compositor touched her elbow and offered her coffee she didn't drink.

At the bottom of the printers' page, in the margin, her father's annotation remained, and she made sure Adele had transcribed it:

*Every one of these men set type. Their presses were confiscated before their persons, because the state fears the alphabet more than the arm. Let whoever reads this understand: they were killed for letters. Therefore the letters must survive them.*

Near dawn, the forme was locked. Bram helped carry it to the press herself — the pressmen let her, maybe because she had earned it, maybe because a woman who sets her own name in type for a death warrant is not a woman you argue with — and she stood at the delivery board as the cylinder made its first pass.

The sound filled the building. Not the modest clatter of her father's proofing press in the foundry's back room, but a full flatbed machine built to turn out thousands, and it roared through the floorboards and up through her boots and into her teeth, and the first sheet came off the delivery tapes and dropped into the stack with a slap like a door closing on the old world.

She picked it up before anyone else could touch it.

Ink still wet and shining. Her name at the top of the second column. Below it, in tight double columns running down the full length of the sheet, the names of Vellmarch — the cooper Havel Brandt, disappeared the ninth of March; the schoolmistress Ilse Ferren, the twenty-second of April; the whole sorrowing census of a river town that had gone quiet one household at a time. Elias Rehn's name at the register's end, dated, described, accounted for at last. And in the margin of the printers' page, her father's words, set solid, refusing oblivion in two languages now instead of one.

Bram held the first sheet and found that triumph, as it turned out, weighed exactly as much as cost. It was not a feeling she could separate into parts. It was one weight, and she had carried heavier — the case over the pass, the ledger through floodwater, the promise at the border stone with blood soaking through her sleeve — but this weight was different, because it was meant to be put down. Meant to be multiplied. Meant to leave her hands entirely and never come back.

"How many?" she asked.

Vogel had come down to the pressroom, sleeves rolled, watching the machine eat paper. "Five thousand for the first run. We'll print until the paper runs out or the ministry arrests me, whichever comes first." He glanced at her sideways. "The consul telegraphed at midnight. The ministry here is 'concerned.' They've requested we withhold publication pending review."

"And?"

"And the review requires the document to be authenticated," Vogel said, and something dry moved at the corner of his mouth, the first expression she'd seen him wear that wasn't exhaustion. "Which requires time. Correspondence. Experts. Perhaps months. In the meantime, the *Grenzbote* is published at four o'clock, as it always is, because we are a newspaper, madam, and newspapers do not wait for ministries to finish being concerned."

Bram looked at the stack growing under the delivery board — fifty sheets, a hundred, the pile thickening with the soft rush of falling paper — and thought of the border stone outside, the granite marker in the middle of the street where one country's cobbles became another's. Five thousand copies. More tomorrow. And across that stone, on the other side of the mountains, a town full of people who had learned to keep their heads down and their doors shut and their grief unspoken, waiting for something they didn't have a word for yet.

"They'll need to get across," she said. "The sheets. The army holds every road through the pass, and Duvall knows my face and yours by now. How does a newspaper cross a closed border?"

Vogel wiped his inky hands on his already-ruined apron and smiled — actually smiled, tired and crooked, like a man about to tell her the best secret of his long stubborn career.

"Madam," he said, "you are looking at the only industry in the world that has never once respected a frontier. Wait until you meet our carriers."

Outside, the presses thundered on, and down the street toward the border stone, in the gray hour before dawn, the first lanterns were already being lit.
