## 36. The Lanterns

The presses in the foreign press office did not stop for three days and two nights.

Bram heard them even in her sleep — a sound like rain on a tin roof, like her father's foundry breathing. She slept in a cot behind the composing room because she could not bear to be far from it, and because every time the press paused to change plates, she woke certain it would not start again. But it always started. The great cylinder turned, the sheets fed through one after another after another, and each sheet came out warm as a living thing.

They had printed four thousand copies before the editor, a grey-bearded man named Corvus who had refused her twice before he believed her once, ordered the type left standing. "We'll run more if we must," he said. "But paper is finite and courage is not. Get these across tonight."

The broadsheets carried no masthead. At Bram's insistence, they carried no argument, no editorial, nothing that could be called sedition by anyone who wished to call it that. They carried only what the ledger held: names. Four hundred and eleven names. Dates of arrest. Dates — where known — of death. Places of burial, or the word *unknown*. And beside them, in smaller type, the testimony: sworn statements from forty-one witnesses, transcribed in the press office over two days while Bram sat with the ledger open on her knees and answered questions until her voice went hoarse.

At the bottom of the front page, in type Bram had cut herself with her father's gravers, in the same hand she had learned at nine years old standing on a stool at his shoulder, ran a single line:

*These are the people of Vellmarch. They existed. We are their proof.*

And beneath it, the signature Elias Rehn had made her promise to print. His name, set among the dead and disappeared, marked not as victim but as *instrument* — the clerk who had signed the requisition orders, who had processed the confiscations, who had stamped papers through checkpoints while knowing what the stamps meant. He had asked for this with the last breath in him, and Bram had cut the letters herself, weeping, and printed them, and felt something in her chest both break and set like cooling metal.

Now the sheets were stacked in bundles of fifty, wrapped in oilcloth against the river damp, and the first carriers were already waiting.

* * *

Marta's ferry crossed at midnight with the fog lying thick enough to drink.

She was old, and the soldiers at the river checkpoint knew her, and that was precisely why she had volunteered. A woman of seventy-one years did not smuggle revolution; a woman of seventy-one years ferried market goods and grieved quietly and was beneath suspicion, which is its own kind of armor. The bundles lay under a false floor in the hold, beneath three hundredweight of turnips that smelled strong enough to make any searching soldier hurry.

The young sergeant at the dock lifted the lantern, peered into the hold, saw turnips and an old woman and a boy hired to pole the second skiff, and waved her through.

"Slow water tonight," he said. "Fog's a liar."

"Fog's honest," Marta said. "It hides everything the same."

She pushed off. In the middle of the river, wrapped in fog so complete that the banks ceased to exist, she let the current carry her and thought about Aldous Winterbourne, who had printed her husband's obituary for free twenty-two years ago because she could not pay and he could not bear to ask twice. She thought about how the man had died, and how his daughter — though the town still said *son*, though the whole story had come back across the border in whispers that Marta had listened to with her mouth shut — had done what the father would have done.

In the dark hold, four hundred and eleven names rode under her feet like ballast, and she steered them home.

* * *

The deserters took them over the mountains.

There were eleven of them now camped above the treeline, men who had walked away from Hessler's regiment at various times for various reasons, most of them the same reason. Their leader was a one-eyed corporal called Fenner whom Bram had met only once, at the chapel ambush, when he had read his brother's name aloud from the ledger pages and then put his rifle down.

He did not put the rifle down now. He slung it across his back and filled a rucksack with bundles instead, and his men did the same, and they moved along the goat tracks at night with the oilcloth packets strapped flat against their chests like bandages over wounds.

"Anyone searched," Fenner told them, "you're carrying bread. You're carrying letters from your mothers. You're carrying anything but this." He tapped the packet over his heart. "Because if they find it on you, they don't shoot you for smuggling. They shoot you for remembering. And there's a difference, and the difference is that smuggling you might survive."

They came down out of the pass in ones and twos, scattered across a week, filtering into villages where nobody asked questions because everybody already knew. In the village of Harrow's Bend, a deserter named Tull handed his bundle to a schoolteacher who read the first page by candlelight and then sat down on the floor of her own classroom and wept, because her husband's name was third from the top, with the date of his death and the word *unmarked* where the grave should have been.

By morning, every house in Harrow's Bend had a copy pasted inside the door.

By the following week, there was not a village within thirty miles of the border that did not have one.

* * *

The farmers were the cleverest, and the slowest, and the most indispensable.

Corvus had understood early that couriers get caught and cargo gets confiscated, and so he had built the distribution the way Bram's father had built type: one letter at a time, each piece ordinary, the meaning assembled only at the end. Grain sacks went south with a single folded sheet sewn into the lining. Seed orders went east with pages interleaved between the invoice copies. A cheese merchant, a tanner, a woman who sold geese at three markets — each carried a few sheets, unaware in some cases of what they carried, told only that the paper was valuable and should reach a named contact undamaged.

And at each end of each route waited someone who would receive the fragments and assemble them, copy them by hand if need be, paste them up on barn doors and church walls and the shuttered windows of requisitioned houses.

The army could stop a courier. The army could burn a print shop. The army could not search every sack of grain in the province, and Colonel Hessler — though he did not yet know it — had already lost.

* * *

Sergeant Duvall stood in the grain gate at Vellmarch and watched a farmer's cart roll through without being stopped, and the not-stopping itched at him like a splinter under the skin.

He had been reassigned — demoted in all but title, sent back to the gate he'd once commanded with his authority pared down to weighing grain and checking permits. He knew why. He had filed a report about the fugitive woman and the ledger, and the report had been received, and then nothing had happened, which in Hessler's army meant everything had happened somewhere above his head. He had been made small. He understood the arithmetic: a sergeant who fails is useful as an example; a sergeant who is right about something the command wants wrong is dangerous, and dangerous things get buried.

So he weighed grain. He checked permits. He watched carts go through.

This one belonged to a man named Pelder, who farmed the low fields by the river and whose son had been taken in the second purge and never returned. Duvall knew the son's name. It was in the file he had pulled himself, months ago, back when pulling files was still his work. The cart rolled past him and the farmer did not meet his eye, and Duvall noticed — because noticing was the last thing they had left him — that the man's hands were shaking, and that the sacks in the cart were sewn shut with new thread, and that new thread meant recently opened, which meant something had gone in or come out.

He could have stopped the cart. He had the authority to stop any cart.

He stood at his post and let it pass, and wrote nothing in the log, and hated himself a little less than he expected to.

* * *

In Vellmarch itself, the first broadsheet appeared on a Tuesday, pasted to the door of the burned-out shell that had been the Winterbourne foundry.

Oskar saw it first. He had been walking past the ruin every day since his surrender to the provisional authorities — no, that wasn't yet true; he was walking past it every day because he had not yet found the courage to surrender to anyone, and he told himself he was scouting, assessing, preparing, while the truth was that he could not stay away from the place where everything he had betrayed had begun.

The paper was pasted low, at a child's height, as if whoever had done it had been small or hurried or both. Oskar crouched and read.

His own name was there. Not in the list of the disappeared — he had expected that, had dreaded it, had wanted it destroyed for exactly this reason — but in the testimony. *Witness statement 29: Oskar Vey, printer, attests to the seizure of the underground press on [date] and to providing names under coercion; the names provided follow.* Eleven names followed his confession in cold columns, and beneath them, in the same unflinching type, the note that six of the eleven were confirmed dead.

He had given those names to save his own life. He had spent two years telling himself that anyone would have, that survival was not a crime, that the men who took the names were the criminals and he merely the map they read from. He had almost convinced himself.

Standing in the street in the grey morning light, looking at his confession printed in ten thousand copies and scattered beyond all recall, Oskar Vey discovered that he felt — of all things — lighter. The secret was gone. The thing he had guarded and hidden and killed to protect was loose in the world, nailed to doors, and it could not be unsaid now, could not be bargained away, could not be traded to another colonel for another year of breath.

He straightened up. An old woman across the street was watching him, and she had seen him read the paper, and she did not look away, and neither did he.

That afternoon, Oskar Vey walked to the offices of the provisional committee that had formed in the town hall, and gave his name to the clerk, and asked to testify.

* * *

They began calling them lanterns within the week.

No one knew who coined it — a ferryman's boy, probably, or one of the deserters' women, or maybe Corvus himself, who had a showman's streak under all that grey caution. But the name fit and it spread the way names do, faster than the thing itself, so that farmers who had never seen a press spoke of *hanging a lantern* for the dead, and children played a game of running through lanes at dusk pasting scraps of paper to walls, shouting *lantern! lantern!* as they ran.

Each page was a lantern lit for a name. That was the phrase that came back to Bram, carried over the mountains by a deserter with a bundle of fresh reports, and she sat with it a long time by the stove in the press office while the machines thundered around her.

Her father had taught her that a letter was a small machine for defeating darkness. Cut it well, ink it well, press it well, and a single mark could carry a voice across a hundred years. She had believed him the way children believe — completely, without testing it. Now she had tested it. Four hundred and eleven voices had crossed a border sewn into grain sacks and strapped to deserters' chests, and they were burning in window after window after window, and the dark that had swallowed them was full of light it could not digest.

Elias should have seen this. The thought arrived every day now, punctual as pain. He should have stood in some village square and watched a farmer's wife smooth the sheet against a door with the flat of her hand, careful, the way you handle a thing that matters. He had signed the orders that started all of it, and he had bought his redemption with the last coin he had, and he would never know whether the purchase had cleared.

Bram decided he would know. Somehow, in whatever way remained to her, she would make sure the record showed not just what Elias Rehn had done but what he had undone — that the signature beside the names was not an accusation alone but a correction, a man setting his own weight against the machinery he had fed. She added a line to her working notes, in pencil, to be set in type when the edition was prepared:

*He signed the requisition. Then he signed the truth. Judge him by both.*

* * *

Hessler learned of the broadsheets on a Thursday, from a subordinate who brought him one folded like an apology.

The colonel read it standing at his window, looking out over the square where his troops drilled, and his face did not change, which those who served under him had learned to fear more than anger. When he finished, he read it again. Then he set it on his desk, aligned precisely with the desk's edge, and said:

"How many?"

"Estimates vary, sir. Thousands. Possibly tens of thousands. They're coming across everywhere — the river, the passes, mixed in with legitimate freight. We've seized perhaps two hundred copies."

"And printing them?"

"The press office is across the border, sir. Foreign soil. There are... diplomatic complications."

Hessler looked at the paper again. Four hundred and eleven names. Dates. Places. Witness statements. His own officers' actions recorded in the flat grammar of evidence, sworn, signed, and now circulating in every village his army occupied. He had spent eighteen months building order out of Vellmarch, and here was disorder in its purest form: memory, multiplied.

"Burn every copy you find," he said. "Arrest whoever distributes them. And find out who carried the master documents across the border. Someone delivered this. Someone always delivers."

The subordinate saluted and left, and Hessler stood alone with the broadsheet, and for a long moment the only sound in the room was the distant rhythm of boots in the square below — hundreds of men, marching in circles, going nowhere.

Then he rang for his aide and dictated an order. The order concerned the officer responsible for border security at the grain gate and the river checkpoint — the man who had failed to intercept the documents, who had reported the fugitive and then inexplicably faded from the investigation, who had been reassigned and had then, according to the checkpoint logs, allowed a farmer's freshly resewn grain sacks through the gate without inspection.

The order directed that Sergeant Duvall be summoned to headquarters to account for himself.

* * *

Duvall received the summons at dusk, pinned to the guard post noticeboard in a sealed envelope with Hessler's seal.

He read it twice. Around him, the evening shift changed; carts clattered over the cobblestones; somewhere down the river a ferry bell rang, steady as a heartbeat. On the wall of the guardhouse, half-hidden behind a posted curfew notice, someone had pasted a broadsheet — one corner torn, the rest intact — and the lamplight caught the columns of names and made them glow.

Duvall looked at the envelope in his hand, and at the names glowing on the wall, and understood that he had come to the end of the road where a man can walk in two directions at once.

He put the envelope in his coat, unread a second time, and went to fetch his report — the true one, the one he had written months ago and buried in his desk, the one that told exactly what he had seen at the third checkpoint and exactly what he had chosen not to do.

Whatever Hessler wanted from him tomorrow, Duvall intended to bring the truth.

Whether he would hand it over, or walk out of the army with it under his arm, he would decide on the way.
