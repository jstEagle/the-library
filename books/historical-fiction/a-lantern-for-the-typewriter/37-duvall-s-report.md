## 37. Duvall's Report

The order came down on a Tuesday, in the flat bureaucratic language that had learned to say anything.

*Re: Ledger of Vellmarch. Confirm destruction. File certificate of disposition. No further inquiry warranted.*

Sergeant Anselm Duvall read it three times at his desk in the garrison annex, the paper trembling not in his hand but somewhere behind his eyes, where the town of no names still lived. The order bore Colonel Hessler's seal. Beneath it, in a clerk's neat script: *Certificate to be countersigned by originating officer.* Originating officer. That was him. That had always been him — the man who matched ink to foundry, who wrote the description of the fugitive, who put the word *woman* into an army document and set the hunt running like a hound off its leash.

He had chased her through fire and lost her. He had watched the checkpoint burn from the ridge road with snow melting down his collar, and he had done nothing. He had stood there with a loaded pistol and a warrant authorizing him to shoot on sight, and he had let two figures vanish into the treeline like smoke returning to a chimney.

They told him later it was the deserter's diversion. They told him the woman and the clerk could not have crossed open ground under aimed fire. They told him a great many things, and he had nodded at all of them, because nodding was what sergeants did, and because the truth — that he had seen her face in the lamplight and lowered his rifle by a degree, one degree, and that one degree was the whole width between duty and whatever he had become since the village with the burned registry — that truth was not the sort you said aloud.

Now they wanted a signature saying the ledger was ash.

Duvall set the order down and looked out the window. Below the annex wall, the grain gate stood open for the morning carts, and the soldiers there were lazy with winter's end, waving through farmers whose wheat was already confiscated and therefore, technically, being waved back and forth across their own property. He had walked through that gate a hundred times. It was the first checkpoint on the road south, the one where a widow with a typewriter case had passed him by — no, not him; he hadn't been there that day, but he had reconstructed it so many times since, from manifests and ferry logs and the ferryman's stony silence, that he sometimes dreamed he'd been standing in the archway when she rolled under the burlap, close enough to touch the case that held every name Hessler wanted dead twice over.

Every name. And his own among them — not as victim but as instrument. He had understood that late, later than Elias Rehn, apparently, who had figured it out early enough to die for the correction.

Elias Rehn. Duvall had signed the requisition for Rehn's arrest the week before the pass. *Clerk, second class, suspected of aiding fugitive.* He remembered the ink drying under his pen. He remembered thinking: *this is the third name I have fed into this machine, and I can feel my own hand learning to enjoy it.* Or not enjoying it. Learning to stop feeling it. Which was worse.

The certificate lay on his desk like something that had died there. *Confirm destruction.*

Here was the thing about destruction certificates: they required a witness, a date, a location, and a method. Burned at such a place, on such a day, in the presence of the undersigned. It was a document designed so that no one would ever check it, because checking it would mean admitting someone had cared enough to fake it. The whole architecture of the requisition state ran on papers nobody verified — orders, warrants, dispositions — a river of signatures flowing downhill toward whatever the colonel wanted believed.

Duvall had spent eleven years as a soldier and six of those as the kind of officer trusted with paperwork. He knew exactly how much a signature weighed and exactly how little.

He took a fresh sheet from the drawer.

* * *

He did not write it that morning. He went first to the evidence room, because if he was going to do what the cold thing in his chest was telling him to do, he needed to see what remained of it all.

The evidence room smelled of gun oil and damp stone. Shelves of confiscated presses, their screws rusting, their type beds pried apart by men who thought letters were just metal. Crates of pamphlets pulped to gray bricks. And on the end shelf, tagged and catalogued, the typewriter case — empty now, its false lining slit open by some technician's knife, its hollow gut exposed like a dissected animal. They had found nothing inside because she had carried the ledger away in her hands, but the case itself had been entered into evidence, which meant it had been photographed, measured, described in triplicate, and would be stored until some future amnesty or bonfire decided otherwise.

Duvall lifted it down. The leather was cracked along the handle. Someone had loved this case once — carried it through rain, patched the corner stitching, worn the brass latch smooth with a thumb. A workman's case. A craftsman's.

Inside the lid, half-scraped by the technician's blade, a paper label survived: *A. Winterbourne, Type Foundry, Vellmarch.* And beneath the label, faint as breath on glass, pencil marks in a small precise hand — measurements, it looked like. The dimensions of type bodies. Points and picas. The handwriting of a man who had spent his life making sure that letters fit together.

Bram Winterbourne. Bram Aldous Winterbourne, son of Aldous, heir to the foundry. Except there was no son. There never had been. Duvall had known it since the mountain pass, since the lamplight found her face, and knowing it had changed precisely nothing about the facts and everything about their weight. She had cut her father's letters for years. She had kept a dead man's trade alive under a dead man's name while the army drilled in the square below her window. Every piece of forged paper she'd ever made was better than the genuine articles Duvall processed daily — he'd said as much in his reports, in the grudging professional language of one craftsman respecting another, though the colonel had only ever read it as *the fugitive is skilled, find her.*

Find her. As if skill were a crime. As if the crime were not the thing she'd been carrying over the mountains.

He set the case back on the shelf, aligning it carefully with the shelf edge, the way his father had aligned plows in the furrows, and went back upstairs to write.

* * *

The report took him four hours, and he wrote it the way he'd been trained to write everything: plainly, chronologically, without adjectives. Adjectives were where lies lived. He'd learned that reading Elias Rehn's old requisitions — the clerk had written with a surgeon's economy, every fact load-bearing, and Duvall had once admired those documents without understanding that admiration was the beginning of conscience.

He started with the ink.

On the eighth of the month — he gave the true date — samples taken from the press room of the Winterbourne foundry were compared against pamphlets seized at three locations. Match confirmed. On the eleventh, authority was requested to detain the Winterbourne heir. On the thirteenth, the heir fled toward the border carrying a master ledger of the disappeared, contents including — and here Duvall made himself write them out, all of them, the categories Hessler's men used — *persons executed, persons deported, persons detained without record, and persons removed from civil registries by administrative order.* Two hundred and forty-one names. He knew the number because he had counted them himself, one long night in the annex, reading the inventory of the seized records, understanding that each line was a person and that the requisition he'd helped process had turned people into lines.

He documented the pursuit. The grain gate passage — unintercepted. The ferry crossing — the manifest showed a widow, M. Corvez, traveling alone with household goods; the ferrywoman Marta Corvez was currently in custody for that same manifest and had said nothing in nine days of questioning except *I owed him,* meaning Aldous Winterbourne, and Duvall had stopped pushing because he understood the debt better than her interrogators did. The storm boarding. The river checkpoint, where Clerk Second Class Rehn stamped the case through and then followed it into the mountains. The chapel ambush. The pass.

At the pass, Duvall wrote, the pursuing party engaged armed deserters. In the engagement, the fugitives escaped into the treeline. Sergeant Duvall was present. Sergeant Duvall fired no shots.

His pen hovered there. He could hear the colonel's voice already: *Amend. In the confusion, shots were exchanged.* He could hear the tribunal version forming: *Sergeant Duvall acted under extreme provocation, having personally identified the traitor.* All true, in the grammar they preferred. None of it true in any grammar that mattered.

He wrote instead: *This officer identified the fugitive at close range and elected not to fire. Reason: the fugitive was unarmed and carrying records, not weapons. This officer had reviewed those records' contents and judged their preservation to outweigh the enforcement of the requisition order under which they were seized.*

There. It was out of him, on army paper, in his own hand. He sat back and felt the strange lightness of a man who has finally set down something heavy and discovered the heaviness was the holding, not the thing.

Then came the part the order actually asked for.

*Regarding the disposition of the master ledger:* he wrote, *the ledger was not destroyed. The ledger reached the foreign press office at [he gave the border town] and its contents were printed and distributed. Copies are in circulation throughout Vellmarch province. This officer has examined one such copy and confirms it is authentic. The ledger cannot be destroyed by this command because it now exists beyond this command's reach. Any certificate stating otherwise would be false, and this officer declines to sign it.*

He read it over twice. Then he added the last paragraph, the one he had been building toward since the village with no names, since the burned church registry, since he'd stood in that gutted nave watching an old priest sift ashes for the charred edge of a baptismal page and understood that this was what victory looked like when his side achieved it: a country where nobody had ever been born, married, buried, or existed.

*Recommendation:* he wrote. *That the ledger be received by this command not as contraband but as record. That its contents be verified against unit files. That responsibility for the deaths it documents be assigned to the officers whose orders produced them. That Colonel Hessler's requisition program be suspended pending inquiry.*

He signed it. Anselm Duvall, Sergeant, Provost Section. The signature came out steady. Eleven years of signing things had taught his hand at least that much discipline.

He made three copies. One for the file, one for the provincial command, and one — this was the part that would either save him or hang him — addressed directly to the provisional authorities assembling in the capital, the ones the broadsheets called the reckoning commission, the ones whose existence the army officially pretended not to notice.

* * *

Colonel Hessler read the report at his desk with the window open, because the man liked cold air, claimed it kept the mind honest. Duvall stood at attention across the desk and watched him read, and what he felt was not fear, or not only fear. It was the particular clarity of a man who has stepped off a ledge and is using the falling time to look around.

"You understand," Hessler said at last, not looking up, "what this document is."

"A report, sir."

"It is a confession." The colonel laid it flat. "It is a confession with your signature on it, delivered to my desk, helpfully, so that I may decide what to do with you before you decide what to do with me."

"The copies are already dispatched, sir."

Something moved behind Hessler's eyes — not anger, Duvall realized. Appraisal. The colonel had built a career on knowing which fights were real, and he was weighing this one now, visibly, the way a merchant weighs coin he suspects is clipped.

"You were a good hunter," Hessler said. "You found the ink. You found the girl. You were the only officer in this province who understood that the ledger mattered more than any single fugitive. And then at the pass you—" He stopped. He picked up the report again, riffled to the page with the sentence about electing not to fire. "One degree," he said quietly. "My adjutant swears your muzzle dropped one degree. Men have been shot for less."

"Yes, sir."

"And now you want me to believe you've spent the interval composing an act of treason so orderly it looks like bookkeeping."

"I spent it composing a report, sir. Everything in it is verifiable. The printed broadsheets are verifiable. The ferry manifest is verifiable. My failure at the pass is verifiable by eleven witnesses." Duvall held his voice level. "I've simply declined to add the fiction the disposition order requires."

Hessler rose. For a moment Duvall thought — hoped? — the man might strike him. Instead the colonel walked to the window and stood looking down at the grain gate, at the carts going through, at the soldiers waving through wheat that belonged to everyone and no one.

"Do you know why the ledger terrifies the general staff?" Hessler said. "Not the names. Names are cheap; we produce new ones daily. It terrifies them because it's *counted.* A massacre is weather. A massacre with an inventory is a crime scene. You've handed the lawyers a crime scene." He turned. "I could shoot you for this."

"Yes, sir."

"But shooting you makes the report interesting. Filing you away as a madman makes it interesting. The only way to bury you is to agree with you that you're nothing — a failed sergeant, resigned, bitter, not worth the ink." The colonel almost smiled. "You've constructed it so that punishing you proves your point. Clever. The girl taught you that, I think. She made herself impossible to destroy quietly."

"She taught me that being seen is the only protection there is, sir. Yes."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down in the square a drill sergeant was shouting the cadence of some pointless formation, the sound rising thin through the cold air.

"Resignation will be accepted," Hessler said finally. "Effective immediately. Your discharge character will note unsatisfactory service. You will leave the garrison within twenty-four hours. If you are found within thirty miles of any military installation after that—" He let it hang. "Get out of my office, Sergeant."

Duvall saluted. Hessler did not return it. Duvall had expected that too, and found, walking out, that the absence of the returned salute hurt less than the presence of the order had.

* * *

Packing took ten minutes. He owned uniforms, boots, a razor, a Bible he'd stopped believing in around the time of the first purge, and a tin box of personal papers he'd kept since conscription — his mother's last letter, a photograph of a house he no longer owned, and the carbon copy of the very first requisition he'd ever processed, kept all these years the way other men kept trophies, though he'd never known until recently whether it was a trophy or a wound. It was both. He left the uniform folded on the bunk, regulation creases intact, and put the letter and the photograph and the carbon copy in his coat.

The carbon copy he intended to deliver. There was a commission sitting in the capital taking testimony, and a woman there cutting her own name into type, and he had a signature she needed — his name beside the instrument, the way Elias Rehn had asked to have his. Duvall had read the smuggled broadsheets. Everyone had. *Printed by B.A. Winterbourne, for the families of Vellmarch.* And beneath the names, in smaller type, a standing notice: *Persons who served the requisition and wish their part recorded may submit sworn statement to this office. Ink forgives no one, but it remembers everyone.*

Ink forgives no one. He'd read that line forty times. It wasn't mercy and it wasn't vengeance; it was the only honest contract the war had produced, and he meant to sign it.

The garrison gate guard barely looked at him. Discharged men leaving with a kit bag were the most common sight in the province that spring; the army was shedding men the way a dog sheds winter coat, officers resigning, deserters flooding the treeline, whole units dissolving like sugar in the rain of bad news. The war was losing. Everyone knew it the way animals know weather.

He walked out past the barracks, past the drill yard where the cadence had gone quiet, past the annex where his desk already stood bare, reassigned to some lieutenant who would fill its drawers with fresh fictions. At the corner of the evidence room wing he paused. He could not say why. Habit, perhaps — the old hunter's instinct to look back at the trail.

Through the high barred window he could see, stacked in the yard awaiting transport, the confiscated presses. And on top of the nearest stack, tagged and forgotten, the typewriter case with its split lining, its brass latch worn smooth by a dead man's thumb. Somebody had left it out for the scrap convoy. In an hour it would be hammered flat or sold for leather.

Duvall stood there for the length of ten heartbeats. Then he went back inside, signed the release chit himself — his signature still had power for another nineteen hours, and he intended to spend all of it — and carried the case out under his arm.

It weighed almost nothing. It had always weighed almost nothing. That was the whole trick of it, he thought; that was the whole genius of the man who'd built it. The most dangerous object in the province looked like junk. A widow could carry it through a checkpoint. A disgraced sergeant could carry it out of an army.

* * *

The grain gate at dusk was nearly empty. One cart of turnips, one muleteer arguing a receipt, two sentries who checked his discharge papers with the bored efficiency of men counting the days to their own discharge, and waved him through.

He stopped in the middle of the archway anyway. Just for a moment. Stone above him, road below, the last light coming down the valley gold and low. Somewhere up that road, over the pass, in a border town that smelled of foreign ink, a woman was setting type by lamplight, cutting letters the way her father had, refusing oblivion one name at a time. Somewhere behind him, in the town he had helped seal and search and starve, ferrymen and farmers and probably children were passing the broadsheets hand to hand in the dark, each page a small fire that couldn't be put out because it lived in memory now, in kitchens, in prayers, in the mouths of people who said the names aloud.

Two hundred and forty-one lanterns. And soon, if the commission took his statement, a second signature to hang beside the first clerk's — proof that the machine had known what it was doing, and that a man inside it had known too, and that knowing, late and insufficient and bought at the price of everything, still counted for something in the ledger of the world.

A sentry coughed. "Move along, citizen."

Citizen. Not Sergeant. The word landed on him like a coat being put on his shoulders by a stranger's hands — unfamiliar, slightly wrong in the fit, and warm.

Duvall shifted the typewriter case under his arm and walked through the gate, out of the army, onto the road south, and did not look back again.

Behind him the sentries resumed their boredom. Ahead of him the road ran down through the thawing fields toward the capital, toward the commission, toward the woman with the ink-stained hands who had once walked this same direction carrying everything he had spent his career trying to burn.

He had thirty miles of prohibition and two hundred miles of road, and for the first time in eleven years, every step of the distance was his own.

The broadsheet in his pocket, folded soft as cloth from handling, bore the notice he'd read forty times. Tonight, in some inn on the south road, he would take out the carbon copy of his first requisition, and the tin box, and a pen, and begin the statement. *I, Anselm Duvall, sergeant of the provost section, swear as follows—*

The lanterns were already lit. He was only walking toward them.
