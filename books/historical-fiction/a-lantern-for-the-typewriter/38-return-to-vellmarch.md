## 38. Return to Vellmarch

The road into Vellmarch had changed its name twice since Bram last walked it, and the stones under her boots had not.

She came on foot, the way she'd left — though she'd left with a typewriter case and a companion, and she returned with neither. The case had gone to the press office months ago. Elias had gone into the ground outside it. What she carried now was lighter than either and heavier than both: a bundle of broadsheets in oilcloth against her back, each one bearing names she knew by heart, printed in type she had cut herself.

The sentry post at the grain gate stood open. No one manned it. The gate itself had been unhinged and leaned against the wall like a drunkard propped there to sleep it off, and through the gap she could see the square where soldiers had once drilled — empty now, except for children playing at something that involved chalk lines and shouting. One of them had drawn a crude star on the cobbles, then scrubbed at it with his sleeve, half-erasing it, unsure whether it was forbidden or fashionable this week.

That was the regime in miniature, she thought. A star half-scrubbed, and no one certain of anything.

Her legs remembered the town better than her eyes did. They carried her down the mill lane past the tannery without her deciding to go that way, and she let them, because the foundry was at the end of that lane and she had been walking toward it since the border stone, since the barn, since Elias's hand going slack around hers with her promise still warm inside it.

*Print my signature beside the names.*

She had. Thirty thousand copies. She had set his name herself, in twelve-point, with the same care her father would have demanded, and when the first sheet came off the press she had touched the ink before it dried and carried the stain on her thumb for three days, unwilling to wash it off.

Now the lane turned, and the foundry was in front of her.

She stopped.

Half-ruined was a generous accounting. The roof above the casting shed had fallen inward, leaving a black socket where the furnace chimney used to breathe. The front windows were boarded — army boards, stenciled with requisition numbers that had faded from authority into mere decoration. Someone had pried one board loose and not bothered to replace it, so the building peered out at her through a single dark eye. The sign above the door was gone entirely. She could see the pale rectangle on the brickwork where it had hung for forty years, the ghost-shadow of *WINTERBOURNE & SON* still legible in soot and weather, the way a scar remembers a wound.

They had taken the sign down when they demolished what they thought was everything else. Duvall's report, she had read it in exile — *the premises razed, the family accounted for.* Accounted for. As if she were a sum that balanced. As if her father's forty years of letters had been entered in a ledger and closed out.

Bram stood in the lane until the light began to fail, and then she went to find out who owned her home now.

* * *

The answer, it turned out, was a woman named Grete Halvorsen, who ran the chandler's shop two doors down and who had known Aldous Winterbourne for thirty years and his "son" for all of hers. Bram had rehearsed nothing on the walk south. She had decided, somewhere on the mountain road, that she was done arriving anywhere as anyone but herself. But standing in the chandler's doorway, watching Grete's face change — watching recognition arrive like weather across it, disbelief hardening into something wet and bright — she understood that coming home honestly was its own kind of rehearsal, and she hadn't practiced enough.

"You're dead," Grete said. Not unkindly. As a matter of record.

"I've been misreported."

"The whole town thinks—" Grete put down the candle mold she was holding, carefully, as if her hands couldn't be trusted with anything. "There was a notice. After the foundry came down. Your father's name and yours, both."

"Mine said *son,* I imagine."

"It said whatever they wanted it to say." Grete came around the counter slowly, the way one approaches a bird that might fly. Then she did not approach at all; she simply looked, long and thorough, at the short hair grown out unevenly, the burn scar along the jaw, the woman's coat over a soldier's boots. "Elsbeth," she said quietly. The true name, spoken aloud for perhaps the fourth time in Bram's adult life. It sounded strange in the shop air, foreign and familiar at once, like an accent she'd lost. "You look like him now. You know that? When you stand like that. Exactly like him."

"He'd be furious about the coat."

"He'd be furious about everything, and then he'd want to see your hands." Grete's eyes went to them, the cutter's hands, nicked and callused in patterns no other trade makes. "Are they still good?"

"They're the only thing about me that never learned to lie."

Something in Grete's face loosened then, some tension held since the demolition, and she laughed — a short, broken sound with grief in it. "The foundry. Do you know who has it?"

"No one, I hope."

"The army pulled out in the spring. Before they left they stripped what was worth carrying and burned the rest in the yard. Type, matrices, the small press — all of it. What they couldn't burn they threw down the well." She paused. "Except one thing. I'll show you tomorrow if you want. Tonight you should eat, and sleep, and let the town learn you're alive gently. There are people here who gave information after you fled. Names given under pressure. Some of them will want to apologize to you and some of them will pray you never look at them, and you can't tell which is which by looking."

"I don't need to tell which is which," Bram said. "I need them to know the ledger didn't forget. That's different from forgiving."

Grete studied her for a moment. "You've changed."

"I got seen." She said it flatly, without pride. "It turns out it's survivable."

* * *

It was not gentle, the town learning she was alive. Word moved faster than any press she'd ever run. By morning there were people in the lane outside the chandler's shop, standing at a distance the way people stand at gravesides, and by afternoon there were more, and among them faces she had cut obituaries for the survivors of, faces she had printed pamphlets to protect, and one face she had not expected: a man named Corvin, who had been a compositor at the rival printworks before the requisition, and who had, she now learned, signed a statement against the Winterbourne foundry in the winter she fled.

He stood at the edge of the lane with his hat in his hands and did not come closer.

Bram crossed the distance herself. She watched him flinch and held his eyes anyway.

"You told them we were printing seditious material," she said. No accusation in it. Just the fact, laid out flat between them like type on a stone.

"I told them what they already knew," Corvin said. His voice was wrecked; he'd clearly rehearsed this speech for a year and it fell apart on delivery. "They had the ink sample. They had Duvall's theory. All I did was confirm what they'd have confirmed by noon without me. I told myself that. For a year I've told myself that."

"And now?"

"Now the tribunal sits next month in the courthouse, and they're taking testimony." He swallowed. "I'm going to say what I did. Out loud. With my name attached." He almost smiled, and it was terrible. "You taught the whole town that trick, Elsbeth Winterbourne. Being seen. Half of us are trying it now and finding out it doesn't kill us. Who knew?"

"Everyone knew," she said. "That was always the problem. We knew, and we printed it anyway."

She did not forgive him. She noted, with some surprise, that she didn't hate him either. Hatred required a cleaner world than the one they all lived in now. She shook his hand because he offered it and because his palm was sweating and shaking and because some transactions are completed by touch alone, and then she went to see what Grete had saved.

* * *

It was in the cellar, of course. Everything important in her life had happened in cellars.

The army had gutted the ground floor — the casting benches smashed, the furnace cracked with crowbars, the great type cabinets overturned and their drawers scattered like a flock of slaughtered birds, sorts everywhere in the ash, lead gone green and dull. But the soldiers had not liked the cellar. Grete explained why with a shrug: too dark, too low, and there had been stories even among the occupation troops about the printer's house being unlucky. Superstition, the last immunity left to the powerless.

So the cellar kept its secrets, and Grete led her down the stairs with a lamp, and there, wrapped in sailcloth behind the false wall her father had built the year Bram was seven — the wall she'd helped him mortar, thinking it was for wine — stood the backup press.

Small. Hand-cranked. A proofing press, really, good for a single page at a time, the kind of machine a man buys when he believes a day may come when the big machines are taken and one page must still be possible.

Bram lifted the sailcloth and stood very still.

Underneath, folded and weighted with a type block, was a sheet of paper. Her father's handwriting. She recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a voice through a wall — the cramped, precise letters of a man who thought in serif forms. It was dated eight days before the requisition. Eight days before he died.

*If you are reading this, you have come home and they have not stopped you, which means the world has tilted back toward sense, or toward chaos, which serves the same purpose for printers.*

*I could not tell you about the case because telling you would have made you responsible for it while you were still only pretending to be brave. You were braver than me every day of your life and I let you believe I didn't notice, because noticing out loud is dangerous.*

*The letters are the thing. Not the machines. Machines can be rebuilt from memory by anyone who loved them. Cut the letters again. Put our name back up, both parts of it — I always meant the sign to carry both, and the carver cheated us on the second line because I haggled him down. Pay him properly this time. He's dead now, probably, but pay someone properly.*

*Light the lantern over the door on printing nights. Let them know where the work happens. We hid long enough.*

Bram read it twice. Then she sat down on the cold cellar floor with her back against the small press and laughed until it became the other thing, and Grete stood at the bottom of the stairs holding the lamp and saying nothing, because some vigils require no words.

He had known. Not the details — not the ledger, not Elias, not the pass — but the shape of it. *I always meant the sign to carry both.* Forty years she had lived inside that second blank line, filling it invisibly, and he had wanted it written in wood and paint above the door since before she could hold a graver.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, stood up, and began pulling the sailcloth fully off the press.

"How soon can you get paint?" she asked.

* * *

The carver's apprentice was not dead. His master was, but the boy — a young man now, twenty-two, named Pell, with his dead master's tools and none of his prices — took the commission the way soldiers take orders they've been waiting years for. He carved *ALDOUS WINTERBOURNE* deep and clean, and beneath it, on the second line, at full size, no haggling:

*ELSBETH WINTERBOURNE*

No ampersand between them. Her father's note had specified that too: *not & son, not & daughter — just the two names, equal, because the work was always both of ours.* She had argued with the dead man's instructions for a full evening before conceding that a man who hid a ledger in a typewriter case for a decade probably knew how he wanted his sign painted.

They hung it on a windless morning in late autumn, six weeks after her return. Half the street came to watch. The boards came off the windows first — Bram and Pell and Corvin, of all people, prying the army's stenciled planks free and stacking them for firewood, letting daylight into the ruined shop for the first time in two years. The glass was gone; they stretched oiled canvas where panes had been, and the light came through amber and soft, like light through beer, like light through the borders of things.

Then the sign. Ropes over the bracket, Pell guiding, Grete calling angles, a dozen neighbors hauling on Bram's count. It swung up into place against the old ghost-shadow on the brick, covering it exactly, the new wood still smelling of pine and paint, and when it settled and hung plumb, nobody cheered. That surprised her at first — and then it didn't. The silence was better than cheering. The whole street stood looking up at two names in black on white, and in the silence you could hear what the silence was made of: everyone who wasn't there to see it, whose names were in the ledger, whose names were on thirty thousand sheets of paper scattered across three provinces, tucked in barns and sewn into coats and read aloud in kitchens by lamplight to children who asked *was he real? was she?* and were answered *yes, here, look, printed.*

Bram climbed down from the ladder and stood beneath the sign with paint on her hands.

It was not triumph. She had imagined, on the long walk home, that this moment would feel like the first sheet off the press at the border office, that hot clean shock of a thing made true. It didn't. It felt like mending — a seam sewn in a torn coat, strong but visible, the tear still part of the garment's history. The roof was still open to the sky over the casting shed. The well still held her father's type, drowned and fused, unrecoverable. Marta was still in the ground by the river. Elias was still under a wooden cross with a forged name on it, because the tribunal had not yet ruled on whether instruments of the regime could be buried under their own.

Homecoming without illusion, she thought. The illusion had been that there was such a thing as return. There wasn't. There was only arrival, and then the work.

And the work was waiting. Inside, the small press stood cleaned and trued on the cellar trestles they'd carried up into the light. On the composing stone, locked in the chase, was the forme she'd been setting for three days: not a pamphlet, not a petition. The first pages of the ledger's published edition — the book the foreign editor had finally agreed to print jointly, verified by her sworn testimony, with appendices still arriving from ferrymen and deserters and farmers who had kept the lantern-broadsheets all this time and were mailing back corrections, additions, names the ledger had missed.

Page one was locked and ready. It needed only the colophon — the little line at the foot of the page, oldest courtesy of the printer's trade, declaring who made the thing and where.

She had left that line empty for three days.

Now Bram stood at the stone, picked a 12-point capital E from the case — one of the few surviving sorts salvaged from the ash pile, green with age, cleaned bright — and set it beside the A, the L, the S. Letter by letter, the way her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see over the bench: *printed at the sign of the lantern, vellmarch, by aldous and elsbeth winterbourne.*

She locked the line. She tightened the quoin. And as she reached for the crank, Grete appeared in the doorway with the evening paper from the provincial capital, folded to the front page, her face doing something complicated.

"Tribunal news?" Bram asked.

"Worse." Grete set the paper on the stone. "Oskar's trial begins Monday. And there's a witness list posted." Her finger found the column. "Look at the second name."

Bram looked.

Sergeant — no, former Sergeant — Duvall. Summoned to testify. Against Hessler's officers. By the provisional court. The man who had hunted her across two provinces, whose delay at the checkpoint had cost him everything, whose conscience had surfaced at the worst possible moment for his career and the best possible one for her — called now to swear an oath in the same courthouse where Corvin would confess and Oskar would read his own damnation aloud.

And beneath the official summons, in the newspaper's gossip column, one line: *the witness is said to have requested, unusually, that the printer of the ledger attend the proceedings in person.*

Bram read it twice. Outside, the first lantern-lighters were making their rounds down the lane, and she heard Pell already dragging the ladder around to hang the new lantern above the door — the one her father had asked for, the one that would burn on printing nights, telling the whole town where the work happened.

"Well," she said to Grete, to the press, to the empty second chair her father would never fill. "He knows where to find me now."

She turned the crank, and the first proof came up out of the machine, wet and black and true.
