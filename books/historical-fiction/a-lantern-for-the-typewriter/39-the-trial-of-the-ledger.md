## 39. The Trial of the Ledger

They had chosen the courthouse on the Kesselplatz because its windows were still whole.

Most of Vellmarch's public buildings had lost their glass to the winter of the retreat — Hessler's men, going out, had shot out whatever reflected their faces — but the old assize court had survived with its tall panes intact, and in the first week of spring the light came through them in long amber columns and lay across the floor like planks laid down for walking on. Bram thought of her father's cellar, of lamplight through bottle-glass, of every hour she had spent cutting letters by a flame no wider than a fist. She had never imagined that daylight could feel like an accusation. But it did. It filled the room without asking anyone's permission, and it fell on everything equally: on the judges' bench, on the prisoners' dock, on the ledger itself, which lay open on a plain trestle table at the center of the court like a body laid out for viewing.

The ledger was bigger than she remembered. Or perhaps it was only that she had carried it so long pressed against her ribs that she had come to think of it as small — a thing you could hide inside a typewriter case, inside a lie, inside a woman. Here, open under the windows, with the pages weighted flat by two river stones because the binding had been damaged by bilgewater, it looked like what it was: four hundred and eleven names, each one entered in Aldous Winterbourne's upright hand, each one dated, each one annotated in the margins with the particulars that the army's own requisition orders had made necessary — *taken from the grain gate, 14 March; taken from the ferry crossing, 2 June; taken from his own press; taken from her mother's house; no charge recorded; no grave recorded.*

The provisional court sat three judges deep behind a table draped in grey cloth. None of them wore robes; there had been no time and no cloth. They were a schoolmaster named Ferencz who had once flogged Bram's father as a boy for printing satirical verses, a ferrymaster's widow who turned out to be Marta's sister and who would not look at Bram for the first two days, and a foreign observer — a thin, careful man from across the border, sent by the very press office where Bram had given her testimony, who took notes in a shorthand she could not read and whose face gave away nothing at all.

Colonel Hessler himself was not in the dock. He had been taken east by the retreating command, or he had fled ahead of it; the provisional government's writ stopped at the mountains, and the men in the dock were the ones the writ could reach — Major Kessler-Voss, who had signed the requisition; Captain Ilsen, who had run the grain gate; Lieutenant Brandt, young and grey-faced, who had processed the detention lists. Five officers in all. They sat in a row like typeset characters waiting for the composing stick, and Bram found herself looking at them the way she looked at a case of type: reading them, sorting them, unable to stop.

She testified on the third day.

* * *

She had sworn her oath under her true name, and the saying of it still felt like setting down a weight she had carried so long her shoulders had grown around it.

"Bram Winterbourne," the clerk read back. "Type cutter. Proprietor of the Winterbourne Foundry, Kessler Lane."

"Proprietor," repeated Major Kessler-Voss's advocate, a pale man with a lawyer's talent for sounding reasonable. "You inherited the foundry from your father, Aldous Winterbourne?"

"I kept it," Bram said. "He left it to me."

"You operated it under your father's name."

"I operated it under his name, yes."

"Why?"

The courtroom was full. It had been full since the first morning — ferrymen standing at the walls, farmers come down from the pass, women who had spent two winters not saying their husbands' names aloud now sitting in the gallery with their hands folded, waiting to hear whether the names worked when spoken into open air. Bram looked up at them and understood that her answer belonged less to the advocate than to the gallery, and that this was the whole strange arithmetic of the trial: nothing said here would bring anyone back, but everything said here would be printed, and print was the only country any of them would ever get to visit again.

"Because a daughter's name on a foundry door would have brought soldiers to ask questions my father didn't want asked," she said. "And later, when he was dead, because 'the son' could go places a woman couldn't. Because invisibility is a trade, like type-cutting. You learn it the way you learn anything. Badly, at first."

A sound went through the gallery — not quite laughter. Something older than laughter.

The advocate turned a page. "You have told this court that the ledger before you was compiled by your father over eleven years."

"Yes."

"With the assistance of persons unknown."

"One person known," Bram said. "Oskar Reiner. He gathered the names. My father set them. Every entry was verified twice — once by whoever reported the taking, and once by my father, who went himself, or sent me, to the house, the street, the ditch, wherever the army said it hadn't been. He believed a name unverified was worse than no name at all. He said it gave the liars a target to shoot at."

"And you stand by these entries."

"I cut the type they're printed in," Bram said. "I'll stand by them until I'm dust."

Kessler-Voss rose then. He was allowed to question witnesses directly — the provisional court's rules were new and rough-hewn — and he stood in the amber light with his collar open, a man who had been handsome once and now looked mostly tired. Bram had expected to hate him. What she felt instead was colder and more useful: she looked at him and saw a character of type, badly cast, with a flaw in the shoulder that would show on every impression.

"Frau Winterbourne." He pronounced her name carefully, as if testing whether it would hold his weight. "Your ledger accuses officers of this army of one hundred and six deaths. Do you know how many men died defending this town from the border raiders in the same years? Two hundred and thirty. Where is their ledger?"

"In the parish registers," Bram said. "In their mothers' kitchens. In the ground with markers on it. Nobody burned their graves, Major. Nobody posted orders declaring they'd never existed. Your dead were mourned. Ours were *processed*." She heard her voice rise and brought it down. "I don't begrudge your two hundred and thirty their grief. I begrudge you the difference between a death and a deletion."

"The requisition orders were lawful instruments of—"

"They were signed by Elias Rehn," Bram said, "a clerk, who hanged himself with paperwork trying to undo them, and died at the border stone so that this book could reach a press. If your law required that, Major, then your law is what this court is here to weigh. Sit down."

The gavel came down late and gently, like someone remembering it existed. Kessler-Voss sat. And in the gallery, the widow who would not look at Bram — Marta's sister, Ilse, who had spent two days staring at her folded hands — lifted her head and looked at her straight on, and something passed between them that no clerk would ever manage to record.

* * *

Oskar came on the sixth day, and he came the way nobody comes to a trial: on foot, alone, through the front doors, with his hands empty.

Bram saw him from the witness bench where she waited to recall testimony about the grain gate, and her first feeling was the old one — the cold drop, the *he sold us* — and her second feeling, arriving half a breath later, was confusion, because Oskar Reiner did not look like a man escaping. He looked like a man arriving. He had shaved. He wore a coat that had been brushed. He walked down the center aisle past the gallery with his chin up, and the faces turning to follow him were faces that knew him — printers, ferrymen, the deserters from the chapel ambush who had come down off the mountain when the amnesty was posted — and not one of them stood, and not one of them called out, and the silence he walked through was worse than any shouting.

He surrendered himself at the bar. The presiding judge — Ferencz, the old schoolmaster, who had softened over six days in ways that showed only around his eyes — asked him his name and his trade.

"Oskar Reiner. Printer. Formerly of the resistance press on Tanner's Row."

"Formerly?"

"Formerly," Oskar said, "because there hasn't been a resistance since the broadsheets crossed the border. There's just Vellmarch again. I'd like to make a statement."

Ferencz looked at the other judges. The foreign observer wrote something in his shorthand and nodded once.

"Make it."

What Oskar did next, Bram understood only afterward, when she realized he had rehearsed it in the nights before he came — rehearsed it the way she had once rehearsed being a widow, practicing the walk, the hands, the forgetting. He asked permission to approach the ledger. Ferencz hesitated, then gestured. Oskar walked to the trestle table, put on a pair of reading spectacles that made him look suddenly, terribly old, and turned the pages with both hands, gently, the way you turn the pages of a book you helped make and cannot bear.

He turned to page ninety-one.

"In the autumn before last," he said, "there was a purge. Not Hessler's purge — an earlier one, quieter, before the requisition, when the army was still pretending to be polite. They arrested nine people from the Tanner's Row cell. I was the tenth name on the list. They offered me the tenth name in exchange for the other nine." His voice held level, but his thumb pressed white against the page edge. "I gave them seven. Two had already fled. Seven names, and I walked out of the arrest house, and I told myself I'd done it to keep the press alive, and the press *did* stay alive, and it printed the pamphlets, and the pamphlets mattered, and every night for two years I've weighed those seven names against everything the press printed and the scale has not moved. Not once. Not a hair."

He read the seven names aloud.

He read them slowly, in the flat carrying voice of a man reading a bill of lading, and the effect in that sunlit room was the opposite of flat. Bram watched Ilse's hands unfold in her lap. She watched Lieutenant Brandt, in the dock, close his eyes. She watched the foreign observer stop writing, which she suspected meant more than any writing he had done all week. And she understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like the click of type locking into a chase, why Oskar had surrendered himself rather than wait to be summoned: because a confession read by counsel was evidence, but a confession read by the confessor, standing, in daylight, in front of the town, was something else entirely. It was a letter cut by hand. It could not be recast.

"My name appears nowhere in this ledger as a victim," Oskar finished, "because I was never taken. But it belongs in it, and I'm asking the court to enter it — not as the accused. As an instrument. The same word used for the man who died at the border stone. Elias Rehn signed papers. I signed away neighbors. We were both pens. Somebody else's hand moved us, but the ink is ours, and I am done letting anyone — including myself — pretend the ink washes off."

Ferencz asked him, quietly, if he understood that his statement could be used against him.

"That's the entire purpose of the statement," Oskar said, and took off his spectacles, and looked out at the gallery for the first time, and Bram saw him find the face he was looking for — a woman near the wall, the sister of one of the seven — and hold it, and neither of them looked away.

* * *

The verdicts came on the ninth day, and they were not clean, because nothing in Vellmarch was going to be clean for a generation. Kessler-Voss and Ilsen were convicted on the counts the ledger supported; Brandt, who had wept openly while Oskar read, received a sentence measured in years rather than the other thing, because two of the seven names Oskar had given were on lists Brandt had refused to process, and the court — Bram among them — had to decide what a refusal was worth, and decided it was worth something, and hated the arithmetic even as they did it.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, in the slanting gold of late afternoon, Oskar stood beside her with his hands in his pockets like a man waiting for a bus.

"You could have let me hang in absentia," Bram said. "It would have been easier for everyone. Easier for you."

"Easier isn't the standard anymore," Oskar said. "You taught the town that. Walked it across a mountain with a typewriter case." He squinted down Kesselplatz, where workmen were re-glazing the bakery window, where someone had already chalked on the fountain: *THE NAMES ARE PRINTED.* "There's talk of publishing the ledger. A proper edition. Bound copies. Your father's annotations, the verification notes, everything."

"It needs setting," Bram said. "Four hundred and eleven names, plus Elias, plus your seven, plus the ones that will surface now that people aren't afraid to count. It's months of composition. Years, maybe."

"Yes," Oskar said. "So."

"So?"

"So I'm told the Winterbourne foundry has a press under a tarpaulin and a sign that needs rehanging," Oskar said, "and I'm told the proprietor hires apprentices." He turned to her then, and beneath the spectacles and the brushed coat he was still the man from Tanner's Row, the man who had taught her to lock a chase by feel in the dark. "I'm not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness is above my pay grade, and always was. I'm asking for a composing stick and a place at the frame. Let me spend the rest of my life setting the names of people I got killed. It's the only sentence I'll serve willingly, and the only one that fits the crime."

Bram looked at him a long moment. Somewhere behind them, in the cooling courtroom, the ledger lay closed between its two river stones, waiting for its next life.

"Report Monday," she said. "Six o'clock. You'll sweep the floor before you touch anything, and you'll learn my father's verification rule before you set a single line — every name checked twice, or it doesn't go in the forme."

Oskar nodded, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

He had gone perhaps ten steps toward the square when a boy came pelting up the courthouse steps — one of the printer's errand boys from the foreign office, cap in hand, breathless — and thrust a folded sheet at Bram, saying words that fell apart halfway to meaning. She unfolded it. It was a galley proof, fresh from the press across the border, still smelling of the good foreign ink: the title page of the published edition, pulled for approval.

Her father's name stood at the head of it, in the type he had cut with his own hands: *Compiled by Aldous Winterbourne.*

Below it, a second line, newly added in the editor's hand, circled in pencil, with a single word beside it: *confirm?*

*Bram Winterbourne.*

She stood on the courthouse steps with the proof trembling very slightly in her fingers, in the last of the light, understanding that the editor was not asking whether the name was spelled correctly. He was asking whether she wanted her name in the book at all — whether she wanted to stand in print forever beside the dead, visible, accountable, hers — and she knew, with a certainty deeper than fear, that there had only ever been one answer.

She borrowed the boy's pencil.
