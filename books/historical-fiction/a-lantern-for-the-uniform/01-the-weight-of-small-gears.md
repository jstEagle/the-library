## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The fog came up the harbor stairs ahead of Odile Crane, the way it always did, tasting of salt and coal smoke and the particular rot of old pilings. Odile climbed with their collar up and their tool case against their ribs, counting steps out of habit — forty-two from the water gate to the observatory road, thirty-one more to the door — because a clockmaker who could not count in the dark was no clockmaker at all.

Verlow's Observatory sat on the shoulder of Gull Hill like an old man who had stopped bothering with his buttons. Below it, the city unspooled in grey tiers: the fish market, the customs house, the long crooked spine of Lantern Row where the lamps burned amber even at noon. And below all of that, invisible beneath the fog but present in every rope and rudder in the harbor, the tide.

That was the trouble. The tide did not care about fog, or about Odile's preference for arriving before anyone who might look too long. The tide ran on schedule, and the schedule ran on the master clock, and the master clock — by Halloway's telegram, received two days prior and read four times — was *losing time*.

Losing time. As though it were coins dropped through a coat lining. Odile had spent the walk composing a reply: *Clocks do not lose time. They surrender it, gear by gear, and something is asking for each piece back.*

They did not send replies. They sent invoices. That was safer.

The observatory door stood unlocked, which meant Halloway wanted them to feel welcome, or wanted them to feel watched; with Halloway the two sensations arrived in the same envelope. Inside, the stairwell smelled of lamp oil and dust and the cold mineral breath of stone. Odile climbed past the telescope room without glancing at it, past the chart room, up the narrow iron stair into the clock chamber itself — a tall square room with one window facing the harbor, and there, filling the wall like a coffin built for a giant, the master clock.

It was a regulator of the old school: weight-driven, deadbeat escapement, a nine-foot pendulum swinging in its case behind glass gone cloudy with age. The dial above showed eleven minutes past six. Odile took out their pocket watch, set it on the sill, and let both clocks speak to each other for a full minute before touching anything.

Seven seconds slow per minute. No — not steady. Seven, then five, then nine. Irregular.

Odile wrote it down. They wrote everything down. A fault catalogued was a fault half-conquered, Aunt Vesper liked to say, usually while conquering nothing herself except a second cup of tea. Odile opened the tool case, laid out the tools in the order they'd be needed — never the order they came in, the order they'd be *needed*, a discipline beaten into them by a mother they barely remembered — and began.

The case door opened on stiff hinges that complained like a gull. Inside, the smell was different: brass polish, graphite, and something older underneath, something like a church. Odile put their head into the machinery the way other people put their heads into prayer.

*Fault one.* The great wheel was riding proud on its arbor, a hair's width, enough to let the teeth mesh shallow and skip under load. They marked it in chalk on the plate. *Fault two:* oil gone to varnish everywhere below the third wheel; someone had oiled this clock with whatever was cheapest and then left it alone for years, and now the oil had cured into amber glue. *Fault three,* found within the first hour and responsible for most of the lost seconds: the pallets of the escapement were worn unevenly, one locking face bright and true, the other pitted like a bad tooth. The pendulum swung, caught, hesitated a fraction — the fraction — and went on. Eleven minutes a day, scattered through the hours like spilled salt.

And *fault four*, which Odile felt rather than saw, low in their stomach, when they put their ear against the case and listened to the train run: a sound like a whisper dragged across silk, somewhere deep, intermittent. Not yet a crack. Becoming one.

Odile sat back on their heels in the dusty light and made themselves breathe.

A cracked wheel in the train of a civic master clock was not a repair. It was a replacement, and replacements meant ordering a part cut and finished to spec, and parts meant money, and money was the one mechanism in Odile's life that had never once run true.

But that was later's problem. Odile had learned early that you survive a day by refusing to think past it — no. Vesper said that. Odile survived a day by thinking past it so thoroughly that nothing could surprise them, and then pretending otherwise. Both things were true. Most things about Odile Crane were two things at once, which was why the census forms of the world fit them so badly.

They worked until the light through the window turned from pearl to pewter, cleaning varnish with spirits, dressing the pivots, easing the great wheel home. Their hands knew the work the way a tongue knows its own teeth. This was the only place Odile Crane was ever entirely legible: inside a clock, where every part had a name and every name was accurate, where nothing was asked what it *was* beyond what it *did*. A pallet does not need to declare whether it is man or woman. It locks or it fails. Odile had sometimes thought, in the small dishonest hours, that they would gladly be a pallet.

Footsteps on the iron stair. Odile straightened, wiped their hands, and became — by reflex so practiced it cost nothing anymore — slightly less present. Shoulders in. Chin level. Eyes on the work, respectful, unremarkable. An apprentice. Nobody.

Halloway filled the doorway like weather. Director of the Observatory, sixty-odd, built along the lines of a wardrobe, with side whiskers that suggested he had been carved by someone in a hurry. He carried a lantern he did not need yet.

"You're the Crane apprentice." Not a question. "Your aunt's shop sends you."

"My aunt's shop sends me," Odile agreed. It was even true. Vesper's shop — *V. Crane & Kin, Clocks Repaired, Patience Free of Charge* — sent Odile anywhere a summons came, and took a percentage, and called it family.

"The harbor board expects this clock keeping time to the second within the month. There are ships in the channel every morning whose masters set their watches by that bell, and if one of them finds the shoal instead of the channel—" Halloway let the sentence die the way men like him killed things, casually, expecting you to finish the work. "Can you do it?"

"I can do the work I can see," Odile said. "I'll know more tomorrow."

Halloway looked at the clock, then at Odile, and something passed over his face that Odile filed away without naming — not doubt exactly, closer to appetite, as though he were comparing what he saw against something he'd been told. "Your mother," he said, "worked here."

The words landed oddly, in the wrong season. "So I'm told," Odile said carefully.

"Before your time. Before mine, nearly." He tapped the case once with a knuckle, an oddly gentle gesture for such a hand. "She kept it better than any man since. Mind you keep your head down and your hands busy. That's how she managed it."

He left before Odile could decide which of the many possible insults lived inside that sentence.

*Keep your head down and your hands busy.* Odile stood in the fading light with chalk dust on their fingers and thought: *he says it like a recipe. Like it's a recipe and not a whole life.*

Because it *was* a whole life. Odile had been practicing invisibility since before they had the word for it — since the schoolmaster in Corrow-on-Sleet who couldn't decide which coat hook to assign them and solved the matter by assigning none; since the registrar who looked up from the form, looked at Odile, looked at the form again, and finally wrote down whatever was easiest for him; since every doorway, every queue, every public convenience and private conversation where the world demanded a binary answer and Odile's whole self declined to give one. You learned. You learned to stand just outside the lamplight. You learned to let people assume, and to correct them only when correction was cheaper than assumption, which was almost never. You learned to make yourself indispensable and unremarkable in the same motion — the apprentice who fixes everything and whom nobody remembers hiring.

Head down. Hands busy. Twenty-three years of it, polished smooth as a pivot.

* * *

Lantern Row, after dark, was the closest thing Odile had to a confession.

The house was the narrowest on the row, three floors squeezed between a sailmaker's and a chandler's, leaning eastward with the general fatigue of Verlow architecture. The ground floor was the shop, windows fogged with steam from the kettle and crowded with other people's broken time: mantel clocks, carriage watches, a ship's chronometer waiting on a promise. Above, the household.

Odile climbed the stairs to the smell of onions and burnt sugar — Vesper cooked the way she did everything, with more conviction than accuracy — and found the kitchen already full.

Aunt Vesper held court at the table in her shawl, gray braid coiled on her head like a sleeping animal, presiding over a pot with a wooden spoon in one hand and yesterday's newspaper in the other. Tobin Quill, fifteen and all elbows, was supposed to be setting the table and was instead reading a pamphlet proof upside down, mouthing the words. And Maren Cole — the lighthouse keeper, off-shift, smelling of pitch and cold air — sat nearest the stove with her boots off and her feet toward the fire like a woman who had earned the right by forty years of standing on rocks in weather.

Nobody looked up when Odile came in, which was the whole point of the place. Here Odile did not have to be less present. Here, being nobody in particular was simply the truth, shared four ways.

"Well?" said Vesper, without looking up. "Is it dying?"

"It's ill," Odile said, hanging their coat. "Worn pallets, filthy oil, a wheel riding loose. And something deeper I don't like the sound of."

"Mm." Vesper turned a page. "The harbor board pays on completion?"

"The harbor board pays when Halloway remembers we exist."

"Then we exist loudly until he does." She said it lightly, but her eyes, when they flicked up, were doing arithmetic. Vesper was always doing arithmetic lately — rents, debts, the price of coal — and hiding the sums behind newspaper. Odile had noticed her breath going short on the stairs. Had noticed and said nothing, because some kindnesses are ambushes, and neither of them was ready.

Tobin dropped a fork, retrieved it, dropped his performance of indifference instead. "Is it true a ship grounded last week? Because of the tides being off?"

"A boat touched the shoal," Maren said mildly. "Tide tables were ten minutes stale. Nobody drowned. This time."

"So if the clock doesn't get fixed—"

"If the clock doesn't get fixed," Odile said, taking the spoon from Vesper and tasting, wincing, adding flour, "ships misjudge the channel. Yes."

"Then it's important." Tobin said it fiercely, as though someone had disputed it, and Odile understood that the boy was building himself a cause out of their repair job because causes were the only thing he owned outright. Three months ago he'd come up the stairs of Lantern Row at midnight, ink to the elbows, a printer's devil fled from a shop that beat its apprentices and printed registration notices on Sundays. Vesper had fed him, Maren had stood in the doorway looking immovable, and Odile had given him their bed and taken the cot, and that was that. Found family, Vesper called it. Odile privately preferred *assembled*: four mismatched parts, none of them manufactured to fit together, running anyway.

Maren spoke to Odile across the table in her slow way, eyes on the fire. "How's the clock's heart?"

"Still beating. For now."

"And your mother's tools holding?"

"They hold." Odile said it evenly. They did not say that the tools — the good ones, the fine ones, her ones, with the initials worn to ghosts on the handles — had been feeling heavier all autumn, for reasons Odile couldn't yet name and didn't want to examine in company.

Vesper folded her paper at last and leveled it at Odile like a pointer. "There's a census man coming round the row this week. Registration Act. New forms." She sniffed. "They'll want names, relations, the nature of each occupant. I've drafted answers. We will present ourselves as a respectable clockmaking establishment with live-in staff, which is even mostly true, and I will handle the talking. Odile — you will be elsewhere."

"Elsewhere," Odile repeated.

"The observatory. Working late. You're very dedicated." She said it with love and strategy in equal measure, and Odile felt the old double weight of it: grateful to be protected, stung to need protecting, ashamed of the sting.

Maren said nothing at all, but she reached over and refilled Odile's tea without asking, which among the people of Lantern Row was a complete paragraph.

Later, with the others abed, Odile stood at the shop window watching the fog close over the row. Somewhere below the hill the master clock ticked against its faults, seven seconds here, nine there, spending the city's time like a drunkard spends wages. Somewhere in its depths that whispered sound waited, becoming a crack.

Head down, Halloway had said. Hands busy.

Odile turned the lamp low, took out the fault ledger, and wrote the day's findings in a neat hand — and then, beneath the technical notes, in smaller script, the thing they would never say aloud:

*A sound in the train, deep. Intermittent. If it is what I fear, the repair will cost more than we have.*

They closed the book. Tomorrow they would go back up the hill and listen again, and pretend, for another day, that listening was enough.

They were halfway up the stairs when the knock came — two raps, precise and official, at half past eleven at night, on the door of a house that had survived twenty years by never being interesting after dark.
