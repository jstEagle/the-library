## 2. Questions on the Doorstep

The knock came at half past nine on a Thursday, which was wrong in two ways. First, no honest caller knocked before ten on a working day. Second, he knocked twice, evenly, like a man keeping time to a clock nobody else could hear.

Odile knew it before they opened the door. They had spent twenty-two years learning the grammar of knocks — the landlady's rap, the doctor's polite tap, Tobin's three-beat signal learned from a printer's code — and this one belonged to none of them. This one was official. Official knocks always sounded as though the knocker believed the door ought to open itself out of respect.

They opened it anyway. Hiding made things worse; that was the first rule of Lantern Row.

"Morning." The man on the step was perhaps forty, grey at the temples in a way that suggested worry rather than age, and dressed in the particular shabbiness of someone who owned one good coat and wore it for work. He held a leather satchel against his chest the way a curate holds a prayer book. "Ambrose Pell. Census and Registration Office." He produced a folded card, then a second card, as if one might not be sufficient proof of himself. "I'm conducting household returns under the new Act."

Odile looked at the cards. They looked at Pell. They said nothing, because saying nothing was a skill, and Odile had sharpened theirs for two decades.

"Is there an adult present?" Pell asked. Not unkindly. But the question had teeth in it — *adult*, meaning *responsible*, meaning *someone whose name can be attached to this door*.

"My aunt," Odile said. "She's resting. You may come into the parlor while I fetch her, if you must."

"If I must," Pell repeated, with something that was almost a smile. He stepped inside, wiped his boots twice on the mat — Odile noticed that; noticed the wiping, the care, the man who did not want to bring the street's dirt into other people's houses — and stood in the narrow hall with his hat in his hands like a schoolboy called before a magistrate.

Lantern Row's houses were built thin and tall, squeezed between a chandler's storeroom and a shuttered pie shop, and everything about the interior announced poverty trying to pass for gentility: the mended wallpaper, the good chairs kept for company and bad ones used for living, the smell of camphor and coal smoke and, underneath it, brass polish, because Odile polished brass even when there was nothing worth polishing. Pell's eyes moved over all of it. Odile watched him not write anything down yet, and understood that he was writing it down anyway, somewhere behind his face.

"I'll get my aunt," Odile said again, and went up the stairs two at a time, taking them quietly on the landing where the third stair creaked.

Vesper Crane was propped against her pillows doing what she always did when she was supposed to be resting: reading a newspaper that was three days old and arguing with it silently. She was sixty-eight years old and thin as a wren, with white hair pinned up in a style twenty years out of fashion and eyes that had never once missed anything Odile had ever done, said, or thought of doing. When she saw their face, she set down the paper.

"Official?" she asked.

"Census and Registration. Name of Pell."

"Ah." Vesper reached for her shawl. "The new Act. Well. We knew it would come knocking eventually." She rose from the bed with the deliberate economy of a woman who conserved every motion because her body charged interest on each one. At the mirror she paused, pinched color into her cheeks with two fingers, and squared her shoulders like a soldier dressing for parade. "How do I look?"

"Like you're about to win an argument."

"Good." She took Odile's arm at the top of the stairs — partly for balance, partly so that whoever waited below would see a frail old woman who needed her kin, and would adjust whatever he intended to say accordingly. It was a weapon, her frailty. She deployed it without shame.

* * *

In the parlor, Pell had seated himself on the very edge of the good chair, satchel on his knees, and was studying the mantelpiece where four objects sat in a row: a chipped figurine of a harbor pilot, a tin of buttons, a photograph gone soft with age, and Maren Cole's old brass compass, which pointed stubbornly north-northwest regardless of where north actually was.

"That compass doesn't agree with itself," Pell said, when they entered.

"It agrees with the sea," said Vesper, sweeping in. "Sit down, Mr. Pell, or don't. Which is it to be?"

He sat. He opened the satchel and drew out a printed form, stiff cream paper with a crown at the top and columns ruled down its length like the lines of a cell. "Household return under the Registration Act, Section Four. I'll need the name of each occupant, their age, their occupation, and—" here his voice flattened slightly, the tone of a man reciting words he'd been given rather than chosen, "—the nature of the relation, if any, subsisting between occupants."

"And if there is no relation?"

"Then I record that there is no relation."

Vesper smiled at him the way a cat smiles at a bird through glass. "Then let us begin, Mr. Pell, since we have nothing to hide and a fire going to waste. Myself: Vesper Crane, aged sixty-eight, widow, occupation none but the running of this house, relation to the next occupant — aunt."

"Aunt by blood?"

"Aunt by blood and by thirty years of putting up with her," Vesper said pleasantly. "Write whichever fits in your column."

Pell wrote *Crane, Vesper.* Then, beneath the column marked RELATION, he wrote something small and quick. Odile, standing by the window with the light behind them, could not read it. They noted that they could not read it, and filed the noting away.

"Next occupant."

"Odile Crane. My sister's child. Clockmaker's apprentice, aged twenty-two." A pause of exactly the right length. "Relation to myself: nephew."

Odile felt the word go through them like a stitch pulled tight. *Nephew.* Vesper said it smoothly, without hesitation, the way she'd been saying it for years whenever forms demanded a category, and every time she said it Odile felt the small violence of it, the way one feels a coat buttoned at the wrong hole — functional, but wrong, and visible to anyone who looked closely at the front of them.

Pell looked up from the form. He looked at Odile — a level, unhurried look, the look of a man comparing a thing against its description — and Odile met it with the practiced blankness they wore the way other people wore gloves. Twenty-two years of practice. Let him see an apprentice clockmaker. Let him see a nephew. Let him see nothing at all.

"You're the apprentice they've got up at the observatory," Pell said. It wasn't a question.

"The master clock," Odile said. "It keeps poor time. Someone has to keep it."

"Important work, that. Tide tables hang off it, don't they. Half the harbor does."

"So I'm told."

Pell wrote *Crane, Odile* — and then, again, that small quick notation beneath the relation column. Twice now. Odile counted it the way they counted the beats of a sick clock: precisely, and with rising unease. A man who wrote things once was recording answers. A man who wrote things twice was recording questions.

"The house is listed as four occupants," Pell said, turning back a page in his ledger. "My predecessor's round last year. Four names, but—" he consulted, "—only two surnames. Crane and Cole. Who else resides here?"

"Tobin Marsh," Vesper said. "Apprentice printer. Seventeen. And Maren Cole, keeper of the East Light, though she lodges here more than she sleeps there, the lighthouse being no place for a woman past fifty in winter, whatever the Harbor Board says."

"Marsh," Pell repeated. "And Miss Cole's relation to the household?"

"Lodger."

"And Mr. Marsh's?"

"Also lodger."

"Both lodgers. No kin among the four of you beyond yourself and your niece—nephew—" and there it was, the smallest hitch, the fraction of a second where Pell corrected himself mid-word, and Odile saw him see what he had seen, and choose to smooth it over, "—yourself and your aunt."

"We are a generous household," Vesper said. "Verlow is full of people the world has finished with, Mr. Pell. Some of them wash up on Lantern Row. We put them to work and we feed them, and if the Act has a column for that, I should like to see it."

Something moved across Pell's face — quickly, like a fish under ice. He bent over the form. Under OCCUPATION beside Tobin's blank space he wrote slowly, and Odile, watching the pen, saw him hesitate before the word *printer's devil* and write instead *apprentice compositor,* which was a promotion Tobin would have laughed himself sick over.

"There are declarations now," Pell said, and his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man reading terms and conditions he did not fully endorse. "Section Seven. Each occupant must declare their lawful status. Persons unable to demonstrate lawful residence or lawful employment within ninety days are liable to removal from the register, and unregistered persons found in a dwelling after the deadline are liable to—" he stopped. Started again. "There are penalties."

"Fines," said Vesper.

"Fines," Pell agreed, "and in some cases indenture. The harbor works take registered laborers."

The word *indenture* sat in the room like a dropped glass. Odile thought of Tobin — seventeen, runaway, no papers, employed off the books at Kessler's print shop for wages paid in coin and silence — and felt the floor tilt a degree. They kept their face still. They had a great deal of practice at that too.

"And the nature of each occupant," Pell said quietly, tapping the column header. "That phrase is new. It means the Office wants particulars. Age, trade, relation — those are bones. The nature of a person is—" he turned his hand over, palm up, a small helpless gesture, "—the Office wants to know what people *are.*"

"And what do you want to know what we are, Mr. Pell?"

He looked up sharply. It was the first thing Odile had said beyond single sentences, and it landed. For a moment neither of them moved. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs Tobin's floorboard creaked — he was home, then, awake, listening at the gap in the boards the way he always did, and Odile prayed silently to no one in particular that he'd stay put.

"What I want," Pell said finally, "and what I'm required to record are frequently different documents." He closed his ledger. "I'll need each occupant to attend the registry office before the deadline to sign their individual declaration. In person. All four."

"All four," Vesper repeated. "In person. How thorough."

"The Act is thorough." He stood, tucked his hat under his arm, and paused at the threshold. "Mrs. Crane. Your niece—" the pause again, brief, deliberate this time, "—your family has an interesting arrangement here. Most households I visit, everyone is everyone's cousin and nobody can prove it. Yours is the opposite. Nobody claims to be anyone, and everyone plainly is." He put on his hat. "I'll see you at the registry office."

The door closed behind him with a click soft as a watch part seating into place.

* * *

For a long moment Vesper didn't move. Then she crossed to the window and watched the street until Pell's figure passed the lamppost and turned toward the harbor road.

"Well," she said.

"He wrote something down twice," Odile said. "Under the relation column. Both times he came to me. Small notes, in the margin."

"How small?"

"Small enough that I couldn't read them from across the room."

Vesper turned. Her face had gone carefully neutral, which on Vesper meant it was anything but. "Notes in margins are how files begin," she said. "I've seen it before. Your mother's file began the same way — a clerk's pencil, a margin, a flag." She caught herself, jaw tightening, but the word was already out.

"*File,*" Odile said. "Whose file? What file?"

But Vesper was already moving, folding the census form's memory away, pulling the bell rope to summon tea she had no intention of drinking, becoming brisk and busy and impenetrable. "It means nothing. It means clerks are clerks. What it means is that Tobin cannot set foot near the registry office, and that Maren must be told, and that you—" she pointed a finger worn crooked with age, "—will go to the observatory and mend that clock, because a household with a registered, wage-earning, indispensable clockmaker in it is a very different animal from one without."

"Vesper." Odile rarely used her name alone. It stopped her the way a hand stops a swinging door. "What file?"

Above them, a floorboard creaked. Tobin, moving away from the gap in the boards, fast, toward the stairs — toward the door, by the sound of it, and the street, and whatever errand he'd been planning all along while he listened.

Vesper held Odile's gaze for three full seconds. Then she said, "Later," and Odile heard in it everything she wasn't saying: *later, when the deadline has passed, when the ship of this has sailed, when it can't hurt you anymore.*

Upstairs, the attic window scraped open.

And down in the hall, already forgotten on the table where Pell had signed his own arrival in the visitors' book Aunt Vesper kept for exactly such occasions, lay his ledger — left open, one page, ink still wet.

Odile crossed to it before Vesper could stop them.

Beside *Crane, Odile,* in the margin, in a small precise hand, the census taker had written the same note twice:

*Nature unclear. Nature unclear.*

And beneath it, smaller, pressed hard enough to dent the paper, as if the man had needed to convince himself:

*Not deceptive. Watchful. Like the clocks.*
