## 3. What the Clock Keeps

The master clock's case smelled of cedar and cold iron, the way old things smell when they have been shut up with their own secrets for a long time.

Odile had been at it three hours by the time the light through the observatory's east windows turned from grey to a thin, watery gold. They worked on a ladder of borrowed scaffolding inside the great case itself, sleeves pinned back to the elbow, fingers moving over the mechanism the way a physician's fingers move over a chest — listening as much as touching. The clock was a monster: eleven feet of oak and brass, weights like ship anchors, a pendulum that swung in a long slow breath behind glass. It had governed Verlow's tides for ninety years, and every tide table printed in the city — every departure bell, every pilot's chart, every fisherman's supper-time argument about the shoals — hung off its single stubborn heartbeat.

And its heartbeat was wrong.

Not broken. Wrong. That distinction mattered, and Odile had known it since their first hour alone with the machine. A broken clock runs fast or slow or stops. This one *hesitated* — a catch in the escapement, a half-second where the pallets seemed to think better of releasing, then let go all at once, so that each minute arrived slightly late and slightly guilty. Over weeks it would drift. Over months, the tide tables would lie. Over years, ships would misjudge the channel by the length of a hull, and Verlow would go on blaming the weather.

Someone had done this. Or something had worn this way, which amounted to the same thing but took longer.

Odile pressed their palm flat against the going train and felt the hesitation travel up through the brass into the bones of their wrist. *There,* they thought. Third wheel. The teeth were fine; the arbor was true; but the pivot bearing on the near side had been shimmed with a sliver of tin, and the shim was wearing through. Someone had shimmed it badly, or deliberately badly, or —

Or someone had shimmed it *well*, decades ago, and time had simply outlasted the mending.

That last possibility sat strangely in Odile's chest, and they didn't examine why until later.

They set down their lamp-hooked to the scaffolding rail — and began the slow, unglamorous work of cataloguing. Aunt Vesper had taught them that much before anything else: *before you fix a thing, write down how it is broken.* Odile kept the notebook in their breast pocket, greasy at the corners, and wrote in a hand too small and too neat for anyone to read over their shoulder. *Escapement: irregular release, 0.5–0.8 sec. Third wheel pivot: tin shim, degraded. Case: sound. Weight cables: two fraying strands, port side. Strike train: silent since — ?*

They paused at that last one.

Halloway had told them, on the first morning, that the strike train had not run in eleven years. "Nobody misses it," he'd said. He was a tall man with the posture of a fence post and the eyes of someone who counted other people's spoons. "The bell cracked. We had it taken down." And then he had watched Odile write that down, watched them the way he watched everything — sideways, patient, a man who believed that if you stared at anything long enough it would eventually confess.

Eleven years was a long time for a city to stop caring whether its own heart made a sound. Odile wrote *strike train: seized? removed?* and underlined nothing, because underlining was a habit of people who wanted to be asked questions.

Their mother had underlined things. Their mother had written in margins, argued with almanacs, corrected tide tables in red ink and mailed them back to the harbormaster with her compliments. Odile had inherited her hands and none of her noise. Where she had burned visible, Odile had learned to smolder quietly, to be *useful* rather than *remarkable*, to answer questions with questions until the asker grew bored. Twenty-six years of practice at being almost invisible. It was a skill, Vesper said, like any other — and like any other skill, it could save your life and cost you everything else.

Odile climbed down, wiped their hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime before dawn, and went looking for the tool room.

* * *

The observatory in daylight was a different animal than at night. By lamplight it had felt like a church — vaulted, hushed, full of instruments that pointed at heaven and pretended not to care about the city below. In daylight it was an office. Clerks moved between desks with ledgers. A boy swept the same corner he had swept when Odile arrived. Somewhere a kettle whistled and was ignored.

Halloway's office stood at the top of the stairs, door open exactly four inches. He liked it open four inches. Odile had measured it without meaning to; measuring things was the one compulsion they allowed themselves.

"Crane." His voice came through the gap before Odile's knuckles reached the wood. "The escapement."

"Third-wheel pivot," Odile said. "Shimmed once, poorly. I can cut a proper bearing bush tomorrow. Two days' work, maybe three."

"You'll need stock."

"Brass rod, hard-drawn. I brought what I could carry."

Halloway appeared in the doorway holding a ledger of his own, and Odile noted — filed away, unexamined — that his ledger was open to a page already dense with figures. "The storeroom is at the end of the west passage. Anything you take goes on my inventory."

"Of course."

"And Crane." He did not move from the doorway. Four inches of him filled it entirely. "You'll find the mechanism has been maintained before you. There are notes."

"I found the notes," Odile said. "In the case. A maintenance log, forty years of them, in six different hands. The last entry is eleven years ago." They let the silence do a small amount of work. "Whoever stopped writing stopped around the time the bell came down."

Something crossed Halloway's face — not guilt, Odile thought. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own name called from a room he thought was empty.

"The log stays in the case," he said. "It's observatory property."

"It's a maintenance record. It belongs with the machine."

"It belongs," Halloway said, "where I put it." And he closed his ledger, and turned back into his office, and the conversation was over in the particular way conversations with Halloway ended — not concluded, but abandoned, like a bridge built halfway across a river.

Odile stood in the passage for a moment, listening to the clock overhead tick its guilty, hesitant tick.

Then they walked to the storeroom, took only the brass rod they needed, entered it on the inventory in their small neat hand — and went back up the scaffold, and opened the case again, and looked at the maintenance log where it lay in its recess above the weight chutes.

Six hands, forty years. Odile had skimmed it yesterday, professionally, the way one skims another tradesman's work. Today they read it properly, lamp held close, and by the fourth page their professional eye had gone somewhere else entirely.

The entries were beautiful. Not ornate — *precise*. Every measurement doubled. Every repair annotated with the reason for the failure, not just the failure itself: *third wheel pivot worn oval — cause: vibration transmitted from strike train, see correction below.* Marginal arithmetic. Little diagrams in a confident line that never needed correcting. And a signature at the bottom of each entry, small and slanted and utterly familiar:

*M. Crane.*

Marguerite Crane. Clockmaker. No kin.

Odile's mother had worked on this clock.

They knew that, of course. Knew it the way you know a thing you've never been told — as atmosphere, as assumption. She had left Lantern Row every day of Odile's childhood with a canvas roll of files and come home smelling of oil, and she had spoken of the observatory the way sailors speak of a particular sea: with respect, and with the certainty that it would outlast her. But knowing a woman worked *at* the observatory was different from finding her handwriting inside its heart, forty years deep, correcting the corrections of men twice her age.

*Cause: vibration transmitted from strike train.* Her hand. Her mind. Her habit of asking not just *what broke* but *what made it break* — the same question Odile had been asking all morning, in the same place, with the same instinct, without ever having been taught it.

The past was not buried. The past was *here*, in brass, running under the city's feet.

Odile sat back on the scaffold and let the feeling move through them — wonder first, wide and bright as a struck bell — and then, following it, the colder thing underneath.

Because there were seven signatures in the log, and the seventh was dated eleven years ago, and after the seventh the pages were blank. And Halloway had said *nobody misses the bell.* And Halloway had said *the log stays where I put it.* And Halloway had watched Odile, from the first hour, with the patience of a man waiting for a confession.

You did not guard a dead woman's handwriting unless the handwriting could hurt you.

* * *

The tea came at four o'clock, as it always did, carried up the winding stair by Maren Cole, who had appointed herself the observatory's unofficial caterer on some principle Odile hadn't yet gotten out of her. Maren was sixty if she was a day, built like the lighthouse she kept — broad at the base, tapering to a point, immovable in wind — and she handled the teapot with the tenderness she denied handling anything else.

"Third day," she said, pouring. "How's the patient?"

"Hesitating." Odile wrapped both hands around the cup. The scaffold was narrow; Maren perched on the case's lower ledge as though she'd been sitting there for decades, and perhaps she had. "A shimmed bearing, wearing through. Whoever fixed it bought the clock twenty years, no more."

"Mm." Maren blew on her tea. "Twenty years is respectable."

"For a person, maybe."

Maren smiled into her cup. She had a way of smiling that suggested she was agreeing with a longer sentence than the one she'd heard. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the clock breathed its long pendulum breath between them, and the afternoon light came through the east glass and laid a pale stripe across the maintenance log where it still sat in its recess.

Maren looked at it. Odile watched her look at it.

"She had good penmanship," Maren said at last.

"You knew."

"I knew she wrote in that book, if that's what you're asking." Maren set her cup on her knee. "I didn't know she'd signed it *Crane*. Though I suppose she would. Your mother never used more name than a job required."

Odile turned the cup slowly in their hands. "She worked here. You knew that too."

"Everyone on Lantern Row knew that. Everyone on Lantern Row knows everything, that's the trouble with rows." Maren's voice was easy, but her eyes had gone careful. "I knew her before the row, though. Before you. We shipped out of Verlow together, the year I was seventeen and she was nineteen and certain about everything except herself."

"You sailed?"

"One season. Coastal packet, coal and salt. She signed on as — " Maren paused, and Odile heard the pause for what it was: a hand hovering over a choice. "As a clockmaker's apprentice. Which was true, in the way that mattered. Ships need their chronometers kept. Nobody aboard cared what else she was or wasn't. That was the whole religion of the deck, Odile. The work was the name."

The work was the name. Odile held the phrase the way they held hot tea, carefully, testing its weight.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you asked me nothing," Maren said, "and you've been asking me nothing for three days, and a person can only stand so much of your politeness." She drank, set down the cup, and stood with the groan of someone whose knees kept their own accounts. At the head of the stair she stopped.

"There's a compartment," she said. "In the case. Below the log shelf, behind the weight chutes, there's a panel that isn't a panel. She showed me once, drunk on rum, swearing me to secrecy, which I am breaking now because she's dead and secrets rot." Maren's jaw worked. "Whatever's in it — she meant it for whoever came next. She just never got to choose who that was."

She went down the stairs. Odile sat very still on the scaffold for the length of ten pendulum swings.

Then they set down the tea, and lifted the lamp, and knelt at the base of the great case with their heart going quicker than any escapement should.

The panel was exactly where Maren said. A false back to the chute housing, held by two screws worn bright at the slots — recently touched, within years, not decades. Odile took out their smallest driver, fitted it, and turned. The screws gave with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: a shallow cedar box, and in the box, papers.

The first was a drawing — a full working elevation of the escapement, ink on linen, annotated in that small slanted hand, with corrections to the pallet geometry that Odile read twice because they were *brilliant*, because they explained the hesitation, because they were the exact diagnosis Odile had written that morning in their own notebook without ever having seen this page. Mother and child, twenty years apart, arriving at the same fault line from opposite ends of time.

The second paper was a letter, folded twice, addressed in the same hand:

*For my successor —*

The third was not paper at all. It was a bank draft, folded soft with handling, made out to *bearer*, drawn on the Merchants & Maritime Bank of Verlow, for a sum that made Odile's breath go shallow. More money than the household saw in a year. Enough to provision a berth on an emigrant ship with room left over. Enough, Odile understood without knowing how they understood it, to buy a life anywhere the tide ran out.

Enough, also — their tradesman's eye did the arithmetic unbidden, cruelly quick — to cut a new escapement wheel, a proper one, hardened steel, the kind this clock deserved instead of shims and prayers.

Odile knelt there a long time, the draft in one hand, the drawing in the other, while the clock above them hesitated through its guilty seconds.

Below, in the yard, gravel crunched. A voice — Halloway's voice, low, speaking to someone Odile couldn't see — said words that drifted up through the floorboards in fragments:

"...no. Nothing in the case. She checked herself, I watched her... the log is accounted for..."

A pause. Gravel again. Then, nearer, climbing: footsteps on the stair, unhurried, four inches of open door ahead of them.

Odile folded the draft into their breast pocket beside the notebook. The drawing they slid beneath the maintenance log, flat, where a log might reasonably keep a diagram. Then they stood, picked up their rag, and was wiping down the third wheel's housing — the picture of a tradesman ending a long day — when Halloway's shadow fell across the case mouth.

He looked at Odile. He looked at the log. He looked, for one second too long, at the space behind the weight chutes, where two freshly-turned screw heads caught the lamplight brighter than the dust around them.

"Leaving?" he said.

"Day's done," Odile said. "Bearing bush tomorrow. Three days, like I told you."

Halloway nodded slowly. His eyes stayed on the case the way a man's eyes stay on a drawer he suspects is locked with his own key inside.

"Mind the log," he said, and turned away.

Odile climbed down, gathered their coat, and walked out into the fog with their mother's fortune pressed against their ribs and her handwriting burning a hole in the dark behind them — wondering, with every step toward Lantern Row, which inheritance the clock intended them to keep, and what price the other one would cost.
