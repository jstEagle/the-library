## 4. The Printer's Devil Runs

The pamphlets weighed almost nothing. That was the trick of them — a hundred sheets of cheap rag paper, inked on the press behind Quill & Quire after old Master Fenwick locked up for the night, and the whole seditious bundle fit inside Tobin's coat like a second heartbeat. He'd counted them twice on the walk down from Lantern Row: forty-eight. Forty-eight little accusations against the Registration Act, each one set by his own hands in ten-point Caslon, the type so familiar under his fingers it might as well have been braille.

*YOUR NAME IS NOT THEIR PROPERTY.* That was the headline. Fenwick had argued for something longer, something with more clauses, because Fenwick believed an argument should be built like a bridge. But Tobin had set the headline himself, alone, at two in the morning with the shop's single lamp turned low, and he knew bridges were for people who already had somewhere to stand.

He had nowhere to stand. That was rather the point of him.

Tobin Ash was seventeen years and four months old, three weeks into his life as a runaway, and he had learned in those three weeks that fear has a texture. At the print shop it had been greasy — the foreman's hands on his shoulder, the smell of tallow and hot metal, the way Mr. Ash's name opened doors Tobin wanted shut. Here, in Verlow, fear was dry as paper dust. It sat in the throat. It made you count things.

He was counting doorways now. The Fishmarket district at dusk was all shuttered stalls and gulls arguing over fish heads, and the census men worked dusk the way fishermen worked dawn. Ambrose Pell had come to Lantern Row on Tuesday with his leather folio and his too-careful questions, and Aunt Vesper had handled him like a woman shelling peas — effortless, almost bored — but Tobin had watched from the stairs and seen the man write something down twice. You didn't write a thing down twice unless it mattered. And if Pell came back, and if he asked about the boy with no surname anyone could verify, then everything Vesper had shelled would spill right back onto the floor.

So: forty-eight pamphlets, distributed in dozens. One dozen to the net-menders' shed, where the women took them without comment and folded them into their aprons like receipts. One dozen tucked into the crate of hymnals outside the Seamen's Bethel, which was either blasphemy or genius depending on who found them first. One dozen left, folded into paper boats, floating in the drainage channel behind the salt warehouses — a flourish Tobin was privately proud of, though Maren would have called it showing off, and Odile would have said nothing at all, just looked at him with those gray eyes that saw straight through a lie to the machinery underneath.

That left twelve. The last dozen were for the taverns along Rope Walk, where the dockhands drank and grumbled and would read anything handed to them for free.

He got through the Anchor & Chain without trouble. He got through the Salt Cellar. It was the third tavern, the one called the Harrow, where the trouble found him — because trouble, Tobin had learned, doesn't hunt you. It waits at the places you can't resist going.

* * *

The Harrow smelled of tar and spilled porter, and its back room held the thing Tobin had walked forty minutes out of his way to see: a hand press, an old Columbian, sitting idle under a canvas sheet because the tavern keeper's brother had died and nobody in the family knew what the iron eagle on top was for. Tobin had heard about it from a net-mender. He'd told himself he was only going to look.

Looking was how it started. Looking was how he'd ended up apprenticed to a print shop at nine years old, because he'd looked through the window of Ash & Sons Letterpress and Presswork and couldn't make himself walk away. His father had taken the looking as ambition. It wasn't ambition. It was hunger — the specific hunger of a boy whose hands needed work the way other lungs needed air.

"You're staring at that press like it owes you money," said the tavern keeper, a broad woman with flour on her forearms.

"Just admiring it," Tobin said. "Columbian, is it? Thirty-some years old?"

"Never you mind." She squinted at him. "You one of them printer boys? You've got the look. Ink under the nails."

Tobin put his hands in his pockets. "I sweep floors," he said, which was true enough these days; he swept the floor at Lantern Row every morning and considered it honest work.

"Well, sweep somewhere else. We're closed for another hour." But she said it without heat, and when he lingered by the press she didn't shoo him again, and Tobin let himself touch the frame — cold iron, good bones, the platen still true by the feel of it — and he was halfway into imagining what he could print on a press like this, right here, no foreman, no father, no ledgers of names —

— when the front door banged open and the room went quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet when the wrong kind of authority walks in.

Two men. Not constables — worse, or at least more dangerous to Tobin specifically: census enforcers, wearing the new gray armbands with the black stamp, the ones the papers called *counters* and everyone else called *tallymen*. They moved through the taproom checking faces against a list, and the list, Tobin understood with a lurch, was not a list of people they were looking for. It was a list of everyone. The Registration Act didn't need suspects. It needed only the unregistered, and the unregistered was a category Tobin occupied the way a fish occupies water.

He was through the kitchen before his mind finished the thought. Past a scullery girl who yelped, past a mountain of unwashed pots, out the propped delivery door into the alley, where the evening fog had come in off the harbor thick as wool. Behind him the tavern keeper's voice rose — loud, indignant, buying him seconds he hadn't earned — *"You can't come back there, this is my kitchen, I don't care what's stamped on your arm—"*

Tobin ran.

He ran the way he'd learned to run in three weeks of Verlow: low, fast, memorizing turns. Left at the cooperage, right through the gap between the rope store and the chandlery where a cart had scraped the plaster away, down the steps behind the sail loft two at a time with his hand on the wet rail. The pamphlets hammered against his ribs with every stride — eleven left, he realized, he'd only planted one at the Harrow before the tallymen came — and some small clear part of his mind noted that this was evidence, this was the whole case against him bouncing in his coat, and that a sensible person would drop them in the harbor.

His hands did not drop them. His hands had set that type. Every letter of *YOUR NAME IS NOT THEIR PROPERTY* had passed through his fingers at two in the morning, and you didn't throw away your own words. Even to save your skin. Maybe especially then.

The footsteps behind him lasted two streets and then gave up — tallymen were paid by the head counted, not the head chased, and there were easier heads. Tobin slowed at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, breathing hard, fog beading on his eyelashes, and allowed himself thirty seconds of shaking before he made himself stop.

Then he did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the only prayer he trusted.

One: the tallymen hadn't seen his face clearly. Probably. Two: the tavern keeper had called him a printer boy, out loud, in front of them. Three: the Harrow stood six streets from the Fishmarket, and the Fishmarket stood twenty minutes from Lantern Row, and census men drew circles around irregular households the way ink draws circles around blots. Four: Ambrose Pell had written something down twice.

Tobin stood in the dark lane and felt the fear change texture again — from dry to wet, from paper dust to something drowning-adjacent — because the thought arrived whole and terrible: *it won't be me they punish.*

Runaways got transported, maybe. Beaten, fined, returned to their fathers, which amounted to the same thing. But a household that harbored an unregistered minor — that sheltered him knowingly, fed him, gave him a bed under its roof — that household could lose the roof. The new statutes were printed in the very shop Tobin had run from; he'd helped bind the proof copies while his father explained, with something like pride, how the law would sort the city into its proper columns. Lodging an unregistered person was *harboring*. Harboring was eviction, forfeiture, and — for the head of the household — possible conscription of any male occupant into the harbor labor gangs.

Aunt Vesper, who could barely climb the stairs. Odile, who answered to no category the form offered and would be conscripted or worse the moment some clerk decided what they were. Maren Cole, sixty-one years old and employed by a light the city depended on but a pension it hadn't granted.

And all of it hanging on a thread, and the thread was whether anyone connected the boy who ran out of the Harrow's kitchen to the extra name on a certain doorstep.

Tobin pressed his back against the wall of Tanner's Lane and breathed until the fog stopped tasting like panic.

Then he did the stupidest, most necessary thing he knew how to do. He went home.

* * *

Lantern Row showed itself to him a piece at a time, the way it always did in fog: first the gas lamp at the corner with its cracked globe, then the crooked chimney pots, then the house itself — narrow, three stories, leaning east like a listener — and finally the light in the parlor window, gold and steady, which meant someone was up and the kettle was on and for a few more minutes nothing in the world was wrong.

He let himself in through the kitchen, quietly, though the house had a way of knowing anyway. Odile was at the table mending a watch under the lamp, tweezers moving with that unhurried precision that made Tobin feel, always, like a badly assembled mechanism himself. They looked up. Gray eyes. Saw straight through.

"Evening," Tobin said.

"Evening." A pause, delicate as a hairspring. "Your boots are wet."

"It rained."

"It didn't."

"It fogged," Tobin said, "aggressively." He hung his coat on the peg — carefully, left side in, so the remaining pamphlets faced the wall — and went to the kettle like a boy with nothing in his pockets but lint.

Odile said nothing more. That was the thing about Odile: they never said anything more, and somehow the nothing was louder than shouting. Tobin poured tea he didn't want and carried it upstairs past Aunt Vesper's door, where lamplight showed underneath and her voice murmured — she talked to herself now, more and more, or perhaps to someone Tobin couldn't see — and past Maren's room, empty, because Maren kept lighthouse hours even on her nights ashore and was probably up on the gallery of the Verlow Light this minute, watching the fog eat the harbor.

In his own narrow room under the eaves, Tobin finally took the coat off and counted. Eleven pamphlets. He slid them under the loose floorboard with the others he'd hidden there — the ones he'd told himself were reserves and knew, honestly, were trophies — and lay down on the bed with his boots off and his heart still going like a press in full cycle.

He had lied to Odile. Small lie, boot-shaped, harmless. But it was the first brick in a wall, and he knew how walls went up between people, because he'd watched one go up between himself and his father over seven years, brick by brick, each one small, each one reasonable, until neither of them could see the other across it.

Downstairs, faintly, the clock in the parlor struck nine — the little mantel clock, not the great master in the observatory tower, which had been failing since before Tobin came to Verlow and which Odile climbed the hill to fight every day. Nine strokes, slightly uneven. Even the small clocks in this house were struggling to keep time.

Tobin stared at the ceiling and rehearsed tomorrow's lie in advance, because that was what fear did to a clever boy: it made him practice. If Vesper asked where he'd been, he'd say the print shop — no, not the print shop, never mention print shops — he'd say he'd been at the harbor watching the ships, which was true on other evenings and therefore easy to hold. If Odile asked about his boots, he'd say he'd cut through the drainage channel. If Pell came back —

If Pell came back, and asked about the boy, and Vesper said *what boy*, and the tallymen had already described a printer boy running from the Harrow with paper in his coat —

Tobin rolled over and pressed his face into the pillow. Somewhere below, the mantel clock ticked on, faithful and failing, and he thought about the great clock up the hill, the one that governed the tide tables, the one keeping every ship in Verlow off the shoals, and how everyone in the city trusted it absolutely, and how it was dying, and how nobody knew.

That was the thing nobody understood about clocks and households and laws. Everything looked like it was running fine right up until the escapement cracked.

He fell asleep with the thought unfinished, and dreamed of iron eagles, and woke once in the night to hear Aunt Vesper's voice through the floor, steady and clear, saying a name he didn't catch — and beneath her voice, far off toward the harbor, the fog bell tolling slow and patient, counting out the hours for a city that had no idea how close it was to silence.
