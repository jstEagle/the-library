## 5. Salt and Brass

The observatory at night was a different animal than the observatory by day.

By day it was all officialdom — Halloway's footsteps in the corridor above, the assistants' chalk-dusted murmurs over their star tables, the smell of lamp oil and ambition. By night it was just Odile and the clock and the sound of the building breathing around them: timber settling, wind worrying the shutters, the sea working at the cliffs below like a patient animal gnawing a bone.

They had been there since six. It was past eleven now, and the master clock had told them three separate lies.

Odile sat on their stool with the case door open, one hand resting flat against the great wheel train like a physician taking a pulse. The going train was true. They'd verified it twice with the poising gauge. But every fourth hour, without fail, the clock lost four seconds — not three, not five, four — as if something inside it were hesitating.

Clocks didn't hesitate. That was the whole point of them. A clock was humanity's answer to its own cowardice about time; you built a machine that would not flinch, would not negotiate, would not say *perhaps* or *I'd rather not*. And yet this one flinched. Every fourth hour.

Odile had spent two evenings chasing it through the escapement, then the pendulum suspension, then the crutch. Nothing. Tonight they'd gone back to first principles and begun disassembling the strike train instead, on the theory that whatever haunted the clock lived where nobody thought to look.

Their hands knew the work. Their hands always knew the work. It was the rest of them that needed managing — the part that kept listening for footsteps in the corridor, that kept cataloguing exits, that kept a running tally of how long they could be alone in a room before someone's absence became someone's suspicion. Twenty years of practice had taught Odile Crane that competence was the safest disguise available to a person who fit nowhere. Be useful. Be quiet. Be so good with your hands that no one thinks to ask what you are.

The trouble was, useful people got noticed eventually. Aunt Vesper said it like a proverb. Tobin said it like a complaint.

The strike train came apart piece by piece onto the baize cloth. Lift wheels, gathering pallet, the great slotted cam that counted out the hours. Odile cleaned each piece in benzine, dried it, held it up to the lamp. On the third wheel from the bottom they found it — a hairline crack in the hub, invisible until the light caught it at an angle, running across the metal like a river seen from a great height.

Every fourth hour. The crack flexed under load, stole a fraction of a beat, let go.

"There you are," Odile murmured, and felt the small fierce satisfaction that was the only luxury they permitted themselves regularly. Found things could be fixed things. Fixed things were honest things.

Then they turned the wheel over, and stopped breathing.

Stamped into the reverse of the hub, small and deep and unmistakable, was a maker's mark: a crane in flight, wings folded forward, struck by some long-dead apprentice's punch. The same mark Odile's mother had stamped on her own work. The same mark that lived, worn nearly smooth, on the face of the little brass watch Odile carried and never wound because winding it meant admitting it mattered.

Her wheel. In the city's master clock. She had made parts for this thing — had stood where Odile stood, maybe, in the lamplight, holding this exact piece of brass.

Odile set the wheel down very carefully, as if it might bruise.

"You're late with your supper."

Maren Cole filled the doorway the way weather fills a harbor — gradually, and then all at once. She was a broad woman gone grey at the temples, dressed in oilskins she hadn't bothered to remove, smelling of tar and cold air. Lighthouse keepers, in Odile's experience, moved through buildings as though walls were a formality.

"I wasn't aware I had a supper," Odile said.

"Everyone has a supper. Most of them just forget." Maren set a covered tin dish on the workbench and began, uninvited, assembling the observatory's battered spirit kettle. "Halloway's gone home. His assistant left at nine. I checked the door when I came up the path and locked it behind me, so we won't be interrupted."

"We?"

"I'm having tea. You're having supper and tea. It's less lonely that way." She got the kettle lit with the economy of a woman who had made ten thousand small fires in bad conditions. "Sit down, Odile. You've been hunched over that clock since sundown and you've got a look about you like a gull that's lost its flock."

Odile looked at the tin dish. Stew, by the smell — barley and onion and something salted. Household stew. Vesper's recipe, which meant Maren had cooked it at Lantern Row and carried it up the cliff path in the fog, which meant Maren had decided, somewhere between the lighthouse and the kitchen, that Odile needed looking after tonight.

That was the thing about Maren Cole. She never asked questions. She simply arrived, already knowing.

Odile sat. The stew was hot and better than anything they'd have made for themselves, and they ate while Maren fussed the tea into existence with a tin pot and two thick mugs that lived, apparently, permanently in the observatory's cupboard. When did that happen? When had Maren Cole installed herself so thoroughly in the machinery of Odile's days?

"Fourth hour," Maren said, nodding at the open case. "It stumbles on the fourth hour. Always has."

Odile's spoon paused. "You know that."

"I've listened to that bell for thirty-one years from the end of the point. You get to know a voice." She poured the tea. "My father kept the light before me, and he complained about the fourth hour before I was born. Said the clock had a limp."

"It has a cracked hub on the third lift wheel. Her work, actually—" Odile stopped.

But Maren had already gone still. Not dramatically. Just still, the way water goes still, the way the whole harbor goes still when the wind drops. She looked at the wheel on the baize cloth, at the crane mark showing faintly in the lamplight, and something crossed her face that Odile had no name for.

"So you found her," Maren said quietly.

"*Her.*"

"Your mother." Maren wrapped both hands around her mug but didn't drink. "Wren Crane. Best wheel-cutter on this coast, man or woman or otherwise, and don't let anyone at the guild tell you different. She cut that hub the year I took the light. I watched her carry it up this hill myself, wrapped in flannel, cursing the weight the whole way." A pause. "She swore like a stevedore. You get that from her too, I expect, though you keep it corked."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Odile set down the spoon. "You knew my mother."

"Everyone on this coast knew Wren Crane. Not everyone *knew* her." Maren's eyes came up, steady and grey as the sea in March. "I did. Thirty years, off and on. She kept the clock's teeth healthy when the observatory wouldn't pay for proper maintenance, and she never once put her name in the work ledgers, because the guild had opinions about who could hold a chisel, and Wren had even stronger ones about being told what she was." She drank finally, slowly. "You have her hands. I saw it the day you walked into my lighthouse with Vesper's shopping list and started straightening my chronometer without asking. Nearly dropped my pipe."

"Why didn't you say something?"

"What was there to say? 'You resemble a dead woman'? You'd have gone pale and polite and left, and I'd have lost my Thursday chess partner." Maren shrugged, vast shoulders moving like a swell. "I figured the truth keeps. Truth's patient. It's lies that need feeding every day."

Odile looked down at the cracked wheel. Her mother's wheel. Her mother's crack, in fact — a flaw in the making, or a flaw in the wearing, thirty years old and still stealing seconds from the city. Somewhere in the mechanism of Odile's chest, gears that had been sitting loose for years quietly meshed and began, very slowly, to turn.

"She worked on this clock," Odile said. "And nobody—"

"Halloway knows. Or his father knew, which comes to the same thing, and neither of them ever wrote it down either." Maren's voice flattened a degree. "There's a lot about that family that isn't written down. Watch him, Odile. He smiles too easily for a man with clean books."

Outside, the fog pressed against the tall windows, and far below, the harbor bell spoke its slow sentence into the dark.

* * *

They drank a second pot. Somewhere in the pouring of it, the conversation loosened its collar.

Maren talked the way lighthouse keepers talk — in long circuits, coming back around to the point from unexpected directions. About the winter the channel froze and she rowed supplies to the light through ice scum. About Tobin, whom she described as "a lit firework somebody handed us and asked us to hold." About Vesper, whose cough she mentioned only by not mentioning it — by talking instead about how much honey the household went through now, and how she'd taken to leaving a jar on the step without a note.

"And you," she said, refilling Odile's mug. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you answer questions with other questions and keep your actual self behind glass." Maren said it without heat, an observation rather than an accusation. "I do it too. Thirty-one years alone on a rock'll teach you the trick. But glass is fine for weather, Odile. It's poor shelter for family."

"We're not—" Odile began, and stopped, because the denial was automatic and also false, and both of them knew it.

"No," Maren agreed mildly. "Of course not. Four strangers sharing a roof on Lantern Row for no reason at all. Vesper keeping a bed aired for you since you were eleven. Me walking a mile uphill in fog with a tin of stew. Tobin sewing buttons on your coat sleeve because he says you dress like a scarecrow with a pension." She smiled into her tea. "No reason at all."

Odile laughed despite themself — a short, surprised sound that bounced oddly off the stone walls. It occurred to them, distantly, that they laughed very little these days, and that this room, this hour, this woman, had produced more laughter in twenty minutes than the last month had managed.

"The census man came back today," Odile said, after a while. It came out before they'd decided to say it. That was the effect of Maren; things fell out of Odile around her like coins from a torn pocket. "Second visit. He had new forms. He asked Vesper about — the nature of each occupant. Those words. And he wrote something down twice."

Maren's smile faded by degrees, like a tide going out. "Wrote what?"

"I couldn't see. He turned the page when I brought the tea. But he wrote, and then he wrote again underneath, and crossed nothing out." Odile turned the mug in their hands. "Vesper handled him beautifully. Told him the household was a boarding arrangement, told him Tobin was her late sister's grandson twice removed, told him I was her ward by employment. Every word true enough to pass. And he nodded along and thanked her and left. But he wrote twice."

"Twice," Maren repeated.

"He's not stupid. That's what frightens me. Stupid men you can manage. He listens like — like I listen to a clock. Like he's trying to hear where the fault is."

Maren set down her mug. For a long moment she looked at the master clock, ticking away in its tall case with its borrowed heart and its hidden history, and when she spoke again her voice had changed register — lower, more careful, each word placed like a foot on wet rock.

"There's a thing I ought to tell you. Have been meaning to tell you for weeks, and kept finding reasons to tell you next week instead." She rubbed her thumb along the mug's chipped rim. "Your mother left something behind. For you. Not money — money there isn't. Something else."

Odile went very still. "What kind of something."

"A paper. A legal paper. Vesper has it — she's kept it in the cedar box under her bed since the funeral, and I've kept the knowledge of it the way I keep everything, quiet and dry." Maren met Odile's eyes. "It concerns a berth, Odile. A paid passage."

"A passage where?"

"Across. On the last emigrant ship of the season. The *Meridian*, out of Verlow harbor, sails before the registration deadline — before the world gets any smaller than it already is." Maren said it plainly, without softening, because Maren did not soften; she illuminated. "One berth. Fully paid, transferable to a single name, and the name is yours to choose. Your mother bought it the year you were born. Paid it off over eleven years, coin by coin, wheel by wheel, and never told a soul except Vesper and me why."

The lamp hissed. The clock ticked. Odile heard all of it from a great distance, the way you hear sounds underwater.

"One berth," they said.

"One berth."

"For one person. Out of four."

Maren didn't flinch and didn't lie. "Yes."

Odile looked at the cracked wheel on the cloth — her mother's hand, her mother's flaw, her mother's thirty-year-old theft of four seconds an hour — and understood suddenly that everything her mother had touched came with the same condition attached. Beauty with a fault line in it. Safety with a cost buried in the hub. A gift of escape, purchased in secret over eleven years, sized for exactly one.

"Why?" Odile asked. Their voice came out strange. "Why buy it? Why hide it? Why give a child a door out and never mention the door existed?"

"Because Wren Crane read the newspapers," Maren said. "Because she could see which way the wind was coming round, twenty years before the rest of us admitted it. Because she loved you the way I love the light — which is to say, she built the thing that would save you, and then she trusted you to find it when you needed it, and she prayed you never would." She rose, collected the mugs, and stood a moment with her back to Odile, facing the fogged windows. "I'm not telling you to go. God knows the thought of this house losing you turns my stomach. I'm telling you it exists. That's all a keeper does — I show you the light. Where you steer is your own affair."

"Maren." Odile stood. "Does Tobin know? Does Vesper want—"

"Ask Vesper." Maren pulled her oilskin collar up. "Tomorrow. Tonight you finish that wheel, because I've listened to that fourth-hour stumble for thirty-one years and I'd take it kindly if my friend's daughter was the one who fixed her grandmother's— " she corrected herself, "—her mother's mistake."

She let herself out. Odile heard her boots on the stair, unhurried, fading, and then the distant clank of the path gate, and then nothing but fog and tide.

* * *

Alone, Odile did not pick up the tools right away.

They stood in front of the master clock with the lamp turned low and looked at the machine that governed the harbor — the civic heartbeat, Halloway called it, the thing that told every ship in the roadstead where the channel lay safe and where the shoal waited with its teeth. One cracked hub, four stolen seconds, and sooner or later the whole edifice of tide-tables and bells and trusting captains would drift out of truth.

Her mother had known. Had maintained it in secret, nameless, off the ledger, because the ledger wasn't allowed to contain her.

Odile thought about the berth. One seat on the *Meridian*, sailing west ahead of the closing walls. Tobin, with his pamphlets and his ink-stained cuffs and his genius for finding trouble — Tobin should have it. Or Vesper, whose lungs were drowning by inches and who would never say so. One name. One. Out of four people who ate at the same table, argued about chess, sewed each other's buttons, and were, by law, nothing to one another at all.

A gift shaped like a knife. Thanks, Mother.

Odile picked up the cracked wheel and held it to the light, turning it slowly, reading the flaw the way you'd read a letter in a language only clockmakers and mothers spoke. It could be brazed, poorly. It could not be repaired properly — not truly, not for a machine this critical. What it needed was a replacement hub, cut fresh, and good steel-and-brass stock of that grade came from exactly one supplier in Verlow, and cost exactly—

Odile did the arithmetic without wanting to. Provisions for an emigrant crossing. A trunk, warm clothes, seed money. Roughly.

Roughly the same figure.

The number hung in the lamplight between the two inheritances like a pendulum at the top of its swing, motionless, deciding.

Far below, the fog bell counted the half hour. And down the cliff road, small and yellow and bobbing through the murk, a lantern appeared — a single walker climbing the observatory path at midnight, at the wrong hour, in the wrong weather, with the deliberate stride of a man carrying papers.

Odile knew that stride. Everyone on Lantern Row had learned that stride in the space of one doorstep visit.

Ambrose Pell, come back after dark.

Odile closed the clock case softly, turned down the lamp, and stood in the shadow between her mother's wheel and her mother's escape route, watching the light climb.
