## 6. A Second Visit

The ledger room of the Census Bureau smelled of cold tallow and older men's opinions, and by half past eight on Monday morning Ambrose Pell had already been told twice to hurry up.

He did not hurry. Hurrying was how errors were born. He had learned that in his first year, when a transposed digit in the Saltmarket returns had put eleven dead men back on the tax rolls and cost him a fortnight of evenings correcting them by hand while his supervisor, Mr. Quill, stood over him saying *the number is the number, Pell, the number is not your friend or your enemy.* Ambrose had taken the lesson to heart in a way he suspected Mr. Quill had never intended. The number was not anyone's friend. That was precisely the problem.

He opened his field notebook to the Lantern Row entries and laid it flat under the lamp, smoothing the page with both hands as though ironing a shirt.

*Household at No. 7 Lantern Row. Occupants: four.*

He read down the column of names he'd taken on Thursday last.

*Vesper Crane, sixty-one, aunt to the clockmaker. Occupation: none stated; "keeper of accounts," she said, and laughed like it was a joke with a blade in it.*

*Odile Crane, twenty-six, apprentice clockmaker. Relation: niece or nephew — subject declined to specify which.*

*Tobin Marsh, seventeen, printer's devil. Relation: "lodger."*

*Maren Cole, fifty-eight, lighthouse keeper. Relation: "lodger."*

Ambrose set his pencil down and looked at the numbers the way a man looks at a scale that won't balance no matter how he shifts the weights.

Four occupants. Three surnames. One aunt who might plausibly claim kinship over exactly one other person in the house, and even that claim came with a hedge attached — *niece or nephew*, subject declined, and what manner of answer was that, what manner of person answered a lawful question with an arithmetic problem? Two lodgers. No wife, no husband, no child, no parent, no servant registered in service, no boarder paying rent on a lease. Under the new Registration Act, Schedule B, a household was either a family or it was nothing. A family had a head, and the head had blood or marriage or contract binding the rest to them like spokes to a hub. Four people sharing one roof with nothing at the center holding them together was not a household. It was, in the language of the statute — Ambrose recited it to himself, because the language mattered, because precision was the whole dignity of his trade — *"an aggregation of unrelated persons occupying premises without lawful relation, liable to dispersal."*

Dispersal. Such a clean word for turning three old people and a boy into the street.

He turned the page and found the note he'd made without quite meaning to make it, the one written twice, pressed hard enough into the paper that it showed through from the reverse like a scar: *the clockmaker would not say what they are.* And beneath it, smaller, in the margin where marginalia had no legal standing at all: *but they mended Mrs. Fenn's kitchen clock for free, per Mrs. Fenn, who has never said a generous word about anyone.*

That was the trouble with door-to-door work. You collected more than names. You collected the whole weather of a street.

"Ambrose."

Mr. Quill's voice arrived before Mr. Quill did, the way weather does. He came down the aisle between the desks with a folder under his arm and the expression of a man perpetually disappointed by the failure of others to be folders of themselves. He was sixty if he was a day, gray as newsprint, and he had spent thirty years counting Verlow without once, so far as Ambrose could determine, having looked at it.

"Your Lantern Row district," Quill said. "You're behind schedule. Ward Six is due for review Thursday."

"I'm aware."

"Are you also aware that the Commissioner wants irregular households flagged by Friday? There's a list going to the Corporation." He dropped the folder on Ambrose's desk. It landed with the soft fat slap of official indifference. "New forms inside. Schedule C. Anything that doesn't reconcile goes on it."

Ambrose did not touch the folder. "Define doesn't reconcile."

Quill gave him a look over the rim of his spectacles, the look of a man being asked to define water. "Numbers that don't add, Pell. Occupants against relations. Names against surnames. Persons claiming to be things the schedules don't have boxes for." He tapped the folder once with a blunt finger. "You wrote in your preliminary notes — I read them Saturday — that there's a house on Lantern Row with four bodies and no family in it. That's exactly the sort of thing we're paid to find."

"I wrote that there were four occupants whose relations I couldn't yet establish," Ambrose said. "Establishment is my job. Flagging is what comes after establishment. Not before."

"Is this pedantry, or is this stalling?"

"It's procedure."

"It's Thursday's problem either way." Quill straightened, and something almost human flickered across his face before the bureaucracy reabsorbed it. "For what it's worth, you're good at this. Best reconciler I've got. Which is why I'm telling you plainly: the Commissioner has been asked by the Harbor Trust to clear the irregular rolls before the shipping season opens. They want the cheap rooms emptied out and the beds filled with registered seasonal labor. Lantern Row is full of cheap rooms." He adjusted his spectacles. "Find me families or find me vacancies, Ambrose. Either is fine. What isn't fine is a man who can't tell me which one he's looking at."

He went back up the aisle, and the room resumed its scratching, forty pencils moving like insects in the lamplight, converting the living city into columns.

Ambrose sat for a long moment with his hands folded over the notebook.

Then he opened it again and began, methodically, to do the thing he was best at. He reconciled.

***

He worked through the morning, and by noon he had built the Crane household out of paper the way an engineer builds a bridge out of straws — every element true, every joint failing.

Vesper Crane appeared in the old registers going back thirty years, always as Crane, always at Lantern Row, first as "widow's sister, housekeeper" to a man named Edmund Crane who had died in '71, then alone. She owned the house. That much was solid. Deeds didn't lie; deeds were the only documents in Verlow that never lied.

Odile Crane first appeared nine years ago, entered in Vesper's own hand on a renewal form: *niece's child, apprenticed.* No given name beyond Odile. No sex recorded — the clerk's box marked with a dash, and the dash initialed by some long-gone supervisor who had evidently decided a dash was less trouble than an inquiry. Ambrose stared at that dash for a long time. Someone had looked at this question before him and chosen to look away. That was either negligence or mercy, and the file did not say which.

Tobin Marsh appeared nowhere. No birth registration Ambrose could find in the city rolls, no apprenticeship indenture filed with the Printers' Guild — and unindented print-shop boys were technically contraband, a fact the Guild enforced with more enthusiasm than the Crown ever had. The boy was seventeen years old and legally he had never happened.

Maren Cole appeared everywhere and belonged nowhere. Trinity House rolls, the Northern Lights Board, a lighthouse service record stretching back to a girlhood posting in the far north — but no residence, no tenancy, nothing tying her to any address until Thursday, when she'd given Ambrose No. 7 Lantern Row with the calm of a woman reading coordinates off a chart.

And the mother. Ambrose had pulled the mother's file almost without deciding to — the name Odile had let slip on the doorstep, when Vesper Crane's deflections had momentarily failed and the young clockmaker had said, quietly, *my mother worked here, in her way,* and Vesper had moved the conversation along like a hand closing a drawer.

*Ilse Crane. Registered 1873. Occupation: clockmaker. Kin: none.*

Clockmaker. Not clockmaker's wife, not widow, not daughter-of. Clockmaker, plain, in 1873, in a register where every other woman was somebody's somebody. And kin: none — though Ilse Crane had certainly had a sister, because Vesper Crane was sitting at No. 7 right now claiming to be an aunt, and sisters were kin by any law in the empire.

Ambrose read the file three times. On the third pass he noticed the flag: a red slip pasted inside the cover, the kind used to mark files requiring supervisory review. The slip was dated 1874. Below it, the disposition line — the line where a reviewer was supposed to write what action was taken —

— was closed with a single signature and no explanation. The signature was illegible, a flourish and a tail, but the office stamp beside it was legible enough: *OBSERVATORY.*

Someone at the observatory had reached into the census twenty years ago and shut a file that should have been opened wide. And the daughter of that file now stood in front of the observatory's master clock with tools in her hands, keeping the city's heartbeat running.

Ambrose closed the file and sat very still.

There was a version of this that was simple. The simple version went: irregular household, four unrelated persons, one contraband minor, one occupant of ambiguous classification, one property owner harboring all of them — flag it, schedule it, disperse it. Done by Friday. Commended by the Commissioner. Perhaps noticed further up. Ambrose was thirty-four and ambitious in the quiet way of men who keep their ambition where they keep their money, hidden and counted nightly.

And there was another version, and it would not leave him alone, and it went like this:

A woman twenty years ago had declared herself a clockmaker and nobody's anything, and someone powerful had decided she was allowed to exist anyway. A dying old woman was keeping a roof over a child the law said was nobody's child. A lighthouse keeper had come ashore after a lifetime of saving strangers and asked to sleep in a cheap room among people who owed her nothing. And a boy who legally had never been born was, by all local testimony — Mrs. Fenn's testimony, the greengrocer's, the ferryman's, the whole murmuring weather of Lantern Row — working twelve-hour days setting type and coming home to a table where someone kept a place for him.

The law said such a house was an aggregation awaiting dispersal.

The law had never eaten supper there.

"Pell." One of the junior clerks, a pink nervous boy named Wexler, hovering at his elbow. "Mr. Quill wants your Schedule C preliminaries before the two o'clock post."

"Tell him they're in progress."

"He said — he said specifically not to let you gold-plate it. He said you've got a reputation for gold-plating."

"Did he." Ambrose did not look up. "Tell him the Lantern Row returns will be on his desk Thursday morning. Complete."

Wexler fled. Ambrose turned back to the notebook and, after a moment, took out a fresh sheet and drew four columns, one for each name, and began filling in what he actually knew — not what the schedules had boxes for, but what was true. *Pays no rent, keeps the books. Mends clocks, mended Fenn's for free. Sets type, unindentured, frightened. Keeps the light, came ashore to die among friends, I think.*

Then, at the bottom, he wrote the question that the form had no space for and that had been sitting in his chest since Thursday like a swallowed stone:

*If dispersal is the remedy — what, precisely, is the disease?*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. Then he did not cross it out. Instead he blotted it, folded the sheet, and put it in his breast pocket, on the left side, where a man keeps the papers that could ruin him.

***

Wednesday evening he walked to Lantern Row without the folder, without the forms, without the badge on its chain tucked in his collar. Just a man in a coat, carrying a loaf he had bought at the corner bakery because arriving empty-handed at a stranger's door was a thing his mother had taught him was rude, and because bread was neutral. Bread took no sides. Bread was the only document he carried that everyone could sign.

The fog had come up off the harbor thick as wool, and the gas lamps along the Row burned in halos, each one a small captive sun. From somewhere below, the harbor bell spoke its slow count — seven, then a pause, then seven again — governed, he knew, by the great clock above the town, the one with the observatory's name on it, the one the Crane girl or boy or neither repaired.

No. 7's windows were lit. Through the frosted glass he could see shadows moving: someone tall bending over a table, someone small crossing quickly, someone seated very still in a chair by the fire, too still, the stillness of the ill.

Ambrose stood across the street for the better part of ten minutes, loaf cooling under his arm, and examined himself the way he examined a ledger, looking for the entry that didn't reconcile.

Duty said: knock, present the Schedule C, ask the questions the law required, write down the answers, and let the machine do what machines do. Duty had fed him and housed him and given his life a spine. He was not a man who betrayed duty lightly; he had seen what became of men without spines, and they lay around Verlow in doorways like dropped coats.

But doubt had gotten in, the way damp gets in, invisibly and everywhere, and the doubt said: you have read the file of Ilse Crane. You know that this law is not old. You know it is new, and newness means somebody wanted it, and you have not yet asked yourself the only question a census man is finally obliged to ask — *counted for whom?*

The bell below struck the quarter hour. In the lit window, the small shadow — the boy, Tobin — stopped at the table and touched the seated shadow's shoulder, briefly, awkwardly, the way the young touch the old when no one has taught them how.

Ambrose crossed the street.

He was halfway to the door when it opened ahead of him, and Odile Crane stepped out onto the step with a shawl around their shoulders and a lamp in their hand, and did not seem surprised to see him at all.

"Mr. Pell," they said. Their voice was level, and their eyes went first to the loaf, then to his empty coat pocket where the folder usually lived. "We wondered when you'd be back. Aunt Vesper says the government always comes twice — once to count you, and once to decide what you're worth."

"I've brought bread," Ambrose said, feeling immediately the absurdity of it, the smallness of the offering against the size of what he'd come to say or hadn't yet decided to say.

"We noticed," Odile said, and stepped back from the doorway, and held the lamp higher, and the light fell out across the step like an invitation or a dare. "She says you'd better come in before the fog gets in behind you. She says fog is just the sea asking questions."

Behind them, down the hall, Ambrose could hear the murmur of the household — Vesper's low voice, the clink of cups, the scrape of a chair — four lives arranged around one table, waiting to see what the second visit would bring.

He stepped up onto the threshold, and the door closed behind him, and somewhere above the fog-bound town the master clock began, faintly, to strike.
