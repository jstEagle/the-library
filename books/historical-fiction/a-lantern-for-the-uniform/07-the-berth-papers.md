## 7. The Berth Papers

Maren Cole had kept the deed folded inside her oilskin coat for eleven years, through gales and griefs and the slow rot of her joints, and she had never once told a living soul it was there. Not because she was ashamed of it. Because she had promised.

*Keep it until the girl — or whatever Odile turns out to be — is old enough to need it. They'll know when they need it. You'll know too.*

Ilse Crane had said that on a Tuesday in October, standing at the rail of nothing at all, just the harbor wall, wind pulling at her collar like a child demanding attention. Maren had laughed at the phrasing then. *Whatever Odile turns out to be.* Ilse had not laughed. Ilse had said, *That's the whole point, Mare. The papers will ask them what they are. Let them have one thing that asks nothing.*

Now the papers were asking everything, every day, from doorsteps and ledgers and the new brass-bound offices down by the customs house, and Maren sat in Vesper's parlor on Lantern Row with the oilskin packet in her lap and her hands flat on top of it like a woman keeping a lid on something that wanted to fly.

Vesper was propped in her chair by the window with two blankets and a shawl, which was one more layer than she'd allowed herself in September. Odile sat across from her, mending — always mending — a watch so small it looked like a beetle in their long fingers. Neither of them was looking at Maren, but both of them had gone still in the particular way of people who have heard a floorboard shift under a heavy secret.

"You said you knew my mother," Odile said. Not a question. They'd been circling it for three days now, since the tea at the observatory, polite as cats around an unexploded shell.

"I did." Maren cleared her throat. Her voice came out lower than she intended, sea-scraped as ever. "Better than most. Better than anyone left living, I'd say."

"Then you'll know why I don't ask twice."

Vesper made a small sound — half warning, half surrender — and Maren decided she was done being careful. Eleven years was long enough to carry anything.

She unwound the cord from the oilskin packet and laid it open on the table between them. Inside: a folded document, yellowed soft as cloth at the creases, sealed once with wax that had cracked along its edge, and beneath it a smaller paper, a ticket stub of heavy cream stock with a printed border of anchors and laurel leaves that some engraver in some happier year had thought fitting.

Odile put down the watch. That was how Maren knew it had landed. In all her months on Lantern Row she had never once seen Odile set down work unfinished.

"What is it?" Vesper asked, though Maren thought — hoped — that some part of the old woman already knew. She had that look. The look of someone hearing a clock strike a hour they'd stopped expecting.

"Read it," Maren said. "Out loud or quiet, your choice. But read it whole. Ilse would've wanted it read whole."

Odile unfolded the larger document first. Their eyes moved down it, and Maren watched them move, and watched the moment — she could have marked it to the second, she who marked tides and bell-times for a living — when understanding arrived and changed the shape of the room.

"*This certifies,*" Odile read, voice level in the way that level voices go when they are holding weight, "*one paid-in-full emigrant berth aboard the vessel designated as final departure of the season from the Port of Verlow, transferable by endorsement to a single named party.*" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "*Issued in the name of Ilse Marigold Crane. Endorsement clause: the bearer may assign this passage to one (1) person only, by signature, prior to the sailing date fixed by the Harbor Commission.*"

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere up the row a cart went by, and its wheels found every rut in the cobbles.

"There's a ship," Maren said gently, into that silence. "Every season's end. Last one out before the winter closures. This year she sails—" she did the arithmetic she'd been doing for weeks, hating it— "eleven weeks. Perhaps ten, if the weather turns early. The *Meridian Star*. Berth paid in full, twenty years ago, by a woman who saved for it out of a clockmaker's wages. Third class, but clean. A bunk, a locker, a place at the mess table, and a port on the far side where nobody has ever heard the word Verlow."

Odile hadn't touched the ticket. Their hands were in their lap now, both of them, folded like a closed case. "She never took it."

"No."

"Why?"

Maren looked at Vesper. Vesper looked back, and something passed between the two old women that had no words in it, or too many.

"Because by the time she'd saved enough to go," Maren said, "there was you. And Ilse Crane was many things, but she was never going to cross an ocean and leave her child in a city that kept trying to file them under *other*. So she stayed. And she told me: *When the laws come — and they'll come, Mare, they always come — give them this. Not so they can run. So they can choose. There's a difference between a cage with an open door and a home.*"

Odile stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there with their back to the room, and Maren saw their shoulders, and saw that they were not shaking, and understood that not-shaking was costing them more than shaking would have.

"She left it to me," Odile said to the glass. "One berth. One name. Out of four of us."

"One name," Maren agreed. "That was the terms. I've read the back of it forty times hoping I'd misread. Transferable by endorsement to a single named party. No amendments. I asked a shipping clerk once, hypothetically, what would happen if you tried to split it four ways. He laughed at me. Then he remembered the new forms and stopped laughing."

Vesper had picked up the cream-colored stub with its anchors and laurels. She held it the way she held anything precious — at arm's length, suspiciously, as though it might make claims on her.

"Well," she said. "That's settled easily enough. It goes to Tobin."

Maren had known this was coming. She had rehearsed her answer on the walk over, walking the seawall with the wind arguing at her, and she had thought herself ready. She was not ready. Hearing it said aloud, so fast, so *finished*, lit something in her chest that had no patience in it at all.

"It does not," Maren said.

Vesper raised her eyebrows, the great grey arches of them, the ones that had quelled dockmasters and debt collectors alike. "It's a berth to anywhere that isn't here, Maren, and the boy is seventeen and already carrying pamphlets that'll see him transported if he's caught twice. He needs it more than any of us. He has the most years to lose."

"He has the most years to *spend*, you mean. Anywhere he lands, he'll spend them starting over alone. Seventeen and friendless in a foreign port with no trade but ink on his hands—"

"He has a trade. He's a printer's devil, he can find a press in any city on earth—"

"—and who teaches him the letters? Who sits with him of an evening? He reads like a man falling downstairs, Vesper. You know it. I've seen you correcting him with your finger on the page when you think nobody's watching." Maren leaned forward, and her hip complained about it, and she ignored it. "He needs this household more than he needs that ticket. He'd be a boy running again. He's done his running."

"And what would you have me do with it?" Vesper's voice didn't rise — it never rose — but it thinned, the way wire thins before it parts. "Shall I take it myself? An old woman with a bad chest, to go and die politely in some warmer latitude, alone, among strangers, while the three of you—" She stopped. Maren watched her stop. Watched the sentence get swallowed whole, the way Vesper swallowed everything, all the worst of it, every day.

"While the three of us what," Odile said quietly, from the window. Still facing out.

Vesper's chin came up. "While the three of you manage."

"Manage," Odile repeated. The word landed flat and cold as a dropped tool.

It was Maren who broke then — not loudly; she'd spent forty years learning to speak against storms and she did not waste volume. But she broke, in the sense that the thing underneath came up through the floorboards.

"Vesper Crane, I have logged ships out of that light for thirty-one years, and I am telling you what I know the way I know it: that chest of yours is not *a bad chest*. I've seen the way you climb the stairs now. I've timed it. Don't glare at me — timing things is my trade. In March you climbed those stairs in forty seconds. Last week it was ninety, and you rested on the landing, and you thought we didn't hear you resting." She held the old woman's gaze and did not flinch, because flinching would have been the cruelest thing she could do. "You're dying, Ves. Slowly, maybe. Maybe slower than the winter, maybe not. And you want to hand the one door out of this city to a boy who doesn't even want it, rather than use it yourself, because using it would mean admitting the door matters."

The parlor went very still. The fire ticked. Down at the harbor, faint through the glass, the evening bell rang the tide change, and Maren counted the strokes out of habit — six, seven, eight — while Vesper sat with the ticket stub in her lap and her knuckles white around its edges.

"That is a vile thing to say to a sick woman," Vesper said at last, evenly.

"It's the truest thing anyone's said to you since March."

"You don't know what you're—"

"Ilse trusted me with this for eleven years," Maren said. "Eleven years I've carried it, waiting, and I will tell you what I've learned in that time: a gift like this isn't a kindness if giving it away costs the giver nothing. Ilse paid for it with years of her life, Vesper. Wages pinched and hours doubled and a child growing up fatherless and mother-tired. It wasn't meant to be passed around the table like bread. It was meant to *matter*."

"To matter to whom?" Vesper demanded. "Whose life does it buy? That's the question, isn't it. Whose life is worth a crossing?"

"Don't," Odile said.

They still hadn't turned around. The word came off the glass and into the room and stopped both women mid-breath.

"Don't," Odile said again, turning now, and their face was the face Maren had learned at the observatory — the composed, courteous, utterly defended face — except that tonight the defense had a crack straight down the middle of it. "Neither of you. You're deciding for each other and for Tobin and for me, and nobody has asked the only person in this room whose mother signed that paper."

"We're asking you now," Maren said.

"No. You're arguing over me like I'm cargo." Odile crossed the room and stood over the table, over the deed and the stub, and for a moment Maren thought they were going to tear the whole packet in half, and part of her — a shameful, storm-dark part of her — wished they would. "You want to know why she left it to me? Not to Tobin, not to Aunt Vesper, not split four ways. To *me*. I'll tell you why. Because she knew exactly what this city would try to make me. A line in someone else's ledger. A question mark on a form. She left me a door because everyone else would spend my whole life telling me which rooms I was allowed in." Their voice didn't shake. It did something worse; it went very precise, each word set like a jewel. "And now the two people I love most in the world are doing the same thing with her last gift. Deciding. Allocating. Assigning me to a category."

"Odile—" Vesper began.

"I'm not done." And they weren't; Maren had never heard them not-done before, and it frightened her a little, the way calm water frightens you when you know what's under it. "Tobin gets no say in this either, and you both know what he'd say. He'd say give it to Vesper, and he'd cry while he said it, and he'd hate himself forever for surviving. And you'd hate yourself too, Vesper, wherever you ended up — here or there — because that's the shape of you, you'd find a way. And Maren would stay regardless, because Maren belongs to that light like the light belongs to the sky. So let's say it plainly, all of it, since we're being plain." They looked from one woman to the other. "Nobody in this house wants to leave. Everybody in this house wants somebody else to be saved. And there's one berth."

The clock on the mantel — Vesper's cheap tin thing, forever four minutes slow — ticked through the silence.

"So we keep it," Odile said. "We endorse no name. We let it sit. If the deadline comes and no one's signed, it dies with the season, and Ilse's money bought us eleven weeks of knowing we could have gone. That's worth something. Knowing is worth something."

"That's not choosing," Maren said softly. "That's letting it lapse. That's throwing away her sacrifice because choosing hurts."

"Yes," Odile said. "Probably."

And that — the honesty of it, the terrible unheroic honesty — was what undid the argument better than winning it could have. Vesper looked down at the ticket in her lap. Maren looked at her hands. Outside, full dark had come down on Lantern Row, and the lamps were being lit one by one by the old lamplighter who still walked his rounds though half the city had gone to electric and the rest had gone to nothing.

Vesper smoothed the stub flat against her knee, once, twice, like soothing an animal.

"We won't decide tonight," she said finally, and her voice had come back to itself, dry and wry and armored. "We're none of us fit. Maren's been spoiling for this fight for a week and I've been spoiling to lose it, and Odile—" she glanced up— "Odile has a master clock to murder and resurrect, if Halloway hasn't dismissed them already for insolence."

"He hasn't," Odile said. "Yet."

"Then put the papers away, Maren, and we'll say no more of it until—" Vesper paused. Maren saw her choose the word carefully, saw her lay it down like a card. "Until there's news. There's always news, in this city, these days. Something will decide for us if we won't decide for ourselves. That's how Verlow works."

Maren wound the cord back around the oilskin packet with fingers that ached from more than the damp. At the door, shrugging into her coat, she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "your mother nearly endorsed it to Vesper, once. Did you know that? Years ago. Before you were born. When Vesper was younger and sicker in a different way. And Vesper refused, and Ilse put the deed in my coat, and neither of them ever spoke of it again." She pulled the door open onto the cold. "Stubborn runs in that family. All of it. Every branch."

The street air hit her face like a slap of brine. Behind her she heard Vesper say something low to Odile, too quiet to catch, and Odile's answer, also too quiet, and then the small domestic sounds of a household closing itself up for the night — the bolt, the banked fire, the tin clock four minutes slow.

Maren walked toward the lighthouse with the packet back inside her coat, lighter by nothing and heavier by everything, and she did her arithmetic again without meaning to. Eleven weeks to the sailing. Perhaps ten.

And it was somewhere past the second turn of the seawall, with the light sweeping its patient arm overhead, that she remembered the other thing — the thing she had not shown them, tucked behind the deed in the oilskin fold. A second paper. A letter, unopened, in Ilse's handwriting, addressed not to Odile at all.

Addressed to the Harbor Commission.

And tomorrow, Maren thought, stopping dead on the path with the wind pulling her coat like a child demanding attention, tomorrow she would have to decide whether Ilse Crane's last words to the city were meant to be read — or buried at sea.
