## 10. Vesper's Long Night

The candle had burned down to its last thumb-width when Vesper Crane decided she would not die before the census deadline.

Not a resolution, exactly. More an act of scheduling. She had spent sixty-one years arranging the unruly world into manageable compartments — bills in the blue tin, grievances aired on Sundays only, love expressed through the medium of soup — and dying was simply one more appointment that could, with sufficient willpower and a strong tisane, be postponed.

She propped herself higher on the pillows. The pillows were Odile's doing; there were five of them now, arranged in a fan behind her back like cards held by a nervous gambler. The child — no, not a child, Vesper corrected herself, though her heart refused the correction — had been fussing over her all evening until Vesper had finally ordered them upstairs to sleep.

"I'll ring if I need anything," she'd said, which was a lie, because she had removed the bell from her bedside that afternoon and hidden it in the drawer with her good gloves. She did not need anything. That was the entire point of her.

The house settled around her the way it always did at night: Tobin's floorboards creaking overhead as he moved between his room and the little attic space where he kept his press, Maren not yet home from the light — she'd see the lamp sweep past the window around two, a slow white blink, punctual as conscience — and Odile's door closed but never locked. Never once locked, in all these years. She took a fierce, private pride in that. A household where nobody locked their doors, in a city that was learning to count its people like coins.

On the washstand beside her bed sat two sheets of paper, blank, and a pen whose nib she'd sharpened herself because she no longer trusted anyone else to do it properly.

*Dictation,* the solicitor's letter would say. *Dictated by Vesper Crane of Lantern Row, being of sound mind and imperfect body.* No — she struck that out mentally. Imperfect body was too close to confession. *Being of sound mind.* Full stop. Let them draw what conclusions they liked; conclusions were cheaper than questions.

She had tried writing the letters herself, three nights running. Her hand betrayed her. The script that had once been the terror of Verlow's merchants — crisp, upright, merciless about arrears — had gone soft at the edges, letters leaning against each other like drunks holding one another up outside a closing tavern. She would not have her last documents look like that. She would not have them look like anything but what she intended.

So: dictation. But dictation required a scribe, and a scribe required waking someone, and waking someone required admitting that the letters existed at all, which required admitting that settling affairs was necessary, which required admitting —

She stopped that thought the way you stop a clock: firmly, by hand, before it could strike something true.

Instead she reached for the drawer where she kept the household's papers, and began, silently, to compose.

*To Mr. Aldous Fenwick, Solicitor, Chancery Lane.*

*Dear Mr. Fenwick. I write concerning the disposition of my estate, such as it is.*

Such as it is. There was honesty for you. Her estate consisted of the leasehold on Lantern Row (nine years remaining, rent paid through spring), a walnut bureau with a loose leg, forty-one volumes of mostly poetry, a silver thimble her mother had carried across an ocean, and the sum of eleven pounds, six shillings in the blue tin, minus whatever Odile had quietly taken out this week for clock parts and quietly not mentioned. Vesper counted the tin every Thursday. She knew its arithmetic the way other women knew scripture.

*I wish to establish that the occupants of this house — Miss Cole, Master Tobin Quill, and my grandnephew Odile Crane — are, in law and in my intention, family.*

There it was. The sentence she'd been circling for three nights. In law. As if law were a room you could walk into and claim a chair. She snorted at herself, alone in the lamplight. Law was not a room. Law was weather, lately — coming off the harbor in grey sheets, soaking everyone equally, except that some people were given umbrellas and others were given forms asking them to account for themselves.

Four occupants. Three surnames. No lawful relation among them, except the thin thread of her own blood running to Odile, and blood so diluted by distance that the census man might well ask why a woman of her age shared a house with a grandnephew who answered to neither *he* nor *she* and seemed perfectly content about it.

She had liked Ambrose Pell, actually. That was the trouble. He had sat in her parlor with his forms squared on his knee and asked his questions in a voice like a man reading a recipe, and when Aunt Vesper had told him — with perfect truth — that Odile was "family, in every sense that matters," he had looked up sharply, and something had passed across his face that wasn't cruelty. It had been almost hunger. A man starving for a different answer than the form allowed.

He had written something down twice. Vesper had seen it from her chair by the window, pretending to mend a sock she hadn't mended in years. She saw most things. It was her one talent, seeing things, and it had served her well through two wars' worth of rumors, one bankruptcy, and the raising-by-proxy of a child the world insisted on mislabeling.

What had Pell written twice? A name? A number? She turned the question over like a coin she suspected was foreign.

It didn't matter tonight. Tonight there were letters to compose, and the first one was nearly done in her head, and she needed only the discipline to hold it there until morning, when she would summon Tobin — no, not Tobin. Tobin couldn't keep a secret longer than a kettle takes to boil. Not Maren either; Maren would take one look at Vesper propped on her pillows dictating final dispositions and would go straight to the doctor, and Dr. Llewellyn had already said his piece in October, in that gentle voice men use when they've given up arguing, and Vesper had thanked him for his time and never sent for him again.

Odile, then. Odile would write what she dictated and ask nothing, because asking nothing was the family religion, practiced devoutly by all four of them. They were a congregation of silence. She both loved and despaired of it.

*Item second,* she composed, watching the candle flame lean. *The berth.*

Ah. The berth. Even in her own head, the words came out hushed.

One paid passage aboard the last emigrant ship of the season, bought twenty-two years ago by a woman with salt-cracked hands and a laugh like rigging in wind — Odile's mother, Wren, who had worked brass at the observatory by day and saved coin by night, who had written to Vesper from wherever she was moored that week: *If anything happens to me, Vess, sell everything but the tools, and put the child on that ship. Anywhere with a horizon. Promise me.*

Vesper had promised. She was very good at promises and very bad at keeping the ones that hurt. So instead of selling, she had taken Wren's savings — the berth deed, transferable, one name only — and folded it into the blue tin beneath the grocery money, and there it had lain for twenty years while the child grew up among gears and didn't drown at sea, and Vesper had told herself every year: *next year I'll tell them, next year.* And now the deadline was the deadline, and the tin held eleven pounds and a paper worth more than the whole street, and the telling could not wait much longer.

Maren knew. Maren had known since Wren, since the light and the harbor and the long friendship of two women who talked more easily about rope than feelings. Vesper had seen it in her eyes last week — *the berth* hovering unspoken between them like a moth.

And Maren wanted it to be Vesper who took it. Vesper had watched her think it, that stubborn lighthouse face. Send the dying woman to warm water and kind doctors. And it was a generous thought, and it was wrong, and Vesper would rather be buried under the doorstep than spend her last months alone in some colony town being pitied by strangers.

No. The berth belonged to Tobin.

She had reasoned it out with the cold clarity the fever gave her, nights like this one, when her body burned and her mind went sharp as a blade laid on ice. Tobin was seventeen and reckless and printed sedition in the attic with ink on his knuckles, and when the registration lists were read out — and they *would* be read out; Vesper remembered other lists, other decades, other countries that had learned to count their people — a boy with no father willing to claim him would be the first name called. The ship would carry him somewhere his pamphlets couldn't hang him. Somewhere with a horizon.

Wren would have understood. Wren, who had crossed oceans twice and believed that survival was a craft like any other, requiring practice.

*Item third,* Vesper composed, and here her composure — her famous, armored, sixty-one-year composure — wobbled. *My wishes regarding Odile.*

Because Odile wouldn't go. She knew it the way she knew rain coming. If she ordered Odile onto that ship, Odile would stand on the quay with their hands at their sides and their eyes level and say, gently, terribly, *No one goes alone, Aunt Vesper* — or worse, nothing at all, just that quiet refusal that had ended every argument Vesper had ever started with them since they were nine years old. You could not argue with Odile. Arguing with Odile was like arguing with a mainspring: enormous stored force, total stillness.

So the letter to Fenwick must do what Vesper could not: make arrangements that held whether she was there to enforce them or not. The lease transferred to joint tenancy — all four names, let Pell choke on it. The bureau to Odile, with the drawers full of Wren's letters. The thimble to Maren, who'd laugh and then cry and then pretend she'd done neither. The books divided however Tobin liked, provided he promised — *promised in writing*, Vesper would insist, knowing it was futile and insisting anyway — to print no more pamphlets for one full year.

A lie even in fiction. He'd be printing again inside a month of her funeral, God rot him, brave stupid boy.

The candle guttered. Outside, the fog had come in off the harbor the way it did this time of year, thick enough to chew, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, and Vesper became aware, gradually and then all at once, of how loud her own breathing was. A wet sound at the bottom of each breath, like a boot pulling out of clay.

Dr. Llewellyn's voice came back to her, October-gentle: *You should put your affairs in order, Mrs. Crane.*

*In order.* As if they'd ever been otherwise. Her affairs were the tidiest thing about her.

She coughed then, really coughed, and had to grip the headboard until it passed, and when it passed she lay back against the five pillows sweating and shaking and thinking, with detached interest, *that was a bad one, that was worse than Tuesday's,* and then, immediately after, with the same tone: *I'll write to Fenwick tomorrow.*

Denial, Maren would call it. Maren had a word for everything and usually the right one. But Maren wasn't lying awake in a fog-bound house counting her own breaths like coins. Denial was a luxury for people who had someone to hand the truth to. Vesper's job — her last job, self-appointed, unpaid, lifelong — was to be the one who *held* the truth so the others didn't have to. Someone in every family had to be the wall the wave broke against. She had volunteered decades ago. She was not resigning now because the work got heavy.

Footsteps overhead. Tobin, padding to the privy or raiding the bread box, probably both. Then silence. Then, far off, the white blink of Maren's lamp sweeping past the window — two o'clock, punctual as ever — and Vesper found herself watching for it the way sailors watch for it, needing the rhythm, needing proof that somebody, somewhere, was keeping faith with the dark.

Her chest ached. She ignored it with professional skill.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow she would keep Odile home from the observatory — feign a better day, a request, a small domestic errand — and dictate the letters. Two of them. Fenwick for the estate.

And the second one.

The second letter was harder, and she had been circling it for weeks the way a wasp circles jam. *To Mr. Edmund Quill, printer, Gildersgate.* Tobin's father. A man Vesper had met exactly once, nine years ago, when he'd come to collect his runaway apprentice-son and Vesper had stood in her doorway with her arms folded and informed him, in a voice that had made stronger men step backward off her step, that the boy would stay where he was fed and loved, and Mr. Quill had looked at her — at the shabby respectable widow, at the strange child peering around her skirts, at the whole improbable household — and said, *You're collecting strays, madam. They bite eventually,* and left without his son.

Nine years. Nine years of Sunday dinners with an empty chair nobody acknowledged. Nine years of Tobin flinching at the sound of a printing press that wasn't his own.

But the letter had to be written. Because if Vesper died — *when*, said the honest voice, the 3 a.m. voice, the one she drowned in tisane — then Tobin would be alone in the eyes of the law. A minor, unclaimed, unregistered, living in a household of strangers under a new Act that asked for *the nature of each occupant* in handwriting. She had to try. One letter, offering the man his son back on paper if not in fact — legitimization, recognition, whatever word made it palatable — so that the boy would have a name to hide behind when the lists were read.

Even if Tobin would rather be hanged. Even if the offer itself might wound the boy worse than any law. She'd seal the letter and instruct Fenwick to hold it, deliver only upon — 

Only upon. She couldn't finish the phrase even in her own head. *Upon my death.* Three words, four syllables, and she could not make her mind say them.

*Upon my retirement,* she wrote instead, in the air with her finger, and laughed — a small dry sound, quickly smothered, because laughing hurt — at her own cowardice. Sixty-one years old and afraid of a phrase.

The fog pressed at the windows. Her breath whistled faintly at the bottom, boot-in-clay, boot-in-clay. She thought of Wren, suddenly and violently — Wren laughing on this exact doorstep with salt in her hair, saying *you worry like a corset, Vess, loosen up or burst* — and her eyes stung, and she let them, briefly, because there was no one awake to see, and even walls are allowed to weep at 3 a.m. if nobody's watching.

Then she straightened the pillows, and composed her face into morning, and rehearsed.

*"Odile, dear, fetch me the ink — no, sit, sit, my eyes are tired tonight, you'll write for me. Nothing important. Just a note to my solicitor about the lease. Yes, the lease. Routine business."*

Routine. The great lie of dying people everywhere: *it's routine.*

She was still rehearsing when the lamp swept past again at three, and still awake at four, and at dawn — when the fog thinned to gauze and the harbor bells began their morning speech and she could hear Odile moving below, lighting the stove, careful of every creak — Vesper Crane closed her eyes and slept at last, one hand resting on the drawer that held the bell she'd hidden, the deed she hadn't shown, and the truth she was saving for later.

Later. There was always later.

Downstairs, a knock came at the front door — early, insistent, official-sounding — and she heard Odile's footsteps pause, and held her breath to listen.

* * *

Through the floorboards, muffled by wool and distance, Vesper heard the door open, and a voice she recognized — smooth, apologetic, the census man's voice — say words she couldn't make out. Then Odile's voice, low and level. Then a silence that lasted too long.

Vesper lay in the grey morning light with her heart hammering like a trapped bird, and understood with sudden, terrible clarity that later had just run out.
