## 13. What Tobin Won't Say

The note came folded inside a broadsheet Tobin hadn't bought.

He found it at the bottom of the stack he was sweeping from the doorstep of Pell & Sons, Stationers — a shop he'd been loitering near for three days without quite admitting why — and the handwriting on it stopped his broom mid-arc. He knew that hand. He'd spent eleven years of his life being corrected by it. *Tobin, your serifs are lazy. Tobin, you ink like a left-handed drunk. Tobin, a Quillan does not slouch.*

*Boy. Tuesday. The Anchor & Chain, six o'clock. Come alone or don't come at all. — F.Q.*

His father had never once signed anything so short as *F.* It was always the full flourish: **Ferris Quillan**, and beneath it, in smaller type because even his signature was typeset, *Proprietor, The Verlow Argus*. But the note was hurried, or meant to look hurried, and Tobin stood there on the step with the broom handle going slick under his grip while the shop's bell jangled behind him and some customer asked if he was going to pay for that paper or just read it for free.

He paid for it. Three coppers. His father's handwriting cost him three coppers, which felt about right.

* * *

He didn't tell anyone.

That was the whole art of it, wasn't it — not telling. He'd gotten good at it since Lantern Row. Good at arriving home smelling of nothing, saying he'd been at Maren's, at the observatory steps, at the chandler's looking for odd work. Good at the small flat lies that kept a household safe, because a household like theirs lived on small flat lies the way a clock lived on oil: invisibly, constantly, or it seized up and died.

But this lie sat different in his chest. This one had weight and corners.

Tuesday came grey and low-tide-smelling, the fog sitting on the harbor like a lid on a pot. Tobin told Odile he'd be out looking for day-work at the rope-walk, and Odile — bent over the kitchen table with a jeweler's loupe screwed into their eye, surrounded by the tiny brass corpses of the master clock's guts — said, "Wear the coat with the patched elbow, they'll take you more serious if you look poor," which was Odile's idea of a blessing, and Tobin went out with his heart hammering against his ribs like something trying to escape before the trouble started.

The Anchor & Chain was a dockside public house all black beams and brown smoke, the kind of place where the ceiling drank the light and gave nothing back. His father had taken a corner table, back to the wall, facing the door — of course he had; Ferris Quillan faced doors the way other men faced their enemies — and he sat with an untouched glass of wine in front of him because Ferris Quillan did not drink public-house wine, he merely displayed his refusal of it.

"Tobin." Not *son*. Just the name, delivered like a proof pulled too early. "Sit."

Tobin sat. Up close his father looked older than the last time Tobin had seen him across the print-shop floor — older and somehow smaller, though the voice hadn't shrunk any. Grey had gotten into the sideburns. There were ink stains on his right cuff that no amount of money would ever wash out, and Tobin felt a stupid, treacherous ache at the sight of them, because his hands had those same stains, or had once, and some part of him still missed the smell of the press room like you miss a scar.

"You've been distributing filth," his father said.

"I've been distributing truth."

"Same thing, in this city, lately." Ferris turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, precisely, the way he'd turn a forme. "Do you know what I do when a new pressman brings me copy I can't use? I don't argue with him. Argument wastes ink. I wait. Everyone who writes rubbish eventually writes something useful, and then I own them, because I paid attention when nobody else bothered." He leaned forward. "I have been paying attention to you, boy. The pamphlets. The *Verlow Register Must Burn* nonsense — clever bit of composition, that, whoever cut the woodblock, the kerning alone—"

"I cut the block."

"—the kerning alone was amateur work," his father continued, as if Tobin hadn't spoken, "but the hand underneath it isn't. You could be good, Tobin. You were good. And now you're throwing type into the harbor for a household of—" he paused, selecting the word with a compositor's care, "—*strays*."

"They're not strays."

"An old woman dying of something she won't name. A lighthouse keeper who smells of tar and scripture. A—" and here his father did pause, genuinely, the first honest hesitation Tobin had seen on his face in years, "—a clockmaker who cannot decide what to be."

"Odile decided a long time ago," Tobin said quietly. "It's everyone else who hasn't caught up."

His father waved this off like smoke. Then he reached into his coat — that coat, the good one, the one that had hung behind the shop door all through Tobin's childhood like a flag of a country he'd never get citizenship in — and laid a document on the table between them.

It was heavy paper. Bond stock, cream-colored, with a printed border. Tobin knew paper the way priests know Latin, and this was expensive paper, and it was addressed to him: *To the Master of the Court of Registration, Verlow.*

"What is it?"

"A petition of legitimation." His father said it the way he'd say *double demy* or *em quad* — a technical term, a tool. "Under the new Act, a registered citizen of standing may claim a minor child — or a child of unregistered status — and bring them under lawful protection. Full registration. Papers. A name in the ledger that opens every door in this city that is currently closing in yours." He tapped the document once with one ink-stained finger. "You'd be a Quillan again. Legitimate. Protected. Employed — my employ, the *Argus*, a proper apprenticeship, wages, a future. In two years you'd be setting columns the grown men in that shop would weep over."

Tobin stared at the paper. The fog outside the window had thickened until the masts in the harbor were ghosts of themselves.

"And the price," Tobin said. Because there was always a price with his father; even love, if it existed, was set in type with margins specified.

"Renunciation." Ferris said the word easily, like naming a font. "You sever the association. Lantern Row, the Crane woman, all of them. You sign a declaration of no further dependence — it's a standard form, the solicitor has it drafted — and you never set foot in that house again. No letters. No visits. As far as the registry is concerned, you were never there. That's what keeps you clean, boy. Distance. Distance is the whole trick of respectability."

Tobin looked down at the petition. His name was already on it — *Tobin Quillan*, in his father's immaculate roman capitals, each letter placed like it had been locked into a chase. Eleven years old again, being handed his own name back like a returned parcel.

And God help him — God help him — for one long moment he wanted it.

He wanted it the way a cold hand wants a fire. He thought about the winter he'd slept in the print shop's paper store because his father had docked his supper for a misprint, and how he'd sworn he'd never be hungry again. He thought about the census taker's knock, the forms, the word *irregular* hanging in the air of Aunt Vesper's parlor like damp. He thought about the fortnight deadline ticking somewhere in the city like the master clock itself, and how tired he was, how young he was, how sixteen was too young to be illegal.

One signature. One clean sheet of cream bond paper. A bed that didn't sag. Supper every night. A trade, a wage, a name that opened doors.

"You'd register me," Tobin said slowly, "but you wouldn't feed them. Even Vesper. She's dying, Father."

"The Crane woman made her choices."

"She took me in off a wet street when you'd thrown me out of yours."

"And look what it's made you. A pamphleteer. A fugitive in your own parish." His father's voice dropped, went soft, went reasonable, which was always the worst sign. "Tobin. Listen to me. I'm offering you the only lifeboat off this wreck. Do you think those people can protect you? A dying aunt, a mad keeper, and that *thing* that fiddles with clocks?" He shook his head, almost pitying. "Charity cases, all of them. Charity cases cling. They'll drag you down with them when the registry comes, and it *will* come, and you'll stand in some queue with your cap in your hands wondering why you traded a future for a roof you didn't even own."

*Charity cases.*

The words went in clean, like a well-cut punch driven through soft lead, and Tobin felt them bite all the way down to where the true things lived.

Because that was it, wasn't it. That was the whole of it. His father didn't hate them because they were criminals or radicals or irregulars. He hated them because they were *charity cases* — because they had taken something in, and taking things in was weakness, and weakness was the one sin Ferris Quillan's whole religion forbade. Every kindness Tobin had been shown on Lantern Row — Aunt Vesper saving him the wing at supper even when her hands shook too badly to carve; Maren teaching him knots until his fingers stopped bleeding; Odile fixing his broken boot-heel at the kitchen table at midnight without one word, just the lamplight and the awl and the smell of wax — every single kindness was, in his father's arithmetic, a debt that degraded the debtor.

And Tobin understood, sitting there with the fog pressing at the window and his own name gleaming up at him off the cream paper, that he had been offered a lifeboat, yes. A lifeboat with one seat, and oars he'd have to row away from drowning people, and a captain who called the drowning *charity cases* while he poured himself another glass of wine he wouldn't drink.

He thought about the pamphlets. *A lantern for the uniform.* That's what the safe houses marked — a lantern lit openly, so the hunted could find them. His father's world had no lanterns. His father's world had lamps behind shuttered windows and locks on every door and a ledger where every soul was entered as an asset or a liability, and Tobin had lived in that ledger for eleven years, and he knew exactly what column he'd be filed under the first time he disappointed Ferris Quillan.

"Say it again," Tobin said.

"Say what?"

"The thing you called them. Say it again and watch my face while you do."

His father frowned — a compositor's frown, checking for a flaw in the type. "Charity cases, I said. It's not an insult, boy, it's a diagnosis—"

"It's an insult." Tobin stood up. The chair scraped. Somewhere in the public house a man laughed too loudly at a joke, and the fog swallowed it. "You're right about one thing, Father. Kerning on that woodblock was sloppy. Everything else—" he picked up the petition, held it a moment, feeling its weight, its expense, its terrible cleanliness, "—everything else is a misprint."

He tore it in half. Then in half again. The cream bond fell onto the table between the untouched wine and his father's ink-stained cuff, four neat quarters of a future he was refusing, and Tobin's hands were shaking but his voice, when it came out, was steady as a clock tick.

"I'm not a charity case. I'm family. There's a difference, and you'd know it if you'd ever taken anything in."

"Tobin—"

"Don't." He was already moving toward the door, coat collar up, heart slamming. "Don't write again. Don't send notes in papers. And Father—" he stopped, hand on the door ring, and gave his father the only inheritance he had left to give, the truest thing in him, "—when the registry comes for people like us, and it's coming, I hope someone lights a lantern you can find."

* * *

He walked home through the fog with the taste of wine he hadn't drunk sour in his mouth, and he cried the whole way along Rope Walk, silently, furiously, wiping his face on the sleeve of the coat with the patched elbow because Odile had told him to wear it and he'd worn it and he'd never been so glad of anything in his life.

By the time he reached Lantern Row the tears were done and the lie was ready — smooth, practiced, oiled like a pivot. *No luck at the rope-walk. Tried the chandler too. Maybe Thursday.*

He got the door open and there was the smell of supper and lamp-oil and Aunt Vesper's cough syrup, and Odile looked up from the kitchen table, loupe still screwed in one eye, and said:

"Rope-walk, was it?"

"That's right."

"Mm." Odile turned back to the escapement, tiny pliers poised, and said, mild as milk, "There's ink on your collar. Printer's ink. Black, high-resin — rope-walk doesn't use ink, and neither does the chandler."

Tobin stood in the doorway with his heart stopping.

"But you're home," Odile went on, and their voice gentled by one degree, which from Odile was a flood, "and supper's hot, and whatever it was — whoever it was — it'll keep till morning. Sit down, Tobin. Eat."

And Tobin, sixteen years old and freshly disinherited for the second time in his life, sat down at the table of the family he had just chosen twice — once by tearing paper, once by staying — and ate.

He did not say what he wouldn't say.

But upstairs, later, pulling off his boots in the dark, he heard Aunt Vesper coughing through the wall — long, wet, wrong — and remembered his father's other words, the ones that had landed truest: *when the registry comes, and it will come.* The deadline was nine days off. The ship was leaving sooner than anyone was saying aloud. And somewhere in the city, a man with his father's patience was paying attention to stray pamphlets and stray boys, and writing things down.

Nine days. Tobin lay in the dark and counted them like beats of a clock that might stop at any moment.
