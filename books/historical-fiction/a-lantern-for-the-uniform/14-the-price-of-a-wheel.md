## 14. The Price of a Wheel

The pawnshop on Cordwainer Street opened at eight, and Odile was there at ten past, standing under the sign with the three brass balls gone green at the seams, holding a canvas roll the weight of a sleeping cat.

They had counted the household's money twice the night before, by lamplight, while Vesper slept and Tobin dreamed whatever runaway boys dream and Maren kept her watch at the light. Eleven crowns, four shillings, and a tin of odd coppers. The escapement wheel — not the cracked one, that was beyond saving, but its replacement, cut true from hardened brass by the firm of Aldous & Pray, who were the only firm in Verlow who could cut it inside a fortnight — was quoted at thirty-eight crowns.

The arithmetic did not care about anyone's feelings. Thirty-eight minus eleven left twenty-seven, and twenty-seven crowns did not appear out of fog. The berth money — the provisioning fund Maren had been keeping in a tea caddy on the high shelf, against the day the ship sailed — held thirty-one crowns and change. It had been meant for salt pork and hard bread and a blanket for whoever went.

Odile had stood in the kitchen for a long time with the caddy in their hands, feeling the coins shift like small cold fish.

They had put it back. They had taken the canvas roll down from above their mother's old workbench instead.

* * *

Inside, the pawnshop smelled of dust and lamp oil and other people's emergencies. The broker was a narrow man named Fitch with spectacles thick as portholes, and he unwrapped the roll on his counter with the delicacy of a surgeon.

"Tools," he said. "Clockmaker's tools."

"My mother's."

He glanced up at that, then back down. There were eleven pieces. The fine pliers with the jaw worn to fit one particular grip. The staking set in its velvet grooves. The burnisher that had gone amber where a hand had held it for twenty years. The loupe with the cracked rim that Odile had been told never to touch and had touched anyway, every day since they were nine.

Fitch turned each piece over, held the pliers to the light, tested the spring of something small and terrible to part with.

"Twenty-six crowns," he said.

"They're worth more than that."

"They're worth what someone will pay me for them, and no one in Verlow is buying clockmaker's tools this month." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Twenty-eight, because they're clean and I'm feeling generous before breakfast."

Odile's throat did something complicated. They swallowed around it. "Thirty."

"Twenty-nine, and I'll hold them ninety days without interest, per the ordinance. You buy them back before the ticket expires, they're yours again."

Ninety days. The ship sailed in twelve days now, moved up ahead of the storm season. The wheel would take the better part of their fee from Halloway to pay off besides. Odile did the arithmetic again, the crueler version, and found it came out the same place: these tools were not coming home.

"Done," they said.

Fitch wrote the ticket. His pen scratched. Somewhere in the back of the shop a cuckoo clock, pawned by some other desperate soul, announced the quarter hour with a sound like a small bird being strangled.

"You'll want to count it," Fitch said, pushing the coins across.

"I trust you."

"Don't. Count it."

Odile counted it. Twenty-nine crowns in mixed silver, heavy and ugly. They folded it into their coat, took the ticket — *one lot clockmaker's tools, eleven pieces* — and walked out into the grey morning without looking at the counter again, because if they looked they would have to see the loupe sitting there under the lamp, and they were not certain their legs would carry them past it.

* * *

Aldous & Pray's workshop was two streets over, all noise and brass filings. Old Pray himself took the order, a broad woman with forearms like hawser rope, and she listened to Odile describe the wheel — teeth count, pitch, the crack pattern — with her head cocked like a bird hearing a worm under snow.

"Escapement wheel for the observatory master," she said. "You're the one they've got on it. The Crane kid."

"The Crane apprentice."

"That's what I said." She grinned through a beard of stubble. "Ten days. Thirty-four crowns, half now."

Odile paid seventeen. It left them eleven crowns and some shillings, which was more than nothing but less than anything, and they walked out of the workshop lighter by a mother and heavier by a promise, and stood on the step breathing the fish-and-coal air of the harbor and understanding, fully, what they had done.

No one had asked them to do it. That was the thing that sat wrong and right at once. Maren hadn't asked. Vesper didn't know the exact figure in the caddy. Tobin would have sold his own boots first, and then his teeth. If Odile had brought it to the table — *the wheel costs thirty-four, we have forty-two in the world if we spend the berth money* — there would have been a war, and the war would have ended with someone on that ship alone, and Odile could not stomach the shape of that sentence.

So they had decided alone. Which was, they supposed, what heads of households did, though no one had made them one and they wanted no such title. They had simply done the thing that kept the harbor bell ringing and the ships off the shoal, and paid for it with the only inheritance that was theirs alone to spend.

Their eyes were doing something inconvenient. They fixed them on the middle distance, on a gull hanging motionless over the fish market, until the inconvenience passed.

That was when they saw Maren Cole across the street.

She was standing outside the chandler's with a coil of tarred twine over one shoulder, and she was not pretending to shop. She was looking directly at Odile with the flat, patient look of a woman who had watched a thousand ships come in and knew exactly which ones were lying about their cargo.

How long had she been there? Long enough. Long enough to have seen them come out of Fitch's, or go in. Long enough, maybe, to have seen the canvas roll go onto the counter.

Odile's whole body prepared a lie. It was an old reflex, well-oiled, kept in good repair like everything else in their life: *I had business, I was pricing parts, Halloway advanced me funds.* The lie assembled itself behind their teeth, neat as a going train.

Maren crossed the street.

She stopped in front of them. She looked at their face, then at their empty hands, then at their face again. The gull screamed somewhere. A cart rattled past hauling ice.

"Maren," Odile said.

She said nothing.

She shifted the twine on her shoulder. She looked up Cordwainer Street toward the pawnshop, then back, and something moved through her face — Odile would spend years trying to name it; grief, maybe, or recognition, or the particular sorrow of a keeper watching a light go dark on schedule — and then it settled into nothing at all.

She did not ask. That was the astonishing thing. She stood there with every question available to her — *what did you sell, how much did you get, what about the caddy, what about the ship, what about your mother's memory, what gives you the right* — and she asked none of them. She just looked at Odile the way she must have looked at weather coming in off the water: taking its measure, deciding there was nothing to be done but walk home in it.

Then she turned, and nodded once down the hill toward Lantern Row, and started walking.

Odile stood on the step for three heartbeats. Then they followed.

* * *

They went down through the market together without a word between them. Maren bought nothing. Odile carried nothing. The city went on around them — fishwives crying the morning catch, a bell buoy clanking out on the water, two constables laughing at something outside the customs house — and the two of them moved through it side by side, an old woman and a person nobody's categories could hold, bound together by a silence so complete it had its own gravity.

It should have been unbearable. It was the opposite. Odile found, walking, that the silence was holding them up. Every step Maren took without asking was a step that said: *I see what you did, and I am not going to make you say it out loud, because saying it out loud would make it real, and you need it to stay a private grief or it will crush you.* Or maybe it said nothing at all and Odile was writing sermons on a blank page. But it felt like that. It felt like being carried.

At the corner of Lantern Row, where the houses leaned together like conspirators, Maren finally stopped.

She unslung the coil of twine, considered it, and held out a length of it, cut clean, about an arm's span.

"For the wheel," she said. "When it comes. You'll want to lash the crate properly or Pray's boys will shake the teeth loose in transit."

Her voice was rough as her hands. Odile took the twine.

"Maren—"

"Don't." Not harsh. Just final, like a door closing softly on a room where someone is sleeping. "Whatever you were going to say. Don't."

She looked at them a moment longer. Her eyes were the color of the harbor in February, and older than the lighthouse, and in them Odile saw — briefly, unmistakably — the same expression he'd seen in the maintenance log, in their mother's handwriting, next to a note about lubricating the great escapement: *Tell whoever follows me that the work matters more than the wage.*

Maren had known. All along, maybe. About the caddy. About the arithmetic. About what Odile would choose, given the choice, because they were their mother's child down to the marrow.

"Ninety days," Maren said quietly. "Fitch holds things ninety days. I've pawned enough in my life to know the ordinance."

Odile said nothing.

"A lot can happen in ninety days," Maren said. And she turned up the path to the house, leaving Odile standing on the cobbles with a length of tarred twine in their fist and no idea whether that had been comfort, prophecy, or a threat.

Behind the door of number six, Aunt Vesper coughed — that deep, wet, wrong-sounding cough that had been getting worse all week — and Tobin's voice rose through the window, bright and too loud, telling her about his day selling newspapers, a day Odile suddenly understood had been invented whole, the way Odile's own day had no honest account either.

Everyone in this house was lying to protect everyone else, Odile thought. Everyone was spending something they couldn't afford for someone who didn't know the price. And the storm charts on Maren's wall showed a low pressure system crawling up from the southwest, and the emigrant ship *Providence* sat in the outer roads with her berths filling, and somewhere in a drawer upstairs lay papers that said one name could leave and three had to stay.

Eleven days, now. The wheel would arrive in ten.

Odile climbed the steps, twine in hand, and let themselves in to the smell of bread and camphor and the sound of Tobin's beautiful lies, and began — silently, without permission, without applause — to clear the bench for the work that would save the harbor and cost them everything their mother had ever pressed into their hands.
