## 15. Storm Glass Falling

The storm glass on the mantel had been falling since Tuesday.

Odile noticed it first, because Odile noticed everything that measured anything. The liquid that should have sat clear as gin had gone cloudy overnight, then furred with feathery crystals, then clouded again — the glass crying wolf, then weeping in earnest. By Wednesday evening the crystals hung dense as frost ferns, and Maren Cole, who had read forty years of weather off the water before she ever learned to read it off a tube of camphor, took one look and said only: "Two days. Maybe three."

Nobody at Lantern Row said the other thing out loud. The other thing being: *the clock isn't ready.*

* * *

**Odile**

Odile climbed to the observatory before dawn on Thursday, taking the cliff path while the harbor lamps still burned yellow through a fog that was not fog at all but the leading edge of weather, a wet wool smell rolling in off the open sea. The wind had already turned. It came from the northeast now, where it never came from this time of year, and it carried a taste like iron filings.

Inside the clock room, Halloway was waiting under the gas lamp with his arms crossed, which meant he had slept badly or not at all.

"Report," he said.

Odile set down their tool roll and unrolled it with the care of someone laying out cards they already knew were bad. "Escapement's seated true. The new wheel runs within two seconds an hour — I've corrected for temperature drift with a bimetallic suspension, my mother's design." They did not say *my mother's design* lightly; saying it made Halloway's eyes do the thing they did, the flick toward the maintenance log, the flick away. "But the strike train is another matter. The lifting pins are worn past truing. I've shimmed them. A shim will hold in calm air."

"And in a storm?"

"In a storm the whole building moves. The tower sways — you know it does, you feel it in your teeth on windy nights. A shimmed pin that holds at rest will chatter under sway. If one lifts wrong—" Odile drew a small, precise gesture, a gear skipping a tooth, a mechanism eating itself. "It jams. And if it jams mid-strike, with the fly weights engaged—"

"I know what happens." Halloway looked old in the lamplight, older than he had three weeks ago, when Odile had first come up these stairs with their head down and their hands folded small. "The Council has asked me — formally asked me — whether the clock will run through the equinox gales. I told them yes. I need you to tell me I didn't lie."

Odile looked at the great case, at the pendulum swinging its slow silver arc, patient as a heartbeat. Two seconds an hour. Four minutes a day. The tide tables printed from its face would be off by four minutes by Sunday, six by Monday, and every pilot in Verlow knew what four minutes meant at the channel mouth when the water was running hard over the shoal.

"It will run," Odile said. "If nothing strikes it harder than the weather does."

Halloway almost smiled. "And if something does?"

"Then I'll be here to catch it."

They both heard what that meant. Neither of them said it either.

**Tobin**

Tobin came home Thursday night soaked to the collarbone and lying about it before he'd got his boots off.

"Printer's errand," he said, wringing his cap over the sink. "Mr. Quill wanted ink from the chandler's. Slipped on the pier steps, that's all."

Aunt Vesper looked at him from her chair by the fire with the particular expression she kept for Tobin's lies — fond, tired, and entirely unconvinced, like a woman watching a dog bury a bone in plain view. "You slipped on the pier steps," she said, "and the pier steps tore your sleeve, and the pier steps left printer's ink on your knuckles. Those are industrious steps."

Tobin shoved his hands behind his back. Under the ink there was a scrape too, actually — he'd gone over a wall on Cordwainer Street with a constable's whistle shrilling behind him, and the top of the wall had been topped with broken glass, and he'd left blood and half his sleeve on it along with forty pamphlets that were now drifting around Verlow like seditious autumn leaves. *THE REGISTER IS A ROPE,* the pamphlets said. *THEY MEASURE THE ROPE IN INCHES.* He'd been proud of that line. He was less proud of it now, standing in the warm kitchen smelling of stew, listening to Vesper breathe.

Because Vesper's breathing had changed. That was the thing nobody said. There was a whistle in it now, faint, at the top of each breath, like wind finding a gap in a window frame. She laughed the same, scolded the same, ruled the kitchen the same — but Tobin had grown up in a print shop where you learned to hear a press going wrong before it went wrong, the small change in rhythm that meant a gear was wearing, and Vesper's breath had that sound.

"You're doing pamphlets," Vesper said quietly, when Odile had gone up to bed and Maren was still at the lighthouse. "Don't insult me twice in one evening."

Tobin stood with his back against the sink. "Someone has to say it out loud. What they're doing. You can't just let them count us into boxes."

"I have let you do exactly that for a month, haven't I." She said it without heat. "I'm asking you to stop until after the deadline. One week, Tobin. After that, print whatever you like and I'll hold the ladder."

"Why after?"

Vesper looked into the fire. The whistle in her breath turned once, caught, cleared. "Because Pell is coming back," she said, "and because whatever he finds in this house next week will decide whether we keep it. I am asking you to give me one quiet week so that I can spend my last good argument on something other than your defense."

*My last good argument.* Tobin heard it. He wasn't sure she'd meant him to. His father's voice rose in him unbidden — *charity cases, gutter-kin, you'll end up exactly like them* — and for one ugly second he thought: *I could take the offer. Legitimate name, real bed, no whistling lungs, no deadline.* Then he looked at Vesper ruling the kitchen from her chair like a general who refused to leave the field, and the second passed, and he hated himself for having had it.

"One week," he said.

She nodded once, satisfied, and picked up her mending, and neither of them said anything else, and the storm glass on the mantel shed another fine feather of crystal into the cloudy dark.

**Maren**

Maren Cole climbed her own lighthouse stairs at midnight and found the barometer lower than she liked and the lamp burning steady, and she sat down with her logbook and did not write in it for a long time.

Instead she took out the berth papers.

They lived in an oilcloth packet inside the logbook, had lived there for twenty years, and she had shown them once and regretted showing them, because showing them had started a war she could not referee. *Transferable to a single name only.* Her mother's friend Lys Crane had bought that passage with money earned soldering clocks in a city that never once wrote her name correctly, and had died before she could use it, and had left it — to whom? That was the question tearing the house on Lantern Row down its seams. Vesper said Tobin: young, male on paper, able to start over somewhere the registration laws hadn't reached. Maren said Vesper: dying, and everyone knew it except Vesper, and a berth was a chance at dry air, at a doctor, at a year more. Nobody had said Odile. That silence had a shape Maren recognized and disliked — the shape of a household deciding, without deciding, that the one who held them all together was also the one who could best afford to lose.

Outside, the sea had changed its voice. Forty years of nights on this rock had taught her the vocabulary: the ordinary long exhale of swell, the mutter of a rising chop, and now underneath both a new note, deep and rhythmic, the sound of water organizing itself into something with intent. She marked the barometer — 28.9 and falling — and wrote in the log, in her square keeper's hand:

*NNE gale expected. Glass very low. All lights trimmed. Pray the clock holds.*

Then she sat a while longer, and admitted to herself the thing she had walked away from at the pawnshop without saying: she had seen Odile slide their mother's tools across the counter, seen the broker weigh thirty years of a dead woman's craft in his brass scales, seen Odile sign the ticket with a steady hand and walk out into the rain with empty pockets and straight shoulders. Provisioning money. Berth money. Gone into an escapement wheel.

Maren closed the logbook. She was willing to lose the argument, she decided. She was willing to lose the berth, even — the sea had taken things from her all her life and she had a professional relationship with loss. What she was not willing to lose was the house on Lantern Row, and the people in it, scattered to four winds by a piece of paper with one name-line on it.

There had to be a fifth thing. Some answer that wasn't one name on a line. She fell asleep in the watch chair trying to build it, gear by gear, the way a clockmaker would.

**Pell**

Ambrose Pell worked late at the census office because his lodgings had become unbearable — the landlady had begun asking him questions, cheerful ones, about his work, and he had discovered he could not answer them without hearing how they sounded.

He spread his files under the office lamp. Crane household, Lantern Row. Four occupants, three surnames, no lawful relation among them, and a head-of-household line that had been left blank on the preliminary form with a boldness he was beginning to suspect was not evasion at all but a kind of argument. Beside it, the old register, twenty years faded: *Lys Crane, clockmaker, no kin* — flagged in red, then closed, the closure annotated with initials he had finally traced to a records clerk long dead. Someone had protected this family before. Someone had looked at the same forms Ambrose was looking at and chosen to shut the file instead of opening the case.

His supervisor's memorandum lay on top of everything: *Irregular households to be prioritized. Deadline firm. Penalties per Schedule C.* Schedule C included conscription of unregistered males between fifteen and thirty, eviction of households failing to designate a lawful head, and — he checked it again, because he kept hoping he'd misread it — referral of persons "of indeterminate registry status" to the municipal physician for classification.

Classification. As though a person were a moth.

Ambrose took out the storm report the harbormaster had circulated that afternoon — gale warning, all shipping advised, emigrant departure advanced — and beneath it the shipping notice he had cut out and told himself he was keeping for administrative reasons: *LAST SAILING OF THE SEASON. DEPARTS NOW DAWN, SATURDAY NEXT, WEATHER PERMITTING.*

Saturday next. Not in two weeks. In nine days. Whatever the household on Lantern Row intended to do with whatever they intended to do, the window for doing it had just slammed most of the way shut.

He thought about the apprentice clockmaker's hands on the doorstep — ink-stained, grease-stained, steady. He thought about the phrase he had written down twice in his notebook without meaning to, the phrase that had been sitting in his chest for a fortnight like a swallowed hook: *with kin of choice.* Odile Crane had said it once, offhand, explaining why the household's relations column couldn't be filled in. Kin of choice. As though kinship were something a person built rather than something a person was sentenced to.

Ambrose looked at Schedule C for a long time. Then he took a fresh sheet of paper and began, slowly, drafting a memorandum of his own — not to his supervisor, but to himself, an argument in numbered points, the kind of document a man writes when he is building the courage to disobey and needs to pretend it's procedure.

Point one: the Act provides, in Section 11, subsection 4, a mutual-dependency provision. He had found it buried under forty years of amendments. Households demonstrating shared economy, shared dwelling, and sworn interdependence might be certified as a single registered unit notwithstanding absence of blood or marriage. It had been written for shipwrecked crews and theatrical companies. It had never once been used in Verlow.

It could be used.

Point two: it required a head of household to sign publicly. By name. On the record, in the ledger, exposed to every consequence the city could devise.

Point three: he could not ask that of anyone. He could only make sure that when they chose it — if they chose it — the law was ready to hold them up instead of break them.

Rain began against the office windows, soft at first, then in ropes. Ambrose blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark listening to the weather arrive, and thought: *nine days.*

**Vesper**

Vesper Crane woke at three in the morning with the wind already worrying the chimney and knew, in the way of the ill, that her arithmetic had run out.

She had been managing her body the way she managed a household budget — stretching, deferring, hiding the worst entries in the back pages. But the whistle in her breath had become a rattle, and the rattle had opinions, and when she tried to cross the cold floor to the window her legs submitted a formal complaint. She stood at the glass anyway, one hand on the sill, and watched the harbor.

The storm glass in the shop window across the lane had gone nearly solid, white as packed snow. Beyond it the masts swung in their black rows, and far out at the harbor mouth the lighthouse turned its patient eye through sheets of moving salt.

Nine days. She had heard Tobin repeat the shipping notice at supper like a boy reading a death sentence. Nine days, and one berth, and four names, and she had spent a month believing that love meant choosing — pointing at Tobin and saying *him, save him* — and tonight, watching the light sweep and sweep, she understood suddenly that she had been wrong. Choosing one was just losing three in an orderly fashion. It was surrender with good manners.

What Odile had done with the tools — Maren had told her, of course, Maren who said nothing at the pawnshop and everything afterward — that was the shape of the answer. Not choosing. Spending. Giving the inheritance away to buy the thing that kept the rest of them alive, and calling it repair work.

Vesper's breath rattled. She gripped the sill and breathed through it, furious and clear-eyed, and made herself a promise with the wind screaming approval: she would live to see the register signed. Whether her name was on it or not, she would stand in that room and watch her family become legal — become *real*, in the eyes of a city that had spent years insisting they weren't — and after that, the rest could take her whenever it liked.

Below, in the workshop, she could hear the small sounds of Odile working past midnight — the tick of calipers, the sigh of a file. Building the clock that had to survive the storm. Building it with a dead woman's tools sold by the ounce.

Vesper went back to bed and lay listening to the two sounds together, the wind and the filing, and could not tell which one frightened her more, or which one she believed in more.

By morning the rain had turned horizontal, the harbor pilots had doubled their watches, and the notice board outside the shipping office bore a fresh placard, paste still wet, letters leaning in the gusts:

*SATURDAY SAILING ADVANCED TO FRIDAY DAWN, WEATHER PERMITTING. NO FURTHER NOTICE WILL BE GIVEN.*

Eight days. Maybe fewer. The storm glass had fallen as low as it could go, and at the observatory, high above the whitening water, the master clock struck seven — clean, clean, then a hitch, a shiver, a single note hanging wrong in the gale —

and Odile, halfway up the cliff path with the rain like flung gravel in their face, heard it, and ran.
