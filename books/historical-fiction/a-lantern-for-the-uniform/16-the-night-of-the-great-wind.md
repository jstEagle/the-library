## 16. The Night of the Great Wind

The observatory door fought them like a living thing. Odile got a shoulder against it, braced both boots on the stone sill, and heaved; it gave all at once and they stumbled through into the dark of the stair hall, rain sheeting off their oilskins in ropes.

"Halloway!" Their voice was swallowed. The building itself had begun to groan — not the wind outside but the structure inside, iron and oak flexing against one another like an old man's joints before weather. "Halloway! It's me!"

A lantern swung around the corner of the stairwell and Halloway's face appeared above it, grey as the sea below. He looked ten years older than he had that morning. He looked like he had been arguing with God and losing.

"It hitched," Odile said. "At seven. I heard it from the cliff path."

"I know." He said it flatly, without blame, which frightened Odile more than blame would have. Halloway blamed everyone for everything; it was how he kept the world at arm's length. A quiet Halloway was a Halloway who had already done his arithmetic. "It struck seven clean enough to be heard across the harbor, and then it struck again — a half-strike, a catch — and stopped. The bell hasn't moved since."

"Where were you when it caught?"

"In my rooms." He lifted the lantern higher, and Odile saw that his hands were shaking. "I'm not going to pretend I understand what you do in there, Crane. I'm going to stand here with this lamp and hold it steady, and you're going to tell me if there's anything else in this world I can do besides that."

Odile looked at him for a long second. Then: "Keep the lamp away from the case. Dry air. And if anyone comes up those stairs tonight, don't let them touch anything."

They went up.

* * *

The clock room was cold in a way rooms shouldn't be cold, as though the storm had reached in through the stones and set up housekeeping. The great case stood against the seaward wall, taller than two men, its glass panel dark, its pendulum hanging dead still for the first time in Odile's memory. Silence in that room was not silence. It was a held breath.

They opened the panel and lit their work lamp, shielding the flame with their body, and looked.

The strike train was the problem. Of course it was. Going and striking shared a barrel but not a temperament; the going side could tolerate a hundred small sins, but the strike train demanded everything be exactly right or it seized like a fist. Odile traced the fault by lamplight, hands steady now, hands always steadier once the work began. The lifting piece on the third wheel had ridden up over its pin instead of under — a half-millimeter of error, accumulated over weeks of wear, of salt air, of a clock running on parts that had been mended too many times by too many hands. When the hammer lever dropped, the lifting piece had jammed against the pin shoulder instead of clearing it. Everything downstream had locked. The whole train hung suspended mid-fall, wound tight as a held scream, waiting only for someone to release it wrong so it could tear itself apart.

Or waiting for someone to release it right.

Odile sat back on their heels. Their heart was going hard, and they made themselves look at it plainly, the way they'd learned to look at everything: *the train is armed. If I free that gear while the weights are driving it, it will complete its fall at full speed, and anything between the wheels will go through the gap in my fingers, or take the fingers.* There was no way to let the power down gently. There was no brake on the strike train. There never had been.

There was only the way their mother had written about it.

They didn't have the log here — it lived wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboard at Lantern Row, where it had lived since the night they found it inside the case — but they had spent twenty years with those pages the way other people spent twenty years with a psalter. They could see the entries behind their eyes, in her slanted, unadorned hand:

*Third wheel lifting piece rides high in damp. Cleared it by hand, train live. Do not pull the lever. Do not stop the fall. Go in below the hammer cam and give the wheel room to finish. It wants to finish. Clocks always want to finish. That is the whole secret of them.*

And beneath it, dated four days later, in the same hand:

*Cleared it again. Hands shook the whole time. Write it down anyway so the next fool knows shaking is allowed.*

Odile laughed — one breath, no sound, gone into the wind-noise. Allowed. She had given them permission across thirty years and a grave.

"All right," they said aloud, to the clock, to her, to the room. "I hear you."

* * *

They prepared the way she would have. Lamp set high and secure, throwing light down into the works without casting their own shadow across the wheels — because a shadow moving across a live escapement can trick the eye at the worst moment. Sleeves rolled past the elbow and pinned. Rings — Tobin's cheap tin ring, worn on a cord since the boy couldn't keep it on his fingers — tucked away. They chalked their palms, as she had written, *chalk dries sweat and shows blood*, and the second part of that sentence they declined to think about further.

Then they opened the lower access, the narrow one behind the pendulum, the one built for a hand and forearm and nothing larger, and understood — fully, finally, in the body and not the mind — why she had written *by hand*. There was no tool that could reach the third wheel's underside. No screwdriver, no hook, no clever bent wire. Only fingers, feeling blind along oiled steel in the dark of the case, finding the lifting piece and easing it back down over its pin while forty pounds of falling weight pressed at the other end of the train like a tide against a door.

The wind chose that moment to hit the tower broadside.

The whole observatory leaned — not visibly, nothing so honest as visible — but Odile felt it in their sternum, the stone itself flexing, and somewhere above them the weathervane shrieked around its spindle, and the lamp flame lay flat and recovered, and lay flat again. Rain came off the glass in fists. Below, faint through everything, the harbor bell buoy clanged its blind alarm out in the channel, over and over, a voice calling into white darkness: *here, here, here* — except the pilots couldn't trust it tonight, couldn't trust any of it, because the master clock was dead and the tide tables it governed were guesses now, and somewhere out in that murk ships were making decisions based on nothing.

Odile put their arm into the clock.

Cold first — the brass drank their warmth instantly — then the slick familiarity of old oil, the fine grit of chalk dust and time. They felt their way past the hammer cam, past the great slow teeth of the fourth wheel, up into the throat of the train. And there it was: the lifting piece, canted up hard against the pin shoulder, holding the entire mechanism in its locked and screaming stillness.

They got two fingertips under its edge.

The weight came on immediately — not sudden, but vast, patient, impersonal, the way water is patient. Forty pounds translated through three wheels into a pressure on their fingertips that wanted, very simply, to continue. Odile breathed out, long and slow, the way Maren had taught them to breathe when hauling line in weather — *out is stronger than in, always* — and eased the piece downward, a hair's width, another, guiding it back toward the track it had abandoned.

Their hand began to shake.

*Shaking is allowed.*

They kept going. Sweat ran into their eyes and they could not wipe it. The chalk squeaked faintly against the brass. Somewhere behind them Halloway's breathing had gone ragged, and Odile realized distantly that the director had come to the doorway despite being told to stay back, that he was watching, that it didn't matter, that nothing existed in the world just now except a half-millimeter of steel and the fingers moving it.

The lifting piece slid to the lip of its track. One more width of a hair. The weight leaned harder, testing them, asking whether they truly meant to let it finish.

*It wants to finish,* she had written. *That is the whole secret of them.*

Odile gave it the last hair's breadth — and took their hand out fast, fast, faster than thought —

— and the clock finished.

The strike train fell through its arc with a rush and a crash like surf, the wheels spinning true, the hammer swinging up and meeting the bell with a note that went through the floor, through the stone, through Odile's chest and out the other side. Once. Twice. Three times. It wasn't the hour; the hour meant nothing now; the count was wrong and glorious, eight strokes ringing into the gale at whatever hour it pleased, because the clock was alive and counting, and counting was all it had ever promised anybody.

Halloway made a sound behind them that might have been a prayer in a language he didn't speak.

Odile sat down on the floor, back against the case, and put their bleeding, chalk-white hand between their knees, and laughed until it turned into something else, and let it.

* * *

They did not go home.

They knew, sitting on the cold flags with their hand wrapped in a rag, that they should — Lantern Row was a mile down the cliff path, and Vesper was alone with Tobin, and the storm was still building, the barometer still falling, the glass reading nothing left to fall to. But the clock had run eleven minutes and hitched again — a smaller catch, deeper in, the kind of fault that came in families the way tempers did — and Odile understood that this was the night. Not Saturday, not Friday dawn. Tonight the clock would either be made whole or die whole, and every hour of it belonged here, in this room, with these gears.

So they sent Halloway home with orders to sleep and a promise extracted twice over that he would return at first light, and they worked.

They worked the way their mother's log taught without ever meaning to teach it: entry after entry, fault after fault, each one written down plain, each failure recorded beside its fix, *write it down anyway so the next fool knows*. They re-seated the third wheel properly and filed the pin shoulder a whisper smoother. They found play in the second arbor and shimmed it with a sliver of brass cut from the edge of a coin. They discovered the real root of everything, near midnight, deep in the going train: a crack, hairline, running through the body of the great wheel itself — the same wheel, Odile realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, that appeared in her final entries. *Great wheel showing hairline at spoke root. Will bear watching. Do not let them patch it with solder; solder hides cracks and tells lies.*

No one had watched. Someone had soldered.

They couldn't replace the wheel tonight. They could bind it — a steel collar, machined down from stock Halloway kept for exactly such emergencies, fitted hot and shrunk cold, clamping the crack the way a splint clamps a bone. It would hold for years if it held through tonight. Odile heated the collar over the work lamp's flame, swore at the draught, heated it again, and set it with their bare thumbs, and felt it grip as it cooled, tightening, tightening, the metal taking hold of its own small vow.

Outside, the wind passed through some invisible boundary and became something with intent.

* * *

Toward the small hours, with the clock running — actually running, going and strike together, the pendulum swinging its long slow metronome of *not yet, not yet, not yet* — Odile climbed the tower stair to the lamp room, because someone should see the sea, and because Maren Cole had spent forty years teaching them that a keeper looks even when looking costs.

The gallery door opened a hand's width and tried to tear itself from their grip. They got it wide, got the storm shutter braced, and stood in the lee of the parapet with the gale trying to peel them off the tower like a label.

The harbor was gone. Not dark — gone. There was no horizon, no water, no lights of the fishing fleet; there was only a single lurching chaos in which the light of the lamp revolved and revealed, for one flash each revolution, a wall of sea standing where no sea should stand. The buoy bell was still sounding, farther away than it should be, dragged off station or half-drowned. And once — Odile would spend the rest of their life certain of it — once, between revolutions, they saw a ship's riding light, low in the trough, moving wrong, moving toward the shoal, and then the light turned and the wind screamed and the sea closed over the place where it had been, and there was no way in the world to know.

They stood until the cold won, then went back down and wrote it in the night book, the way Maren had drilled into them since childhood: *what was seen, what time, what bearing, no conclusions.* Let the morning sort it out. Let the pilots who doubled their watches earn their double pay.

The clock ticked behind them, steady as conscience.

* * *

Grey came late and sideways, the dawn arriving less as light than as a gradual admission that the night hadn't killed them. The storm still blew, but it had lost its intent; it was retreating now, sullen, taking the sea with it in stages. Odile sat on the floor of the clock room, back against the warm case, eating a heel of bread Halloway had produced from nowhere, watching the pendulum swing.

The clock had survived. Through the worst hours, through gusts that had shaken grit down from the vaulted ceiling, through a pressure change that had made every joint in the building weep, it had gone on striking the hours — true hours now, corrected against Odile's watch, against the stars when the clouds briefly tore. The harbor would have its tides today. The pilots would have their tables. Whatever had happened out in that darkness, the city's heartbeat had not stopped.

Odile looked at their hands. Chalk in every line, blood at two knuckles, a bruise coming up along the forearm where the access frame had caught them. Their mother's hands, they thought. The same hands, doing the same work, in the same room, sixty years apart, and neither of them ever once asked whether the work was women's work or men's work or anything's work. It was clock work. It was theirs.

There was a noise on the stair — boots, two sets, hurrying. Halloway first, lantern high, and behind him a shape Odile knew from a thousand mornings on Lantern Row, a shape that had no business being upright on a cliff path in the aftermath of the Great Wind.

Tobin. Soaked through, hatless, ink still on his fingers from something printed in the night, his face carrying an expression Odile had never seen on him before and never wanted to see again.

"They've taken the pamphlet presses," Tobin said, before he was even fully through the door, breathless, words tumbling. "This morning, at the harbor — Pell was there, Pell stood them down, Odile, he stood between them and the printers, I saw it — but that's not—" He stopped. Made himself start again, slower, and his voice broke on the second try. "Vesper. Aunt Vesper collapsed an hour ago. Maren's with her. She keeps asking for you, and she keeps saying—" His eyes went to the clock, to the pendulum still swinging its faithful arc, and something in him flared, young and furious and grieving all at once. "She keeps saying the berth. She says you have to decide about the berth *now*, before the ship sails, and Odile — the ship sails at dawn tomorrow. Friday dawn. There's no time left to not decide."

The pendulum swung. *Not yet, not yet, not yet.*

Except, Odile thought, rising, taking the lamp, taking the stairs two at a time — except now. Now was exactly what there was.
