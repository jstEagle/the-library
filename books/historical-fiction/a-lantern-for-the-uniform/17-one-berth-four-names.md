## 17. One Berth, Four Names

### Vesper

The storm had been at the shutters since midnight, and Vesper Crane had been awake to hear every hour of it, because dying teaches you that sleep is a country you may not be permitted to enter again.

She lay in her bed under three blankets and listened to the wind worry the house like a dog with a bone, and she thought: *the ship leaves at dawn.* She thought it the way one thinks of a train schedule in another language — knowing the words, unable to feel their weight.

Downstairs, the lamp still burned. She could see its glow under the door, and hear the low murmur of voices that had been going on for two hours without once rising above a murmur, which was worse than shouting. Shouting meant someone was winning.

She got up. It took a while. It took the bedpost and the washstand and the wall and a long pause at the top of the stairs with her hand pressed flat against the cold plaster, gathering herself the way you gather spilled grain. But she had buried a husband and outlived two doctors' predictions and raised a child who answered to no category the census takers could print, and she would be damned by no God worth serving if she let this argument happen below stairs without her.

They were all four in the kitchen when she came down: Odile standing at the window with their arms crossed, Tobin perched on the dresser like a starling that hadn't decided whether to flee, Maren Cole seated with her hands around a cold cup of tea, and on the table, square and awful in the lamplight, the berth deed.

Vesper took the last stair slowly so they would hear her coming and have time to arrange their faces. They did not manage it. Good. She didn't want them managing anything tonight.

"Well," she said, "I can see from the geometry of this room that nobody has agreed to anything."

"Vesper—" Maren began.

"Sit me down and pour me something hot, and then we will settle this the way families settle things, which is badly but finally."

Odile pulled out the chair. Odile always pulled out the chair. They had been doing it since they were nine years old and small enough that the chair barely noticed, and Vesper thought, not for the first time, that she had spent thirty years teaching them to be invisible when what she should have been teaching them was how loudly to take up space.

She sat. The tea came. The deed lay where it lay.

"The berth goes to Tobin," she said. "That is my word as head of this house, such as the law allows it to be."

Tobin's head came up like a struck match. "No."

"You are sixteen years old—"

"Seventeen in March."

"—you are seventeen years old, you have a whole life folded up in you like a letter that hasn't been opened, and there is a ship at dawn that will carry you somewhere the Registration Act has never heard of. Somewhere with printers who need apprentices and don't ask for papers. You will take the berth."

"I said *no*." He was off the dresser now, standing, and his voice cracked straight down the middle the way boys' voices do, half of it a man's and half of it a child's, and both halves were shaking. "You're dying. You're all pretending you're not, but I do the washing, I've seen the — I know." His eyes went to Maren, to Odile, back to Vesper. "You want to give me your life and call it love. It isn't. It's just dying with extra steps."

The wind hit the house so hard the lamp flame lay flat. Nobody spoke until it passed.

"Tobin," Vesper said quietly, "I have watched you come through that door every evening for two years with ink to your elbows and lies on your tongue about where you've been, and every time, my heart did the same thing it did when Odile was small and came home having been called something cruel in the street. It said: *not this one too. Not this one too.* You are mine, boy. Not by blood. Better than blood. And mine go first through doors."

"Then we all go," he said. "There's four names. There's one berth. Somebody does the arithmetic and tells me how that works."

Nobody did the arithmetic. That was the whole trouble.

* * *

### Tobin

Later — after Vesper had gone back up the stairs leaning on Odile's arm, after the lamp had been turned down to its last inch — Tobin sat on the cold step of the back porch with his knees drawn up and let the rain find the gaps in his collar.

He had run from a print shop once already. He knew exactly what a berth looked like from the inside: the smell of the hold, the way the water sounds under the floorboards, the particular loneliness of a bunk that belongs to somebody whose name isn't yours. He'd slept in worse. That wasn't the problem.

The problem was the door behind him.

It was a bad door. It stuck in damp weather and Aunt Vesper kept meaning to plane it and never did, and the hinge sang a note when it opened that Tobin could identify blindfolded, and there was a lantern bracket beside it that was empty right now because — because. Because of everything that had happened, because of Pell and the forms and the fortnight that had collapsed into hours. And Tobin sat on its step in the rain and understood with terrible clarity that if he walked down to the harbor at dawn and climbed aboard that ship, he would spend the rest of his life hearing that hinge sing in his sleep.

His father had offered him a door too, three weeks ago. A better door, on paper. A legal name, a bed, a place in a shop that smelled of nothing but money. All it cost was renouncing "that charity house and its collection of freaks." His father had actually said *freaks*, over the pastry he'd bought as though pastry could buy a son.

Tobin had walked out of that room and kept walking and ended up, without deciding to, at Lantern Row, and Odile had opened the sticking door and said nothing at all, which was the most anyone had ever said to him.

So. No.

But saying no to a berth was easier than watching Vesper die in a city that wanted them all counted and catalogued, and Tobin found he couldn't hold both truths in his chest at once, and the storm didn't care which one he dropped.

The door opened behind him — the hinge sang — and Maren came out with an oilskin over her shoulders and put it around his instead.

"She'll outlive us all out of pure spite," Maren said. "Come inside before you rust."

"She shouldn't get to decide," Tobin said. "None of us should get to decide for the others. That's the trap. That's exactly the trap the law set — it wants us to be one person's dependents so it knows whose signature owns us. And here we are, doing it to each other for free."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of the oilskin hood between them.

"When did you get wise?" she said.

"Pamphlets," Tobin said. "You read enough of your own arguments, you start believing them."

Inside, Odile was sitting at the table with the deed in front of them, turning it over and over, and Tobin saw their face and understood that whatever came next would come from Odile, and braced himself.

* * *

### Maren

Maren Cole had kept the light at Gannet Head for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one thing above all others: you cannot argue with weather. You can only batten down, keep your flame clean, and be still there when it passes.

The berth deed was weather.

She had known about it for twenty years — had helped Elowen Crane hide those papers inside a hollowed-out tide log the week before Elowen died, had sworn an oath over them with salt on her tongue. *One passage,* Elowen had said. *For whoever needs it most, when the needing comes. They'll know.* And Maren had carried the secret the way she carried everything, steadily, alone, and now the needing had come all at once, for everyone, and Elowen's clever kindness had turned into a knife the family was passing around trying not to cut themselves.

They wanted her to take it. That was the shape the argument had settled into by the small hours — Vesper for Tobin, Tobin for Vesper, and both of them, in the pauses, looking at Maren, because Maren was old and alone and had no trade the new laws honored.

"No," she said, before anyone could arrange it into words.

"You haven't heard—"

"I've heard it building for a fortnight." She stood. Her knees complained; she ignored them; thirty-one years of stairs taught you which complaints mattered. "I am seventy-one years old. I have a lighthouse. Do you understand? I have a *light*. There is a lamp up on Gannet Head that I have not let go dark for one single night in thirty-one years, through gales and griefs and two governments, and if you think I will board a ship and sail away from it while some deputy with a certificate learns which way to trim the wick, then none of you have been listening to me at all, ever."

The kitchen was very quiet. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

"My post is here," she said. "My dead are here — Elowen is under the turf at Saint Brannoc's and I have walked out to her every Sunday for twenty years and I will walk out to her every Sunday until it's my turn. And more than that—" her voice caught, and she let it, because she was too old to waste time smoothing herself over— "more than that, I am *registered*. Have been since before any of this nonsense. Keeper of Gannet Light, occupation lawful, person accounted for. They cannot touch me. The Act is aimed at people who don't fit their columns. I fit. I fit so well it makes me sick, but I fit, and that means my job in this family is not to run. My job is to stand in the open where they can see me and say: this household is mine, these people are mine, and I am known to the law, and you will account for me."

Tobin was staring at her. Odile's hands had stopped moving on the deed.

"And the berth?" Vesper's voice came thin from the doorway. She had come back down; of course she had; the woman could no more stay in bed during a crisis than the tide could skip a turn. "Someone must sail, Maren. Someone must live past this winter."

Maren looked at the deed. Elowen's handwriting on the transfer line, faded brown. *To whomsoever most needs.*

"Elowen meant it for escape," she said slowly. "Twenty years ago, escape was the answer. But I watched that woman work, Vesper. I watched her mend clocks that other folk said were beyond saving. She never once threw away a mechanism that could be repaired. She'd have hated this — hated that we're spending her last gift on running away instead of holding the works together." She turned to Odile. "Your mother believed things could be mended. Ask yourself what she'd do with that paper now."

Odile looked up.

And Maren, who had spent her life reading weather off water, saw the change come over their face like a front moving in — grief and resolve arriving together, the barometer falling fast — and knew the answer was already formed.

* * *

### Odile

Odile had been silent for three hours, which was nothing new; silence was their native country. But this silence had weight in it, and they felt the moment arrive when keeping it became its own kind of lie.

They laid the deed flat on the table and put both hands on it, the way they'd put hands on a clock case to feel what the mechanism was telling them.

"I've been listening to us argue," they said, "and every argument is the same argument. Vesper gives it away because she thinks her life weighs least. Tobin refuses because he thinks ours weigh more. Maren won't leave the light. Everyone is measuring themselves and finding themselves the one who ought to be spent." They looked around the table. "Do you hear it? We're doing the census taker's arithmetic for him. One berth, four names — one of us counts, three of us don't. That's his sum. I won't carry it."

"Odile—" Vesper started.

"*No one goes alone.*" Their voice cracked on it and they let it crack. "That's the whole thing. That's everything I've worked out in twenty-nine years. If Tobin sails, he sails alone into a country where he doesn't know a soul, carrying our deaths in his pocket like ballast. If Vesper sails, she dies in a foreign port among strangers while we stand on a dock here growing old with the guilt. If any one of us takes that berth, the rest of us spend our lives being the ones who stayed, and every meal, every morning, every strike of that clock will have a hole in it shaped like the one who left. I have been the hole-shaped absence in other people's lives. I know what it does."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere out in the dark the harbor bell swung wild in the gusts, counting hours no ship could trust.

"So the answer is no one," Odile said. "No one goes. We tear it up or we frame it or we bury it with Mother, but no one boards that ship. We stay. All four. In this house, on this street, in this city that wants to number us — and tomorrow, when the deadline comes, we do not hide and we do not run. We sign. Whatever it costs."

"That could mean conscription for Tobin. Eviction for the rest—" Maren began.

"It could. Or it could mean a register that says, in official ink, that we exist. That this household is real. I'm done being real only to the people in this kitchen. I'm done with invisibility. It kept us alive and it kept us small and I am finished with it." They pushed the deed to the middle of the table, face-up, the blank transfer line catching the lamplight. "Mother left it for whoever needed it most. What we need most isn't passage. It's each other, whole."

Tobin made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. Vesper had both hands pressed to her mouth. And Maren reached across and rested her weathered fingers on the paper, and then Tobin's ink-stained ones beside hers, and then Vesper's trembling ones on top, and Odile put theirs last, four hands stacked on one berth like four planks nailed over a door —

— and that was when the knock came.

Four sharp raps at the front door, unhurried, unmistakable, at half past three in the morning with a storm screaming off the harbor. The kind of knock that did not belong to neighbors.

Everyone's hands went still on the deed.

Through the dark window, past the streaming glass, a figure stood in the lane with a lantern held high against the wind — and even before the light steadied enough to show the bowler hat and the leather satchel, Odile knew.

Ambrose Pell had come at the end of the world's worst night, and the deadline was dawn, and there was nowhere left to hide — which was, Odile realized with a strange calm settling over them, exactly what they had just finished saying they no longer wished to do.

Maren blew out the lamp.
