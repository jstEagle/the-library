## 18. Signature

Ambrose Pell had been a census taker for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one truth about doorsteps: people lied on them. Not always — not even mostly — but the threshold did something to them. It turned the honest evasive and the frightened bold. He had watched widows swear they lived alone with two children peering through the curtains behind them. He had watched men claim kinship with boarders they'd known a fortnight. The doorstep was where the law met the household, and the household almost always flinched.

He climbed Lantern Row that morning expecting a flinch.

The storm had passed in the night, hauling itself out over the harbor around three o'clock, but it had left its signature everywhere — shutters hanging by one nail, a fishing smack driven up onto the cobbles two streets down, gulls walking the gutters like men hungover from some municipal drunk. Ambrose's boots squelched. His satchel, holding the register, the forms, the stiff blue folder of penalties, felt heavier than usual, though he had not added a page to it in days.

That was the trouble. He had stopped adding pages.

He had spent the storm night — he would admit this to no supervisor, no magistrate, no God — standing at his own window with the shutter cracked, listening to the wind try to peel the roof off Verlow, and thinking about a clockmaker who was neither man nor woman and answered to neither, and about an old woman's voice through a door, quick as a card sharp: *relations, Mr. Pell? Why, we are related by breakfast.*

He had written things down twice at that first visit. He remembered it now with a peculiar shame, the way one remembers cheating at cards against a child. *Occupant four: Cole, Maren. Occupation: keeper of lights.* And beneath it, smaller, in the margin where margins were meant for nothing: *no lawful relation among any of them. None.*

The Registration Act of 1893 required every household in the port district to declare a head, a set of relations or tenancies, and the "nature of each occupant" — a phrase whose vagueness was its function. Ambrose had explained this to trainees himself, back when he'd believed explaining was the same as believing. *Nature,* he used to say, *means what they are to the house.* But everyone in Verlow knew what nature meant now. It meant which side of the categories you fell on. It meant names that could be matched against conscription rolls and workhouse ledgers and the shipping manifests of ships that were not coming back.

Four occupants. Three surnames. No relation the law could see.

His supervisor, Mr. Quill — a man built like a filing cabinet and about as warm — had underlined the Crane file twice in red and left it on Ambrose's desk with a note: *Irregulars cluster. Deadline is the 14th. Certify or refer. No third option.*

There was always a third option. That was what nobody told you about the law. The law was full of doors that only opened if somebody stood in front of them long enough and refused to stop knocking. Ambrose had found one such door eleven years ago, in his second month of service, when an old widow named Petra Ilse had registered her dead sister as living because she could not bear to write *alone* in the column marked HOUSEHOLD COMPOSITION, and Ambrose had certified it because — he had told himself — grief made clerical errors, and mercy made worse ones, and between the two he chose the error he could sleep beside. He had never told anyone. He carried it the way other men carried a bad tooth: privately, and with a certain tenderness.

Today he would have to be either a clerk or a coward, and he no longer knew which was which.

* * *

Lantern Row looked different after a storm. Softer, somehow, the way faces look different after crying. A gutter had come loose along Vesper Crane's eaves and hung down like a dropped arm. Someone had already begun sweeping the walk — he could see the broom leaning against the step, still wet.

He knocked. He heard, from inside, a chair scrape, footsteps — more than one set — and then silence, the particular held-breath silence of a household deciding what to be when the door opens.

Odile Crane opened it.

They were ink-stained to the elbow. There was a smear of brass polish along one jawbone like war paint applied by accident, and their eyes had the flat shine of someone who had not slept, which was true of half the city this morning. Behind them, dimly, Ambrose could see the parlor: a table set with four cups, an old woman wrapped in blankets in a chair by the fire, a lighthouse keeper's oilskin coat dripping on its peg, a boy — Tobin, the printer's devil — hovering near the stairs with the look of a cat deciding whether to bolt.

"Mr. Pell," Odile said. Not a greeting. An identification. As if cataloguing him.

"Ambrose Pell, census office." He said it out of habit and then hated the sound of it, the officious little drumroll of it. He held up the register. "The fourteenth. I've come for your declaration."

"I know what day it is."

"I imagine you do." He cleared his throat. The speech he had prepared — the one about penalties and cooperation and how things went easier for households that came forward honestly — sat in his mouth like a coin he couldn't make change for. "Before you sign anything, I'm obligated to inform you that false declarations carry—"

"We know what they carry," said the old woman from the fireside. Vesper Crane's voice was thinner than it had been at the first visit; the storm had cost her something, or the winter had, or the dying had simply continued at its own pace regardless of storms and registers and the whole machinery of the state grinding past her window. "We've read your pamphlet, Mr. Pell. Well — Tobin's read it aloud, doing all the voices."

Tobin went scarlet. Maren Cole, rising from the hearth with the unhurried mass of a woman who had spent forty years being moved by weather, said nothing, but she positioned herself — Ambrose noticed it precisely, the way he noticed everything now — between the boy and the door.

"May I come in?" Ambrose asked.

That surprised them. He could see it land, the small detonation of it. Census takers did not ask. They arrived, they demanded, they wrote. Asking was not in the manual.

"You may," Vesper said, "if you take tea. We're not barbarians, whatever the Act says."

* * *

He sat in their parlor with his register on his knees and did not open it.

The room smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and something medicinal, bitter, kept in a brown bottle on the mantel. Four cups on the table, as promised. A fifth place had been laid and then, apparently on reflection, left empty — or perhaps not empty; there was a photograph propped against the fifth cup, a young woman in a clockmaker's apron squinting into sun, and Ambrose understood with a small cold jolt that the empty seat was not empty at all.

"The harbor bell rang again last night," Maren said, conversationally, watching him. "Midnight, one, two, three, four. Clean strikes. You'd have heard it if your windows weren't boarded."

"I heard it."

"That clock nearly ate the girl getting it running again. Climbed right up inside the works while the thing was striking, hands in the gear train, wind coming through the tower like the end of the world. I held the lantern." Maren's voice did something complicated on the word *lantern*. "Her mother's notes were in the case. Twenty years old. Told her exactly where the escapement would bind. Exactly." She let that sit. "Some inheritances don't show up in any ledger of yours, Mr. Pell."

"Maren," Odile said quietly. A warning or a plea.

"No," Ambrose said. "Let her talk. Please." He looked down at his register, at the blue cover with its gilt stamp — REGISTRATION ACT, PORT DISTRICT OF VERLOW — and felt the full absurd weight of it, the little book that thought it could hold a household in its columns. "I have a confession to make, and then I have a proposition, and I would rather make both before anyone signs anything, because I have watched people sign things out of fear my entire career and I am tired of it."

Vesper raised her chin a fraction. "Go on."

"The confession is this." He turned the register face-down on his knee. "I wrote your household up as irregular in September. I flagged it. My supervisor has it underlined in red. By rights I should be here with a summons, not a teacup. The penalty schedule for an uncertified irregular household is eviction within thirty days and referral of each occupant for individual assessment. Individual assessment means—" He stopped.

"It means the workhouse for me," Vesper finished, pleasantly. "The press gang for the boy. Assessment for Odile, which means whatever ward they decide fits, and wards fit whoever holds the keys. And Maren goes back to the light alone, which she'd survive, being granite, and hate every day of."

"Yes," Ambrose said. "That."

"And the proposition?" Odile asked. Their voice was level. Their hands, in their lap, were not quite — Ambrose saw the knuckles whiten and release, whiten and release, the small tide of a person keeping themselves very still.

"The proposition requires me to tell you about a clause."

"There's a clause?"

"There is always a clause. Nobody reads the schedules. Schedule Nine, paragraph four — it's older than the Act itself, carried over from the Poor Law amendments of '71. It provides that a household may be certified as legitimate where its members stand in *mutual dependency* — those are the words — *mutual dependency of maintenance and care*, notwithstanding the absence of blood relation or tenancy in common. It was written for almshouses. For religious orders. For crews of ships, actually, that's the strangest origin — sailors' boarding associations, so that a crew ashore counted as a household for rations and relief." He heard his own voice speeding up and slowed it. "It has been invoked in this district perhaps six times. It requires a head of household willing to sign by name, in public record, and declare the nature of each occupant truthfully. Truthfully, Mrs. Crane. All of it. On the page. Where the assessors can read it."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a cart horse stamped.

"You're asking us to hang ourselves in plain sight," Odile said, "and call the rope a certificate."

"I'm asking you to be *visible*," Ambrose said. "Which is harder, yes. Which is exactly why the clause exists and nobody uses it — everyone wants to hide, and hiding is what makes them prey. But a household certified under Nine-Four cannot be broken up without a magistrate's review. Cannot be assessed individually. Its members stand or fall together, legally, as one body." He looked around the room — at the dying woman upright by sheer will and spite, at the runaway boy gone very quiet, at the keeper, at the clockmaker with ink to the elbows and their mother's photograph propped against a teacup. "I have spent eleven years writing down what people are afraid of. I would like, once, before they retire me to a desk in the archives, to write down what people are *to each other*. That is what nature means. It's what it always should have meant."

Silence. The good kind, Ambrose thought — the kind that comes before something is decided, not the kind that comes instead of deciding.

Then Vesper Crane began, softly, to laugh. It cost her; he could see it cost her, each breath a small negotiation; but she laughed anyway, the way she deflected, the way she fought — spending herself freely.

"Well," she said. "Eleven years, and the man finally asks a question worth answering."

* * *

They gathered at the table. Ambrose opened the register at last, flattened the page, uncapped his pen. The columns waited: HEAD OF HOUSEHOLD. RELATION OR DEPENDENCY. NATURE OF OCCUPANT.

"Who signs?" he asked. "Legally it should be the eldest, or the principal earner, or—"

"Me," Odile said.

No hesitation. Ambrose looked up. The clockmaker stood at the table's edge, and behind them the whole household had gone still in a way that was not surprise — that was recognition, rather, the way a mechanism recognizes its mainspring.

"You understand what it means," Ambrose said. "Your name. Public record. Every assessor, every clerk, every busybody with a subscription to the *Verlow Gazette*—"

"My mother signed a register in this city twenty years ago." Odile took the pen from him. "'Crane. Clockmaker. No kin.'" Their voice caught on the last words, barely. "She wrote *no kin* because the form gave her nowhere else to put us, and she died with a ship's berth in her name and four people who loved her and no legal word for any of it. I'm not making her mistake. Give me the line."

He gave them the line.

Odile Crane bent over the register and wrote, in a careful clockmaker's hand, each letter placed like a gear set true:

*Head of Household: Crane, Odile. Trade: clockmaker.*
*Occupants: Crane, Vesper — kin of choice, elder, keeper of the house.*
*Cole, Maren — kin of choice, keeper of the light.*
*Tobin Wren — kin of choice, apprentice of the press.*
*Nature of household: mutual dependency of maintenance and care, per Schedule Nine, para. 4. No member goes alone.*

The last sentence was not on any form. Ambrose watched them write it anyway, watched the pen move without stopping, and made no motion toward the blotter. Some lines you leave in. Some lines are the whole point.

Below, in the certification space, he wrote: *Certified lawful household, Port District of Verlow, this 14th day of the registration period. Examined and attested: A. Pell, census officer.* And then, because his hand seemed to decide before he did, he added beneath his name: *Under protest of no one. The examiner found nothing here requiring correction.*

He blotted it. He closed the book. It was done — a family existed now in the eyes of the Port District of Verlow, four names and no blood, held together by one honest clerk's willingness to read the fine print mercifully.

Vesper was crying without any noise at all, which frightened Ambrose more than wailing would have. Maren put a hand on her shoulder. Tobin stared at the page like it was a pamphlet he hadn't dared print.

"There's a ship," Ambrose said quietly, into the middle of it. He hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, because he was a man who wrote things down and had lately learned to say them too. "The emigrant packet. *Meridian Star.* They moved her departure up after the storm — she clears the harbor at dawn tomorrow. If there's a berth booked in any name connected to this house..." He looked at Odile. "You should know the tide tables say she'll need the channel buoy lit by four in the morning, and there's only one keeper in this city I'd trust to see it done."

Maren Cole was already reaching for her oilskins.

* * *

Outside, the fog was coming in off the water, early and thick, the kind that swallowed lamplight whole. Ambrose stood on the doorstep of Lantern Row with his register under his arm and understood that he had just ended his career or begun it, and could not yet tell which.

Behind him, through the closing door, he heard the household moving — chairs pushed back, the brown bottle rattling, Vesper's voice rising fierce and thin: *"Well, don't just stand there, we've a dawn to argue with—"*

And Odile's answer, clear as a struck bell:

"No one goes alone."
