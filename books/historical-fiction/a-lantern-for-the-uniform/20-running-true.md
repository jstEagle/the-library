## 20. Running True

The clock struck noon for the first time in forty-one days, and Odile Crane stood inside its case to feel it.

That was the thing nobody understood about a master clock — you didn't hear it so much as wear it. The strike came up through the frame, through the great iron posts their mother had once called *the ribs*, through the soles of the feet and into the teeth, and if you stood with your palm flat against the going-train like Odile did now, you could feel each bell note arrive in the metal a half-second before it reached the air. Twelve strokes. Even. Unhurried. Each one landing on the second it was sworn to.

Not one of them late. Not one of them doubled or swallowed or dragged, the way they had been since before Odile ever climbed these stairs — since before Halloway, even, though he'd never admit the clock had been sick longer than his directorship. Forty-one days ago it had stopped altogether, and the harbor bell had fallen silent, and a coal barge called the *Merrivale* had grounded on the shoal with her hull opened like a dropped melon, and the whole city of Verlow had looked up at this tower and asked who was responsible.

Odile pressed their hand harder against the train and let the last stroke die out of the brass. Then they wiped their palms on their apron, backed out through the case door, and closed it on the mechanism the way you'd close a bedroom door on a sleeping child.

Halloway was standing by the south window with his hands clasped behind his back, which was what he did when he'd been rehearsing a sentence.

"It struck," he said.

"It struck."

"Cleanly."

"Cleanly." Odile crossed to the workbench and began laying out tools in their proper places — not because the bench needed tidying but because Halloway talked better when neither of them was looking at him. "The new escapement is holding. The impulse jewel seats true, the lock is within a hair of spec, and I corrected the lift angles on the pallets the way—" They stopped. Started again. "The way the original design intended. There were errors introduced during a repair in 1871. Someone replaced the escape wheel with an undersized pattern and never compensated the pallets. It's been running slow and striking ragged for twenty years because of that wheel."

"And you knew this how?"

Odile picked up the maintenance log — their mother's log, the one that had spent decades hidden in the compartment behind the minute wheel — and set it on the bench between them without ceremony. "She wrote it down. Page thirty-eight. She flagged the undersized wheel, recommended the correct pattern, and noted that her recommendation was overruled." A beat. "By the director at the time."

Halloway looked at the log the way a man looks at a document that has just rearranged his understanding of his own building. He put on his spectacles. He read. The fog moved past the window behind him in slow grey sheets, and somewhere below them the harbor bell rang the quarter-hour — a small, clean sound now, obedient to the twelve strokes above it, and every pilot in Verlow hearing it and knowing exactly where the tide stood.

"She worked here," Halloway said at last. It wasn't a question. He'd known since the day he'd found Odile with the compartment open; he'd watched them too closely for too long for it to have been ignorance. But saying it aloud was different, and Odile watched him decide to say it anyway. "Your mother."

"My mother worked here. As a clockmaker. Under her own name, in the register, with no kin listed and no explanation required." Odile kept their voice level. "Twenty years ago this tower thought that was ordinary enough to write down. Something changed between then and now. Not in the clock. In the city."

"The Registration Act changed," Halloway said.

"The Act didn't change anything. It just made people say out loud what they'd already decided to believe." Odile closed the log. "The clock doesn't care what anyone believes. That's the whole point of it. That's why cities build them."

For a long moment Halloway said nothing. Then he took off his spectacles, folded them, and did the thing with his hands behind his back again, which meant the rehearsed sentence was coming.

"I've drafted a letter to the Harbor Commission taking full responsibility for the *Merrivale* grounding," he said. "It states that the failure originated in a repair decision made in 1871, that the fault was documented by a previous keeper of this clock and concealed from successive directors, and that it was identified and corrected under my administration by my appointed horologist." He paused. "I need a name to put in that last clause."

"You have my name. You've had it since the census forms went round."

"I have the name you gave Pell." Halloway's mouth did something complicated. "Which is a fine name. Which is the only name I've ever needed on a work order. What I'm offering isn't a form, Crane. It's a post. Keeper of the Clock. Permanent appointment, salary attached, your signature on the maintenance register beside hers." He nodded at the log. "The Commission will want a head of household listed on the appointment papers — they want everything listed on everything now, God help us — but the post itself is mine to give, and I'm giving it. To you. Whatever you are, whatever the census taker writes in his ledger about you. This clock has not run true in twenty years, and as of noon today it runs true, and there are perhaps four people in Verlow who understand why that matters, and three of them are dead or retired."

Odile stood very still.

They had spent their whole life learning how to be passed over. It was a craft, like clockmaking — a matter of small tolerances, of keeping yourself just outside the reach of attention, of answering questions with questions and standing near doorways and never, ever being the most interesting thing in a room. Their mother had taught them some of it before she died, and Lantern Row had taught them the rest: Aunt Vesper's brilliant deflections, Maren's salt-plain invisibility, Tobin's quick hands and quicker lies. A whole household of people practicing the same discipline from four different directions, holding still the way you hold still when you're sure the ground beneath you is borrowed.

And here was a man in a good coat saying *I see you, I've always seen you, take the post.*

"You know what happens," Odile said quietly, "if you appoint someone the census office has flagged."

"Pell hasn't flagged you. I've asked."

"You've *asked* him?"

"I wrote to the registration office two weeks ago requesting verification of your file. Ambrose Pell answered personally. His letter said — I have it here —" Halloway produced an envelope from his coat with the air of a man producing a winning card, then seemed to reconsider, and put it back. "His letter said your household stands certified under the mutual-dependency provision, subsection nine, and that any further inquiry into your fitness for civic employment would be, quote, *an expenditure of the office's time that serves no purpose the law recognizes*. End quote. I've been a director for eleven years, Crane, and I have never received a government letter that sounded so much like a man choosing his words like steps across ice." He almost smiled. "I believe we owe that young man something. I haven't yet worked out what."

"He owes us nothing either," Odile said. "That's rather the point of him."

* * *

They went down the tower stairs slowly, because Odile's knees had opinions after six weeks of fourteen-hour days, and because Halloway walked like a man who believed stairs were a negotiation. At the third landing they passed the old tide-board where the duty clerk chalked the predicted high waters each morning, and Odile stopped without meaning to.

Tomorrow's figures were already chalked up. High water, 4:17 and 16:42. Spring tide, the board warned, in a clerk's neat hand. And beneath it, fresh, the notation that made Odile's chest tighten:

*Barometer falling. Storm expected from the southwest. All vessels advised.*

"How certain are they?" Odile asked.

"Certain enough." Halloway followed their eyes to the board. "The glass has been dropping all morning at the signal station. Maren Cole sent word up an hour ago — she says the light's going to be worth its keep tomorrow night, and she'd know." He paused on the stair above Odile, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its committee-room polish. "The clock will hold?"

Odile thought about the new escapement, seated and oiled and true. Thought about the pallets re-cut to their mother's angles. Thought about the great barrel, wound, and the weight descending through its allotted week of time like a held breath.

"The clock will hold," Odile said. "The clock would hold in a hurricane. It was built by people who expected hurricanes." They touched the stone of the stair wall, the same stone their mother's hands had touched ten thousand times. "What I can't promise is the tower roof, or the bell louvres, or the windows on the north face, which are older than the Act, the Commission, and possibly the city. If the storm wants the building, it can have the building. The clock will forgive us. Buildings are supposed to be replaceable. That's what buildings are *for*."

"That is a deeply strange philosophy of architecture."

"It's a philosophy of clocks. Everything else follows."

* * *

Lantern Row at dusk looked like a painting of itself, and Odile stood across the street a moment to look at it before going in, because some days you had to.

The house was narrow and crooked and its front step dipped in the middle from fifty years of feet. Aunt Vesper's window glowed amber on the first floor — she was awake, then, and upright, which was a victory you learned not to celebrate out loud anymore. Tobin's silhouette moved behind the kitchen curtain with the restless energy of a boy who had spent a year running pamphlets through forbidden streets and hadn't yet learned how to be safe and didn't entirely want to. And above the door —

Above the door hung a lantern. New-hung, Tobin's doing, only two nights past. An ordinary storm lantern on an iron bracket, unlit in the daylight, waiting. Odile had come home the night Tobin raised it and found them all standing in the street looking up at it like idiots: Vesper wrapped in two shawls, Maren straight off the night watch smelling of oil and cold air, Tobin flushed and defiant as though daring anyone to ask what it meant. Nobody had asked. Everybody knew. It was the mark Tobin's underground had used to signpost safe houses — a lit lantern meaning *you can knock here, you can sleep here, no one in this house will ask you to explain yourself.*

Hung openly now. On their own door. In full view of the street, the neighbors, and the registration office's entire filing system.

"We're either the bravest household in Verlow or the biggest target," Vesper had said that night, "and I have genuinely lost the ability to tell the difference."

"Both," Maren had said. "That's usually how bravery works."

Odile crossed the street and let themselves in.

The smell hit them first and always: coal smoke, bread, Vesper's medicinal honey-and-ginger, the faint iron tang of Maren's kit drying by the stove. Tobin was setting the table with the aggressive efficiency of someone avoiding conversation, and Vesper sat in her chair by the fire with a blanket over her knees and a letter open in her lap, and when she saw Odile she didn't ask how the clock was. She just looked at their face, and whatever she found there made her fold the letter shut.

"Well?" she said.

"Twelve strokes at noon. Clean as a christening bell." Odile untied their apron. "Halloway's offered me the keeper's post. Permanent. My mother's name goes back in the register next to mine."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then Vesper began to cry — not the wet exhausted crying of the bad weeks, but something else, short and fierce, and she pressed the folded letter against her mouth until she could speak.

"She'd have hated the fuss," Vesper managed, "and demanded a raise."

"She'd have demanded two."

Tobin had gone still by the dresser, holding a fork like he'd forgotten forks existed. "Keeper of the Clock," he said. "That's — that's a real title. That's a title they print in the almanac. People will know your name."

"People will know my name," Odile agreed, and heard how strange it sounded, and how good.

Vesper held out her hand, and Odile took it, and her grip — thinner than it used to be, always thinner now — was nonetheless exactly as imperious as it had been the day she'd taken in a frightened apprentice with nowhere else to go and informed them the spare room was theirs and no, there was nothing to discuss. "Then it's settled. We are officially the household of a public officer. Heaven help Verlow." Her eyes went to the window, where the last grey light was failing and the barometer's fall showed in the restless way the gulls were flying. "Now sit down and eat, all of you, because Maren says tomorrow night will be the worst blow in five years, and I intend for this family to face it fed."

From upstairs came the sound of Tobin bolting the shutters, one window at a time, working toward the little room under the eaves where the lantern bracket was screwed fast to the sill — where, when the wind rose and the dark came down, that lamp would burn whether the storm approved or not.

Outside, far off toward the harbor mouth, the first long moan of the lighthouse foghorn began, steady as a heartbeat, calling home every ship that could still hear it.
