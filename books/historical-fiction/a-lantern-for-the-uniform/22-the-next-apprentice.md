## 22. The Next Apprentice

One year, Odile learned, was exactly long enough for a city to forget it had almost gone dark.

Verlow in autumn still smelled of salt and coal smoke, but the fog had lost its teeth. The harbor bell rang on schedule now — every quarter hour, faithful as a heartbeat — and the fishermen had stopped glancing up at the observatory with that flat, superstitious distrust they'd worn the year of the grounding. They had new superstitions instead. Odile had heard one at the chandler's just that morning: that the master clock ran true because a ghost kept it. A woman's ghost, who'd died at her work and never left.

Odile hadn't corrected him. There were worse things to be than a ghost. And besides, it wasn't entirely wrong.

The clockwork above them was their mother's work now as much as anyone's. The escapement wheel Halloway had grudgingly purchased with the pawned tools' money still turned at the heart of the mechanism, but around it Odile had rebuilt and corrected according to the notes in that hidden log — the counterweight ratios, the pallet angles, the small genius of a woman who'd signed her name *Crane, clockmaker, no kin* and been erased for it. Halloway had stopped calling Odile "the apprentice" sometime in the spring. He said *Keeper* now, stiffly, like a man swallowing a fishbone, and paid accordingly.

The pawn tickets sat in Odile's breast pocket where they'd sat for a year. Redeemable until midwinter. Some nights Odile took them out and looked at them — the fine paper, the clerk's cramped hand describing a set of jeweler's screwdrivers and a loupe and a burnishing tool that had shaped two generations of Cranes — and some nights they almost went. But the money wasn't there yet, not quite, and there was no hurry. Tools waited. That was the whole point of tools. They held their shape through anything.

The household on Lantern Row held its shape too, though it had changed the way living things change — by adding.

The register hung framed in the front hall now, because Vesper insisted on framing it. *Crane, clockmaker, head of household; Cole, lighthouse keeper (retired); Crane-Vesper, aunt, dependent; Tobin, printer, kin of choice.* Pell's certification seal at the bottom, blue wax cracked but intact. Underneath, in Ambrose's careful hand, the clause he'd invoked: *mutual dependency, duly witnessed.* Four names that had no lawful right to share a roof, made lawful by one honest man's willingness to read the law against its own grain. Tobin called it the most subversive document in Verlow and had offered, twice, to print a broadsheet about it. Both times Vesper had talked him out of it with tea and logic. Both times he'd sulked for a day and then started writing pamphlets about something else.

Vesper was stable. That was the word Dr. Imray used, and for once the word meant what it said. The bad winter had passed; the spring had been kind; she could walk to the corner garden now without resting, and she'd taken over the kitchen again with the tyrannical authority of a general returning from exile. Her letters still went out — to solicitors, to old friends, once astonishingly to a bishop — but they were letters of business now, not farewell. She had stopped settling affairs and started conducting them.

Maren still climbed the lighthouse stairs every evening, though the light itself was automated these last years and she was officially nothing but a caretaker. She logged the weather, the ships, the seasons. In the back pages of her logbook, past the official entries, she kept a different record: names. People who'd come up the cliff path looking for help and found it. It was getting to be a long list.

And Tobin — Tobin had an apprenticeship of his own now, at Halloran & Sons, the only print shop in Verlow whose proprietor had read one of Tobin's illegal pamphlets and hired him *because* of it rather than despite it. He came home smelling of ink and triumph, arguing politics at dinner with a fluency that would have gotten him beaten in his father's shop. His father had sent one letter since that night on the docks. Tobin had burned it unopened in the kitchen stove, and Vesper had watched him do it and said nothing, and passed him the potatoes.

This was the shape of things on the evening the knock came.

* * *

It was raining the way Verlow rained in October — sideways, intimate, finding every gap in the weatherboarding. Odile was at the workbench in the front room, fitting a new mainspring into a ship's chronometer, when the knock sounded. Three knocks, then silence, then two more. The rhythm of someone deciding, at each interval, whether to run.

They opened the door on a stranger.

Young — younger than Tobin, maybe seventeen, maybe less, hard to tell under the rain and the hollows. A boy or a girl or neither; the soaked coat hid everything except the shaking. One hand gripped the doorframe. The other held something out, cupped protectively, the way you'd carry a bird with a broken wing.

A watch. Silver-cased, dented, its crystal starred with a crack. Odile knew it before it was fully extended into the lamplight — knew it by the pawnbroker's tag still knotted to its chain, by the number on that tag, by the shop it came from. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

"That's mine," Odile said. Not unkindly. Just certain.

The stranger flinched. "I didn't steal it. I bought it. I bought it back, I mean — I—" The words tangled and snapped. "The man at the shop said it'd been sitting unredeemed near a year. He said if nobody claimed it by midwinter—" A breath, ragged. "I've been saving. Since spring. It took everything."

"You bought my mother's watch," Odile said, and watched the stranger's face do three things at once: confusion, horror, and the particular despair of someone who has walked a long way on the last of their strength only to find the door shut after all.

"I'll give it back," the stranger whispered. "I don't want trouble. I only wanted—"

"Come inside."

The stranger didn't move. Rain ran off their chin. "I can't pay for lodging. I have four coppers."

"It's not lodging." Odile stepped back and pulled the door wide — wide the way Maren had once described pulling doors wide, arms aching, lamp held high. "It's dinner."

For a moment the stranger stood in the rain like a creature deciding whether the lit doorway was real. Then they crossed the threshold, and Odile closed the storm behind them.

* * *

The watch sat on the workbench between them, dripping gently onto a cloth. Up close, Odile could see the whole sad story of its year in a pawnshop case: the moisture in the movement, the mainspring let down hard, someone's clumsy attempt at opening the case back that had scored the metal. But the works were sound. Odile's mother's works. She'd bought it secondhand herself, the log had said — a watch for a clockmaker who spent her life mending other people's time and never owned any of her own.

The stranger sat at the kitchen table with a bowl of Vesper's barley soup going cold in front of them, too wary to eat and too hungry not to. Up close, in good light, Odile revised their estimate: sixteen, perhaps. Eyes that had done arithmetic on danger for a long time. Hands — Odile noticed hands always — small, scarred, with the faint calluses of someone who'd worked at something fine and repetitive. Sewing, maybe. Typesetting, maybe. Something that wanted teaching.

"Tobin will want to know how you got the pamphlet," Tobin said, dropping into a chair with his ink-stained fingers laced behind his head. He'd come home mid-soup, assessed the situation with a printer's speed, and decided the stranger was either a spy or family, with odds he liked.

"Pamphlet?"

"The lantern one. Safe houses. You're carrying a pawn ticket from Marlowe's, which means you came through the Salt Quarter, which means somebody gave you the lantern mark, because nobody crosses the Salt Quarter at night without one anymore." He grinned. "I wrote it. Well, I set the type. Maren approved the routes."

The stranger looked from Tobin to Odile to the framed register visible through the kitchen doorway, and something in their shoulders came down half an inch — the first loosening of a knot tied very tight and very long ago.

"A woman at the fish market," they said finally. "She saw my hands. She said — she said if I ever needed a place, look for the door with the lantern. She said the people there don't ask what you are first." A pause. The dangerous question, approached sideways, the way everyone approached it now that the law made names into weapons: "Do you?"

"Do we what?" Vesper asked, ladling more soup into the bowl before the stranger could refuse it.

"Ask."

Vesper set the ladle down with the deliberate care of a woman laying down cards she'd already won with. "Child," she said, "look around this table. A clockmaker nobody can categorize. A lighthouse keeper who married the sea because the sea doesn't file paperwork. A printer's devil who disowned his own blood over soup. And me — an old woman registered as her own niece's dependent, which is legally impossible and factually true. You are among the least qualified people in Verlow to be asking anybody what they are."

The stranger stared. Then, slowly, unbelievably, they laughed — a rusty, startled sound, like a mechanism turning over after years in a drawer — and picked up the spoon.

Odile watched them eat and felt the year settle into place around them like a finished movement. The berth money, spent on a wheel. The wheel, keeping the harbor alive. The harbor, feeding this city, which had fed this table, which now fed a stranger holding a redeemed watch and four coppers and nothing else in the world. Their mother had left one passage on one ship and Odile had traded it for gears and stayed, and every consequence of that trade had led here, to steam on a window and a laugh cracking open a frightened face.

Inheritance, Odile thought, was not a thing you received. It was a thing you handed on.

* * *

Later — much later, the fire banked, Vesper asleep, Tobin's light out, Maren's lantern climbing the cliff path home — the stranger stood at the workbench where the watch lay open, its guts exposed under the lamp.

"I broke it," they said quietly. "Opening the case. I wanted to see if there was anything inside. People hide things in watches." A pause. "There wasn't. Just the works. But the works were beautiful."

"They're repairable," Odile said. "Everything's repairable. That's the entire trade."

The stranger touched the scored case back with one fingertip, apologetic. "I'm quick with small things," they said, to the watch, to the room, to nobody. "At the factory they had me doing eyelets. Ten thousand a day. Then the registration came to the dormitories and I—" The sentence ended the way so many sentences ended now, in the place where the law reached out and lives scattered. "I've been running since August."

Odile looked at the small scarred hands. At the eye that had found the beauty inside a broken watch without being taught to look. At the person standing in their mother's kitchen holding their mother's time in their mothering hands, asking nothing, expecting less.

"There's a stool," Odile said, "that needs breaking in. And a loupe that fits small faces better than mine. And a trade that asks nothing about you except whether your hands are steady and your patience holds." They slid the watch across the bench. "Start tomorrow. First lesson: you never force a stuck gear. You find out why it's stuck."

The stranger looked up. In the lamplight their face did what faces did in this house — came apart a little, and reassembled differently, and stayed.

"Why?" they asked. The oldest question. The one Pell had carried to this door in a leather folder full of forms. The one the whole law of Verlow had been built to answer wrongly.

Odile thought of a hidden compartment in a clock case. Of a log signed *no kin*. Of a lantern hung by a door, lit now every single night, burning oil the whole household paid for gladly, saying to the fog and the street and the dark what no form or ledger had ever managed to say plainly: *you are welcome here; come in out of the weather; there is a place set.*

"Because someone left me the works," Odile said. "I'm just passing them on."

Outside, the rain eased. Somewhere across the sleeping harbor, the master clock gathered itself and struck the hour — clean, unhurried, true — and in the kitchen of the house on Lantern Row, a woman laid another bowl beside the others on the shelf, and turned the lamp up, and left it burning.
