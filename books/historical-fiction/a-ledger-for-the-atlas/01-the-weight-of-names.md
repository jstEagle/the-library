## 1. The Weight of Names

The lamp guttered twice before Ansel trimmed the wick, and in the space between those two failures of light she thought she saw her father's name on the page — *Iversen, Halvard* — though when the flame steadied it was only the entry above it, a diver named Oskar Lindqvist, and beneath his name the column where she would write what the sea had done with him.

The counting room of the Halvard Trading Company sat above the workshop floor like a skull atop a body. Through its single window Ansel could hear, even at this hour, the muffled industry below: the clank of the helmet racks being scrubbed of salt, the wet slap of canvas suits hung to dry, someone laughing too loudly because the laughter was cheaper than weeping. She had worked this desk for six years, since the age of eleven, when her father's debts had passed down through his drowning like an inheritance no one had asked for. Six years of ink. Her right middle finger carried a callus hard as a limpet shell from the pen's pressure, and when she closed her eyes at night she still saw columns of figures drifting behind them like fish.

She dipped the pen.

*Lindqvist, Oskar. Entered service 1891. Debt outstanding: 214 kroner, 40 øre.*

The figures were easy. The figures were always easy. It was the next line that required something she did not have a word for — not courage exactly, nor callousness, but some third thing that lived between them and let her hand move.

*Status: deceased. Recovered 14 October by boat crew off the Gullskær shoals. Cause: air line fouled on wreckage, helmet flooded. Body delivered to widow, 15 October.*

There. Four lines. A man of thirty-one years who had once, in summer, taught her to whistle through a blade of grass on the breakwater — reduced to four lines in a ledger bound in green buckram, in a hand so steady it looked as if it belonged to no one at all. That was the trick of it, she'd learned early. Write small. Write level. Write as though the numbers had fallen from the sky rather than been pulled out of men's lungs, one breath of compressed air sold back to them at interest until they owed the company the very act of breathing underwater.

Ansel blew across the page to dry the ink and told herself, as she always did, that she was only recording history. The sea made widows; she merely noted them. The company set the rates; she merely entered them. A ledger was a mirror, not a knife. Her father had believed in ledgers too — had signed his contract against the advice of every man on the quay, saying, *Paper is honest, little duck. Paper remembers.* Well. Paper remembered, and here was his daughter keeping the memory, and if there was cruelty in it, the cruelty was not hers. She had built this belief the way a mussel builds a shell, layer over layer, year over year, until she could carry it without cutting herself.

Below her, the workshop door opened onto the street, letting in a blade of cold October evening and, with it, the widow.

Ansel heard her before she saw her — the particular uneven tread of a woman walking in shoes not her own, borrowed shoes, funeral shoes. Then Sigrid Lindqvist was on the stairs, and then she was in the doorway of the counting room, and Ansel understood that she should have gone down to the house this afternoon instead of sending the boy with the note. Company policy. Notification in writing. As if grief could be invoiced.

The widow filled the doorway the way weather fills a window. She was a big woman gone suddenly shapeless, still wearing her apron, as though she'd come straight from gutting fish and simply never stopped, as though her hands might keep working if her mind refused to join them. Her eyes found Ansel across the lamplit room — found the desk, the ledger, the pen — and Ansel watched her understand, in one long terrible moment, exactly what this room was and what it had done.

"You wrote him," the widow said. Not a question.

"I recorded—" Ansel began, and stopped, because there was no ending to that sentence that wasn't a lie or an insult.

"You wrote him." Sigrid came forward into the light. Up close her face was a map of the harbor itself, all channels and shoals, and Ansel saw that she had been crying earlier, hours ago, the kind of crying that dries into salt like everything else in Skarnvik. "Fourteen years he went down for this company. He brought up their copper and their amber and their godless pearls, and I have stood on this quay in every weather watching his bubbles, counting his bubbles, because that is how you know a man alive under the water, do you understand? By his bubbles. And now your book says four lines."

"The record is standard—"

"Show me."

Ansel's hands did not move. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a hammer rang against an anvil plate, one bright note, and was silent.

"Show me what my husband weighs," the widow said, quieter now, which was worse. "In kroner and øre. Show me what the water took and what your master says I owe for the privilege of losing him."

And Ansel — trained, obedient, six years practiced at the level hand — turned the ledger around. She didn't know why. Later she would tell herself it was to end the scene, to give the woman the thing she demanded and send her home, but in the moment it felt more like opening a door she'd spent years pretending was a wall. She turned the green book so that Sigrid Lindqvist could read her husband's life rendered in a stranger's careful hand, and the widow read it, her lips moving, her finger tracing the line *debt outstanding* as though the number were a wound she could probe.

"Two hundred fourteen," she whispered. "He died owing them two hundred fourteen kroner." She looked up, and her eyes were dry again, which meant the worst of it had gone somewhere deeper than tears. "They will take it out of his kit. They will take his boots. You know they will. You're the one who enters it."

"I don't set the deductions," Ansel said. "I only—"

"Write." The word cracked like ice underfoot. "You only write."

Sigrid straightened then, gathered herself up out of whatever country grief had taken her to, and spat. It was not wild, not flung — it was deliberate, considered, aimed, a small dark coin of contempt that landed on the floorboards precisely between Ansel's feet, close enough that she felt the warmth of its intention. Then the widow turned and walked out, her borrowed shoes marking the stairs all the way down, through the workshop, into the street, and the door swung shut behind her and the cold blade of evening closed up like water over a sinking thing.

Ansel sat for a long time without moving.

The spittle dried on the boards. Below, the workshop noises resumed their rhythm, indifferent, and the lamp burned, and the ink in the well grew a skin of dark film that she would later have to skim away with her fingernail. She looked at the ledger — *Lindqvist, Oskar* — and tried to find the old comfort in it, tried to see a mirror, a faithful record, history merely passing through her pen the way tide passes through a mooring post without moving it.

But the words wouldn't sit right anymore. Something had shifted in her chest the way ballast shifts in a rolling hull, slow and irreversible, and now everything written after this moment would list, would lean, would be weighed differently than everything before.

Because the truth — the truth that rose in her now like a bubble she couldn't swallow — was that the ledger did not remember the way paper remembers. It remembered the way a weapon remembers. Every figure she entered became real in the world: it deducted boots from dead men's kits, it sent collectors to doors, it decided which families ate in March. The sea killed divers, yes. But the ledger decided what the killing cost, and to whom, and it had done so through her hand, her steady, level, blameless hand, six years running.

*I only write,* she had almost said. And the widow had answered it better than any argument could: *You only write.*

Ansel picked up the pen. She had three more entries to make before the night bell — two divers renewed, one new indenture, a boy of sixteen signing his father's debt forward into his own name, another inheritance nobody asked for. The work waited the way it always waited, patient as debt itself.

But when she reached for the inkwell, her hand hesitated above it, hovering — the first hesitation in six unbroken years — and in that small suspension she felt the whole future tremble, like a needle finding north.

She did not yet know why the lamp oil smelled strange tonight, sweeter than usual, or why the company master, Herr Voss, had climbed the stairs at dusk and stood a long while in the doorway looking at the ledger shelves with an expression she had never seen on him before — an expression she would learn, much too late and exactly soon enough, to recognize as a man counting what he means to burn.

She dipped the pen and wrote.

Outside, far down the harbor road, where the town gave way to the dark water, a light appeared that did not belong to any fishing boat — white, steady, riding low — and it was joined by another, and then a third, standing in toward Skarnvik through the night like a sentence being delivered.
