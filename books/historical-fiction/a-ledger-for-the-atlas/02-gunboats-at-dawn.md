## 2. Gunboats at Dawn

The fog came in off the Skarn Sea the way it always did before a hard season, thick as wool and smelling of iron, and Ansel Iversen was on the drying racks when the sound reached her — not the foghorn, which she knew the way she knew her own heartbeat, but something lower and more patient beneath it. A thrumming. A pulse under the water's skin.

She set down the rack of cod she was turning and went still.

Out past the breakwater, where the harbor light burned its small yellow defiance against the gray, three shapes were resolving out of the mist. Not fishing boats. Not company steamers with their patched funnels and their names stenciled in flaking paint. These had hulls the color of wet slate and superstructures that rose in clean, brutal tiers, and from each of them flew a flag she had seen only on the tax notices that came twice a year and were never paid.

Army steamers. Three of them, anchored in a line like fingers closing into a fist.

Ansel stood among the drying racks with salt stiffening on her hands and watched the first longboat put off toward the pier. It cut through the fog in a straight, unhurried line, and the oars dipped without splashing — soldiers, then, trained ones, not the harbor guards who rowed like men bailing out their own sinking tempers. She counted eleven figures in the boat. Then twelve. The twelfth sat apart in the stern wearing a coat too good for the others, and did not row.

"Ansel." Old Sivert was beside her, one hand shading his eyes though there was nothing to shade them from but fog. He had been turning racks since before her father was born, and his voice came out flat as slate. "You see them?"

"I see them."

"Company sent for them, you think? About the debts?"

The debts. There was always a debt somewhere in Skarnvik; the whole town lived inside the Halvard Trading Company's ledger the way a fly lives inside amber. Ansel thought of the pages she had written only last night — Halvard owed to by forty-one divers, interest accruing at the winter rate, and one entry in careful copperplate: *Johannes Vik, drowned 14 October, balance outstanding.* She had written that line with the same pen, the same ink, the same steady hand as every other line, because that was what a ledger clerk did. That was all she was.

"Company didn't send for them," she said, and heard how her voice had changed without her permission. "Companies don't send for the army. Armies come on their own."

Sivert grunted and went back to his fish, because there was nothing else a man could do with a morning, but Ansel stayed on the racks until her feet went numb, watching the longboat reach the pier and disgorge its soldiers onto planks that had never held anything heavier than herring barrels and grief.

* * *

By noon the fog had burned away and Skarnvik could see itself plainly, and what it saw was occupation.

Soldiers on the breakwater. Soldiers at the warehouse doors. Soldiers walking the length of the drying racks with their hands clasped behind them, looking at the fish and the women mending nets and the children hauling brine carts as though taking inventory of everything they intended to own. A lieutenant with a red band on his cap had posted a paper on the customs house door, and a crowd had gathered around it in the particular dense silence of people who cannot read or dare not say aloud what they've read.

Ansel read it for them, because reading aloud was half her trade.

"*By order of the Provisional Military Administration,*" she read, "*the harbor, warehouses, wharves, and appurtenant properties of the Halvard Trading Company are hereby requisitioned for the duration of national emergency. All records, accounts, registers, and instruments of debt are declared property of the Crown and subject to audit. Persons holding claims against said company will present themselves for registration—*" She stopped.

"Go on," said Maren Voss.

Maren could not hear, but she could see, better than anyone in Skarnvik, and she was watching Ansel's face rather than the paper, reading the words there instead. Ansel finished it anyway, flatly: "*—for registration within fourteen days. Claims unsupported by documentation will be dismissed.*"

A murmur went through the crowd, low and ugly. Unsupported claims. Every claim in Skarnvik against the Halvard Company lived in exactly one place: the workshop ledger, chained to its desk, kept by a diver's daughter who wrote down other people's lives in a fair hand. And now the army wanted it.

"They're going to audit us," said Widow Grettir bitterly, loud enough for everyone. "Fourteen days to prove we're owed anything. As if the army ever took a fisherman's word over a company's stamp."

"As if the company ever took our word at all," someone answered, and the murmur turned darker.

Ansel folded the notice along its printed creases and gave it back to no one. She walked home along the seawall with the soldiers' boot-heels marking time behind her, and what she felt was not fear, or not only fear. Underneath it, coiling tighter with every step, was something colder and more useful: the knowledge that she was probably the only person in this town who understood precisely what was about to happen to those pages.

* * *

Master Halvard — Eilif Halvard, third of that name, a man built like a strongbox and polished like one — received the officers in the counting room with the ledgers already laid out on the long table, which told Ansel he had known since before dawn. She carried the tea tray in because that was also part of her trade: indentured clerks poured tea for captains. Her contract hung in its frame above the workshop door, and her father's name hung in every page of the debt register, and so she poured tea for five officers while they discussed her town like cargo.

"The requisition order is quite specific," said the senior officer, a major with a face like a closed ledger himself. "All records transfer to military custody."

"Naturally," Master Halvard said. "Though I'd counsel care with some of these volumes, Major. They are the town's memory. Debts, wages, births registered against contracts — careless handling would cause suffering among the workforce, and suffering workforces are unproductive workforces." He smiled with his whole mouth and none of his eyes. "I have always kept meticulous accounts. You'll find everything in order."

"We don't doubt it." The major turned a page with one gloved finger. "We'll begin with the current register. And the correspondence files. And the debt schedules going back—" he consulted a paper, "—twelve years."

Twelve years. Ansel's hand did not tremble as she set the major's cup on its saucer, but something behind her ribs pulled tight as a net drawn closed. Twelve years covered her father's drowning. Covered Johannes Vik's drowning last autumn. Covered every contract signed by a widow too desperate to read the interest column.

"You'll want the seal," Master Halvard said smoothly. "Of course. I'll have the ledgers waxed and sealed tonight — company seal and military seal together — pending your audit. Nothing leaves this room. Nothing is copied, nothing is consulted. For the integrity of the record." His gaze crossed the room and rested on Ansel for half a heartbeat, mild as milk. "My clerk will prepare the sealing wax."

"Yes, sir," Ansel said.

The major nodded, satisfied, and the meeting moved on to coal tonnage and billeting assignments, and Ansel gathered cups and saucers and listened with her head bowed and her mind moving fast and cold through everything she'd just heard. Sealed. Nothing copied. Nothing consulted. And when the army's auditors were done — when they had extracted whatever the company and the crown wanted extracted — what happened to the volumes then?

She remembered a story her father used to tell, back before the sea took him: about a fire in a southern port, years ago, where a shipping house burned with all its papers one lucky night, and every debt in the district died with them. Her father had laughed telling it. *Ash forgives everybody,* he'd say.

Master Halvard was smiling again across the table. Ash forgives everybody. But ash also erases everybody, and Ansel understood, standing in the counting room with an armful of porcelain, that between the company's seal and the army's seal lay a very short road and a can of lamp oil at the end of it.

The names were going to burn. All of them — the living debtors, the widows, the drowned men whose balances were still marked outstanding as though the sea might yet be invoiced. Fourteen days to file a claim, and the only proof of any claim in existence was scheduled to become smoke.

* * *

That evening she prepared the sealing wax as ordered. She melted it over the lamp flame in the workshop, watching it go from solid to liquid to a slow red pool, and Master Halvard pressed the company stamp into the first seal himself while a soldier pressed the crown's stamp beside it, and the great ledger closed under both.

"Careful work," Master Halvard said to her, not unkindly, in the way of a man complimenting a horse. "They may want to question you during the audit. Answer plainly. Clerks who embellish find themselves transferred."

Transferred. The word sat in the air between them, and Ansel looked at him — really looked, for the first time in nine years of servitude — and saw that he knew exactly what the army intended, and had invited it, and would sleep soundly tonight.

"Yes, sir," she said again, and hated how easily the words came, how nine years of saying yes had worn the groove so deep she could speak obedience in her sleep.

She walked home in the dark past soldiers' cookfires glowing along the breakwater, and the dread that had been sitting in her chest since dawn shifted, settled, hardened. It stopped being a weight and became a spine.

Because here was the thing the seals could not touch, the thing no audit could reach: she had been keeping that ledger for nine years, and a ledger keeper knows her book. She knew which diver owed what, which widow had signed what, which page held which drowning. It lived in her hands the way net-mending lived in Maren's. If the originals burned, the truth would not entirely burn with them — not if she acted, not if she worked, not if she was willing to risk the only life the company had left her.

It was madness, and she knew it was madness, and she stood a long time at her window looking at the sealed counting-room windows across the yard, lit yellow against the night, with the shadow of a sentry crossing them at intervals like a pendulum.

Then she blew out her lamp, and in the dark she began, silently, to write the first name in the air with her finger, the way she'd write it in ink: *Johannes Vik, drowned 14 October.*

There were four hundred and six names after that one.

And tomorrow — tomorrow she would need help.
