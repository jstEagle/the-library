## 3. What the Net-Mender Saw

Maren Solheim had been deaf for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned that the world did most of its talking with its hands.

Not only hands — mouths, yes, when they turned toward her; shoulders, brows, the restless feet of liars. But hands above all. The fishermen who came to her bench to have their nets mended spoke to her in gestures because speech was useless between them, and in gestures they were honest. A man might lie with his mouth all afternoon, but his hands told her which season he feared, which daughter he was proud of, whether he had drunk away the money for winter oil. She read them the way the town read weather: without thinking, without mercy.

So it was not strange to her — though it would have been strange to anyone else — that she knew about the officers before Ansel Iversen did.

She had seen it three nights past, from her window above the net shed, while she worked a torn trammel by moonlight because sleep had gone sour on her again. Two men stood at the end of the pier where the lamp didn't reach, and one of them was Captain Voss, the requisition officer with the gray gloves and the patient way of standing, and the other was Halvard's own master, Eirik Sund, and their mouths were moving in the low economical way of men discussing property. Maren could not hear a word and did not need to. Sund's hands were the tell. He kept clasping them behind his back and then bringing them forward again, opening and closing, the way a man handles something he intends to destroy and cannot yet decide the manner of it. Paper, she thought. He is talking about paper. And Voss nodded twice — not agreement exactly, but permission — and looked out at the black water as if the harbor were already his.

The next morning she watched the soldiers carry crates into the counting house, more crates than inventory required, and she watched Sund stand over them the way a man stands over a coffin he has ordered too small.

*They mean to burn it,* she thought, and her hands went still on the net twine, and the thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *They mean to burn the names.*

* * *

Ansel Iversen came to the net shed on the fourth day after the gunboats anchored, and Maren saw her coming long before she arrived, because everyone in Skarnvik was visible from everywhere; the town was built like an amphitheater around its own misfortunes.

The girl walked the way she always walked since her father drowned — spine straight as a mast, chin level, hands folded in front of her like a woman carrying water she refused to spill. The ledger-keeper's walk. Maren had watched that walk for nine years, from girlhood onward, and she had formed her opinion of it early and held it stubbornly: *that one thinks the pen is innocent.* That one believes writing down a thing is different from doing the thing. It was the comfortable lie of clerks and priests, and Maren had never had the luxury of comfortable lies. When you cannot hear, you see everything, and seeing everything means knowing that no one is innocent, only variously useful.

But the widow's spit had changed something. Maren had been there for that — everyone had, it happened on the workshop steps in full daylight, Greta Lund spitting at Ansel's boots after the girl recorded Torvald Lund dead in the company book, *lost with gear, debt transferred,* in a hand so neat it looked printed. And Ansel had not wiped the spit away. She had stood there and let it dry on her boot leather and walked into the workshop with it still there, and Maren, watching from across the yard, had thought: *ah. There it is. The crack.*

Everyone breaks somewhere. The question was what they became after.

Now the girl came up the shingle path to the net shed, and instead of going to the workshop door she turned aside, deliberately, visibly, and walked to Maren's bench. People did not do this. They shouted at Maren from a distance or gestured crudely or simply talked over her as if deafness were also blindness and stupidity, but they did not come sit at her bench unless they wanted mending done.

Ansel sat down on the upturned crate across from her. Her mouth began moving.

Maren let her go on for a full minute, watching the lips shape words she could half-catch — the light was wrong, slanting off the water and up under the girl's chin, washing out everything below the nose — and then she raised one hand, palm flat, and pointed deliberately at her own eyes and then at Ansel's mouth, and tilted her head toward the window where the light fell clean.

Ansel understood at once, which surprised her. The girl shifted her stool sideways into the good light, faced her fully, and began again, slower, each word given room.

*I need to ask you something,* she said, and her lips were careful, the lips of someone who had practiced speaking to the deaf before, probably to old Halvard himself in his last silent years. *And I need you to answer me truly.*

Maren kept her hands moving on the net — a figure-eight mend, nothing that needed thought — and gave the smallest nod.

*The soldiers,* Ansel said. *You watch the harbor. Everyone watches you watching. What did you see?*

There it was, then. No preamble, no pretending this was about nets. Maren studied her. Up close the ledger-keeper's face was not the cold mask it showed the yard; there were fine lines of strain around the mouth, and the eyes had the particular shine of someone who had not slept properly since the steamers came in. Nineteen years old, maybe twenty, and carrying the weight of a whole town's accounts in ink.

Maren set down the twine. She answered the way she answered everything, in signs and in the rough spoken words she could produce but never verify — her own voice was a locked room even to her — and she watched Ansel's face to make sure she was understood.

*Two men,* she signed, then said aloud, flat and unlovely: "Pier. Night. Sund. Gray-glove." She made the clasping-hands gesture, opening, closing. *Paper.* Then she pointed toward the counting house and made a fist, opened it, let the fingers flutter upward like ash.

Burn.

Ansel had gone very still. "When?" she said aloud, forgetting to shape it clearly, and Maren caught it anyway from the shape alone — the word was simple enough.

She held up four fingers. Nights ago. Before the audit order. Before the sealing of the ledgers that was supposed to be an honest clerk's precaution.

"You're certain," Ansel said, and now her lips were careful again, deliberate. "It wasn't — supplies? Records being moved inland?"

Maren almost laughed. Almost. Instead she reached into the basket at her feet and brought out the thing she had been keeping for two days without knowing why — a scrap of tarred canvas, torn from a discarded wrapping near the counting-house back door, printed with the army quartermaster's stamp and reeking of lamp-oil. Kerosene does not smell like anything else, and kerosene does not travel in crates marked *archival stores* unless it is meant for paper.

She put it in Ansel's hand and closed the girl's fingers over it.

Ansel looked down at the scrap for a long time. When she looked up, her eyes were wet but nothing spilled, and her jaw was set in the ledger-keeper's line, and Maren revised her opinion by one degree. Not *the pen is innocent*, then. Something harder underneath. *The pen is guilty, and knows it.*

"They'll burn the true names," Ansel said slowly, shaping every word as if teaching it to her. "Every diver. Every debt. Every man the company killed and called a loss. If the books burn, none of it ever happened. The army takes the town and the people become — numbers. Transfers. Nobody."

Maren nodded. *Yes. You see it. Now what will you do?*

Because that was the real question, and they both knew it. Knowing was cheap. The whole town half-knew; the whole town would do nothing, the way the whole town had done nothing for thirty years while divers went down into the cold and came up owing more than they'd descended with.

"I'm going to copy it," Ansel said. "All of it. The names. Before they burn the original." Her hands, Maren noticed, had begun to move as she spoke — small unconscious gestures, echoing her own words, as if the urgency needed two languages at once. "I can't do it alone. I need someone who watches the soldiers' hours. Who sees what I can't see when I'm inside the workshop."

Maren looked at her for a long moment.

Here was the thing nobody understood about the deaf: silence was not emptiness. Silence was a country, and Maren had been its citizen so long she had stopped wanting papers for anywhere else. To invite another person into that country was not a small thing. And this girl — the company's pen, the writer of Torvald Lund's death, the keeper of the book that had bound Maren's own brother's name to a debt he never owed — this girl was asking not for help but for complicity. For witness. *Be my eyes,* she was saying, *and I will be your hand.*

"You know what they'll say," Maren said aloud, her voice coming out in its unpracticed rasp. "About us. About this. Nobody believes—" she ran out of spoken words and finished with signs, quick and bitter: *deaf woman, indentured girl — who listens to us?*

"Nobody," Ansel agreed, and her mouth bent into something that was not quite a smile. "That's rather the point. No one watches what no one believes matters."

Outside, across the yard, a file of soldiers crossed between the drying racks, and both women went quiet and busy — Maren with her twine, Ansel turning the tarred canvas scrap over as if it were a piece of mending she'd come about. The soldiers passed without looking. In the counting house, through the high windows, lamplight moved where lamplight should not be burning at midday.

When they were alone again, Maren made a decision with the same economy she used for knots.

She reached under the bench and brought up a second stool, worn smooth, that had sat there empty since her mother died, and set it beside her own. Then she pointed at it, and at Ansel, and then at the counting-house windows.

*Sit here. Tell me everything you copy. Everything you fear. I will watch the rest.*

Ansel looked at the stool a moment too long. Maren saw her throat work once, saw her blink hard and fast, the way people do when kindness arrives in a place where they'd braced for nothing at all.

"All right," the girl said softly. "All right."

She sat. And for an hour, in the smell of tar and salt, the ledger-keeper of Skarnvik talked with her hands and her careful lips, and Maren mended nets and listened with her eyes, and neither of them said aloud — because it was too new, too breakable — the word for what was beginning between them.

It was nearly dusk when Ansel rose to go, and Maren caught her sleeve and asked the last question, the one that mattered:

*If they catch you copying — what happens to you?*

Ansel hesitated. And in the hesitation Maren read her as cleanly as any pair of hands: the girl already knew the answer and had decided it was acceptable.

"Nothing worse than what they plan for everyone else," Ansel said, and shaped it clearly so there could be no mistaking. Then she paused at the shed door, glancing back toward the counting house, and added, almost to herself, words Maren caught only because she never stopped reading: *"I need to find Tobias Brandt. If anyone knows how those ledgers are truly kept—"* and she was gone down the darkening path, straight-spined as a mast, carrying her invisible water.

Maren sat alone with the nets and the last light and the tarred canvas scrap still lying on her bench like a small black prophecy.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would begin watching which soldier carried the keys to the archive room.
