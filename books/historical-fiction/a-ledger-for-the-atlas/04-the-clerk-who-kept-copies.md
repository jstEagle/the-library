## 4. The Clerk Who Kept Copies

Tobias Brandt had learned, in the two years since the Halvard Trading Company cast him out, to measure his ruin in small units. Not in the whole of it — the whole was unbearable — but in increments. In the loss of the good chair by the counting-room stove. In the way the fishmonger's wife no longer met his eye when she weighed out herring. In the sound of his own name spoken in the tone one reserves for spoiled milk: *Brandt*, flat and finished, a word that had been wrung out.

He lived now above the chandlery on Quay Street, in a room that smelled of tallow and rope-endings, where the ceiling sloped so low he could touch it standing at the window. From that window he could see, if he leaned his forehead against the cold glass and looked hard past the drying racks, the workshop where Ansel Iversen kept the ledger by lamplight long after the other lamps of Skarnvik went dark.

He watched her the way a man watches a fire he started.

* * *

The trouble with falsifying entries, Tobias had told the inquiry board two winters ago, is that you must do it *badly* or not at all. They had not understood him then. He could see they did not understand him now, all these years later, replaying it as he shaved with cold water in the grey light.

Master Halvard's order had come down through three hands before it reached Tobias, the way dirty work always does, each intermediary washing a little more of it off onto the man below. The entry concerned the brig *Solveig*, whose cargo of dried cod had gone down off the Vester Rocks in a November squall. The company wished the loss recorded as insured salvage — a fiction worth eleven hundred crowns against their ledgers. And Master Halvard, who was careful in all things, had given one instruction above all others: make it look real.

Tobias had made it look real for six years. He knew every tell the company auditors hunted for: the ink aged too evenly, the hand too steady, the flourishes copied rather than grown. A true forgery must carry the small tremors of honest labor. So he had trembled his pen deliberately, blotted in the right places, left a margin note in another clerk's borrowed style.

The board had found the forgery in nine days.

That was how he understood, kneeling there with his coat buttons undone while Halvard's men carried out his desk and his good chair, what he had actually been ordered to do. Not to forge well. To forge badly enough to be caught, so that when the auditors came — and they were coming, some dispute among the owners having soured — they would find exactly one false entry, one disgraced clerk, and stop looking. He was not a forger. He was an offering. A scapegoat rendered up in clerical wool, bleating, to satisfy the wolves' arithmetic.

They had asked him at the inquiry, gently even, whether he acted alone. He had said yes. What else could he say? The order had come verbally; the intermediaries had denied everything with such smoothness that he understood they were untouchable, gilded all the way up. His honesty would only have bought him a longer fall.

So he took the disgrace like a coat handed to him, and put it on, and wore it two years in Skarnvik's weather.

But he had not been entirely unprepared. That was the thing nobody knew.

* * *

In his final week at the counting room, humiliated and already half-decided, Tobias had done the one act of his life he considered neither cowardice nor cleverness but simple bookkeeping. He had copied the ledger.

Not the public ledger — anyone might read that, and it lied the way all public things lie, pleasantly and in full sentences. He had copied the *second* ledger: the true columns, kept in cipher, in which the company recorded what it actually believed about its divers, its debts, its dead. The sums advanced against lungs and limbs. The interest compounded on widows. The entries marked with the small black cross that meant *written off* — which never once, in forty years of the company's existence, had referred to a debt forgiven. It referred to the debtor.

Ansel Iversen thought she kept the workshop's ledger. She did, in a sense — she kept the visible half of it, the half that faced outward, her fine clear hand recording depths and durations and the occasional death with a steadiness Tobias had admired from across the counting room for years. But every page she wrote was transcribed upward into cipher, corrected, adjusted. The company did not trust her numbers. It trusted no one's numbers. It trusted only its own secret arithmetic, and that arithmetic lived in a strongbox under the floor of the counting room, and Tobias Brandt had spent four sleepless nights copying it into a duplicate bound in sailcloth and hidden inside the hollow leg of the worktable he had built himself, the table now sitting in his rented room above the chandlery, holding his wash basin and his cold lamp.

He had told himself, at the time, that he copied it as insurance. Leverage. Something to bargain with when the fall came. Two years he had told himself this, and two years the sailcloth bundle had sat there untouched, and he understood finally that leverage was a lie he'd told a younger, more cowardly man. You did not copy forty years of drowned men's names as leverage. You copied them because someone had to, and no one else had.

The shame was not that he had forged an entry. The shame was that he had waited.

* * *

She came to him on a Tuesday, in the blue hour between the lamplighting and full dark, and she did not knock so much as arrive, pushing open the stair door with the certainty of a woman who has already decided.

"I know you're awake," Ansel said. "Your lamp's lit."

"My lamp is always lit. It's the only luxury I have left."

"Then we have that in common." She climbed the last stairs and stood in his doorway, taking in the room with one sweep — the low ceiling, the hollow-legged table, the man diminished inside it. She was nineteen or twenty, with her father's broad knuckles and a face that gave away nothing except, tonight, something moving behind the stillness like fish under ice. "I need to learn the cipher."

Tobias set down his cup very slowly. "What cipher."

"Don't." She stepped inside uninvited, the way people do when courtesy has become a luxury they can't afford. "I've been keeping the workshop ledger for six years. Every entry goes up to the counting room twice — my fair copy and a summary in figures. The figures don't match mine. Not the amounts. Not the dates. I checked against the tide tables and the pay sheets. Someone above me is rewriting the truth in a code of numbers, and you're the only clerk this town ever produced who was fired for touching the books." She turned to face him fully. "You didn't do it, whatever they say. No one gets caught that fast by accident. You were *made* to be caught. So either you know the cipher, or you know who does, and either way you owe me an answer."

The lamp guttered. Outside, a dog barked itself out along the quay.

"Who told you I was made to be caught?" Tobias asked quietly.

"No one. I read ledgers. I know what a planted error looks like — it looks *tidy*. Your forgery was tidy. Real mistakes are ugly." She folded her arms, those broad diver's knuckles white. "And there's another thing. Maren Solheim says soldiers are coming. Gunboats, requisition orders, the army taking the harbor. And when the army comes for a company's papers, the first thing a guilty company does is burn them. If the true record burns, every name in it becomes whatever the company says it was. My father becomes a debtor instead of a drowned man. Do you understand? The paper is the only witness any of us will ever get."

Tobias looked at her for a long moment. He had imagined this conversation a hundred times across two years, usually at three in the morning, and in none of those imaginings had the visitor been so young, so direct, or so entirely correct.

"There is a second ledger," he said.

Her breath went out of her, controlled, but he saw her hands loosen.

"It's in cipher — a columnar substitution, company-made, changed every fiscal year so no single clerk could ever hold the whole key. I held three years of keys before they dismissed me. I can teach you the system, and the system cracks the rest, because the company is arrogant and reuses its roots." He crossed to the worktable, knelt, and pressed the panel in the leg that only his hand knew. The sailcloth bundle came out heavy as a drowned bird. He laid it on the table between them and unwrapped it, and Ansel Iversen looked down at forty years of the company's true memory, and Tobias watched her face and saw the exact moment the fish stopped moving under the ice and surfaced.

"They sold debts," she said. Her finger rested on a column without touching the ink, as though the ink might be hot. "Look — these transfers. Quarter by quarter. Divers' debts, bundled and transferred to—" She stopped. Read again. "To military accounts. Why would the army buy a fisherman's debt?"

"That," Tobias said, "is the question I have been unable to answer alone for two years."

"And the black crosses?"

"Written off," he said. "It means written off."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something steadier than anger — the voice, he thought, of a woman who had decided.

"Three of us, then. There's Maren Solheim — she reads lips across the whole town, she sees everything that's said in it. She'll stand watch. She'll know things we can't." Ansel looked up at him. "I can't promise you your name back, Brandt. I can't promise anything except that the copy gets made and gets out, and that when the company falls, it falls on the record."

"You understand what happens to us if we're caught," Tobias said. "Not prison. The company doesn't use prisons."

"I'm indentured to them since I was thirteen. Everything after that is improvement."

Something in Tobias's chest, rusted shut for two years, moved on its axle. He pulled his chair — the bad chair, the only chair — to the table, took down his cold lamp, and turned the wick high.

"The current year's key starts from the founding date of the Vester station," he said. "We'll begin there. Bring candle stubs tomorrow — and bring nothing written in your own hand. From tonight, everything we commit to paper, we write as the company writes it. Their cipher. Their habits. If they ever find it, it must look like theirs, not ours." He opened the sailcloth bundle flat and smoothed the first page with both palms, the way a man smooths a shroud. "Now watch my hands."

Outside, far down the harbor road, a lantern swung — Maren Solheim's signal, one pass and a pause, the coast clear. Three of them, then. A disgraced clerk, an indentured girl, and a deaf woman with the whole town's words in her eyes.

Tobias dipped the pen and began, for the first time in two years, to write honestly — which is to say, in cipher, in the enemy's language, telling the truth.

By Thursday, he would learn how little time they had. By Friday, the gunboats would be hulls on the horizon, and the burning would already be scheduled.
