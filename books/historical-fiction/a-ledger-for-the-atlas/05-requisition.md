## 5. Requisition

They came at the hour when the workshop smelled of tar and cold coffee, twelve soldiers in gray wool with their boots ringing on the planking like a second tide. Ansel heard them from the counting room, where she had been reconciling the October entries against Tobias's cipher key, and by the time she reached the doorway they were already spreading through the space as though they owned it — which, she understood with a lurch that settled quickly into something colder and more useful, they now did.

The officer in charge was young, pink-cheeked despite the wind off the harbor, and he carried himself with the particular stiffness of a man performing authority before an audience of his own men. He introduced himself as Lieutenant Voss and informed her that the workshop was requisitioned under Article Nine of the Emergency Maritime Statute, that the Halvard Trading Company's premises had passed into army custody pending audit, and that all personnel would be reassigned to labor appropriate to their contracts.

"Appropriate to their contracts," Ansel repeated.

Voss consulted a folder. "You are Iversen, Ansel. Indentured, Halvard Company, term of fourteen years, six elapsed." He looked up, and his eyes did the arithmetic on her face — twenty-three, perhaps, tired beyond it. "Your contract transfers to army administration effective this morning. You'll remain at your current station. The clerical work continues."

"The ledgers," Ansel said carefully, "are sealed."

"Sealed for audit," Voss agreed, and something in the way he said *audit* — lightly, like a man setting down a cup — told her everything the word no longer meant. "You'll have access only as required for continuity of operations. The quartermaster will determine what that means."

She inclined her head. She had learned, in six years of indenture, the precise geometry of a bow that concedes nothing: spine straight, eyes lowered just enough to seem obedient, hands folded so they could not tremble visibly. Voss took it for submission. Men almost always did.

* * *

They quartered eight of them in the loft above the drying racks, where the nets used to hang in their salt-stiff curtains, and four more in the counting room itself — *the counting room*, where the ledger lived in its iron cabinet, where Ansel had spent half her life bent over those pages by lamplight. They dragged their cots between the shelves of shipping manifests. They hung their rifles on pegs meant for oilskins. One of them, a boy who could not have been older than seventeen, asked her shyly whether the stove worked, and she showed him, and hated herself a little for how gently she did it.

By nightfall the workshop had transformed. It smelled of pipe smoke and boot grease instead of pitch and brine. The soldiers' voices filled the rafters — card games, complaints about the ration bread, a harmonica played badly and then, mercifully, put away. Ten feet above her head, as she sat at her desk pretending to copy correspondence, they slept. She could hear them breathing. One snored in a rhythm she would come to know as well as her own heartbeat, a broken wheeze-and-catch, wheeze-and-catch, marking the hours she could not sleep either.

The ledger cabinet stood against the far wall. A padlock now — army issue, brass-bright, absurdly new against the old iron. But the cabinet had been opened that first morning, before Voss posted his sentry, and Ansel had watched three sergeants carry out a crate of documents with the unhurried confidence of men removing furniture. She had counted what left the room: one crate, and within it — she had seen the spines as they loaded them — the 1893 debt rolls, the consignment books for the northern fleet, and the mortality register for the years her father's name would have appeared in it.

Gone. Or moved. She did not yet know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like swallowed stone.

But not everything had gone. She made her inventory across three days of serving tea, and the inventory was this: the master debt ledger remained. The diver indenture rolls remained. The payment schedules, the interest accruals, the quarterly reconciliations — all remained, locked behind the brass padlock, ten feet from where the enemy slept.

* * *

The tea became her ritual and her instrument.

Lieutenant Voss took his at seven each morning, black, no sugar, while reviewing orders at the desk he had claimed near the window. Sergeant Dahl took his at noon with enough sugar to make syrup, and liked to talk — about his farm outside Bergen, about his wife's hens, about nothing at all — and Ansel learned that a lonely man mid-conversation forgets to watch what his own hands are doing. The youngest soldier, Eirik, whose name she learned because he told her unprompted on the second day, drank his standing at the door and always glanced toward the harbor, toward home, and she filed that away too: a boy who wanted the war elsewhere.

She poured, she smiled, she said *of course, sergeant* and *careful, it's hot* and *yes, lieutenant, right away*, and beneath the smile her mind worked like a dredge, hauling up details and sorting them in the dark.

Monday: Voss received a dispatch and burned it in the stove after reading. He read it twice. His mouth went thin at whatever the second reading found.

Tuesday: Dahl mentioned, complaining about the damp, that the archive crates had gone to the customs house rather than the fort — *someone's idea of secure storage*, he said, rolling his eyes at the army's logistics — and Ansel kept her face smooth as glass while inside her something turned over and began to plan.

Wednesday: Eirik, restless on sentry duty outside the counting room, confessed to her that he'd been ordered to log every person who approached the cabinet, but that nobody had told him what to do about the cat. The company cat, a scarred gray thing named Kråke, slept on top of the cabinet most afternoons. "Do I write down the cat?" Eirik asked, genuinely troubled, and Ansel said, "I'd write down the cat," and he laughed, and she laughed with him, and both of them meant none of it.

That was the shape of her days now: performance laid over purpose like paint over rot. She memorized the rotation of the sentries — two hours on, four off, sloppy after midnight. She noted that Voss carried the cabinet key on a chain inside his coat, not on his belt, and that he removed his coat each evening at nine and hung it on the back of his chair until reveille. She learned which pages had already been pulled from the files left in the open shelving — there were gaps, clean rectangles of less-faded paper where documents had been lifted out, and she catalogued the gaps the way Maren catalogued weather: precisely, obsessively, because lives might depend on the count.

Forty-one gaps in the open files. Forty-one documents vanished since the requisition. She wrote the list in her head each night before sleep took her, and recited it again at dawn, and if the numbers ever shifted she would know the army was still feeding.

* * *

Maren came twice a week with mended nets for the soldiers' patrol boats, and these visits were the only hours Ansel breathed freely.

They spoke in the yard, ostensibly about canvas and cork floats, while a bored sentry watched from the door. Maren's hands moved in their private shorthand — the signs they had built over years, part gesture, part invention, invisible to anyone who didn't know that the net-mender's fingers talked. *How many?* Maren signed, nodding toward the building. *Eight sleeping, four waking, one key on his chest,* Ansel answered aloud, in the flat cadence of inventory talk, while her hands said the rest. *Crates at the customs house. Forty-one pages gone.*

Maren's face never changed; a lifetime of being spoken about without hearing had taught her a stillness deeper than most people's sleep. But her hands, coiling a net edge, tightened on the twine until the knuckles whitened. Then, deliberately, she held up the mended net between them — a wide panel of new mesh, knots fresh and strong — and let it fall folded over her arm.

Ansel understood. *We have a container.* When the time came, the truth could leave wrapped in ordinary work, hidden in plain sight among a hundred identical bundles, carried out by a deaf woman no one thought to search because no one remembered she could be told anything worth stealing.

"Good work," Ansel said aloud, for the sentry. "The boats will hold."

"They'll hold," Maren said, meeting her eyes, and her mouth curved in the smallest possible smile — the smile of two women passing a secret through a wall of watching men, unopened, undetected, alive.

* * *

On the ninth night, unable to sleep beneath the wheeze-and-catch of the snoring loft, Ansel rose quietly and stood at the counting room window. Frost feathered the glass. Beyond it, Skarnvik lay dark except for the customs house, where lamplight burned in a second-floor room that had no reason to burn past midnight unless someone was working — reading, copying, deciding the fate of forty-one stolen pages and everything still locked downstairs.

A floorboard creaked behind her. She turned, heart steady, face arranged into mild insomniac innocence, and found Lieutenant Voss standing in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, watching her with an expression she could not fully read.

"You're up late, Iversen."

"The frost on the glass, sir. It reminded me of—" she hesitated, choosing the truth because lies needed energy she was saving, "—of patterns. Entries in a ledger. Everything repeating."

Voss came to stand beside her, close enough that she smelled the tobacco on him, and followed her gaze to the lit window across the water. For a long moment neither spoke. Then he said, quietly, almost to himself: "There are men in this town who think paper is harmless. That it's only paper." He turned and looked at her directly, and his young pink face was suddenly much older. "I've begun to think they're wrong."

He wished her good night and went up the stairs to bed.

Ansel stood alone in the dark, her pulse loud in her ears, turning his words over like a coin she couldn't identify — warning, confession, or trap. Across the harbor the light in the customs house went out.

Whatever was coming, it was coming soon. And she had not yet copied the master debt roll.
