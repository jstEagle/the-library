## 6. A Debt Paid in Salt

Maren felt the conscription before she saw it — a change in the way the town's mouths moved.

She had learned long ago that Skarnvik spoke twice: once with voices and once with faces. The voices were mostly lost to her, drowned years ago in the fever that took her hearing the same winter it took her father's boat, but the second language never left. She read it across the fish market, across the drying racks, across the church steps where the women gathered after service to say what they would not say inside. And on the morning the soldiers came down Harbor Row with their lists, every face in Skarnvik turned toward her brother's door.

Eirik was mending his own nets on his own porch, which should have been warning enough. Eirik did not mend nets. He bought and sold herring by the barrel, loud as a gull and twice as greedy, and he had not touched a needle since he was twelve. But there he sat in the grey light, working a knot badly, and when Maren came up the path he looked at her hands first — always her hands, because hers were the only voice he trusted — and signed, small and fast against his thigh: *They have my name.*

She knew before he finished the sign. She had known since dawn, since she'd seen the officer's lips move outside the requisition office, shaping words that traveled through the crowd like sparks: *debtor. arrears. labor levy.* She had watched the town's second language carry those words door to door faster than any crier could shout them.

*What debt,* she signed back, though her hands already shook.

*Fourteen kroner. Interest compounded. Dated six years past.* Eirik's face had gone the color of cod belly. *I never owed fourteen kroner in my life, Mare. I've never owed Halvard a thing. I pay cash. You know I pay cash.*

She knew. Everyone in Skarnvik knew that Eirik Vold paid cash and counted the coins back twice. It was one of the few things about her brother that had ever made Maren proud.

*Show me the paper,* she signed.

He showed her. A company chit, stamped and initialed, the debt entered in a neat clerk's hand. Maren read numbers well enough — numbers were honest even to the deaf — and then she read something else, something that stopped her breath in her throat like cold seawater.

The initials at the bottom of the entry. *A.I.*

Ansel Iversen's hand. Ansel's careful, upright figures, the ones Maren had watched her set down night after night in the workshop ledger by lamplight, the ones that named every diver and debtor and dead man in Skarnvik. The handwriting of the girl who had become, without either of them ever saying the word aloud, family.

*This is her writing,* Maren signed, and held the paper so hard the stamp bent under her thumb.

Eirik looked away. He had never liked that Maren spent her evenings with the diver's daughter and the disgraced clerk, three people whispering — no, not whispering, worse, *signing*, in a language he couldn't follow, keeping secrets in his own harbor. Now his mouth worked around words he didn't know she could half-follow from the shape of them: *doesn't matter whose writing. They'll take me Thursday. Labor levy. Two years minimum, they're sending men north to break rock for the fortifications.*

Two years. The officers said two years. Maren had watched enough soldiers' mouths this week to know what two years meant when the army said it, and it did not mean two years.

*We can fix it,* she signed. *There's still time. There are people who—*

*Who? You?* Eirik's laugh came out silent, just the baring of teeth. *A deaf woman and a thief's ledger? Mare. Go home.*

She went home. Then she went to the workshop.

* * *

The workshop smelled of tar and lamp oil, and underneath both now, faintly, of soldiers — boot grease and pipe smoke soaked into the walls where men had slept for five nights running. Ansel sat at the ledger table with the false book open before her, the one the company kept for officers' eyes, making entries in her beautiful honest-looking hand while Sergeant Dahl dozed in the corner chair with his rifle across his knees.

Maren stood in the doorway until Ansel looked up. Then she stepped inside, closed the door with exaggerated care, and let her hands speak.

*My brother. Eirik Vold. Conscripted Thursday. Forged debt. Your initials.*

She made herself go slowly, so there could be no mistake, no room for the soft blur of excuse. Ansel's face went white in stages, like a photograph developing in reverse. She rose halfway out of her chair, and her lips formed Eirik's name, and then *forged*, and then nothing at all, because Dahl stirred in the corner and everything dangerous had to fold itself small again.

*Not here,* Maren signed. *Tonight. The net shed. Bring nothing.*

* * *

Tobias Brandt arrived first, slipping in through the seaward door of the net shed with his collar up and his eyes doing their quick clerk's arithmetic of the dark — exits, shadows, sight lines. Ansel came twenty minutes later, having walked the long way around the racks, and when she saw Maren's face she stopped as if she'd hit glass.

*Say it isn't true,* Maren signed.

"It isn't mine." Ansel said it aloud and signed it too, her hands clumsy but earnest, the way she'd taught herself over two winters of lamplit lessons. "Maren, I swear on my father — I never wrote that entry. I never saw that entry."

"Then how is your hand on it?" Tobias asked quietly. He'd learned to watch Maren's signs and infer Ansel's replies from context, and he was better at it than he admitted. "Forged well enough to pass an officer's audit?"

"I don't know." Ansel's voice cracked on the second word. "I write hundreds of entries. Hundreds, Tobias. If someone copied my hand — if Master Halvard had a sample and wanted an entry that couldn't be traced to any living clerk—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "It's my name on the page. That's all that matters. My name sent him north."

Maren watched the girl come apart and felt something in her chest tear along an old seam. She had lost her hearing and her father in one winter and learned to survive it by trusting only what she could see. What she could see now was a nineteen-year-old who kept other people's debts in her own blood, and who had just discovered that the pen could be stolen from the writer's hand and used as a knife.

But grief is not patient, and guilt does not wait its turn. What Maren felt rising in her was simpler and older than sympathy.

*You wrote me once,* she signed, harsh, fast. *Three years ago. My net license fee, doubled. I thought it was a mistake. I paid it. Did you write that one too?*

"I—" Ansel stared. "That was real. That was ordered, but it was real."

*Ordered.* Maren's hands cut the air. *Everything is ordered. Everything has a master's name behind it and yours on top. And I sat across from you all winter teaching you my language while your hand buried my brother.*

"Maren." Tobias stepped between them, palms up, the old peacemaker from the counting house days. "Listen to me. Both of you. This is exactly what they want — us at each other's throats instead of looking north at who holds the stamps." He turned to Ansel. "And you — I'm sorry, girl, but I told you. I told you the moment the gunboats anchored. We finish this copy and we are dead, all three, and now we're dead plus one man breaking rock in the north. The entry was forged to flush us. Don't you see it? Someone suspects. Someone put your initials on a lie and waited to see who came running to the net shed tonight." He gestured around the dark shed, at the nets hanging like shrouds. "Take what we have. The pages are good, half the cipher is broken. We go south on the coal barge Friday and give it to a newspaper in Bergen and let God sort the rest. Living witnesses beat perfect ledgers."

"And Eirik?" Maren signed, and made him look at her hands. *Your escape leaves my brother in the quarry. Your escape leaves every name you haven't copied yet erased Thursday when they burn the archive. Is that the arithmetic you learned in the counting house, Tobias Brandt?*

"The arithmetic," Tobias said, and his voice went low and bitter, "is that I have already been arrested once for this company's paperwork. I know how it ends. It ends with one person in a locked room and everyone else gone."

"Then it ends with me," Ansel said.

Both of them turned. She had gone very still, the way she did over a difficult column of figures, and when she lifted her chin Maren recognized the expression — she had seen it on drowning men, once, from the shore, before anyone understood what they were doing. Not panic. Resolution.

"They forged my hand because my hand is believed. Fine. Then my hand is the weapon they handed me. Every day that ledger sits unburned is a day I can copy. Eirik ships Thursday. Which means the archive burns after — the order hasn't come yet, or the sergeant wouldn't still be sleeping off his pipe in the corner chair. There is time. Three days, maybe four." Her voice steadied with each word, terrible and bright. "I can finish the true names. All of them. Including whatever page explains why the company suddenly needs a debt on a man who never owed one — because that forgery didn't come from nowhere, Tobias. Debts get invented for one reason. Someone is *selling* them."

The shed went quiet except for the sea working at the pilings below.

Maren stood in that silence and hated every person in it, herself included. She thought of her brother's bad knots on the porch, of the coin-counting pride of him, of two years turning to stone in a northern quarry. She thought of a girl by lamplight, patient as tide, writing down the town's whole drowning in a fair and careful hand.

Can you forgive the pen? she asked herself. The pen that was also a daughter, that was also the only pair of hands in Skarnvik moving fast enough?

She crossed the shed. She took Ansel's face in her two rough palms the way she used to hold her brother's, when they were children and the fever was deciding which of them to keep. Then she let go and signed, slow enough that even Tobias could follow:

*Thursday. Before the boat takes him, we find the page that forged his debt. Then you finish your copy. And if you fail — if one more name of mine ends up in your book — I will testify myself that you wrote every lie in it.*

Ansel nodded. Her eyes were wet but her hands, answering, did not shake.

*Agreed.*

From the dark of the loft above them came a sound none of them had heard enter: a board settling, or boots shifting, or nothing at all. Tobias froze with one finger raised. Maren watched the window, where the moon laid the shadow of the drying racks across the glass like bars.

Someone had been listening.
