## 7. The Cipher Night

The candle stubs came from everywhere and nowhere. One from the officers' mess, pocketed under a saucer of spilled tea. Two from the chapel, where the army chaplain burned votives down to nothing and never counted what remained. The last—thick, tallow-scented, half its wick drowned—Tobias produced from his coat with the sheepish air of a man presenting a stolen relic.

"Four hours of light," he whispered. "Maybe five if we don't breathe too much."

Ansel set the stubs in a row along the workbench like soldiers awaiting orders. Beyond the workshop's shuttered windows, Skarnvik lay under curfew, the harbor lights doused since dusk, the gunboats at anchor showing only their riding lamps—a scatter of red points on black water, patient as ticks. Somewhere out there, sentries walked the drying racks where she had watched the steamers come. Somewhere closer, in the counting room above them, Master Halvard's son slept with a pistol by his bed and a key ring beneath his pillow that no longer matched every lock it was meant to open.

She pulled the second ledger from beneath the false floor of the tar chest, where Tobias had kept it hidden for eleven years, through his disgrace and dismissal and the long gray years after, when the company let him stay in town only because a man who knew too much was more useful alive than dead and questioned.

It was smaller than the true ledger Ansel kept. Bound in gray sailcloth, its corners worn to softness, its pages dense with columns of figures in two hands—one neat and clerical, one hurried and slanting. The cipher sat between them like weather: numbers where words should be, words where names should be, every diver's debt rendered as a sequence that looked, to any auditor's eye, like freight tonnage.

"You'll want to start with the key," Tobias said. "Most people do. They hunt for the key like it's a treasure."

"Isn't it?"

"No." He turned the ledger toward her, opened to the first page, and laid his finger on a column of figures. "The key is a habit. Halvard's father invented this cipher when he was young and clever and hadn't yet learned that cleverness is cheapest when it's cruel. It doesn't use a word or a number you can steal. It uses *the ledger itself*. Every entry is read against the entry before it. The difference between two numbers is a letter. You can't decode one page. You can only ever decode the whole book, in order, forward." He smiled without humor. "He thought it made the system elegant. What it actually made was a confession that could only be read all at once."

*Like a drowning,* Ansel thought. *You cannot understand it partway.*

Maren settled onto the stool by the window, her mending basket on her knees, a half-finished net draped across her lap so that her hands would have honest work if anyone looked in. Her eyes moved over Ansel's face, reading it the way she read everything—lips, brows, the angle of a jaw—and then she signed, quick and economical: *I watch. You work. Don't look up unless I knock twice.*

*How long since you slept?* Ansel signed back.

*Sleep is for people whose brothers aren't conscripted.* But Maren's mouth softened as she said it, and she reached across the table and pressed her thumb once against the back of Ansel's hand—the sign they had made between themselves, the one that meant *enough*, the one that meant *I'm here*.

They began.

* * *

The first hour was agony. Tobias taught; Ansel absorbed; the candle stubs burned down in their slow, uneven way, each guttering flame throwing shadows that leapt whenever the sea wind pressed at the shutters. The cipher resisted her the way deep water resists a lantern. She would solve three entries and lose the thread on the fourth, because the fourth depended on an error in the third—an error deliberately planted, Tobias explained, so that a forged copy would unravel. The company's whole architecture was built on the assumption that no one would ever have the patience to read it entire.

"But you have patience," Tobias said, watching her work. "That's what I understood about you, back when I still worked there and you were just the diver's daughter who fetched ink. You don't flinch from tedium. Most people treat a ledger like a chore. You treat it like a tide chart. You believe it's going somewhere."

"It is going somewhere," Ansel said. "Everything goes somewhere. That's the problem."

Her hands moved through the decoding with a fluency that surprised even her—subtraction, letter-assignment, cross-reference against the true ledger's dates. She had spent nine years recording other people's debts in plain script while this parallel text recorded them again in disguise. Every name she had ever written appeared here twice: once as herself had known it, once as the company had sold it.

And then, near midnight, near the bottom of the third candle, the sold part became visible.

She had decoded a run of entries from the spring of 1894—the year the herring failed and the divers borrowed desperately against their own lungs. In the true ledger these were marked *extended*, which she had always read as mercy. Here, in cipher, the same entries carried a different notation, repeated over and over with small variations:

*Transferred — Northern Military Provisioning Authority — consideration received.*

She read it again. And again. Her lips formed the words silently, and she was distantly aware of Maren turning from the window to watch her mouth move, reading the shape of a catastrophe being born.

"Tobias." Her voice came out very level, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "What is the Northern Military Provisioning Authority?"

He leaned over. He was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, it was barely above breath. "Read me the surrounding entries."

"The consideration column—it's not coin. Look—" She turned the page, decoded faster now, her fear sharpening her rather than blunting her, the way cold water sharpens. "March 1894, Ivar Sund's debt, four hundred crowns, transferred. April, the Oskssen brothers, eleven hundred crowns between them, transferred. June—my father's. My father's debt, transferred. Consideration received: *one hundred twenty rifles, carriage and ammunition included.*"

The workshop was silent except for the candle flames breathing.

"They didn't lend money to keep the divers diving," Ansel said. Each word arrived separately, like stones set down one at a time. "They lent money to acquire the divers. The debts were the cargo. They were selling men to the army before the army ever anchored in our harbor. The conscription lists—the ones the officers are using now—they're not new documents. They're *invoices*. We've been inventory for years."

"Not we," Tobias said quietly. "Them. And yes—you. Your father's debt bought rifles, Ansel. I'm sorry. God knows I'm sorry. I processed transfers like that for six years before I understood what the cipher was carrying, and when I did, I started copying it instead of filing it, and I did it badly on purpose, and they caught me, and they let me live because my falsifications were so obvious they proved I wasn't clever enough to be dangerous." He laughed, a dry, terrible sound. "Stupidity as an alibi. I've dined on it for a decade."

Ansel stared down at the page. Her father's name—not his name, his *number*, 447, but she knew the way the clerk's hand had curved the 4, knew it from a thousand entries—sat in a column beside the price of rifles. He had gone into the water owing four hundred crowns and he had died owing the army a debt no widow could pay and no grave could close. The company had traded his drowning southward like bales of salt fish.

She thought of Widow Sund spitting at her feet. She thought of Maren's brother, marched off on the strength of a forged entry in Ansel's own handwriting. She thought: *I am not the pen. I am the barrel. I am the rifle they paid for.*

"Keep going," she said.

"Ansel—"

"*Keep going.* If it leads upward, I want to see how high."

* * *

It led upward.

By the third hour the pattern was undeniable. Transfers in 1894. Renewals in 1895. In 1896 the cipher's notation changed—new hand, new habits, Tobias identified the slant as young Halvard's—and with the change came expansion. Not just debts now. *Contracts*. Divers' future labor assigned to military salvage operations. Harbor rights. Warehouse leases pre-signed against an occupation that would not arrive for two more years. The requisition that had fallen on Skarnvik like a hammer last week had been drafted in this workshop before Ansel was old enough to hold a pen straight, priced and initialed and filed away, waiting for the government's convenience to catch up with the company's ambition.

And at the top of the final coded page, in the clear hand of old Halvard himself, a summary line that needed no cipher at all, because it was written to be read only by whoever already knew everything:

*The archive is the asset. Protect the paper above the people. Paper convicts upward; people convict downward. Burn nothing until the paper's value is exhausted, then burn everything.*

"There it is," Tobias said. His voice had gone thin. "There's the order. It's been sitting there the whole time. Burn nothing until—" He stopped.

"Until the audit finishes," Ansel said. "Until the army confirms the transfers. Until the officers have extracted whatever they need to bury their own signatures. And then the workshop burns, and the fire is called an accident, and the only evidence that anyone in this town was ever sold is ash." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "Burning it doesn't destroy the crime. Burning it protects the guilty. That's why they gave me the torch. That's why it has to be me—I keep the records, so I must be seen to end them. My hands make the story true."

Across the room, Maren had risen from her stool without either of them noticing. She stood at the window, motionless except for her hands, which moved in the dark with terrible clarity, signing not to Ansel now but to herself, rehearsing, repeating, the way she did when something had to be remembered exactly. Then she caught Ansel looking and turned and signed directly, slowly, so there could be no mistake:

*Then we finish the copy. All of it. Before they give you the torch.*

"We don't have time," Tobias said. "Four hundred names, cross-referenced, in cipher and in plain—weeks of work—"

Maren's hands cut him off mid-word. *You had eleven years.* Her eyes flashed in the candlelight, furious and grief-stricken and absolutely certain. *My brother had six days. We have whatever nights are left, and we will not waste them arguing.*

Ansel looked down at the gray sailcloth ledger, at the four candle stubs burning low together, at the red riding lamps strung across the black harbor beyond the window—each one marking a ship full of men who believed they were collecting debts, when they were in fact completing a purchase.

The trail went upward. Past the master's office. Past the counting house. Past the harbor, past the coast, into ministries and ministries' masters, into rooms she would never see, held by men who had never once smelled the water their fortunes were dredged from.

But paper traveled. Paper was patient. Paper, properly smuggled, could go where she never would.

"Maren's right," Ansel said. She drew the true ledger out from under the floorboards and set it beside its shadowed twin, the two books facing each other like a man and his reflection. "We start tonight. Every name. Both hands." She pinched out the shortest stub to save its wax, and in the sudden dimming her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because tomorrow, or the day after, someone is going to hand me a torch—and I intend to hand back something they can't burn fast enough to catch."
