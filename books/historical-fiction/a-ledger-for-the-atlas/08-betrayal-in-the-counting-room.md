## 8. Betrayal in the Counting Room

Tobias Brandt had spent eleven years learning to count other men's losses, and in all that time he had never once counted on the smell of burning tallow to frighten him.

It was the candle stubs that undid them. He understood that later, sitting in the locked room with his hands cuffed to the iron ring of a shelf bracket, turning the arithmetic over and over the way he used to turn over a suspect column until it confessed. The company kept its own inventory of everything — rope, pitch, lamp oil — and somewhere in that inventory was a line for candles, and the line had begun to run short by exactly the amount a man would need to read a ledger through two hundred pages of cipher, night after night, with a girl copying beside him and a deaf woman watching the window.

He should have stolen more. That was the bitter joke of it: the one crime of his life done *well*, and undone by thrift.

The knock came at the counting room door on a Tuesday morning thick with fog off the sound. Tobias was at his borrowed desk — they had given him back a desk after the requisition, as if the army's presence had laundered him into respectability again — reconciling cordage receipts against the master's new manifest. Ansel was across the room at her ledger stand, her pen moving with the small, even strokes he had come to love the way other men loved a face or a voice: the way she crossed her sevens, the way she left no blot, ever, not once in four years of entries about drowned men.

Master Halvard came in without knocking, which was itself an answer. Halvard knocked. Halvard was a man who believed doors were part of the order of things, like ledgers and God. Behind him came Lieutenant Voss in army grey, and behind Voss came two soldiers who did not look at anything but the exits, because they had been told to look at nothing but the exits.

"Tobias." Halvard set a ledger down on the desk between them. It was the second ledger — or rather, the shell of it, the decoy volume Tobias had built from a ruined shipping book, filled with plausible nonsense in the company's own hand. "You will explain this."

Tobias looked at the open page. He felt the strange calm that had visited him once before, the day they'd fired him — the day he'd falsified an entry badly on purpose, so badly that any auditor would find it, so that the fraud would be *visible*, a flare lit inside the books themselves. They had beaten him then, in the small ways companies beat men: dismissal, blacklisting, the slow hunger of years. He had thought he had already paid.

"Explain what, sir?" he said.

Halvard's finger came down on a figure. "This entry. Dated March. The *Vestri* cargo. I signed no such entry."

"No, sir," Tobias agreed pleasantly. "I wrote it."

The silence stretched. Across the room he heard Ansel's pen stop. Not scratch out, not falter — simply stop, the way water stops when a hand is laid flat on its surface.

"You wrote it," Halvard repeated.

"I've been writing entries since before you were master here, sir. Some of them were true."

Lieutenant Voss shifted his weight. He was younger than Tobias expected — twenty-six, perhaps, with a soft jaw and the impatient eyes of a man who had been promised a quick war and been given paperwork instead. "Mr. Brandt," Voss said, "this office has reason to believe certain pages have been removed from the company archive. Pages relating to diver debt transfers." He let the word *transfers* sit there, naked, between them. "Where are those pages?"

"I couldn't say, Lieutenant."

"You could say. You are the only man in this building who could say. You kept copies once before — we found your old work in the Kristiania files. A clerk who falsifies once falsifies always."

*Falsifies.* Tobias almost smiled. They still didn't understand him, these men of clean hands and dirty money. He had never falsified anything in his life except toward the truth. Every crooked number he'd ever written had been a message scratched on a cell wall, hoping someone someday would read it. And now someone had. Someone with crossed sevens and steady hands was at this moment carrying the whole rotten cathedral of Halvard's accounts in her head and her heart, and the only thing standing between her and this room was the speed of his confession.

So he confessed.

"It was me," he said. He heard his own voice go flat and clerical, the voice of depositions. "The missing pages. I took them. I've been selling company information for two years — to a broker in Bergen, if you want a name. You won't find him, he doesn't exist, but you'll waste a month looking, and I'll enjoy knowing it."

"Tobias—" Ansel's voice, sharp with alarm she was too young to hide.

"Be quiet," Tobias said, and made himself look at her, and made his eyes do what her father's eyes must have done when he signed the indenture papers: nothing kind, nothing readable. "You've got your work to finish, girl. Don't make it worse."

He saw her understand. It passed across her face like weather — fury first, then grief, then the terrible discipline she kept under both, the discipline of a woman who had learned at fifteen that feeling things loudly gets people killed. She bent back over her ledger. Her pen began to move. If her strokes wavered, only Tobias could have seen it, and he was already being taken by the arm.

* * *

The room they put him in had been a salt store once. The walls were stone gone soft with damp, furred white with efflorescence, and the single window was boarded so that the light came through the gaps in stripes, like a cage drawn on the floor. They cuffed him to the bracket of an old shelving unit and left him alone for three hours, which he recognized as technique. He had read enough interrogation manuals in the Kristiania archive — copied them, actually, in his days of quiet rebellion, tucked between invoices like prayers in a psalter — to know the shape of what would come.

First the waiting. Then the friendly officer with tea. Then the unfriendly officer. Then the long return to the friendly officer, who would now feel like a brother. Then the questions that had no right answers, asked in different orders until the answers contradicted each other. Then the offer. There was always an offer. Men broke on offers, not on fists; fists only taught you what the offer was worth.

Lieutenant Voss came himself, which flattered Tobias more than it should have. He brought the tea personally, in a proper cup, and sat on an upturned crate with his knees together like a boy at church.

"Mr. Brandt. May I call you Tobias?"

"You may call me whatever helps you sleep."

Voss smiled thinly. "You're protecting someone. That's clear enough. The question is who, and how much you care whether they hang." He sipped his own tea. "There's a girl in that workshop. Indentured. Iversen. Her father drowned in debt to your employer — you knew that, I imagine. Interesting thing about indentures, Tobias. Under martial law they transfer. The army can conscript her tomorrow, send her north to the labor camps, and no court in this country will hear her name for five years. Or —" he set the cup down with a click — "you tell me where the pages went tonight, and I lose interest in her entirely. A disgraced clerk acting alone. It happens all the time. Greed, mostly."

"She's a child," Tobias said.

"She's a criminal, same as you. But she's a *young* criminal, and young ones break fast, and what breaks them is usually hearing that someone they trust sold them out first." Voss leaned forward. "So sell her out. Beat me to it. It's the kindest arithmetic available to either of you."

Tobias looked at his hands. The cuffs had worn a red line into each wrist, and he found he was grateful for the pain; it gave the moment a spine. Somewhere beyond the boarded window he could hear the harbor sounds — gulls, a winch, the army steamer coughing at anchor. Maren's town. Ansel's town. A town where every name in every book had a body attached to it, and the bodies floated up gray in the nets some mornings, and nobody had ever once been made to answer for it.

He thought about the night they'd decoded the debt transfers — Ansel's candle guttering, her finger stopping on a line, her voice going very quiet: *They sold Papa. They sold him twice. Once to the sea and once to the army.* He thought about Maren signing through the window glass, quick and urgent, *Boat coming, hide it,* her hands fluent as birds. He thought about how he had spent eleven years believing himself a coward because he had survived, and how these last weeks had taught him the opposite lesson: that survival could be a craft, practiced daily, refined, aimed — that staying alive near the truth was itself a kind of courage, until the day it wasn't enough, and then you spent yourself.

"Here's my counteroffer," Tobias said. "I acted alone. I forged the Vestri entry. I removed the pages. I sold them to a broker in Bergen. Write it down. I'll sign it in front of witnesses, in triplicate — I know how you people love triplicate. And I will keep saying it, every hour, in every room, until it bores you into forgetting there was ever anyone else in this building worth noticing."

"And the girl?"

"What girl?" Tobias said, and smiled, and it cost him everything he had, and he paid it gladly. "There's dozens of girls in this town. Half of them owe the company money. Ask them all."

Voss studied him for a long time. Then he stood, collected the cups, and paused at the door. "You know what happens to men who lie to the army under martial law?"

"I've read the statutes."

"Then you know I can hold you indefinitely. No charge, no trial. Just this room, and me, and eventually the cold." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "Everyone talks, Tobias. It's only a question of how much of them is left when they do."

The door closed. The lock turned. The striped light crawled another inch across the floor.

* * *

They came at intervals. Hours, or what he judged were hours — the fog ate the sun and his sense of time went soft as wet paper. Voss twice. A sergeant once, who hit him open-handed across the ear and apologized while doing it, which told Tobias the orders had come down gentle-for-now from above. They took away the tea after the second session, then the crate to sit on, then his boots, for reasons that were pure theater and worked anyway. Cold climbs. Cold finds the seams in a man.

In the gaps, Tobias rehearsed.

That was all interrogation was, in the end — a duel of rehearsals. They rehearsed their questions; he rehearsed his answers, saying them silently until they wore smooth as river stones, until no phrasing could be shaken loose from any other phrasing, until the story of the Bergen broker was more real to him than his own mother. He gave them details freely: the fictional man's fictional lodgings, his fictional habits, a scar on his left hand Tobias invented with real tenderness. He signed statements. He corrected his own signatures for consistency. He was, he realized somewhere in the third night, doing the best accounting of his life: every figure false, every column balanced, every entry designed to be found.

Once, in a lucid hour near dawn, he caught himself wondering whether Ansel had finished. Whether the copy was complete, hidden, moving — whether Maren's nets were already folded around it somewhere in the dark like a mother around a child. He decided it was better not to wonder. Wondering showed in the face. He filed the question away unopened, the way he'd learned to file everything that mattered, and looked instead at the stripe of light on the floor, and waited for it to move.

On what he calculated was the fifth day, the lock turned at an unusual hour, and it was not Voss who entered.

It was Halvard. Alone. No soldiers, no notebook, no tea. The company master stood in the doorway a moment, and Tobias saw something in his face he had never seen in thirty years of counting rooms: fear. Real fear, the animal kind, the kind that comes off a man like heat.

"They're burning it," Halvard said quietly. "Tonight. The whole archive — army orders, top to bottom, everything. And you know what that means, Brandt? It means someone above me believes the paper is dangerous. Which means somewhere, someone has *seen* it." His voice dropped further. "If you've moved anything — if anything of mine leaves this town —"

"Yours," Tobias said. His throat was raw and the word came out like gravel, and it was worth it. "*Yours.*"

Halvard stared at him. Outside, far off across the water, a bell began to ring — not the harbor bell, but the church bell, wrong, urgent, the alarm peal that Skarnvik hadn't sounded in a generation. And beneath it, fainter, the sound Tobias would carry to the end of his days: shouting, and the smell of smoke beginning to thread through the salt-stone walls.

Ansel was in the workshop.

The realization went through him colder than the floor, colder than the cuffs. They were burning the archive *tonight* — and they would make her stand there, make her watch, maybe make her light it herself, the diver's daughter burning her father's name —

Tobias rose as far as the chain allowed, wrenching his wrists against the iron, and the sound he made was not words at all.

Halvard stepped back into the corridor, and the last thing Tobias saw before the door swung shut was the lieutenant's silhouette in the passage beyond — and the flicker of orange light already climbing the frosted window at the end of the hall.
