## 9. Fire Is Also a Record

They gave her the oil at dusk.

Sergeant Halversen set the tin on the counting table between them, the way a man lays down a dead bird he's shot and expects to be thanked for. The lamplight caught the company seal on its shoulder — *Halvard Trading Co., Skarnvik Station* — and Ansel thought, absurdly, that even the fire had been purchased through the ledger. Even the burning was an entry. *One tin lamp-oil, issued for destruction of records, per order of Captain Voss.* Somewhere in her head she wrote it out in full, dated it, initialed it. It steadied her hands.

"You'll start with the debt rolls," Halversen said. "Master Eriksen wants them gone first."

"Of course," she said.

He lingered. He had been quartered in the workshop eleven days now, sleeping behind the canvas partition with two other men, and in that time he had eaten her bread, complained about her coffee, and once — when he thought she wasn't looking — stood over the open ledger reading names with his lips moving, like a boy sounding out scripture. She wondered if any of those names belonged to him. If some woman in some village south of here had watched him march away under a requisition order written by men who never learned to write anything but orders.

"The captain says you're to do it yourself," Halversen added. "No one else. He says you know which pages matter."

*Which pages matter.* As if the pages were not all of one flesh. As if you could burn the arm off a body and call the remainder whole.

"Yes," she said. "I know which pages matter."

He took her at her word. That was the thing about soldiers, she had learned: they believed whatever a quiet woman with ink-stained fingers told them, because their imaginations did not extend to what such a woman might do with eleven days, three candle stubs, and a deaf ally who could read lips through a window at forty feet.

She carried the tin of oil into the archive room and set it down beneath the shelf where the debt rolls lived — where they had lived until four nights ago, when Maren's nets came down from the drying racks heavy as a hauled-in catch and went back up heavier still.

Then she sat at the long table, opened the true ledger to its final unmarked page, and began the last entry of her life in Skarnvik.

The copy was nearly done. Eleven days of stolen hours — Tobias's cipher transcribed into plain script, the sold debts laid bare line by line, every diver's name matched to the army contract that had bought him. Her father's name was in there. She had saved it for last without deciding to; the pen simply refused it, night after night, the way a tongue refuses a bitter word. Tonight there would be no more refusing.

Outside, the harbor light swung its slow arc. Somewhere beyond the drying racks, Maren sat mending a net that needed no mending, her broad hands moving in their patient rhythm, her eyes on the workshop door. They had agreed on everything in signs and whispers: the bundle would leave at the changing of the watch, wrapped inside the largest net, carried to the boathouse, hidden under the tarred tarpaulin over the second skiff. Tobias would be waiting at the north road with the cart. By morning the truth would be ten miles inland and gaining.

And Ansel would be here, standing in the ashes, holding an empty oil tin.

*They will ask why the archive burned so completely,* she had rehearsed. *And I will say I fed the fire myself. Every page. I am thorough. Ask anyone.*

The lie was beautiful because it was almost true. She *had* fed the fire — fed it the false ledger, the one Tobias had built years ago out of ordered lies so badly made that they'd ruined him, the one no court would ever mourn. Let the soldiers sift those ashes forever. Let Master Eriksen sleep soundly believing his debts were smoke.

Ansel dipped her pen. Wrote her father's name. *Iversen, Anders. Diver. Debt assumed 1889 upon death of debtor. Balance outstanding: 340 kroner.* Beneath it, in letters small enough to hide in the margin but large enough that no clerk could later claim illegibility: *Balance collected in full by sale of debt to Army Requisition Office, contract 44-C. See annex.*

There. It was done. Ten years of Skarnvik, copied entire, and the weight of it left her body all at once, the way a rope goes slack when the load comes free. She closed the book and pressed her palm flat against its cover, and for a moment she let herself feel it — not triumph, not yet, just the terrible tenderness of a thing finished. Her father's ghost, which had haunted her since chapter one of her life, sat down beside her at the table and said nothing, and the nothing sounded like *well done, girl.*

* * *

The torching began at the far end, in the drying shed, which meant either the soldiers were impatient or someone had given them a schedule. Ansel heard the first shouts, smelled the first sweet reek of pitch going up, and moved.

Oil into the hearth of the counting room. Oil across the archive floor — around the false ledger, which lay open and inviting on the table like a decoy duck. Oil along the shelf-ends where the flame would climb fastest. She worked quickly and without artistry; the army wanted a bonfire, and she saw no reason to disappoint them.

At the window, three sharp taps. Then two. Then three again.

*Maren. Now.*

Ansel wrapped the true ledger in oilcloth, bound it with cord, and carried it out the back door into the blue dark. The nets hung from the racks like the rigging of drowned ships, silvered with frost, smelling of salt and weed and thirty years of other women's hands. Maren met her there, breath steaming, and together they fed the bundle into the belly of the great net — deep, deep, into the folds where a searching hand would find only knots. Maren gathered the whole mass onto her shoulder with the ease of a woman who had carried half the town's catch since she was nine, and looked at Ansel, and her face in the firelight said everything her voice never had.

*Come with us,* that face said. *Walk out now. No one is watching the water. We can be in the boat before they know.*

"I have to be seen," Ansel said aloud, because some things must be spoken even to the deaf. "If I'm not here when it burns, they'll hunt the town. They'll search every net, every boat, every house. But if I'm standing in it—" She touched Maren's cheek, briefly, the way her father used to touch hers. "Read my lips, old friend. *Finish it.* Whatever happens to me. Finish it."

Maren's jaw tightened. For one naked moment she looked ready to put the net down and simply refuse — to stand there, immovable, a wall of a woman against the whole Norwegian army. Then somewhere behind them a beam collapsed in a fountain of sparks, and duty won the argument the way duty always does, loudly and unwillingly. Maren signed something fast and fierce — Ansel caught only *alive* and *I will find you* before she turned and walked into the dark with the truth balanced on her shoulder, steady as a woman carrying eggs.

Ansel went back inside.

* * *

The workshop was beautiful on fire. She hated herself for thinking it, and thought it anyway. The flames turned the counting room gold, gilded the shelves, made the ledger table glow like an altar. Heat pressed against her face. Smoke lay down along the ceiling like weather. Through the front windows she could see soldiers gathering in the yard, faces lit orange, and among them Master Eriksen in his good coat, watching his debts ascend to heaven with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy flattering.

"Where is the girl?" someone shouted. "The clerk said she had the—"

Here.

She stepped out of the doorway into the yard with the empty oil tin still in her hand, soot to the elbows, hair singed at one temple, and let them see her. Let them see exactly what they expected: the diver's daughter, the indentured pen, the obedient instrument who had done as ordered.

"It's done," she said. Her voice cracked, and she let them believe the crack was grief. "Every page. I checked the bindings myself."

Eriksen crossed the yard slowly. Up close, in the firelight, she could see how old he had grown since the gunboats came — how the requisition had eaten him too, though he'd supped at the same table as the eaters. He studied her face for a long moment, and something flickered behind his eyes, some accountant's instinct whispering that the numbers did not quite sum.

"You were thorough," he said.

"I was thorough," she agreed.

Behind him, down the lane, a cart creaked northward into the dark, unhurried, unremarkable. A woman walked beside it pulling a net cart of her own. Neither of them looked back. Ansel kept her eyes on Eriksen and did not look back either, and it was the hardest thing she had ever done with her body, harder than holding her father's cold hand in the boathouse ten years ago, harder than writing the widow's name in the death column.

The sergeant took her arm. "You'll come with us, miss. Captain wants a statement about the destruction."

"Of course," Ansel said. "I have a very good memory."

They marched her past the burning workshop, past the drying racks already stripped bare, past the harbor where the army steamers rode at anchor like fat black beetles. At the edge of the yard she allowed herself one glance backward — a small one, a servant's glance, the glance of a woman mourning her life's work — and what she saw was the fire eating the false ledger, page by ordered lie, until nothing remained of Tobias Brandt's shame but light.

Let them read those ashes, she thought, as the gate closed behind her. Let the courts come, let the journalists come, let them dig through the cinders of Skarnvik and find exactly the story the company wanted buried — and then let someone, someday, spread Maren's nets on a dock in another country and shake out the catch.

She was still smiling when they locked the door.

It was, they would note in their own ledgers, a strange thing for a prisoner to do.
