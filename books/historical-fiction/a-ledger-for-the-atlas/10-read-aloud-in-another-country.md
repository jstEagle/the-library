## 10. Read Aloud in Another Country

The workhouse at Vesterhavn had no clock that Ansel could see from the laundry yard, and so she had learned to tell time the way she had once told weather off the water: by the angle of light on stone, by the shift of wind through the pines behind the wall, by the sound of the noon bell rolling down out of the town she could not enter. Nine years in this place and she still counted hours like a diver counting breath — not because she feared running out, but because counting was a habit of survival, and survival was the one skill they could not confiscate.

Her hands had changed. That was what struck her some mornings, wringing sheets in water cold enough to ache. The hands that had kept the Halvard ledger were a clerk's hands once — ink-stained at the second knuckle, precise, quick. These were laundress's hands now, cracked at the heels, nails cut blunt against infection, a white scar across the left palm where a boiling vat had taught her humility in her first winter. But the fingers still remembered. Some nights, lying in the narrow cot with its blanket rough as herring skin, she would move them against the mattress in the old cipher strokes, writing names into the dark where no one could burn them.

She had written every name nine hundred times by now. Perhaps more. It did not matter. A ledger's purpose was not to be read once; it was to *hold*.

The news came on a Tuesday, though she would not know it was Tuesday until later, when the young man arrived and told her so. It came the way most things came to Vesterhavn — sideways, in fragments, through women who traded gossip like contraband cigarettes. Greta, who worked the mangle beside hers, leaned close over the steam and said, without looking up, "They're saying the Halvard Company is finished. Collapsed. The newspapers in the capital have been printing nothing else for a week." She said it flatly, the way you report a death, but her eyes were bright. "They're saying there's a ledger."

Ansel kept wringing the sheet. Her arms knew their work even when the rest of her went somewhere distant — back across the water to Skarnvik, to the workshop, to the night the fire turned the rafters into falling constellations and she had walked out the front door with Maren's nets heavy on her shoulder and the true ledger sewn inside the mesh like a heart inside a ribcage. They had caught her at the gate, of course. She had let them catch her. That had been the whole design of the thing, designed in the space of three breathless days among three people who had no right to hope and had hoped anyway.

"Are you going to say anything?" Greta asked.

"What is there to say?" Ansel said. "Paper burns. What matters is what gets copied first."

But that night she could not sleep for the noise inside her own chest, which was not grief and was not joy but something older than both — the sound of a debt finally being called in against the people who thought themselves creditors of the world.

* * *

The young man came eleven days later, in the gray hour after morning ration, wearing a coat too good for the workhouse road and carrying a leather satchel that he held the way Tobias Brandt had once held his hidden ledger — as though it might be confiscated at any moment, as though everything worth keeping lived inside it.

He introduced himself at the matron's desk as a clerk. Not a journalist, he said carefully, though Ansel saw the folded newspapers in his satchel and recognized the dense columns of print that had begun circulating even here, passed hand to hand like relics. He was perhaps twenty-four. His penmanship, when he laid out his papers, was beautiful and slightly nervous, each letter standing very straight as if afraid of being corrected.

"I've come to take your testimony," he said. "If you'll give it."

"And if I won't?"

He looked up then, and something in his face made her think of herself at nineteen, lamplit, stubborn, certain that ink was neutral and learning otherwise. "Then I'll write down that you refused," he said, "and even that will be more honesty than the company ever put on its pages."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. Instead she sat, folding her scarred hands on the table between them, and studied him the way she had once studied new divers' entries — looking for the flaw, the falsification, the ordered lie.

"Who sent you?"

"The commission. The parliamentary inquiry into the Halvard Trading Company." He hesitated. "And before that — a woman named Maren. And a man who spent six years in custody and walked out in March with his head held so high the guards had to duck. They gave me your name and told me—" his voice caught, steadied— "they told me you'd know why I was here before I explained it. They said you'd been waiting."

Nine years of waiting, compressed into a stranger's certainty. Ansel looked past him, out the high barred window, to where the sea showed itself in a bright strip between rooftops. Somewhere beyond that horizon lay Skarnvik, its harbor requisitioned and returned, its drying racks empty or full again — she did not know which. Somewhere there was Maren, who read lips across a whole town and had carried nets heavier than any fisherman should ask of a woman. Somewhere was Erik, Maren's brother, whose forged debt entry Ansel had recorded with her own quick young hand, and who had come home — home! — from army labor that killed men slower than drowning but just as surely.

"They said he came back," Ansel said quietly. "Maren's brother."

"He came back in April. Thin as wire. He stands at the harbor mouth every morning and looks at the water, and then he goes to the net sheds and works beside her." The clerk's voice softened. "Maren says — well, Maren says very little with her voice, but she signs, and there are enough of us learning now. She signs that you wrote him back into the world. That the false entry died with the company, and the true record brought him home."

Ansel turned her hands over on the table. The scar caught the window light.

"I didn't bring anyone home," she said. "I wrote down what was true. Other people carried it."

"That," said the young clerk, uncapping his pen, "is exactly what the inquiry needs to hear."

* * *

He took her testimony for four hours. He was better at it than she expected — patient, precise, unwilling to hurry a name. When she reached the list of the dead, the divers lost to the deep and entered in the Halvard ledger as *asset depreciation*, he stopped writing for a moment and pressed his lips together until he had control of his face again.

"How many?" he asked.

"Forty-one during my years at the ledger. Before me, I couldn't count. The earlier pages used a different shorthand — Tobias could read it. He taught me." She watched him write *Tobias Brandt* and felt something loosen in her chest that had been knotted since the night soldiers dragged the old clerk away as her decoy while she stood smiling at men who smelled of gun oil and tea. Six years he had given for silence kept. Six years, and he had walked out unbent, they said, asking immediately whether the nets had made it north.

"They did make it," the clerk said, when she asked. "Through three countries. A fishing family carried them first, then a customs officer who'd lost a brother to a company contract, then—" he consulted his notes— "a schoolteacher in the capital who understood the cipher, because her grandfather had been a company clerk too. They decoded it in a boarding house over one winter. The newspapers began printing extracts in the spring."

"Extracts," Ansel repeated. The word tasted strange. For nine years the ledger had existed only in her memory, recited nightly like a prayer against forgetting. Now it was being read aloud in another country, in drawing rooms and courtrooms, by strangers with clean hands.

"The widow's testimony helped," the clerk went on. "Sigrid Lom, of Skarnvik. She identified her husband's entry. Depreciation, it said. She stood up in the hearing and read the page aloud herself, and then she spat on the floor of the chamber, and no one stopped her."

Ansel closed her eyes. Forty-one dead, and one widow's spit traveling nine years to land where it belonged.

When the clerk had filled his last sheet, he packed his satchel with the care of a man handling something consecrated, and then paused, half-risen from his chair.

"There's one more thing," he said. "It isn't official. The commission can't order it, and I may lose my position for saying it. But Mr. Brandt asked me to tell you himself, and I promised I would rather than let it arrive secondhand." He set down the satchel. "He's coming here. With Maren. As soon as travel papers allow — a few weeks, perhaps less. They mean to stand outside these walls every visiting day until someone lets them in, and if no one does, they say they'll wait anyway, because they've gotten quite good at waiting." He managed a small, unsteady smile. "Mr. Brandt says — his words — 'Tell her the copy survived, and so did we, and none of us is finished keeping records.'"

Ansel looked at the young clerk for a long moment. Then she looked down at her hands, the laundress's hands, the ledger-keeper's hands, and found she could not speak at all — not because the words were gone, but because there were suddenly too many, crowding like names into a margin too small to hold them.

"Tell them," she said at last, and her voice held, the way it had held through fire and interrogation and nine years of other women's linens, "that I kept count. Every day. Tell them the account is current."

* * *

That evening the bell rang for supper and Ansel did not go at once. She stood at the barred window watching the light fail over the sea, and in the glass her own reflection looked back at her — older, grayer, unbroken — and behind it, faint and impossible, the lamplit workshop of another life, the ledger open under her hand, the names marching down the page in her careful script.

They had taken her freedom and thought that settled the account. They had burned the original and thought that ended the story. But paper was only paper, and truth was a thing copied hand to hand, mouth to mouth, net to net — carried by ordinary people across borders no company owned, read aloud at last in rooms where power had to sit and listen.

Somewhere out on that darkening water, a boat would be coming soon. Two people on it, waiting on her.

Ansel smiled, slow as dawn coming up over the drying racks at Skarnvik, and went down to supper with her head high — a free woman in all the ways that mattered, serving out the last small sentence of a debt that had never, in truth, been hers.
