## 1. What the Orders Weigh

The river came for them in the third week of the rains, and after that the hospital lived with water in its boots.

Coral Okafor woke before dawn to the sound of it — not rain now, but the slower, patient noise of a flood finding its level, lapping at the tent stakes, seeping under the canvas walls in thin silver tongues. She lay on her cot with her boots on, as she had every night since the water rose, and listened to Private Wren coughing in the far row. The cough had changed overnight. It had gone from wet to hollow, and hollow was the sound that preceded the long silence.

She did not light the lamp yet. There was an hour — maybe less — before the orderlies stirred and the day began its arithmetic, and this hour belonged to her alone.

Under her pillow, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the ledger.

It was not an official document. It had never been issued to her; it had no stamp, no number, no place in any quartermaster's inventory. It had begun as a ration book, half-filled with columns of flour and salt beef, and when the rations ran short she had ruled fresh lines beneath them with a pencil stub and started writing names. That had been fourteen months ago, at a dressing station upriver that no longer existed except in her memory and, now, in hers alone.

She unwrapped it by feel. The pages had gone soft as cloth from handling.

*Wren, T., private,* she wrote, by the grey light coming through the canvas. *Fever, lungs. Third night. If he sees morning he may see many more.*

It was not a medical record. Sister Imelda kept those, immaculate, in a ledger of her own that would be packed into the regimental chest when they moved. What Coral wrote was something else. It was what the other book left out.

She thought of yesterday's report, the one Lieutenant Sassine had dictated to his clerk while she stitched a man's shoulder three feet away. *Casualties, engagement at the ford: four wounded, two dead.* Two dead. She had counted five herself before the stretchers stopped coming, and one of the five — Osei, a sapper, a boy who sang hymns off-key while he laid charges — had died on the table with his hand closed around hers, and his name had appeared nowhere. Not in the report, not in the burial detail's list, not in the letter some captain's clerk would eventually compose to a mother in a town she'd never see. He had simply been subtracted. Folded into the general confusion of the action, absorbed, erased — because to acknowledge him was to admit the patrol had crossed the ford where orders said it must not, and admissions had a way of climbing the chain of command until they found a throat to close around.

So Coral wrote him down. *Osei, D., sapper. Died 14th, wounds to the belly. Sang on the 12th. His mother's name is not known to me. Someone should know he was here.*

The pencil needed sharpening. She sharpened it with a knife she kept for the purpose, curling the shavings into the flooded floor where they floated a moment among the pale petals of plaster dust, then sank.

Outside, the river bridge stood black against the lightening sky. From the hospital you could just see its spine through the mist — the stone arches the engineers said would fall within the month if the water rose another foot, and which both armies nevertheless intended to hold, because holding bridges was what wars were made of, apparently, though no one she'd asked could explain what the bridge actually connected anymore. A road to a road. A reason to a reason.

She measured her days in what could be carried.

This was not a figure of speech; it had become an actual discipline, a nightly audit. When the evacuation order came — and everyone knew it would come, the water saw to that — each nurse would be permitted one kit bag, and the kit bags were small. So Coral had learned to weigh everything. Morphine tablets: carried, always, whatever else was sacrificed. Her scissors: carried. The spare shirt Imelda pressed on her last month: left behind without regret. Letters from men who would not survive to send them, entrusted to her in trembling hands: carried, every one, though they filled a whole satchel now and the satchel grew heavy as sin.

And the ledger. Always the ledger. It weighed less than a Bible and more than a mountain.

* * *

By full light the hospital was awake and drowning in work.

They had forty-one patients under canvas and canvas enough for thirty, and the water had claimed the lowest row entirely, so that the worst cases slept on trestles raised on ammunition crates, islands in an inland sea that smelled of mud and carbolic. Coral moved down the rows with a basin in each hand, and the basin work was endless — the washing, the cooling, the small unglamorous mercies that constituted most of nursing and none of its glory.

"Private Adeyemi," she said to the boy in the fourth cot, who was seventeen and had a bullet through his calf and the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to be brave. "How is the leg this morning?"

"It's fine, miss."

"It isn't fine. It's angry, and it has every right to be." She peeled back the dressing and studied the wound with a professional eye and a personal dread. The flesh around it had gone the wrong color in the night. "You'll let me open it again, and you'll curse me while I do it, and tomorrow it will be better."

"Will I go home after?"

She met his eyes. This was the cruelty of field hospitals: the questions were simple and the answers were not. "After the fever breaks," she said, which was true, and which was all she could afford.

While she worked, the men talked. They always talked, and she had learned that listening was a kind of medicine too, so she let the talk wash over her the way the river washed under the floorboards.

"—my cousin writes that the tribunal people are in the port city already. Adaeze Brandt herself, he says. Printing things about Kessel Ford."

"There's nothing to print about Kessel Ford. My sergeant says—"

"Your sergeant says what he's told to say. My cousin says Brandt's offering column inches to anyone with testimony. Real testimony."

A laugh from somewhere down the row, bitter as iodine. "Testimony. Half these men can't write their own names."

"They don't have to," said the first voice. "That's what the woman does. She writes it down for them. Puts it in print, back in the capital, where the generals have to read it."

Coral kept her face still and her hands busy. She thought of the weight of the oilcloth under her pillow, of fourteen months of names, and felt something move in her chest like a fish turning in shallow water.

Kessel Ford. Even the name had gone strange in the telling — two years old now, spoken only in the past tense, a battle officially remembered as a hard-fought withdrawal, a tragedy of weather and bad luck, closed, buried, done. She had been at a station sixty miles south when the survivors came through. She remembered how few there were, and how quiet, and how their commanding officer had met them at the road with a signed statement each man was required to copy into his service record before he could eat. She had not known what the statements said. She had known what fear looked like on forty faces at once, and she had never forgotten it.

"You believe that?" the second voice again, younger, needing to be told no. "That some journalist in the capital cares what happened to us?"

"I believe paper's the only thing they can't shoot," said the first.

*Paper's the only thing they can't shoot.* Coral turned the phrase over as she worked, testing its edges. She was not sure it was true. She had watched paper burn — orders, reports, letters — with the same efficiency as anything else. But she wanted it to be true, and wanting was itself a kind of supply, hoarded like morphine against a bad night.

* * *

Midmorning brought the officers' rounds, and with them Lieutenant Sassine, whose boots were dry, which meant he had come by horse rather than the causeway, and whose presence in the ward always rearranged the air the way a cold front does.

Sassine was not cruel. Coral had decided long ago that cruelty would almost be easier. He was a tidy man doing untidy work, and he performed it with the mild regret of an accountant forced to round down.

"Sister Almeida." He consulted a leather folder. "Your census."

"Forty-one. Three fevers, eleven surgical, the rest convalescent and dysentery."

"And your losses this week?"

"One. The sapper, on the fourteenth."

He made a note. She watched him make it, watched the pencil move, and knew with certainty that the note would die somewhere between this tent and regimental headquarters, filtered, diluted, gone. "The burial detail logged only the artillery lieutenant."

"The sapper died during surgery. He may have been entered under my station instead of the detail." She offered this the way one offers a rope to a drowning man who insists he can swim — knowing he'll refuse, knowing the refusal is the point.

"Mm." He scanned the ward with eyes that catalogued without seeing, the way a man counts coins without reading the dates. "You should prepare for movement, Sister. The water's against us. Within the week, I should think."

"We're ready," said Coral, and heard the lie sit comfortably in her mouth, which frightened her more than the lie itself. "We drill the packing daily."

"Good. The colonel wants the bridge garrison reinforced, so the transport will be thin. Prioritize your ambulatory cases. The others—" He paused, and in the pause Coral felt the whole machinery of the war turn over, vast and indifferent, selecting. "—the others will follow as transport permits."

*As transport permits.* She had nursed long enough to translate. The others would follow if the others followed. If the water rose faster than the wagons came, the others would be left with Imelda and the chaplain and whatever mercy the enemy extended, which varied, and was not policy.

"Yes, sir," she said.

At the tent flap he stopped, half-turned. "You've served at how many stations now? Six?"

"Seven, sir."

"Seven." He nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "You must have seen a great deal that never made it into the reports."

The question hung there, innocent or otherwise, and Coral felt the ledger's weight shift from her pillow to her ribs, as if the book had migrated into her body sometime in the night and now beat there alongside her heart.

"I see what I see, sir," she said. "Most of it goes into the reports."

"Most," he repeated, pleasantly, and was gone.

She stood a moment with the basin water going still in her hands. Then she went back to work, because work was the only prayer she trusted.

* * *

Evening came down grey and dripping, and with it the ritual.

She waited for the ward to settle — the fevers burning low, the talk guttering out, Wren's cough reduced to a whisper that was somehow worse than the roar — and then she took out the lamp and turned its wick so low the flame was barely a seed, and she wrote.

This was the part no one knew about. Not Imelda, not the orderlies, not the men themselves. In fourteen months she had never shown the ledger to a living soul, and sometimes she wondered whether she kept it secret out of prudence or out of something closer to shame — the shame of a woman who could not stop picking at a wound the whole world had agreed to call healed.

Tonight's entries were short. *Osei, D.*, confirmed and dated. *Two dysentery cases improving, names for the record of survival, since the record keeps only the dead.* And then a new page, because the old habit demanded it:

*Names omitted this week:* and she listed them. Osei. A gunner called Ferro whose death at the forward post had been reported as desertion, though she had held the man's dog tags himself — herself, she corrected, Ferro was a woman, one of the three, and the report had made her a deserter because deserters forfeit pensions and pensions are cheaper than honors. A stretcher-bearer lost at the weir whose name Coral knew only as Bem, first name unknown, and she wrote that too: *first name unknown, but he had a scar shaped like a river on his left forearm, and someone, somewhere, knows the rest.*

She read back through the pages by the seed of lamplight. Fourteen months. One hundred and six names. Some she had full particulars for — ranks, regiments, next of kin scratched onto scraps of paper pressed into her hands by dying men who understood, with the terrible clarity of the dying, exactly what the official record would and would not contain. Some were fragments. A voice. A wound. A joke told once in a flooded tent that deserved, by any honest accounting, to outlive the teller.

One hundred and six names that existed nowhere else on earth except in this softening book and in her memory, and when she died they would exist in one place only, and when she forgot—

She closed the ledger. Wrapped it. Slid it back beneath the pillow where it lay against her skull all night like a second conscience.

Through the canvas wall she could hear the river, higher tonight than yesterday. The engineers had been out at dusk driving stakes and shaking their heads. Somewhere across the water, on the far bank, the enemy guns were silent, but silence from that quarter had never once meant peace.

Evacuation within the week. Transport for the ambulatory. *As transport permits.*

She lay in the dark and did her arithmetic, the other arithmetic, the one that mattered. Kit bag: scissors, morphine, the letters — nine pounds. Ledger: half a pound, and worth the rest of the bag combined. And what could not be carried: Wren, who could not walk; Adeyemi, whose leg would rot before a wagon reached him; the boy in the corner whose name she had not yet written because he might live, please, let him live, and the living went in a different book, the hopeful book, the one with room left in it.

She measured her days in what could be carried, and the measure was always short, and she had stopped expecting it to balance.

She was nearly asleep when the sentry challenged someone at the perimeter — a single sharp voice, then the wet slap of footsteps on the duckboards, coming closer. Not a stretcher party; they moved in fours and said nothing. This was one set of feet, stumbling, and between the stumbles a sound she had learned in seven stations and hoped never to stop recognizing: the breathing of a man who has run past the end of his strength and is running anyway.

Coral sat up. Her hand went under the pillow, not for the pistol she didn't own, but for the ledger, and she was moving toward the tent flap before she fully knew why — before the flap itself was thrown back and the lamplight spilled out onto a stranger standing in the rain, caked in marsh mud to the waist, swaying like a cut sapling, with one arm clutched against his chest as if holding shut a door that something inside him was trying to escape through.

Behind him, far off across the drowned fields, a single lantern swung along the river road. Coming this way. Or perhaps not. In this war, Coral thought, you rarely got to know until it arrived.

The stranger looked at her, and then at the tent behind her — at the rows of cots, the raised trestles, the whole fragile ark of the wounded — and his mouth opened, and what came out was not a plea for help.

"My name's Vann," he said. "I need a nurse who keeps records."
