## 2. The Stranger at the Tent Flap

The rain had been falling for three days, and by the fourth the hospital had begun to float.

Not visibly — not yet — but Coral could feel it in the way the tent poles leaned, in the soft give of ground that had once held firm beneath her boots, in the smell rising from the canvas floor: river water and rot and the sweet-sour ghost of carbolic. The bridge had flooded its banks on Tuesday night, and now the war seemed to be trying to wash itself away, one wounded man at a time.

She moved down the row of cots with her ledger tucked inside her apron, where it rode against her hip like a second pulse. Sixty-one patients. Fourteen too many for the tents, so the overflow lay on pallets under oilcloth in what had been the supply store, moaning softly whenever the wind shifted. Three had died since dawn. She would write their names tonight, after lights-out, when Sister Imelda was doing her rounds with the lamp and Coral could pretend to be asleep until the ward went quiet.

*Private Tomas Beck. Corporal Ansel Ferro. Gunner's mate Willem Ott.* Men whose papers said they'd been transferred to a depot that did not exist, or attached to a company that had never filed a muster roll, or simply — in the elegant bureaucratic phrasing she had learned to hate — *unaccounted for.*

Unaccounted for. As if the men were coins that had rolled under a piece of furniture.

"Coral." Imelda's voice, low, from the tent flap. "There's a man at the perimeter asking for the hospital. Civilians aren't permitted past the wire."

"Is he wounded?"

"He's half-dead, which is different." The old nun's face was unreadable in the grey light, but something flickered behind it — the particular weariness of a woman who had spent thirty years watching rules fail to save anyone. "He won't go to the aid station. He keeps saying he has to see someone who keeps records."

Coral's hand went to her hip before she could stop it.

"I'll see him," she said. "Outside the flap, if he can stand. On a cot just inside if he can't."

Imelda studied her a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she nodded once and let the canvas fall.

* * *

He could barely stand.

Coral saw him first through the gap between tents: a tall man folded almost double, supported on either side by two orderlies who looked as though they were carrying furniture. Mud had claimed him entirely — boots, trousers, coat, the ruin of what might once have been a clerk's collar. His face was the colour of the river, grey-green and swollen, and his lips were cracked into a map of fine bleeding lines. He had walked a long way, she thought. Weeks, maybe. And recently he had run.

They got him onto a cot in the receiving tent, the one reserved for triage, and Coral knelt to work while the orderlies retreated into the rain. She cut away his coat sleeve — the left arm was lacerated, a long ragged tear from elbow to wrist, thorn-cut or wire-cut, infected at the edges — and cleaned it while he watched her with eyes that were fever-bright but level. That surprised her. Most men at this stage of exhaustion watched nothing. Their eyes slid off the world like rain off oilcloth.

"You're the nurse," he said. Not a question. His voice was wrecked, sanded down to the grain. "Okafor. Coral Okafor."

"And you're a fool who crossed a military perimeter during an active evacuation window," she said. "Name."

"Elias Vann." He swallowed, winced. "Civilian courier. Contract mail service, southern routes."

"Civilians don't run the southern routes anymore. They've been closed since spring."

"The company still honors its contracts."

"The company is a name on a shuttered office door." She tied off the dressing, efficient, impersonal. "Where's your wound from?"

"Briar field. There's a stretch between here and the marshes where they've strung wire low, under the water line. You can't see it until—"

"I know where it is. I've pulled six men out of it." She reached for the splint board, kept her voice flat. "You came through the marshes alone? In this weather?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

For the first time since he'd collapsed at the tent flap, Elias Vann hesitated. It was a small thing — a hitch in the breathing, a flicker of the eyes toward the tent wall, toward the orderlies' shadows beyond it — but Coral had spent three years learning to read men's bodies the way officers read maps, and she knew a lie assembling itself when she saw one.

"I told you," he said. "I have a delivery."

"For whom?"

"For—" A cough took him, deep and wet, folding him forward. When he straightened, there was blood on his lips, pink and thin, and his fever had climbed another degree in the space of a minute. "For whoever keeps records," he finished. "I was told — I heard there was a nurse here who wrote things down. Names."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere across the compound, a man began to sing — tuneless, looping, the fragment of a hymn worn down to three notes.

"Who told you that?" Coral asked quietly.

"A dying man in the marshes."

* * *

She should have called the guard then. She knew it even as she knelt there, wrapping his arm, feeling the heat come off his skin like a stove door left open. A civilian courier off closed routes, guided to a military hospital by word of a dead man, asking specifically for the nurse who kept records — any competent officer would have him in irons within the hour, and Major Halloway's inspectors had been circling the river sector for a fortnight like gulls over a gutting table.

But the fever was climbing fast now, and fevered men say true things before they say clever ones, and Coral wanted — needed — to hear the true thing first.

"Let me see this delivery," she said.

"It's not addressed to you."

"Nothing in this war is addressed to anyone. Half my patients are letters the army lost." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited.

He fought it. She could see the fight happening behind his face — the courier's instinct to protect the cargo warring against the body's simple arithmetic, which said that he was done, that his legs were done, that his mind was a candle burning down into the wax. In the end the body won, the way it always did in hospitals. His hand went inside his shirt, moving like a man underwater, and drew out an oilskin packet that he had worn against his chest so long the fabric of his undershirt had worn through in a patch the exact shape of it.

He pressed it into her palm without letting go at first, fingers white-knuckled around hers, and looked at her with an expression she had seen only on the dying: the look of a man handing off something heavier than hands can hold.

"Don't read it here," he whispered. "Please. If you're going to refuse — refuse, and I'll take it and go. But don't read it here."

Then his eyes rolled back and he went out, all at once, like a lamp snuffed between finger and thumb.

* * *

She got him stable. It took two hours, a second pair of hands borrowed from Imelda without explanation, and every trick she knew for pulling a man back from the far shore of sepsis. By the time his breathing evened and the shivering stopped, dusk had come down over the camp like a lid, and the lamps in the ward threw their weak yellow circles against the rain-hammered canvas.

Coral sat on an empty supply crate in the corner of the receiving tent, alone, the oilskin packet on her knees.

She told herself she was going to open it because protocol required identifying all correspondence entering the hospital. She told herself it was triage. She was aware, distantly, that these were lies — the same tidy little lies she catalogued daily in other men's mouths, *unaccounted for*, *transferred*, *lost in transit* — and that made her no more able to stop than a woman can stop falling once the floor has gone.

She broke the seal. Inside the oilskin, wrapped in a scrap of waxed linen stained dark along one edge — blood, old and brown, not the courier's — was a single sheet of paper, folded twice.

The handwriting hit her before the words did.

She knew that hand. She had a letter bearing it sewn into the lining of her footlocker at this very moment, carried through two evacuations and a shellfire winter: *To Nurse Okafor, with the gratitude of thirty-one men who will not forget your hands.* Captain Rowan Ashe had written like a draftsman — upright, precise, each letter built rather than written, the crossbars of his t's perfectly level as if ruled, except for one small flaw, a signature flaw, a tiny hesitation in the descender of his y's, a stutter of ink where his pen paused a fraction too long. She had read that thank-you letter perhaps a hundred times in the worst months, the way other women read scripture. She knew the way he formed his capital R the way she knew her own heartbeat.

The letter in her hands began: *To whoever finds this — to anyone — to everyone.*

And it was Rowan Ashe's hand, stroke for stroke, pause for pause, down to the stutter in the y of *everyone.*

Rowan Ashe had been dead for two years. Officially. Formally. With a citation and a ceremony and a name carved eventually onto the memorial wall in Harrow's Cross, which Coral had passed through last autumn and read with her own eyes: *Capt. R. Ashe, killed in action, Kessel Ford sector.* She remembered standing before it thinking that the stonecutter had given his y's the same hesitation the living man gave them, and finding that thought comforting, and then finding it unbearable, and putting it away in the place where she put everything she couldn't afford to carry.

She read the letter twice. Her hands did not shake until the second reading, and then they shook so badly she had to press the page flat against her thigh.

It spoke of Kessel Ford. Not the official Kessel Ford — the engagement, the regrettable but lawful action, the casualties consistent with enemy strength — but the other one, the one that had happened in the hour before the official one began. It named officers. It named numbers. It described what the captain had been ordered to do afterward, and what he had refused to do, and what had been done to him instead — the arrest without charges, the transfer, the prison, the paperwork of a death arranged with the same neat precision as the paperwork of the massacre itself. *They did not kill me,* the letter said, in that upright draftsman's hand. *They erased me. Erasure is cheaper. I am writing this so that someone, somewhere, will know the difference.*

At the bottom, undated, unsigned: *If I do not live to send this myself, trust the man who carries it. He was my clerk. He asked one question too many, and they dismissed him for it, and he is the only honest number left in my battalion's arithmetic.*

Coral sat very still in the lamplight and listened to the rain and felt the ground shifting beneath the hospital the way it had shifted all week, the flood coming up slow and patient under everything she stood on.

Because she had a ledger sewn into her apron with fourteen names in it from this month alone, and forty more besides, and she had always told herself she was keeping it for the families, someday, after — a private act of mourning, harmless, hidden, hers. But the dates in that ledger went back further than this month. They went back to the autumn two years ago, to the cluster of men who had come through her aid station near the Ford with wounds that didn't match the reports, men listed as deserters or transferred or unaccounted for, and she had written their names down because no one else would, and she had never understood why the reports were wrong, only that they were.

Now she understood.

Now the names had a shape, and the shape had a author, and the author was a man whose handwriting matched a dead captain's exactly, and the stranger sleeping three cots away had walked out of the marshes carrying it like a coal in his bare hands, hunted by people who — she was suddenly, coldly certain of it — burned anything that remembered.

Through the canvas wall, faintly, she could hear the sentry at the perimeter calling the change of watch, and beneath it, from the ward, the sound of Elias Vann's fevered breathing, quick and shallow, a man running even in his sleep.

Coral folded the letter along its creases, wrapped it back in the bloodstained linen, and slid it inside her apron beside the ledger, where it lay against her hip with a weight that had nothing to do with paper.

*What have you brought to my hospital?* she thought, looking at the stranger's grey, exhausted face in the lamplight. *What have you brought to my door?*

Outside, the rain kept falling on the flooded river, on the bridge, on the road south along which, somewhere in the dark, whatever had driven this man through wire and marsh was still coming — patient, thorough, and very good at making things unaccounted for.
