## 3. A Hand That Should Be Dust

The marshes did not give up their dead. They kept them.

Elias Vann learned this in his third hour of wading through the reeds, when his boot found something soft and yielding beneath the black water and he had to tell himself, firmly, that it was only a root. Only a root. The war had been over for everyone else for months — the armistice signed in a capital he'd never seen, celebrated with bells he'd never heard — but out here along the drowned flats below Kessel Ford, it went on quietly, in the manner of things forgotten: bodies surfacing with the spring thaw, cartridges rusting into orange blooms, and men like him moving through it all with their collars up and their eyes down.

He was not supposed to be here. No one was supposed to be here. That was rather the point of marshes.

Ahead of him, the reeds opened onto a pool of standing water the color of old tea, and in the middle of it, half-submerged against a fallen alder, was a man. Elias froze, hand going to the knife at his belt out of habit rather than courage — he had never once used the knife, and the man looked past using or being used by anything. He was a soldier once; you could read the shape of him even through the mud. Now he was mostly ribs and rainwater, propped against the trunk as though he'd sat down to rest a moment ago and simply never resumed the argument with gravity.

Elias should have walked on. Every instinct honed across two years of clerking and four months of running said *walk on, dead men are someone else's paperwork now*. But the man's chest moved. Barely. A flutter at the throat, like a bird trapped under cloth.

"Hello," Elias said, and felt immediately foolish. Hello. To a dying man in a swamp. As if they'd met at a market stall.

The man's eyes opened. They were a pale, washed-out gray, the color of the sky overhead, and they fixed on Elias with an effort that seemed to cost him something physical. His lips moved. Elias crouched at the edge of the pool, water soaking through his trousers, and leaned close enough to catch the words.

"You're not them."

It wasn't a question. It sounded like a man checking his arithmetic one last time.

"No," Elias said. "I'm nobody."

Something that might have been a laugh moved through the soldier's chest and came out as a wet rattle. "Nobody's exactly who I need." One hand lifted from the water — it took visible time, the fingers uncurling like something unfurling from winter — and pressed against the front of his tunic. Beneath the mud-crusted cloth there was a stiffness that wasn't flesh. Oilcloth. Wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord. "Take it off me. Please. I've been carrying it since the ford and I can't—" A cough bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his chin, thin and bright against the gray of his face. "I can't die holding it. It needs hands that walk."

Elias didn't move. Later, in the weeks of flight that followed, he would return to this moment again and again and ask himself why — why he waded forward instead of back — and he would never find a better answer than this: because the man said *please*. Because in four months of being hunted, of doors closing and faces turning away, no one had said please to him about anything.

He waded in. The water climbed his thighs, cold as confession. Up close the soldier smelled of river rot and the sweetish stink of a wound gone bad, and his uniform, when Elias scraped away the mud with two fingers, bore no insignia at all — the brass torn off, the buttons changed. A deserter, then, or a man dressed as one. He'd been careful. Even dying, he'd been careful.

"The cord," the man whispered. "Cut it. Don't open it here. In the name of God, don't open it where the reeds can hear."

Elias cut the cord with the knife he'd never used. The oilcloth packet was smaller than a prayer book and heavier than it had any right to be. The soldier's whole body sagged when it left him, as if it had been the last timber propping up a collapsing house.

"There's a woman," the man said. His voice was going somewhere Elias couldn't follow, each word arriving fainter than the last. "On the river road. A nurse. They say she keeps a book." His eyes, which had been drifting toward the flat white sky, dragged themselves back with terrible effort. "They say she writes down the names. The ones they strike. You'll know her by—"

But whatever sign would mark her was lost. The coughing took him, then the shivering, then, somewhere between one breath and the next, nothing at all. His hand slid from his chest into the water and lay there open, palm up, as though finally setting down a weight.

Elias stayed crouched in the cold until the ripples stopped reaching the reeds. Then he stood, and the packet sat against his ribs inside his coat like a second heartbeat, and he understood that he had just become a man things could happen to.

* * *

That was nine days ago. Nine days, three towns, and forty miles behind him, and still he could feel the marsh water in his bones, could feel the exact weight of the packet, could see the soldier's open palm filling slowly with rain.

Now he stood in the lee of a flooded field hospital near the river bridge, watching a woman move between cots with a lamp in one hand, and told himself for the hundredth time that he did not believe the letter.

Because he'd read it. Of course he'd read it. He'd lasted until the first hedgerow outside the marsh, maybe half a mile, before he'd ducked down among sheep and torn the oilcloth open with shaking hands. He was a clerk by nature and training; secrets addressed to *no one and everyone* were an abomination he couldn't leave sealed.

Inside was a letter, folded twice, and a smaller scrap — a prison ration chit, stamped with a depot number Elias recognized, because he had processed supplies requisitions for that depot for eighteen months before they dismissed him. The letter was written in a hand he knew even better than his own. Neat, upright, slightly left-leaning, the capital letters formed with a draftsman's precision. He had transcribed that hand into fair copy a thousand times. He had filed it. He had, in the worst months, dreamed of it.

*Captain Rowan Ashe* had written to him from a grave he'd been lying in for two years.

Elias had sat among the sheep and read it four times, and the letter said what the dying man presumably couldn't: that Rowan Ashe lived. That he had lived through Kessel Ford, that he had seen what happened there — not a defeat, not a rout, but a massacre, ordered, methodical, carried out on men who had already surrendered — and that for seeing it he had been declared dead. Not killed. *Declared.* There was a difference, the letter said, and the difference had a signature, and the signature protected a general. Ashe wrote that he was held at the depot whose number matched the chit. He wrote that others had carried copies of this letter. He named three names, and asked, with the terrible courtesy that had always made his subordinates love him, whether any of them had made it further than he had.

Elias had checked. In the first town, at risk of everything, he'd asked a gravedigger after the names. Two were in the ground, officially dead of fever, buried within a week of Kessel Ford. The third had no grave at all. Struck from the rolls. Erased so cleanly that when Elias asked after him, the town clerk looked at him as if he'd mentioned a ghost, which in a sense he had.

So either the letter was true, and the army had murdered its own captain's reputation to bury a massacre — or someone had forged the most dangerous document in the province and was letting fools like Elias carry it into the fire.

He didn't know which. That was the honest truth of it, the truth he kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: *he didn't know.* But he knew other things. He knew that four days after he'd left the marsh, a patrol had come through the village where he'd slept, asking questions not about deserters generally but about a courier specifically, describing his coat, his height, his limp — the limp, God help him, which he'd acquired only two days prior, which meant they'd talked to someone who'd seen him *after* the letter. He knew that in the second town the innkeeper's daughter had looked at him too long and too carefully, and that he'd left before dawn without paying. And he knew that yesterday, from a ridge above the river road, he had watched three riders in undress uniforms stop at a shrine, dismount, and burn something in the votive flame — casually, thoroughly, the way men burn things when burning is their trade.

Men were hunting him. Not soldiers doing their duty; hunters doing their work. He had spent eighteen months in the army's administrative guts and he knew the difference in his spine.

And still he did not believe the letter. Or rather — he believed it the way you believe weather coming over a ridge. Not as a proposition. As a fact approaching.

* * *

The hospital tent leaked. He could see it from outside: rain finding the seams, dripping onto bandages, onto foreheads, onto the ledger—

No. Onto paper. He was getting ahead of himself.

The woman — the nurse — had come out to the water barrels an hour ago, and Elias had watched her the way he'd once watched quartermasters count crates: looking for the discrepancy, the thing that didn't match the manifest. She didn't match. She moved through the ward like someone keeping accounts, and when she paused between patients she didn't rest, she *tallied* — he could almost see the arithmetic running behind her eyes. And when she thought no one was watching she took a small book from her apron pocket and wrote in it, quickly, furtively, sheltering it from the rain with her body the way you'd shield a flame.

A nurse who kept a book. The dying man's words, or the rumor that had reached him through the deserter's network before the marsh: *there's a woman on the river road who writes down the names.*

Names. The ones they strike. Elias had spent two years processing the strikes — the neat red lines through service records, the *missing, presumed* entries that turned men into weather damage, acts of God, nobody's fault. He'd questioned it exactly once, early, politely, and had been dismissed within the month with a character reference that praised his diligence and made clear his diligence was the problem. He had told himself for two years that he'd lost his post over a filing dispute. Standing in the rain watching this woman tally her invisible dead, he admitted, finally, what he'd known all along: he'd lost it over a massacre he'd never seen, in records he'd never read closely enough, for a captain whose handwriting he still knew better than his own mother's.

Rowan Ashe had once written him a note — a single line, appended to a supply return Elias had flagged and corrected: *"Vann — the only man in this office who reads. R.A."* Elias had kept it. Of course he'd kept it. It was in the lining of his coat, along with everything else he owned, which was the coat, the knife he'd never used, the oilcloth packet, and now, apparently, a purpose.

He could still walk away. That was the thing he kept circling, the exit he refused to look at directly. The letter was addressed to no one and everyone. Technically, he was neither. He could bury the packet in the marsh mud, walk north, take a new name, vanish into the great postwar anonymity of men with nothing left to lose. The hunters wanted a courier. If there was no courier, there was nothing to hunt.

Except.

Except that a man had died in a swamp holding the thing, and his last word to a stranger had been *please*. Except that three carriers' names were listed in the letter and Elias had personally confirmed two graves and one erasure. Except that somewhere behind those burned papers and those patient riders was a general whose signature turned murder into fever, and every day Elias ran and said nothing, the ink dried a little harder, the lie set a little deeper into stone, into memorial walls, into history.

And except that Rowan Ashe — dead, erased, imprisoned, if any of it was true — had once called him *the only man in this office who reads.*

Elias pushed off the wall and limped toward the tent flap. Rain got under his collar immediately, as it had for nine days, as it would for nine more if it pleased. His heart was going hard, not with fear exactly — though there was plenty of fear, fear was his constant companion now, they were practically married — but with the particular dread of a clerk about to submit a report he knows is wrong and knows is necessary.

The sentry at the flap barely glanced at him. Mud-caked civilians were weather here, not people.

Inside: lamp-smoke, carbolic, the sweet-rot smell he'd hoped to leave in the marsh, and rows of cots, and the woman at the far end writing in her book. He got three steps in before his leg — the bad one, the liar's leg, the one that had betrayed him to the patrol's informants — buckled on the wet boards, and the tent floor came up fast, and the last thing he saw before the dark took him was the nurse turning, the small book still open in her hand, her face registering not alarm but recognition, as if she'd been expecting exactly one stranger and had just finished counting.

Then the floor, and the lamp swinging away overhead, and the thought that arrived with absurd clarity as consciousness left him:

*Don't let go of the packet. Whatever happens — don't let go.*

His fist was still closed around it when she knelt beside him.

* * *

He woke to lamplight and the smell of carbolic, and a woman sitting near enough that he could see her clearly for the first time: tired eyes, steady hands, a face that had decided long ago how much it would show the world and never renegotiated. Her gaze went to his closed fist, then to his face.

"You collapsed," she said. "You've a fever in that leg. And you've been clutching that bundle since before I got to you hard enough to bruise."

Elias's mouth was sand. "It's letters," he said. "Personal. From my brother."

The lie came easily and tasted like ash, because the truth was worse and stranger and would get them both killed: it was letters from a dead man, written by a man who should be dust, claiming a massacre that official stone had already ruled a fever — and he had carried it forty miles through rain and hunters because a drowning stranger in a marsh had said *please*, and because somewhere in the lining of his coat a dead officer had once called him the only man who reads.

The nurse looked at him for a long moment. She had the eyes of someone who triaged lies for a living and knew exactly which ones concealed the worst wounds.

"Your brother," she said quietly, "writes a very familiar hand."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the rain kept falling on the flooded flats, on the marshes that kept their dead, on the road where riders were even now asking after a courier in a mud-colored coat.

Elias's fist tightened around the packet, and he understood that the choice he'd been refusing for nine days had just walked up to him wearing a nurse's apron and holding a small, forbidden book.

"I think," he said, "you'd better sit down."
