## 4. The Ledger's Arithmetic

The rain came back an hour after midnight, soft as an apology, drumming on the canvas above the supply tent where Coral had gone to count what was left of them. Morphine ampoules: eleven. Clean dressings: a stack she could hold in one hand. Lice powder: none. Iodine enough for perhaps two more days of the river bringing her its nightly harvest of torn men.

She counted by lamplight because counting was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only thing the quartermaster couldn't requisition.

The letter lay beneath her ledger, where it had lain since Elias Vann had been carried to the fever ward at dusk, delirious, his hands still curled around the oilskin packet as though the fingers had forgotten how to do anything else. Sister Imelda had pried it loose with the brisk tenderness she used on everything — wounds, secrets, dying men — and handed it to Coral without comment. *Yours, I think,* was all she'd said. *You're the one who keeps things.*

Coral had not opened it. Not yet. All evening she'd moved among the cots with the letter's weight against her ribs like a second heart, and all evening the thought of it had kept time with her pulse. A dead man's handwriting. She knew that was what Vann had said, before he went under — slurred, half-conscious, gripping her wrist with surprising strength: *It's Ashe's hand. You'll know it. He wrote to you once.*

He had. She remembered it the way you remember a coin found in a coat pocket years later — small, unexpected, still warm somehow.

Two winters ago, when the hospital had been tents on higher ground instead of a flooded mill beside a bridge nobody wanted, a parcel had arrived with no sender's name, only a field post number. Inside: a single sheet, and tucked behind it a paper twist of real sugar, the white kind, hoarded somewhere by someone who understood what sugar meant to nurses watching men die sweetless. The letter had said, in a hand so even it looked printed:

*Sister Okafor — I am told you sat up three nights with my corporal, Denny Locke, though he was past knowing anyone sat with him. I am told you wrote to his mother in your own hand rather than let a form go. I cannot repay that. I only want you to know it was seen. Men believe they are forgotten out here. You are the reason some of mine don't. — R. Ashe, Capt., 4th Rifles*

She had kept the letter folded inside her kit through two relocations and one bombardment, and lost it — she'd assumed — somewhere between the old camp and the mill. Perhaps it hadn't been lost. Perhaps it was in the bottom of this very trunk now, beneath the bandage rolls, and some part of her had known all along that she would need it tonight.

She found it on the third search, wedged under a tin of carbolic, the ink faded but unmistakable.

Then she set the two letters side by side on the ammunition crate that served as her desk, and made herself look.

* * *

The new letter — Vann's letter, the bloodstained one, addressed *To whoever is still keeping count* — was longer. The handwriting was shakier. Two years will do that, if a man is alive to be shaken. But the R of the signature broke the same way, a little kick at the foot like a man stepping over something in the dark. The same hand. There was no honest way around it.

*They tell you I died at Kessel Ford,* the letter began. *I have read my own death notice. It was very flattering.*

Coral read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way she palpated a wound — gently at first, then with pressure, to find where it really hurt.

*I did not die at Kessel Ford. Kessel Ford happened. What happened afterward was arithmetic. Forty-one men went into the water under fire because the order to cross was given to protect a withdrawal that had already finished, and forty-one men did not come out, and the report that reached division listed nine. Nine, sister. Nine men drowned, regrettable, weather-related, closed. The other thirty-two were entered nowhere at all. I signed nothing. When I said I would sign nothing, I became one of the thirty-three.*

Thirty-three. The number snagged in her like a fishhook.

She pulled the ledger across the crate and untied its string.

It was not a beautiful object. It had begun life as a ration-account book, its printed columns half-erased with a razor so she could rule her own lines, and it smelled of carbolic and mildew and the particular iron sweetness that clung to everything near the ward. On its pages, in her small upright hand — the hand of a woman who had once kept her father's shop accounts and could not stop keeping accounts, even here — were names. Names with dates. Names with whatever she knew of how they'd ended. Names that appeared on no report, because the reports had been written by officers whose careers required that certain men never have existed.

She had started it the way you start any bad habit: once, and then helplessly.

Now she turned to the entries from the autumn before last, the season the war had spent downriver, and laid the ledger open beside the letter, and began the arithmetic.

*Okafor, C.,* she wrote to herself silently, *you are about to do something you cannot undo.*

*Whitlock, J., pvt., 4th Rifles. Taken at the crossing, drowned. Body not recovered.*

*Amara, T., pvt., 4th Rifles. Same night. Same river. Witness: none surviving.*

*Mbeki, S., lance-cpl. Same night.*

She read the letter's list against her own. Whitlock. Amara. Mbeki. Osei. Ferrand. A name she'd had to ask a sergeant to spell twice — Kowalczyk — who had been nineteen and had written poetry in the margins of his pay book and had gone into the water at Kessel Ford with the rest of them.

Every name in the letter's suppressed column was in her ledger. Every date aligned. Not approximately — exactly. The ninth of Brumaire, the fourteenth, the twenty-second. The nights the river had taken its harvest while the official record recorded a drought.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange thing. She noticed their steadiness the way you notice a clock ticking in a silent room, and she thought: *this is what shock looks like from the inside.* Somewhere below her ribs, in the place where fear actually lived, something was moving furniture around, quietly, getting ready for a longer stay.

Because she understood what she was holding. She had understood it for months, in the vague way you understand that the water rising around your ankles has somewhere to go. But understanding it vaguely and reading Rowan Ashe's handwriting saying it plainly — *I am alive, and the thirty-two are dead, and both facts were arranged* — were different animals entirely.

Her private grief had just become evidence.

* * *

She got up and checked the ward.

It was a habit dressed as an excuse. Eleven cots, eleven breathers, the lantern trimmed low, Imelda asleep sitting up in her chair by the fever cases with her rosary slack in her fingers. One boy — Pell, seventeen, gut-shot, the kind of wound that bought days and charged interest — murmured at her approach and settled. She stood over him a moment. His face in sleep was the face of every boy she'd failed to save, which is to say it was no particular face at all, just youth, just the raw material of other people's reports.

*If they knew this existed,* she thought, looking down the row of cots, *they wouldn't come for me. They'd come through here.*

That was the new arithmetic too, and she hated it. For two years the ledger had been a debt she owed privately — paid nightly, witnessed by no one, costing no one but her sleep. Now it was contraband. Now it was the kind of thing Major Halloway's breed of officer burned whole wards to find, and she didn't mean that as a figure of speech; she had seen what remained of the aid station at Marrow Creek.

She went back to the supply tent and made herself finish the work, because half-comparisons were worse than useless. If she was going to be afraid, she would at least be afraid accurately.

The letter continued past the list into instructions, or the wreckage of instructions — the ink blotched, the writer clearly interrupted more than once.

*There is a journalist in the port city. Brandt, Adaeze, working for the tribunal commission. If this reaches her, the thirty-two get their names read aloud where the generals can't leave the room. I know there is a nurse on the river road who keeps records. The deserters talk about her like a superstition — the woman who writes down the ones the army deletes. If this letter has found you, then the superstition is true, and I am asking you to do the most dangerous thing a person can do in this war, which is to make a piece of paper tell the truth.*

*I don't know your name. Forgive me. I remember your sugar.*

Coral set the letter down. For a long moment she could not see the page for the water standing in her eyes, and she laughed once, short and wet, at the absurdity of it — the sugar, after everything, the sugar.

Then she blew her nose on a square of lint, because sentiment was a luxury and lint was free, and she forced herself to think like an accountant, because accountants survive.

Fact: Captain Rowan Ashe, declared dead two years, was very likely alive, and very likely a prisoner, because a man who refuses to falsify a massacre report does not get released; he gets stored.

Fact: the courier who carried this letter — the mud-caked stranger sleeping off fever in her ward — was hunted. He'd said so in the few words he managed before collapsing, and the mud on him had been marsh mud, and there had been, she now admitted to herself, the sound of horses earlier that evening on the river road, riders passing without slowing, the way men ride when they are looking at the ground for tracks rather than the horizon for enemies.

Fact: her ledger contained thirty-two names — more than thirty-two, over these two years she had gathered sixty-one — that corroborated a document the army had killed men to suppress.

Conclusion: she was no longer a woman who kept a private grief. She was a witness. And witnesses on this river road had a life expectancy she preferred not to calculate.

* * *

She did the last thing she could think of to do, which was to test the alignment again, coldly, looking for the flaw. There was always a flaw. Hope was a fever and you treated fevers by finding the source.

She checked dates against dates, initials against muster lists she'd copied off ambulance manifests, the regimental numbers she'd coaxed out of delirious men against the numbers in Ashe's list. They held. Worse than held — they dovetailed. Her ledger had details Ashe's list could not have given her: Whitlock's mother lived in Corvey and had a limp; Amara had carried a lock of his daughter's hair in a waxed paper fold; Kowalczyk had recited his own poetry while the morphine took him. And Ashe's list had what hers lacked: the shape of the thing. The order. The nine reported against the forty-one lost. The deliberate arithmetic of erasure.

Together, the two documents were not memories. They were a case.

And a case needed to reach Adaeze Brandt, and Brandt was a hundred and forty miles away behind three checkpoints, and between here and there was a countryside filling up with Halloway's riders the way a wound fills with pus.

Coral sat back on the ammunition crate and listened to the rain and felt the terror arrive properly at last — not as a flood but as a slow tide, patient, certain, climbing the pilings of everything she'd built her ordinary days on. She had imagined, in her worst moments, being caught: imagined explaining the ledger, imagining the confiscation, the burning, maybe the charge of spreading defeatism. She had budgeted for that. She had not budgeted for this — for mattering. For the possibility that the little black book was not a memorial but a weapon, and that weapons, once forged, implicate the smith.

She looked at the ledger lying open beside a dead man's living hand, and she understood that she had crossed a line sometime in the last hour without feeling the crossing, the way you cross a border in your sleep on a train.

*You can still burn it,* said a voice in her that sounded reasonable and slightly out of breath. *Tear out those pages. Feed them to the stove. Tell yourself the letter was fever-talk, that the stranger is a liar or a madman, that sixty-one names in a ration book prove nothing a tribunal would touch. Go back to being a nurse. Nurses are allowed to live.*

She thought about it honestly. That was the discipline of her — she gave the coward's argument its full hearing, the way she gave every patient's complaint its full hearing, because arguments dismissed too quickly come back at night.

But she kept seeing the memorial wall she'd heard about in the river towns, the one where Kessel Ford's dead were carved as fever victims, the lie already hardening into stone where grandchildren would someday read it as history. Stone outlasts anger. Paper outlasts almost nothing — unless someone carries it.

Sixty-one names. Thirty-two of them hers to answer for now, whether she'd asked for the duty or not.

"No," she said aloud, to the tent, to the rain, to the reasonable voice. It came out steadier than she felt. "No."

She repacked the ledger, tied its string, and then — because she was an accountant before she was anything else — she took a fresh sheet and began, in her small upright hand, a working summary. Dates. Numbers. Cross-references. The arithmetic laid out clean, so that a stranger — a journalist, a commissioner, a court — could follow it without her there to explain. She worked until the lamp guttered, and the rain eased toward dawn, and the summary filled three pages that frightened her more than the original ever had, because summaries could be understood by anyone, including the wrong anyone.

When she finished, the sky beyond the canvas had gone the grey of unbleached linen. From the ward came the sounds of morning — Imelda rousing the ambulatory, the kettle, a man groaning himself awake. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that still believed itself intact.

And under them, faint but unmistakable, from the river road: hoofbeats again. Several horses. Riding slowly this time.

Coral put the letters inside her coat, next to her skin, and went to see who had come to count.
