## 5. Major Halloway Comes to Count

The rain had stopped an hour before the Major arrived, and Coral would remember that — how the sky went clean and pale, as if washed for company.

She was changing a dressing when Sister Imelda's voice came down the ward like weather. "Officers," she said, not loudly, but in the tone that made every nurse's hands still for half a second before resuming. "Wipe your faces. Straighten your beds."

Major Halloway entered the flooded field hospital the way a man enters a church he owns: hat under his arm, boots polished to a dull shine despite the mud outside, his aide carrying a leather folio at parade rest behind him. He was younger than Coral expected — forty perhaps, with gray coming in early at the temples, a face built for portraits rather than battlefields. Handsome in the way that made her immediately distrust him. He smiled before he spoke, and the smile reached all the way up, which somehow made it worse.

"Ladies," he said. "Gentlemen." A nod toward the two orderlies hauling a pail of gray water toward the drainage trench. "I'm told you've had flooding. The river has been unkind to everyone this season."

"Unkind to everyone equally, sir," Imelda said, dry as lint.

"Equally." He tasted the word. "Yes. I imagine so."

Coral kept her head down over the dressing she was working on — Private Dengue, nineteen, shrapnel through the calf, brave enough to be embarrassed about whimpering. She wound the bandage, tucked the end, smoothed it flat. Her hands were steady. That was the strange thing. Later she would expect them to shake and they never did; they only shook afterward, in the dark of her tent, when there was nothing left for them to hold.

Halloway moved down the row of beds slowly, and she understood within thirty seconds that this was no courtesy inspection. He counted. His eyes moved bed to bed the way an auditor's pen moves down a column, pausing on faces, lingering on bandages. At each cot he asked the patient one question — *Where are you from? How long since you were wounded? Which action?* — and each answer went into the aide's folio with a soft scratch of pencil.

He was building a list. Coral felt the knowledge settle into her stomach like swallowed stone.

He reached Private Dengue's cot and Coral rose, positioning herself between them with the practiced ease of a nurse claiming territory. "This man needs rest, sir. The dressing took some doing."

"Then I'll thank you for the doing." He looked past her shoulder at Dengue, whose face had gone the color of tallow. "Which engagement, son?"

"The—the bridge skirmish, sir. Two weeks back."

"Two weeks." Halloway nodded pleasantly. "And where were you posted before that?"

Dengue's eyes flicked sideways, hunting for help. Coral supplied it without seeming to. "Convalescent transfer," she said. "Half my ward is men shuffled between postings. Their papers are worse than their wounds, I'm afraid. If you want the army's paperwork audited, Major, you'll need more than an afternoon."

It wasn't quite a lie. It was something better than a lie — it was true enough to pass inspection and shaped just wrong enough to close a door. Halloway turned his attention to her fully for the first time, and she saw that he had a habit of looking at people slightly too long after they finished speaking, as though waiting to see if anything else fell out of them.

"Sister Okafor," he read from the tag pinned inside her collar. "You'll forgive me. Names are rather my trade these days."

"I'd have thought counting was, sir."

Something flickered behind his pleasantness — surprise, maybe, or appetite. "Both," he said. "Counting is only names that haven't been written yet." He gestured vaguely at the ward. "You keep records here?"

"Every hospital keeps records."

"But you personally? I've heard—" he paused, selecting the word with visible care, "—that the nursing staff here is unusually thorough. That men who arrive without tags leave with names attached anyway. Some officers find that irregular."

*Some officers find that irregular.* The sentence hung in the wet air between them, smelling of carbolic and river water.

"We do our best," Coral said. "A man who dies nameless dies twice, in my experience. Once in the body and once everywhere else. We try to prevent the second."

"A generous theology." He smiled again, and this time it didn't reach as far. "Though I'd caution against poetry in official contexts, Sister. Records have purposes. Purposes have owners." He stepped closer, lowering his voice into something almost confiding. "Tell me — have you seen any strangers recently? On the river road, or here? A civilian type. Traveling alone. Possibly injured, possibly not."

Her heart did not speed up. She noted that fact with a kind of clinical wonder, the way she might note a pulse that stayed level through hemorrhage. Elias Vann was lying forty yards away in the cholera tent, wrapped in someone else's fever-sweat, his face smeared with the ash Imelda had rubbed on his skin to suggest contagion. Forty yards and one canvas wall between this man's questions and that man's bloodstained letter.

"No one traveling alone," Coral said. "We get deserters sometimes. They come in wounded or fevered, we patch them, the provost collects them. Is there a particular deserter you're missing, Major?"

The question was a gamble and she knew it — offer the enemy the shape of his own suspicion and he often loses interest in searching for it. Halloway studied her for three full seconds. Then he laughed, softly, genuinely, and Coral hated herself for noticing that the laugh was warm.

"You're wasted on dressings, Sister Okafor." He replaced his hat. "I'll be in the area several days. Counting. If anything occurs to you — any face you can't place, any story that doesn't sit right — my aide will be at the command post." He produced a card from his breast pocket and offered it to her with both hands, formally, as if decorating her. "Names are rather my trade."

She took the card. It was heavier paper than any soldier in the ward would ever touch.

* * *

He spent four hours in the hospital.

That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over afterward, while she crushed charcoal for Dengue's next dressing and tried to breathe normally. Not twenty minutes, not a swift officer's once-over — hours. He inspected the pharmacy stores and asked why the morphine ledger showed a discrepancy of eleven grains. (Imelda answered that one, smoothly, and Coral filed away the fact that the head nurse lied even better than she did.) He examined the burial register and asked why three graves in the plot behind the tents held men whose death dates preceded their admission dates. (Transcription errors, Imelda said. Rain. Bad ink.) He walked the perimeter, paused at the cholera tent, and stood there for a long moment looking at the flap.

"He knows," Coral said that evening, finding Imelda boiling instruments in the mess tent. Steam curled around the older woman's forearms; her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, her forearms red to the wrist from decades of lye.

"Of course he knows something," Imelda said, not looking up. "Men like that always know something. The trick is making sure what he knows isn't worth acting on." She lifted a clamp out of the water with tongs, watched it drip. "Your stranger's fever is convenient. Keep it convenient. Sweat is honest — nobody suspects a sweating man of lying."

"And when the fever ends?"

Imelda set the clamp down and finally looked at her. Thirty years of wards lived in that look, and underneath the discipline something tired was moving, something that had buried too many boys to believe the rules had ever saved one. "Then you move him," she said quietly. "Before it ends. There's a supply convoy going downriver Thursday. Corpse-haulers take bodies to the quarry kilns. Nobody searches the dead, Coral. Nobody has ever once searched the dead." She picked the tongs back up. "Now go sleep. You lie better rested."

* * *

But sleep didn't come, and near midnight Coral gave up and sat in her tent with the ledger open on her knees, reading it by stub candlelight — not writing tonight, just reading, the way you'd check on sleeping children. Seventy-one names now. She touched each entry with her thumb as she passed it, a rosary of the erased.

She thought about how easily the lies had come. That was the part that frightened her, more than Halloway himself, more than the folio and the card and the too-long looks. All her life she had been a truth-teller — blunt to a fault, her mother used to say, the girl who tells you the soup is bad while you're eating it. And today, facing a man whose smile was a weapon, she had fabricated and deflected and redirected as naturally as breathing, and had *enjoyed* the competence of it, the clean click of the lock turning.

*Loyalty to her patients,* she told herself. That was what the lying served. Dengue in his cot, Elias in his sweat, the seventy-one names in her lap — every falsehood today had been a bandage applied to someone real. That was the arithmetic she could live with.

Except.

Except that Halloway hadn't come to arrest anyone. Not yet. He'd come to count, and counting was patient work, and somewhere in the aide's folio was a growing list of discrepancies — eleven grains of morphine, three impossible graves, a sister of mercy with poetry in her mouth — and every item on that list was a thread, and every thread led eventually to the cholera tent, and the cholera tent led to a letter written in a dead man's hand, and the letter led to Kessel Ford, and Kessel Ford led to whatever it was that Major Halloway had been sent to make sure no one ever said aloud.

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the river.

Thursday, then. The corpse cart. The kiln road.

And in the morning — she knew this the way you know rain is coming, from the ache in old wounds — in the morning there would be new orders on the command post door, or a fresh inspection, or an aide with a folio asking for her by name. Men like Halloway did not spend four hours in a flooded hospital and ride away satisfied. They circled. They returned. They counted twice.

Outside, far down the river road, she heard hooves — a single rider moving fast through the dark, toward the command post, not away from it. She lay very still and listened to the sound grow louder, and louder, until it stopped.

Someone had news. Someone always did.
