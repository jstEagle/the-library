## 6. Elias Tells the Truth Badly

The fever tent smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and Elias Vann had been lying in it for three days, which was two days longer than any honest courier should have needed to recover from a sprained ankle.

He knew she was coming before he heard her. He'd learned her footsteps — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a woman who had decided long ago that running anywhere was a waste of strength she might need later. Coral Okafor moved through this hospital like a tide: inevitable, indifferent to obstacles, leaving things rearranged in her wake.

She pulled back the canvas flap and stood there with a tin cup of something that pretended to be tea.

"You're walking better," she said.

"Miracle of modern medicine."

"Mm." She set the cup on the crate beside his cot and did not sit. She never sat when she was working a patient. "The sentry at the north road stopped a supply cart this morning. Asked the driver if he'd seen a civilian come off the marsh road last week. Thin man, mud to the eyebrows, carrying nothing anyone could see."

Elias kept his face still. It was a skill he'd perfected over four years as a clerk — the art of looking like furniture while important men said terrible things nearby.

"I wouldn't know anything about that," he said.

"No," Coral agreed pleasantly. "I don't suppose you would."

She picked up the cup again, considered it, then handed it to him anyway. Their fingers didn't touch; she'd made sure of it, tilting the cup so he had to take the handle. Everything about her was precise like that. Economical. A woman who wasted no motion because she'd calculated exactly how much of herself the war got to have, and drew a hard line at the remainder.

"You've been here three days," she said. "In three days you haven't asked me a single question about this hospital. Not how many wounded, not where the supplies come from, not whether the shelling comes closer at night. Couriers ask those questions. Couriers are curious people — it's how they stay alive, knowing which roads are open." She folded her arms. "You haven't asked me anything except where I sleep. And you asked that badly."

Heat crawled up his neck. "I asked for practical reasons—"

"You asked me where I keep my personal belongings. At eleven o'clock at night. While pretending to be half-asleep." Her eyes were dark and absolutely without cruelty, which somehow made them worse. "I've been lied to by professionals, Mr. Vann. Officers do it beautifully. You do it like a man reciting a speech he wrote in the privy."

The tea trembled faintly in his hands. He set it down before she could see.

"So here is what happens now," Coral continued. "Either you tell me what you're actually doing here, or tomorrow morning I mention to Sister Imelda that my mysterious ankle patient has been asking after my effects, and she'll have the provosts in here before the porridge cools. They're very thorough with strangers since the desertions started. Thorough men don't ask questions first."

"They don't," Elias agreed quietly. "That's rather the problem, isn't it? Men who don't ask questions first."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly banked. She hadn't expected him to sound like that. Most people didn't. He'd spent years being mistaken for a simple man because he was quiet, and quiet men were assumed to be empty ones.

"Captain Rowan Ashe wrote you a letter once," he said. "Thanking you. For your care of his men after the action at Harrow's Mill."

Coral went very still. In the flooded ward behind them, a man moaned in his sleep, calling for someone named Peter, and the sound filled the silence between them like water filling a boot.

"How could you possibly know that," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had dropped into a register he suspected she used for counting the dead.

"Because I wrote the fair copy."

* * *

She made him wait while she finished rounds. Of course she did. Whatever storm was building behind her eyes, she triaged it like everything else — noted its severity, tagged it, and moved it to the end of the queue. Elias lay on his cot and watched her work and understood, with a sinking clarity, why Ashe had trusted her enough to write to her at all.

She worked the far row of cots first, where the new arrivals lay. A boy with a bandaged jaw who couldn't speak got an extra blanket and a slate. An older sergeant feigning sleep got his pulse taken anyway — Coral checked on the ones who pretended hardest, Elias noticed. She read their bodies the way he read ledgers, line by line, trusting only what was written in the flesh.

Then she came back, tied the flap shut, and stood with her arms crossed.

"Talk," she said.

And so Elias told the truth, badly, because that was the only way he knew how to tell it anymore — in pieces, out of order, with all the shame leaking through the seams.

"My name is Elias Vann. That part was true. I was Captain Ashe's clerk for three years. Regimental records, casualty returns, requisitions — the whole paper skeleton of a company of men." He swallowed. "I'm good with paper. Always have been. My father was a customs clerk in Marrow Bay; I grew up understanding that everything true in the world eventually gets written down somewhere, and everything false does too, and most people can't tell the difference."

"And Ashe?"

"Ashe was the best officer I ever served. Which isn't saying much, but it's saying something. He read every casualty return himself. Every name. He'd sit with the lists for hours sometimes, and I used to think it was sentimentality until I realized what he was doing — he was checking. Comparing the returns against the muster rolls against the burial details. Making sure nobody got lost in the arithmetic." Elias laughed, and it came out wrong. "I thought that was just thoroughness. I didn't understand he was keeping watch."

"Watch against what?"

Against Kessel Ford. The words stuck in his throat like fishbones.

"In the spring, two years ago, we got orders to advance across the ford. Ashe argued against it — put it in writing, formally, twice. The orders came down anyway. Afterward, the official return listed forty-one dead. I know it was forty-one because I wrote it, and I wrote it because Major Halloway's adjutant brought me the number already typed, and told me the field reports were lost due to enemy action." His hands had started shaking; he watched them as though they belonged to someone more interesting. "But I'd handled the burial detail paperwork myself. I counted the graves. There were ninety-two."

The moaning man called for Peter again. Somewhere a lamp guttered.

"Ninety-two," Coral repeated. No inflection. Just weighing it.

"Fifty-one men who officially never died. Because they were killed doing something the army couldn't afford to explain — and I never found out what, because Ashe ordered me out of the tent when he confronted Halloway, and three days later Ashe was dead. Officially. Killed in a rearguard action at a place nobody's ever been able to find on a map. Closed coffin. No escort." Elias looked up at her. "I questioned the report. Formally. One letter too many, one question too pointed, and I was dismissed within the month. Administrative discharge. Halloway signed it personally and shook my hand and thanked me for my service, and I want you to understand that his hand was warm and dry and completely human, and that has haunted me more than anything else about him."

Coral hadn't moved. But something in the quality of her stillness had changed — from a woman listening for lies to a woman listening for confirmation.

"So you weren't a courier."

"No."

"And the letter you carry."

His mouth went dry. "It's Ashe's handwriting. I copied that handwriting thousands of times. I could forge it myself, that's how well I know it — and knowing that, knowing I *could* be fooled, I've turned the thing over in my mind ten thousand times since the marshes, and I keep coming back to one detail." He reached into his shirt, slowly, showing her his empty palm first, and drew out the oilcloth packet. "There's a smudge on the third page. Ink transferred from the heel of his left hand. He was left-handed and sloppy and he always dragged his hand through fresh ink, and I spent three years blotting his letters before they went out. Forgers copy what they see. They don't reproduce mistakes."

Coral took the packet. She unwrapped it with the same unhurried care she gave a wound, and read, and Elias watched her face do very little while everything behind it rearranged itself.

"The deserter who gave me this," Elias said, "told me Ashe is alive. Imprisoned. That his death was arranged the same week the Kessel Ford reports were closed, and that the letter names names — Halloway's, and others above him. And then he told me the rest, which is that men have been hunting everyone who ever touched this paper, and he didn't survive the telling." He hesitated. "He also told me something else. That there was a nurse on the river road who kept a book. Who wrote down the names the officers left off their reports. He said—" Elias stopped, embarrassed by the shape of it. "He said if the letter was real, she was real too. Two halves of the same proof. The letter says what happened. The ledger says who it happened to."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Then she wrapped the letter back into its oilcloth with surgical neatness and held it against her chest, and Elias saw that her hands were not steady after all.

"You let me believe you were a courier," she said. "For three days. While I hid you, fed you, treated you, and lied to a superior officer's scouts about you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the truth sounds insane." The words came out of him in a rush now, three days of fear finally cracking the dam. "A dead captain alive, a massacre nobody will confirm, a major who falsifies reports and smiles while he does it — say it aloud and you sound like a madman, and madmen get restrained and searched and their papers burned. I needed you to trust me first. I told myself that. And partly it was even true." He looked down at his ruined hands, ink-stained at the knuckles even after two years out of service, as if the work had seeped into the bone. "But mostly I was a coward. I've been a coward since the day I signed my name under a number I knew was false, and I've been trying to un-sign it ever since, and I thought finding you would be enough. Like handing you the letter settled the debt. It doesn't, does it?"

"No," Coral said quietly. "It doesn't."

* * *

She paced then — three steps, turn, three steps — the first restless motion he'd ever seen her make. Outside, rain had begun again, drumming on the canvas roof, and through the flap he could see the river bridge in the distance, black and dripping, strung with lanterns like a necklace on a drowned woman.

"If your deserter was right about me," she said, "he may have been right about the rest."

"That's what I keep hoping. And keep doubting."

"Doubting is cheaper." She stopped pacing. "Show me the dates. On the letter. Every date it mentions — deaths, transfers, the date of the massacre itself, whatever Ashe recorded. I need to check them against my book."

"You'll check them against your—" He stared at her. "You have entries from Kessel Ford? The official record says those men died at the ford. In battle. Honorable."

"The official record says forty-one." Her voice had gone flat and hard as slate. "My ledger says otherwise. I've been writing down the erased ones for two years, Mr. Vann. Wounded men who passed through here and died of wounds that reports never mentioned. Prisoners who were transferred and never arrived. A burial party that came back short three men and no one asked why. I didn't know what I was collecting." Something moved behind her eyes — not fear yet, but its herald. "I thought I was keeping a private grief. You're telling me I've been keeping evidence."

They spread their materials on the foot of his cot like conspirators: the letter, opened flat, and her ledger — a plain quartermaster's notebook, cloth-bound, its corners soft with handling. Elias read the dates aloud in a whisper while Coral traced her columns with one finger, lips moving, and the rain came harder, covering them like a curtain.

The third date matched. The seventh. The eleventh.

By the fifteenth, Coral had stopped checking and simply sat there, both hands flat on the open ledger as though holding it down against wind.

"It aligns," she said. "Every single one. Either your letter is genuine, or someone built an extraordinarily elaborate forgery to match a book only I knew existed." She looked up at him, and Elias saw the moment the fear crested and broke into something else — resolve, cold and bright as a scalpel. "Which means I am no longer a nurse with a private grief. I am a witness. And witnesses get silenced."

"Yes," Elias said. "Which is why the deserter wanted it all in one place. Paper survives. People are—" He gestured vaguely at the ward, at the moaning, the lamplight, the whole bleeding enterprise around them. "People are temporary."

"Paper survives," Coral echoed. She closed the ledger gently, the way she closed patients' eyes. "Then we make sure some of it does."

"We?" He heard the word hang in the air, absurd, presumptuous. "Sister Okafor, I lied to you for three days. I used your charity as cover. You owe me nothing but a quick word to the provosts."

"I know precisely what I owe you," she said. "Nothing. That's what makes this a choice instead of an obligation, and I've found that obligations break under pressure while choices hold." She tucked the ledger into the fold of her apron, and for the first time since he'd met her, she smiled — a small, dry, unsentimental thing, gone almost before it arrived. "Besides. You tell the truth badly, Mr. Vann. But you tell it completely. Once you started, you couldn't stop. That's rarer than honesty, in my experience. Honest men edit."

Outside, the rain slackened, and in the sudden quiet they both heard it at the same instant: hooves. More than one horse, coming up the north road at an orderly trot — the trot of men arriving on schedule, not fleeing.

Coral was at the flap in two strides. Elias joined her, and together they watched a small column of riders resolve out of the dusk, uniforms clean, horses groomed, an officer's pennant snapping wet in the wind.

At their head rode a broad, silver-templed man whose posture suggested he was arriving at a dinner party rather than a flood hospital. Even at this distance, even through rain, Elias recognized the set of the shoulders — he'd taken dictation across a desk from those shoulders for two years.

"That's him," Elias breathed. "Halloway."

Below, a hospital orderly was already running to meet the riders, and Coral's hand found the ledger through her apron, pressing it flat against her body as if it already weighed more than she could carry.

"He's here to count," she said softly. "So we'd better decide, right now, what number we're going to be."
