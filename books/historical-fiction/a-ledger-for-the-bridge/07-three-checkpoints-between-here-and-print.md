## 7. Three Checkpoints Between Here and Print

The rain had stopped by the time Coral spread the map across the dispensary table, weighting its corners with four amber medicine bottles and a tin of forceps. The canvas was old — requisitioned from a dead surveyor's kit two winters ago, its ink softened by damp into something like bruise-colored veins. The port city sat at the bottom right edge, marked with a faded anchor symbol and the name *Verrenhol* in a cartographer's confident hand. Two hundred and eleven miles, if the scale bar could be believed. She had walked the distance twice with her thumb before she let herself believe it.

Two hundred and eleven miles. Three checkpoints. No papers.

She said it aloud, because saying things aloud had always been how she made them real enough to fight. "Three checkpoints. No papers."

Across the table, Elias Vann stood with his arms folded too tightly, as though holding himself together at the seams. He had slept four hours on a stretcher in the supply tent, and it had not been enough to soften him. The letter lay between them on the map, that bloodstained rectangle of paper that had turned her orderly world inside out in a single evening. Rowan Ashe's hand. A man two years dead, writing about Kessel Ford in a script that slanted left like a man leaning away from his own words.

"We don't need papers," Elias said. "We need to look like people who have them."

"That is either wisdom or madness."

"Usually both." He leaned over the map, and his finger — blunt, ink-stained at the cuticle, a clerk's finger even now — traced the river road east. "I came this way. Marsh country for the first thirty miles, then the road climbs along the old quarry line. There's a sentry post where the road crosses the bridge at Tallowmere." His finger stopped. "That's the first one."

"The bridge," Coral said. "Of course it's the bridge."

Every army in history, she thought, put its teeth at crossings. The bridge at Tallowmere was the only span over the Sere that a cart could cross for forty miles in either direction; she had ferried wounded convoys over it three times and each time watched the same ritual — lanterns, ledgers, a sergeant with a bayonet used less for fighting than for prying open crates and, occasionally, suspicion. They searched for deserters there now, Elias said. Had been since autumn. Men who walked away from Kessel Ford, or from what Halloway's reports insisted Kessel Ford had never been.

"They're not really looking for deserters," Coral said slowly. "They're looking for witnesses. Deserters can be shot quietly. Witnesses talk."

Elias looked at her with something new in his face — respect, maybe, or the recognition of a person discovering they've hired a sharper mind than they'd budgeted for. "That's exactly what Ashe wrote. In the letter. *They will not hunt me for running. They will hunt me for remembering.*"

She pulled her ledger out from under the map — her real one, the small oilcloth-wrapped book that lived against her ribs — and set it beside Ashe's letter. Two documents, side by side on a surveyor's map of a country trying to forget itself. Forty-one names in hers. She had counted again last night by candle stub, touching each entry like a rosary. Eleven of those names belonged to men whose units reported them lost at Kessel Ford. Eleven men whose deaths had been recorded before they'd ever happened, filed neat as prescriptions.

"It has to be Brandt," she said. "Not any journalist. Not some correspondent who'll sit on it until the war ends and everyone agrees to be gentle. Adaeze Brandt. She's at Verrenhol gathering testimony for the tribunal — Imelda heard it from a Red Cross driver who heard it from a customs clerk. If anyone will print names without asking permission from a general first, it's her."

"You know her?"

"I've read her." Coral tapped the ledger. "Two years ago she published an account of the Vey camp — actual names, actual dates, printed while the war was still on. They burned the edition in the capital and she printed it again from Verrenhol. She doesn't scare."

"People who don't scare get hanged."

"Yes," Coral agreed. "And people who do scare get forgotten. I've spent this whole war choosing which I'd rather be."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the lull between casualty wagons, and by lamplight after supper, when the ward's moaning settled into its nighttime rhythm and Sister Imelda's footsteps creaked their rounds overhead. Coral drew the route in pencil, then erased it and drew it again, because a map in the wrong hands was a confession.

"Say it back to me," she told Elias, in the tone she used on orderlies learning wound-dressing. "All three. In order. With what stands between us and each one."

Elias straightened like a schoolboy called on, and something in her chest twisted — he was Ashe's clerk, she reminded herself, a man who had spent years being exact for a living, and being exact was a kind of courage too.

"One." He held up a finger. "Tallowmere Bridge. Thirty-one miles east of here, where the road crosses the Sere. Garrison checkpoint, full search protocol — carts, papers, persons. Sergeant's discretion on anything ambiguous." 

"What does discretion mean at Tallowmere?"

"It means Sergeant Brohm decides whether you're worth the paperwork of arresting. I saw him once, two years ago, when I carried Ashe's dispatches through. He took a bribe from a smuggler right in front of me and wrote the man's name in his book anyway. He likes the book more than the money."

"A man who likes his book," Coral murmured. "Then we must give him nothing to write."

"Two." A second finger. "The quarry pass, day three if we make good time. It's a chokepoint in the hills where the old stone road narrows between cut faces. Manned by conscripts — garrison scraps, boys mostly, because nobody wastes good soldiers guarding rock. Corporal's command. Poorly paid, poorly fed, and they shake down travelers for cigarettes more than anything else."

"And the third."

Elias's hand hesitated over the last stretch of the map, where the river widened and the anchor mark waited. "The customs house at Verrenhol harbor. That's not a checkpoint, that's a fortress of paperwork. Every crate, every traveler, every letter entering the port goes through customs inspection — and since the tribunal announced hearings, Halloway's officers have been seconded there. They know someone's carrying testimony to Brandt. They may already have descriptions."

Coral sat back. Lamplight moved on the wet canvas outside, and somewhere in the ward a man called out for his mother in a language none of the nurses spoke, and another answered him in it, and quiet fell again.

"Papers," she said. "Each one wants papers we don't have."

"I can forge travel passes. I was a clerk; I forged Ashe's signature well enough that he stopped writing his own letters and just dictated." A thin smile. "But forged paper only survives a bored inspector. At Tallowmere they'll check the registry numbers. At Verrenhol they'll check twice."

"Then we don't carry forged papers. We carry true ones — the wrong ones." She was thinking aloud now, pacing the narrow aisle between cot frames stacked with folded linen. "A nurse travels with different papers than a courier. A corpse-hauler travels with different papers than either. Nobody questions a man moving bodies to burial — it's the one trade this war respects."

Elias stared at her. "You want to ride to the bridge in a death cart."

"I want to pass the bridge in a death cart. There's a difference, and the difference is that we arrive on the far side alive." She stopped pacing. "Imelda requisitions burial transport every week. The dead go downriver to the consecrated ground past Tallowmere. The hauler is a civilian contractor named Odo Renn — half-deaf, wholly indifferent, paid by the load. If we're in his cart under shrouds among his cargo, Sergeant Brohm will lift a corner of canvas, see what he expects to see, and wave us through. Grief is the best disguise ever issued. Nobody searches grief."

"And the quarry pass? We'll be on foot by then, out of the cart."

Coral considered. Outside, the rain started again, patient as bureaucracy. "Boys at a chokepoint want two things: to go home, and to feel like soldiers doing something. We can't give them the first." Her jaw tightened. "Maybe we give them the second. Or maybe—" and here she paused, because the thought arrived fully formed and frightened her a little, "—maybe we tell them the truth. A piece of it. Enough that looking away feels like mercy instead of laziness. Conscripts aren't loyal to Halloway. They're loyal to surviving, and so are we. That's a common language."

"And Verrenhol?" Elias asked quietly. "The fortress of paperwork?"

She didn't answer immediately. She went to the tent flap and looked out at the dark, at the river running black and swollen toward the sea, carrying everything it was given — rain, silt, ash from burned farmhouses upstream, and once, she knew, bodies that no report had ever named. Everything the river received, the river carried. That was its whole doctrine.

"By Verrenhol," she said finally, "we'll know what we're facing. One problem at a time. That's triage — you treat what's killing the patient fastest. Right now that's the bridge."

* * *

Near midnight, when even Imelda's footsteps had gone still, Coral laid out the arithmetic of the thing on a scrap of blotting paper, in her smallest hand, the hand she kept for entries she might someday have to swallow.

Distance: two hundred eleven miles. Days, allowing for the cart, the hills, the weather: six, seven if pursued. Checkpoints: three. Papers in hand: none that would survive a second look. Allies: one half-deaf hauler to be persuaded, one head nurse not yet told, one corporal not yet met, one journalist not yet reached.

Odds: she did not write them down. Some sums were better left undone.

What she wrote instead was a single line beneath the figures, and she read it back to herself twice:

*Everything needed is heavy, but everything needed can be carried.*

It was the sentence she'd built the whole war around, the private creed of a woman who measured her days by what fit in a kit bag when evacuation orders came. A ledger weighed nine ounces. A letter weighed nothing at all. Guilt weighed the most, and guilt, thank God, did not require transport — it traveled inside you, pre-carried.

She fed the blotting paper to the stove flame and watched the arithmetic curl and brighten and go.

Elias appeared at the flap, drawn by the light or unable to sleep. "You've decided something."

"I've decided we leave the day after tomorrow, with Odo Renn's morning cart. Which means tomorrow I have to convince a deaf man to smuggle two living passengers in a cart of corpses, and convince Sister Imelda to lie to a garrison quartermaster about the manifest." She closed the ledger and pressed it flat against her ribs, where it lived. "So no, Mr. Vann. I haven't decided anything. I've merely scheduled my decisions."

He almost smiled — the first time she'd seen his face attempt it. "Ashe used to say that about battle plans. Schedule the decisions and the fear never catches up."

"Captain Ashe," Coral said, " sounds like a man I'm increasingly glad is alive. And increasingly afraid for." She banked the lamp low. "Sleep. Tomorrow we begin lying professionally."

Outside, the river ran on toward the sea, patient and indiscriminate, and Coral lay down in the dark with her hand over the ledger and began, methodically, to rehearse the shape of a lie — and did not yet know that by week's end she would be rehearsing a far harder speech, one delivered to a young corporal in a stone corridor with the truth held out like a lamp between them.
