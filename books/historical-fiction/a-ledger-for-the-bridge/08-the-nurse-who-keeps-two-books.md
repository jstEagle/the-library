## 8. The Nurse Who Keeps Two Books

Sister Imelda had buried three wars and one husband, and she had learned that the difference between the two was only a matter of paperwork.

She was counting morphine tablets when she heard the rain change its mind against the canvas — a softer sound now, almost apologetic, as though the sky itself were embarrassed by what it had done to their hospital all week. The flooded ward smelled of carbolic and river mud and the sweetish rot of bandages past saving. Forty-one men lay in rows on pallets raised on beer crates, and forty-one men breathed at different speeds, and Sister Imelda had learned to read those breaths the way other women read letters from home.

The morphine count was wrong.

Not by much. One tablet's worth, perhaps two, if her eyes — still good, thank God, still good at fifty-eight — had not betrayed her. She counted again, touching each brown lozenge with one dry fingertip as she moved them across the tin lid, the way she had done every evening for thirty years, since the fever camps outside Vellacourt where she'd first learned that God kept no records and so someone had to.

Two tablets. Over three nights, by the look of the wear on the paper seal — someone had been opening it carefully, pressing it closed again, the way a child smooths the wrappings of a gift they've peeked inside.

Imelda did not sigh. Sighing was a luxury for women whose wards did not leak. She set the tin down, wiped her hands on her apron out of habit rather than need, and began to think about Nurse Okafor.

* * *

Coral Okafor was the best nurse Imelda had trained in eleven years of training nurses, and Imelda included herself in that accounting without bitterness. The girl had hands that knew what they were doing before her mind caught up, which was the whole secret of the work — the mind hesitated, the mind grieved, the mind wanted to stop and weep over the third amputation of the day, but good hands went on. Coral had good hands and a better spine and a face that gave away nothing to officers, which in this war was a professional skill worth more than any diploma.

Which was precisely why the missing morphine troubled her.

A thief stole to sell or to soothe. A frightened nurse stole to sleep. But Coral Okafor neither sold nor slept nor trembled. She worked eighteen hours a day, ate standing up like a horse at a trough, efficient and uncomplaining, and gave away nothing — not her thoughts, not her history, not so much as a photograph of anyone waiting for her anywhere. In thirty years Imelda had learned that the ones who hid things well were the ones hiding the largest things, and the size of the thing could usually be measured by how quietly they carried it.

Coral carried silence like a woman carrying water across a long distance: level, careful, and never spilling a drop.

Imelda finished her rounds, checked the chest wound of a boy who should have been sent to the rear a week ago and would die here instead because the evacuation orders kept saying *soon*, and then, on her way back to the dispensary tent, she made a decision that thirty years of discipline told her was none of her business and thirty years of experience told her was entirely her business.

She waited until the ward lamp was turned low. She waited until Coral took her turn walking the rows with the opium tincture, moving pallet to pallet in the dark like a ferryman among moored boats. And then Imelda let herself into the small supply alcove behind the dispensary — the one with the false back in the linen shelf that she herself had built during the second winter, when they'd needed somewhere to keep the quinine away from the requisition officers — and she sat down on the crate there to wait, because Coral's errands after lights-out always ended in this alcove eventually, and Imelda had known that for years too, and had never once asked why.

Rules, she thought, folding her hands in her lap. Rules were what you obeyed until the day you understood them, and after that day you either broke them knowingly or went on obeying them like a sleepwalker, and Imelda had watched too many sleepwalkers walk off into the dark to want to be one.

She heard Coral come in at half past eleven. Heard the girl stand very still in the dispensary — she always stood still first, listening, another habit Imelda approved of — and then the soft scrape of the shelf panel, and then the strike of a match.

Imelda leaned forward and looked through the gap between two folded blankets, and saw Nurse Okafor sitting cross-legged on the floor with a small oil lamp and a book open across her knees, writing.

* * *

It was not a diary. Imelda had expected a diary — girls kept diaries, even girls with faces like locked doors; especially those girls. Diaries held love and grievance and the small treacheries of the heart, and Imelda would have read it, God forgive her, and put it back, and said nothing forever.

This was not a diary.

This was a ledger. Columns. Dates. Names written in a small, fierce, upright hand, each name followed by regiment, by wound, by the words the reports had used and the words the truth required. *Missing, presumed drowned* — and beneath it, in Coral's hand: *Found. Died of his wounds 14th, in my arms. Buried north bank, marked with his identification disc under a stone.* *Deserted at Kessel Ford* — beneath it: *Did not desert. Died 12th of gas, brought in by stretcher-bearers who left before reporting him. I have his paybook.*

Page after page. Dozens of names. Men the army had mislaid, misreported, erased — and beside each one, the correction, entered like an act of worship by a woman who believed in no church Imelda recognized.

And at the bottom of the current page, fresh ink, still shining: *Ashe, Capt. R. — declared dead 2 yrs. Letter exists. Handwriting confirmed. Man named Vann claims he lives.*

Imelda sat very still on her crate in the dark and felt something move in her chest that she had not permitted herself to feel since Vellacourt, since the year the fever took four hundred souls and the official report recorded *eighty-seven deaths, cause: dysentery, unavoidable.* She had been young then. She had gone to the camp commandant with her own list, handwritten, four hundred names in her careful convent school script, and the commandant — a decent man, tired, afraid of his superiors — had taken the pages, looked at them a long time, and fed them into his stove one sheet at a time while she stood at attention and watched.

"Numbers frighten people, Sister," he had said. "Eighty-seven is a tragedy. Four hundred is an inquiry."

She had gone back to her ward and burned her draft copy herself, alone, and prayed afterward, and the prayer had been a lie and she had known it was a lie: *Lord, let me trust that You remember what I cannot keep.* Thirty years of trusting that. Thirty years of watching names go into stoves and into rivers and into the great soft forgetting of official arithmetic, and telling herself obedience was its own kind of holiness.

Obedience had never saved anyone. It had only ever made the forgetting orderly.

* * *

Coral sensed her — of course she did; the girl missed nothing — some shift in the breathing behind the blankets, some animal knowledge. The pen stopped. Imelda heard the ledger close with the care of someone closing a coffin lid.

"You may as well come out, Sister," Coral said quietly. "You've been breathing like a bellows for ten minutes."

Imelda pushed the blanket aside and stepped into the lamplight, and the two women regarded each other across three feet of packed earth, and neither of them apologized, because apology would have been a lie and both of them were too tired for lies at half past eleven.

"How long?" Imelda asked.

"Since my first posting. Four years." Coral's chin came up slightly, defiance arriving late, after the fact, like cavalry to a battle already lost. "Are you going to report me?"

"For keeping accounts?" Imelda lowered herself onto the supply crate with a grunt her knees insisted upon. "Child, I have spent thirty years keeping accounts. Mine are just the sort the army approves of. Morphine in, morphine out, and never a question about why the out column is so much longer than it needs to be." She nodded at the ledger. "Read me the last page again. The Ashe entry."

Something flickered across Coral's face — the first expression Imelda had ever seen her fail to suppress, and it was fear, raw and new. "Sister—"

"I can read, Nurse. My eyes are old but they work in the dark, apparently better than my conscience has." Imelda folded her hands. "A letter. In the hand of a dead captain. Brought by a man sleeping in our convalescent tent who claims to be a civilian courier and snores like a man who has been running for his life. I may be fifty-eight, but I am not blind, and I count everything — tablets, bandages, lies." She paused. "Two tablets, by the way. From my morphine tin. Three nights running."

Color rose in Coral's cheeks. "For the Kessel boy in bed six. The one with the burns. The orderlies' ration was cut and he screams when the dressings change and—" She stopped. "I was going to tell you. I was working out how."

"You were working out how to tell me without telling me anything else," Imelda said. "I know. I invented that arithmetic." She held out her hand, palm up. "Show me the letter."

* * *

Coral hesitated for the length of three heartbeats — Imelda counted them, because she counted everything — and then she reached into the bodice of her uniform and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the tenderness women reserve for infants and relics.

The letter was bloodstained along one edge. The handwriting marched across the page in a strong slanted script, and Imelda, who had signed off on thousands of soldiers' letters home as censor for this sector, felt the hair rise on her forearms, because she knew that hand. Every nurse in this sector knew that hand. Captain Rowan Ashe had written to thank the hospital staff two years ago — a courteous, old-fashioned note, *to the sisters and nurses whose care my men received*, and Imelda had filed it in the drawer where she kept the only pieces of paper from this war worth keeping.

Same hand. Older, harder, pressed deeper into the paper, the way a man writes when he is writing against time or in pain. But the same.

"He's alive," Coral said. Her voice was flat, controlled, and shaking underneath like ice over water. "He's been alive for two years while his own death report sits in a file somewhere, and the letter says why, and the reason is Kessel Ford, and the reason is names, Sister. Names like the ones in my ledger. Men who died there and were reported elsewhere, or never reported at all, so that no one would ever count them in one place and ask what happened."

"And the man Vann?"

"Courier, he says." Coral's mouth tightened. "He says a lot of things badly."

Imelda looked at the letter a long moment. Then she looked at the ledger lying closed on Coral's knee, and she thought about stoves, and about four hundred names, and about the boy in bed six who screamed when his dressings changed and whom the army had already written off in a column labeled *expected losses, non-combat.*

"If Halloway's men find this," she said, "they will burn it. If they find you holding it, they will burn you with it, or worse, and they will fill out a form afterward and the form will be accurate, because that is the one thing the army does accurately." She met Coral's eyes. "Do you understand what you are carrying?"

"Yes, Sister."

"Do you understand that I am required — by regulation, by oath, by thirty years of habit — to take that letter and that book to Major Halloway myself?"

"Yes, Sister."

Imelda sat with it. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere out in the ward a man called for his mother in a language none of the staff spoke, and the rain resumed, patient, indifferent, the way it had been falling on this war since before either of them arrived.

"I was at Vellacourt," Imelda said at last. "Second fever year. You'll have seen the memorial plaque in the chapel at Base Hospital Seven. Eighty-seven names."

Coral frowned. "I've read it."

"The true number was four hundred and six. I know because I wrote them all down, and then I watched a decent man burn them, and then I burned my copy myself, and then I knelt in a chapel for six hours asking God to do my remembering for me." Her voice did not break; it had forgotten how, decades ago. "He didn't answer, child. He never answers that particular prayer. Which means the remembering falls to us, and it falls to whoever is stubborn enough and foolish enough to pick up a pen."

Coral was staring at her as though seeing her for the first time — as though the grey-haired disciplinarian with the rosary beads and the iron rules had suddenly turned out to be made of the same material as herself. Perhaps, Imelda thought, she always had been. Perhaps that was the joke God had been letting sit for thirty years.

"So," Imelda said, and stood, and tucked the letter back into Coral's hands and closed the girl's fingers over it. "Here is what will happen. Tomorrow morning I will reprimand you publicly for irregularities in the dispensary counts, and you will accept it with proper contrition, and no one — including any visiting officer — will think to look further at the most closely supervised nurse in this hospital. Vann moves to the isolation tent tonight; he is officially a typhoid suspect, which keeps everyone away from him and gives me a legitimate reason to bring him meals myself." She picked up the lamp. "And when your orders come — and they will come soon, the river is rising and headquarters wants this ward moved — you will not be on the convoy list. I will see to it. I have been falsifying duty rosters since before you were born; it is the one sin I practice fluently."

"Sister Imelda." Coral's voice cracked on the name, just slightly. "Why?"

Imelda paused at the shelf panel, one hand on the wood, and considered the question honestly, because the girl had earned an honest answer and honesty had been in short supply in her life.

"Because I have obeyed every rule this army and this Church ever gave me," she said, "and the only thing obedience ever saved was my own comfort. Four hundred and six names, Nurse. I intend to be of some use regarding at least the ones in that book." She stepped through into the dispensary and blew out the matter with the last of the lamp's air: "Now go and check bed six before his fever turns, and give him the full tablet — I'll enter it as spillage. Spillage happens constantly in wartime. It's practically patriotic."

Behind her, very softly, she heard Coral laugh — one short, startled breath of it, the first Imelda had ever heard from her — and then the quick sure footsteps going toward the ward, and Imelda stood alone in the dark among the linens with her heart going hard and strange in her chest, like a drum before a march.

At dawn, when the orderlies came stumbling up from the river road white-faced and shouting that riders were on the quarry road — army riders, moving fast, flying no colors she recognized — Sister Imelda was already awake, already dressed, and already reaching for the duty roster to write the lie that might save them.
