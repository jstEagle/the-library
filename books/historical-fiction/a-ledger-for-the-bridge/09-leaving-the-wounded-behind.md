## 9. Leaving the Wounded Behind

The rain began at dawn and by noon it had eaten the road.

Coral heard it before she saw it — the particular sound of wheels too heavy for the mud they were drowning in, a grinding complaint of iron rim on flooded clay that meant supply wagons, or worse. She was elbow-deep in a basin of grey water, wringing out a cloth for Private Ansel's forehead, when Sister Imelda appeared at the tent flap holding an order chit between two fingers as though it were something dead she'd found in the drain.

"Evacuation," Imelda said.

The word went through the ward like cold water through cloth. Coral had heard it twice before in three years of war — once at Marrow Creek, once at the ridge hospital whose name nobody used anymore because there was nothing left to name. It meant the front had shifted, or the river had risen, or some major two days' ride away had decided that this stretch of mud no longer justified its wounded. It meant convoys, canvas tarps, men stacked like cordwood with their dressings seeping through their uniforms. It meant choices made at speed, by people who would not be the ones carrying anyone.

"How many can we move?" Coral asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant way, the way one is proud of a stranger's composure.

"As many as the army decides we can." Imelda handed her the chit. "Twenty litters to the bridge convoy. The rest stay."

"The rest *stay*?" Coral looked down the ward. Forty-one patients. She knew the number without counting; she kept it in the ledger, along with everything else. Forty-one men, and twenty litters, and a road dissolving into soup. "Half of them can't sit up. Half of them can't—"

"I know what half of them can do," Imelda said quietly. "I've been doing this since before you could hold a basin, girl. I'm telling you what the paper says, not what's right. Those are different documents and you'd best learn the difference now."

*You'd best learn.* Coral almost laughed. If only Imelda knew how well she had already learned it — learned it so thoroughly that she kept a second book hidden beneath the floorboards of the supply tent, sewn into oilcloth, filled with the names the first book would never carry. She had learned the difference between the documents so completely that she had become the difference herself.

She folded the chit once, twice, and put it in her apron pocket where it lay against her hip like a stone.

* * *

The rest of the day was arithmetic performed with hands.

That was how Coral thought of triage on evacuation days: not medicine but mathematics, cruel and quick. Twenty litters. Weigh each man against each man. The ones who could walk walked, even when walking opened their stitches, even when they wept doing it — because a man who could walk took up no litter, and a litter was a life, and everyone in the ward understood the exchange rate without being told. They had all become fluent in it.

She moved down the rows with Imelda, marking foreheads in grease pencil. A small cross for convoy. A circle for remain. The pencil squeaked on wet skin. Nobody argued. That was the worst part, Coral thought — the silence. At Marrow Creek a sergeant had begged her to mark his friend instead of him, and she had done it, and both men had died anyway, one on the road and one in the mud the convoy left behind, and ever since she had carried the memory of the begging more than the memory of either death.

Private Ansel got a cross. Fevered, lungs rattling like a loose shutter, nineteen years old, from a town whose name Coral had written in the ledger because no report had bothered to record it. He was the sickest man in the ward and therefore, by the cold logic of the day, he should have gotten a circle — left behind, kept comfortable, given whatever the morphine jar still held. But Coral marked him for convoy anyway, and Imelda watched her do it and said nothing, and Coral understood that Imelda was watching to see whether she would break her own rules, and that breaking them just this once was itself a kind of confession.

"He'll die on the road," Imelda said later, low, as they repacked a chest of bandages.

"He'll die here too. At least on the road someone will have to look at him."

Imelda's hands paused over the bandages. "That is either the most compassionate thing I've heard this year or the cruelest. With you it's often difficult to tell."

"It's usually both," Coral said.

Outside, the rain kept working at the world. Wagons groaned past on the road, going toward the river bridge, and every one of them reminded her of the other thing she was carrying, the thing heavier than any litter: the letter folded inside her coat lining now, Ashe's impossible hand, and beside it in her mind the ledger's columns of names that matched Kessel Ford date for date, man for man. Elias was in the supply tent pretending to inventory morphine tablets while actually sewing the ledger into the lining of her spare coat — his idea, done with the fumbling competence of a clerk, a double row of stitches that would take a knife or a very determined search to find. She hadn't wanted him touching it. She had let him anyway. There wasn't time for her hands to do everything, and that, too, was becoming a lesson of this week: conviction required delegation, and delegation required trust, and trust was just another word for something that could go wrong.

* * *

At dusk the convoy formed in the yard, and the yard turned into a country she recognized.

Lanterns swinging. Horses steaming. Men who could walk standing in the rain in their bandages like ghosts waiting for a ferry. Orderlies shouting about axle clearance. Somewhere a chaplain singing, badly, the hymn they always sang at departures, the one about crossing over, which nobody had thought to retire despite its obvious cruelty.

Coral stood at the tailboard of the fourth wagon and did the thing she always did at these moments, the ritual that had started the whole terrible habit of her ledger in the first place. She looked at every man being loaded and said his name silently, once, complete with rank and hometown if she knew them. Sergeant Oduya. Corporal Fenwick. Private Lamb, who was seventeen and lied about it badly. She measured her days this way — not in hours or in miles but in what could be carried when the orders came. Some days almost nothing. Today, twenty litters' worth, minus the ones who would die before the bridge.

And then there was the rest of the arithmetic, the part no order chit covered.

Elias came to stand beside her, hood up, hands jammed in his pockets like a man trying to make himself smaller than his guilt. "The riders passed through Vessel Cross this morning," he said. "A fisherman told me. Three of them, army remounts, asking after a civilian courier on the river road. Asking after—" He stopped. "Asking questions with your description in them, Coral. A nurse. Dark-skinned, they said, keeps records. That's how the fisherman put it. *Keeps records.* Like it's a disease."

"They're close, then."

"A day behind us. Maybe less, in this rain, if they've got good horses." He looked at the wagons, at the ward tents sagging under the water's weight, at the lanterns. "There's a place on the convoy for you. Imelda's already arranged it — a seat in the second wagon, papers saying you're transferred with the wounded. You could be at the garrison hospital by Friday. Safe. Documented. Invisible."

"And the ledger?"

"Sewn into your coat, going wherever you go. And the mission drowned in this mud." His jaw worked. "I'm not telling you to abandon it. I'm telling you that staying is not brave, it's arithmetic, and the numbers are bad. Two of us against three riders and a major who burns things. You've seen what he does to paper."

She had not seen it. She had only imagined it, nightly, in detail — Halloway standing over a brazier with her pages curling, the ink lifting off the paper like souls. The imagining was worse than seeing. Seeing ended.

"Ansel goes on the second wagon," she said.

"What?"

"Private Ansel. My sickest patient. He rides in the wagon I was supposed to be in, with my transfer papers, under my name if he needs one. He's delirious; he won't know the difference. Imelda will see him settled at the garrison." Coral heard her own voice planning this and marveled faintly at it, at how smoothly the lie assembled itself now, how the machinery of deception that had seized up so terribly under Halloway's charming eye now ran like a well-oiled cart. "Someone has to occupy my seat on that convoy. Someone has to be the nurse who left with the wounded. Otherwise the first person Halloway asks will say she stayed behind with a civilian, heading east."

Elias stared at her. Rain ran off the brim of his hood. "You're giving your escape route to a dying boy."

"I'm filling my seat," she said. "It's arithmetic. You taught me that this week, whether you meant to or not."

"That's not what I taught you."

"No," she agreed. "But it's what I learned."

* * *

She found Ansel propped on his litter, shivering, his fever climbing toward the place where fevers stop being illnesses and become weather. She sat with him while the orderlies strapped the litter to the wagon bed, and she held the tin cup to his lips even though he swallowed less than half of what she offered.

"Nurse Okafor," he said. His eyes were bright and unfocused, fixed on something above her head. "Am I going home?"

"Soon," she said.

"Mum doesn't know I'm hurt. I didn't write it down. I wrote that I'm fine." A cough took him and shook the whole litter. "I wrote I'm fine, Nurse. Is that a sin? Writing things that aren't true?"

Coral's throat closed. She thought of the ledger beneath the floorboards, forty-one names and more, every one of them a sentence the official reports refused to speak. *Is that a sin?* She had asked herself a version of that question every night for two years, and tonight, finally, she had an answer she could live with.

"No," she said. "Sometimes writing things true is the sin, and writing them fine is mercy. It depends who has to read them." She smoothed his hair back from his forehead, greased with sweat. "Your mother's going to read your name soon, Ansel. In the proper places. Written properly. I promise you that."

He smiled, drifting, and called her Mum, and did not know what he had just made her promise, and she felt the promise settle into her chest alongside the letter and the ledger and every other weight she had volunteered to carry.

Imelda was waiting at the wagon when the orderlies lifted Ansel aboard. The older woman's face was unreadable in the lantern light, which Coral had learned was its most expressive setting.

"You're not getting on this wagon," Imelda said. It was not a question.

"No."

"Halloway's riders are a day out. Maybe less. You're walking east into the rain with a fugitive and a book full of treason, and the only thing between you and a firing squad is mud." Imelda adjusted her wimple against the wet, a small fastidious gesture that seemed to belong to another, gentler century. "I have delivered babies, buried soldiers, and reported three head nurses for theft in thirty years of service. I am telling you this so you understand that I am not a romantic woman."

"Yes, Sister."

"When the boy dies — and he may die, Coral, fever like that doesn't negotiate — I will write to his mother myself. I'll tell her he was tended to the end by a nurse who kept faith with him. Every word of that will be true." Imelda's eyes came up, sharp as lancets. "But hear me. I am not covering for you because I admire you. I am covering for you because thirty years ago I promised God I would serve the sick, and somewhere in those thirty years the army decided that serving the sick meant serving its reports, and I signed my name to that arrangement too many times without noticing. This is me noticing." She pressed something into Coral's palm — small, wrapped in waxed cloth. "Quinine. Contraband, technically. It buys favors at checkpoints, if you find the right tired officer. Bribery is a sacrament in this army; use it accordingly."

Coral closed her fingers around it. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence with its indifferent industry.

"Sister Imelda," Coral said finally. "If they come here asking—"

"They will come here asking. And I will tell them exactly what I saw: a nurse evacuated with her convoy, per orders, witnessed by forty men." Imelda almost smiled. It was a frightening thing, that smile, like sun on a blade. "I've spent my whole career making records agree with the truth. Consider this my retirement project: making one record disagree with it."

Then, gruffly, before Coral could answer: "Go. Before I remember I'm a rule-keeper and change my mind."

* * *

The convoy pulled out at full dark, lanterns bobbing away down the drowned road toward the bridge, and Coral stood in the yard until the last light vanished and the last sound of wheels faded under the rain.

Forty-one minus twenty. Twenty-one men remaining in the ward, in Imelda's keeping, in the path of whatever Halloway's riders brought with them. She recited the twenty-one names in her head, standing in the mud, and hated herself with a clean and thorough hatred, and did not move.

Guilt, she was learning, was not a weight you set down before a journey. It was ballast. You took it aboard precisely so that you could not turn aside.

Elias touched her shoulder. "We should be at the quarry fork by morning if we cut the towpath."

"I know."

"Coral." Gently. "The men staying behind — they're not yours to carry. They're Imelda's now. That's why you gave them to her."

"That's what I keep telling myself."

"And?"

"And I don't believe myself yet." She pulled the spare coat on over her uniform — the coat with the ledger sleeping against her spine in its cocoon of oilcloth and Elias's careful stitching, forty-one names riding between her shoulders like a hunch, like wings. "Give it time."

They went out through the rear of the camp, past the latrine trenches and the burial detail's empty stretcher racks, and then they were in the fields, and the fields were water, and the rain came down with the patience of something that had all night and intended to use it. The towpath, when they found it, was a ribbon of higher ground along the canal, and they walked it single file, heads down, the dark ahead of them absolute except where lightning stitched it open for a half-second at a time — enough, each time, to see the river far to their left running high and brown, and beyond it the far bank, and beyond that the port city where a journalist named Adaeze Brandt was collecting testimony, and between here and there, three checkpoints, three sets of papers they did not have, and a major whose men were already on the road behind them.

Somewhere out in the black, a horse whinnied. Both of them stopped. The sound came again, closer to the road than to the canal, moving north — away, probably. Probably.

"We'll reach the bridge by tomorrow dusk," Elias murmured. "The checkpoint sits at the near end. Sentries, a barrier chain, a search shed for carts." He hesitated. "I've been thinking about carts."

Coral looked at him, rain streaming between them, and waited.

"There'll be burial details on that road," he said. "There always are, after an evacuation — corpse-haulers taking the dead downriver. A cart full of shrouded bodies gets waved through faster than anything alive." His voice was steady, but she could hear the cost of the idea underneath it, the shape of what he was about to ask, already forming. "The question is whether one of us can lie still enough to pass among the dead."
