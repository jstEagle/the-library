## 10. The Bridge at Dusk

*Coral*

The dead men came to her at dusk, as they always did.

Elias had borrowed the cart from a burial detail two miles upriver — borrowed was his word; the driver lay sleeping off a bottle of contraband rum in a ditch, and would wake tomorrow with an empty cart and a story no one would believe. The corpses were real. That was the terrible economy of it. Three soldiers dead of wounds and fever from the hospital's overflow tent, unclaimed by any regiment, and the army was glad enough to have them hauled to the consecrated ground past the bridge rather than rotting where they lay. Elias wore the hauler's coat and cap. He had smeared mud on the cart's side boards until it read like a hundred other carts that crossed the river every week.

"You'll lie under the middle one," he said, not looking at her while he said it. He'd been not looking at her all afternoon.

"I know."

"It isn't too late to—"

"Elias." She let his name sit between them like a stone dropped into still water. "We've been over it four times. If they search the driver, they find a man with no papers worth trusting. If they search you alone, they find a corpse-hauler with a letter sewn into his boot and no explanation for why a burial detail pays in advance. But if they search *me*, they find a nurse with travel chits signed by Sister Imelda, going home to her mother in Marrow Bend because the hospital's evacuating. That's true. Every word of it is true except the direction I'm walking afterward."

He looked at her then, finally, and she saw that what frightened him wasn't the plan. It was that she could say *true* so many times without flinching.

"The shroud goes over your face," he said.

"I know that too."

The rain had stopped an hour before, but the air hadn't noticed; everything hung wet and heavy, the kind of dusk where color drains out of the world early. The river ran brown and swollen beneath the bridge, carrying sticks, leaves, a drowned sheep, things the war didn't want back. On the far bank the checkpoint lamps were already lit — three of them, yellow smudges in the gray, and beneath them the shapes of sentries moving among the evening traffic like slow insects. Carts. A mule train. An ambulance with its canvas flapping.

Coral changed behind the tent one last time. Out of the nurse's apron. Into the plain dress and shawl Imelda had pressed into her hands without a word, along with a paper of travel chits and — this was the part that undid her — a small tin of boiled sweets, the kind Imelda kept for children brought in with the wounded.

"For the road," the old woman had said. "Don't thank me. Thanking me means we both know what we're doing."

Then she'd looked at Coral for a long moment with those eyes that had watched thirty years of men die politely, according to regulation, and added: "Whatever you're carrying — carry it well. The dead hate a careless courier more than a cowardly one."

Now Coral lifted the ledger one last time before it went into hiding. It was heavier than it should have been. Forty-one names. Forty-one lines of ink in her small, careful hand: name, regiment if she'd gotten one, wound or fever, date, and the single word *unreported* beside each. Some entries had only descriptions — *private, young, called himself Tull, burned* — because death had taken the names faster than she could ask for them. She read through them once, the way a priest reads a psalm he knows he will need in the dark, then wrapped the ledger in oilcloth and bound it flat against her chest with the strip of linen Imelda had provided, knotting it high, just below the collarbone, where a searching hand would be least welcome and least thorough.

Beneath her heart. She'd told Elias that, and he'd nodded as though it were tactics. It wasn't tactics. It was where she intended it to live until it could live somewhere better.

"Ready," she said.

Elias helped her into the cart. The first body was stiff and cold through its canvas shroud; she climbed over it as gently as if it could feel, apologizing under her breath — *forgive me, forgive me* — and settled into the space between it and the middle shroud. The cart bed smelled of pitch and old blood and the sweetish rot the rain coaxed out of everything. Elias drew the middle shroud up over her body, tucking the canvas around her shoulders, and paused with his hand at her face.

"Through the nose," she said. "Leave my mouth free. If I can't breathe slow, I'll cough, and coughing is worse than anything they'll do to us."

"If they lift it—"

"They won't. And if they do—" She made herself smile at him through the falling canvas. "I'm a dead woman who died of fever. Dead women don't answer questions."

The canvas came down. The world went to a dim amber-gray, close as a coffin lid, smelling of carbolic and wet wool.

She heard Elias climb onto the bench. Heard him speak to the mule — he'd learned its name from the sleeping driver, Bess, and he used it now with a naturalness that surprised her; the clerk had a country boy buried somewhere inside him after all. The cart lurched, rolled, found its ruts.

They went down toward the bridge.

---

*Elias*

He had imagined the checkpoint a hundred times since dawn, and every imagining was wrong.

In his head it had been a wall of bayonets, a barked challenge, a lantern thrust through the canvas. In truth it was boredom. The sentries at the bridge were tired men at the end of a long shift, and the war had taught them that nothing interesting crossed this river anymore. A sergeant with a ledger book sat at a plank table. Two privates walked the line of waiting carts, lifting tarps, prodding sacks, moving with the joyless rhythm of men doing arithmetic they hated.

Elias counted the traffic ahead of them. Four carts. A mule train. The ambulance. He made himself breathe the way Coral had taught him that morning — *four counts in, six out, the breath longer than the fear* — and made himself look at nothing. Men who looked at nothing got waved through. Men who looked at the water, at the far bank, at the sergeant's face, got remembered.

"Halt." A private stood at Bess's bridle, young, cheeks raw from the damp. "Burial detail?"

"That's right." Elias pitched his voice low and flat, the way the driver's had been. "Three for the ground past Marsh Lane. Consecrated side."

"Papers."

He handed over the driver's chit and the burial order, both genuine, both bearing the ink-smudge of a quartermaster who signed things the way other men swatted flies. The private read them upside down first, corrected himself, read them again. Behind Elias, in the cart, a woman he had known for nine days lay pretending to be dead, and forty-one names rode against her heart, and somewhere behind them Major Halloway's riders were asking questions in towns whose names Elias only half knew.

The private handed the papers back. "Open up."

That was the sentence. Elias had built the whole day around never hearing it, and here it was anyway, casual as weather.

"Mind the middle one," Elias heard himself say. "Fever case. Orders are nobody breathes over him."

It was a good lie. It was the best thing he'd ever said, maybe, and it came out of him smooth and cold, and he hated how much it sounded like Halloway must sound — a man managing paperwork, managing people, managing the distance between what happened and what got written down. The private hesitated. Fever cases made privates generous.

"Just the ends," the private said. "Sergeant wants the count."

Elias climbed down. His knees felt like borrowed hinges. He untied the rear flap of the canvas and folded it back, exposing the last shroud — Corporal Dench, twenty-six, dead of a wound to the stomach that Coral had fought for two days — and stepped aside. The private prodded Dench's shoulder with his baton. Prodded again, harder, the way you test fruit.

"Stiff," the private reported to no one.

"They get like that by evening," Elias said.

The private moved to the front of the cart, threw back the forward corner of the leading shroud, glanced at the dead man's gray face, and let the canvas fall. Then he came around to the middle. To the shroud that covered Coral. He took hold of the edge.

And stopped.

A voice from the table — the sergeant, bored, already looking past them. "Owen! Count 'em and move on, there's a whole queue and the light's going."

"Fever case," Owen said. "Hauler says orders are don't uncover it."

"So don't uncover it. You want the fever? Count it by the feet like everyone else does."

Owen looked at Elias. Elias looked back with the blank patience of a man who has hauled corpses until corpses are the only thing left to look at. Inside the shroud, Coral was breathing — he couldn't see it, wouldn't let himself imagine it, but he knew, the way you know a heartbeat in a quiet room, four counts in and six counts out, the breath longer than the fear.

The private bent, checked something at the foot of the middle shroud — the toe tag, Elias realized, the tag he'd forged by lamplight with a stolen pen — and straightened up. "Three," he announced. "All tagged. Wave him through."

Elias tied down the flap. His fingers worked fine; it was the rest of him that shook, somewhere underneath, in the place where his brother had died in the marshes holding a letter against his chest the way Coral held her ledger against hers. He climbed onto the bench. Took up the reins.

"Bess," he said, and the mule leaned into the collar, and the cart rolled forward toward the plank table and the sergeant's bored eyes and the narrow throat of the bridge itself.

---

*Coral*

Inside the canvas, Coral had learned new things about darkness.

It had texture. It had weight. It moved when the cart moved, settling against her like a second skin, and it amplified everything: the creak of the axle became a groan of timber, the mule's hooves became footsteps on a floor above her grave. She had lain with the dying often enough to know the sounds bodies make when they stop, and lying among the stopped ones, listening to her own blood move, she understood for the first time why the dying gripped the hands of nurses so hard. Not for comfort. For proof.

Four counts in. Six counts out. The breath longer than the fear.

The private's baton had touched her boot. She had felt it through the sole, a tap, polite as a knock at a door. Then the voice overhead — *fever case, orders are don't uncover it* — and Elias's voice answering, and the strangest sensation bloomed in her chest, beneath the oilcloth, beneath the forty-one names: pride. Not hers. His. The clerk who told the truth badly had just lied perfectly, and she had listened to him do it from inside a shroud, and she had wanted to applaud.

Then the canvas edge had lifted near her face. An inch. An inch of dying daylight, gray-gold, and beyond it the blurred shape of a young man's face leaning over hers, close enough that she could see the rain-beads in the stubble on his jaw.

She had done the only thing left. She had let her head loll — slow, boneless, the way the dead loll — and rolled her eyes back, and parted her lips, and stared at nothing with the practiced vacancy of a woman who has watched hundreds of faces arrive at exactly that expression.

The private stared down at her for three full seconds. She counted them. Three seconds is very long when you are pretending to be finished with time.

His hand shook, just slightly. Young, she thought. Younger than Tull, the boy who called himself Tull. He smells carbolic and he sees a dead nurse and some part of him is doing arithmetic about his own chances.

"Sergeant says count by the feet," the older voice said again, sharper.

The canvas fell. Darkness closed like water over her face, and she breathed — four in, six out — and the cart rolled on.

At the plank table there were boots, voices, the scratch of a pen. "Burial detail, three, Marsh Lane ground," the sergeant recited, half to Elias, half to his ledger book. Papers shuffled. And then, absurdly, gently: "Rain coming again. Mind the hill past the chapel, hauler — wheel ruts'll take an axle this season."

"Thank you, sir."

The cart moved. The wheels sang on timber — the bridge planks now, she knew them by sound, she'd crossed on foot twice in the spring — and beneath her, through the bed slats, through the canvas, she felt the river passing. The brown swollen water that carried everything away and gave nothing back. Somewhere upstream was Kessel Ford, and the names that weren't in any report, and the captain whose handwriting had come to her in a stranger's bloody fist. Somewhere downstream was the port, and Adaeze Brandt, and print.

She passed over the middle of the bridge — she knew it by the change in the wheels' song, a hollow note, the river directly below — and thought: *I am crossing over with them.* All of them. Tull, and Dench, and the boy with the burned hands, and the thirty-eight others, riding in a cart at dusk across the last river any of them would cross, toward a stone that might someday say their true names instead of *fever.*

Let this be the first stone, she thought. Let this crossing count.

The wheels found earth again. Grass-rutted track. The sergeant's voice faded behind them, calling the next cart forward, and the yellow lamps shrank, and the dark came down honest and open around her, and still she did not move. Not yet. Not until Elias stopped the cart, which he did at last a mile on, in a stand of alders where the chapel bell was ringing vespers faint and far.

The canvas lifted. Dusk poured in, real dusk, endless dusk, and Coral lay in it a moment longer, blinking, breathing air that moved.

"Well?" Elias's voice cracked on the word.

"We're across," she said. Her own voice was rust. She sat up slowly, and the dead men shifted around her, and she put her hand briefly on Corporal Dench's shrouded shoulder. "Rest easy," she told him. "You carried me. I won't forget the debt."

Elias helped her down. Her legs shook; she let them, privately, holding his arm. They stood in the alders with the bell tolling and the first drops of the new rain starting through the branches, and for one moment neither of them spoke, because there was a kind of speech that only works when you don't attempt it.

Then Elias turned back toward the cart to cover the shrouds against the wet, and stopped, and went very still.

"What?" Coral said.

He pointed. On the track behind them, back toward the bridge, dust rose in the last light — not the dust of carts. Faster than carts. Horsemen, five or six of them, coming down the far bank road at the trot, and even at this distance she could see the lead rider wearing an officer's grey cloak with the collar up, and even at this distance something in the set of his shoulders was familiar from a single afternoon in her tent, from a charming voice asking precise questions about deserters on the river road.

"They've reached the checkpoint," Elias whispered. "They're asking the sergeant who crossed tonight."

Coral pulled the ledger's knots tight against her breastbone and looked at the darkening road ahead.

"Then we don't sleep in any town with a gate," she said. "*Walk.*"
