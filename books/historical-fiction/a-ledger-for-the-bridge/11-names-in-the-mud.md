## 11. Names in the Mud

The far bank of the river was a country made of mud.

Elias Vann stood at the water's edge while the corpse-hauler's cart was waved through behind him, and he watched Coral Okafor climb down from among the shrouded dead as though she were stepping out of a coach at a church social. She brushed straw from her skirt. Her hands, when she held them out to the gray light, did not shake. His own had not stopped shaking since the sergeant's bayonet had hovered an inch above her face and then, by some mercy Elias would never be able to account for, dropped away.

"You can breathe now," she said.

He realized he'd been holding his breath since the bridge. He let it out in a long, shuddering stream that fogged the morning air. "I counted," he said. "The whole time we were on the bridge, I counted. I got to four hundred and eleven."

"What happens at four hundred and twelve?"

"I don't know. I've never gotten there."

She almost smiled. It didn't reach her eyes — nothing reached her eyes anymore; he'd noticed that in the hospital, a woman who rationed out expression like a quartermaster rationing bread — but it touched the corner of her mouth, and for a moment she looked young. Younger than him, even, though he doubted she was.

They walked. There was no road on this side of the river, only a towpath gone soft with rain, hemmed by reeds that whispered even when there was no wind, which felt to Elias like a conspiracy. Behind them the bridge checkpoint dwindled into the mist, and ahead the marshes opened up, brown and silver, the water lying flat as hammered tin under a sky the color of old bandages.

The letter rode against his chest where it had ridden for nineteen days now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his body. He had carried it across three provinces. He had carried it through marshes where his boots filled with water and stayed filled. He had carried it past a dead man in a ditch who had looked, in the half-light, so much like someone else that Elias had knelt beside the body for a full minute before he could make himself move.

And now it was going to be carried further, by both of them, toward something that might actually matter. He should have felt lighter. He mostly felt hollowed out, like a gourd with the seeds scraped clean.

Coral stopped walking at a bend in the towpath where a fallen willow made a bench of itself, half-submerged, its roots torn up from the bank in some flood years ago. She sat down on the dry part of the trunk without asking if he wanted to rest, which told him she needed to rest and was dressing the need as consideration.

"Eat," she said, and handed him half of the hard biscuit Imelda had pressed on them at the hospital door, along with a look Elias hadn't been able to read — a look between Sister Imelda and Coral Okafor that had passed over his head like weather.

He ate. The biscuit tasted of nothing, which was how all food tasted now, but his stomach took it gratefully.

For a while neither of them spoke. The river ran behind them, carrying its cargo of mist. Somewhere far off, on the other side, artillery muttered in its sleep — the war clearing its throat, reminding everyone it was still there.

"The man in the marsh," Coral said finally. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the water. "The one who gave you the letter. You never said his name."

Elias's chewing slowed. He swallowed what was in his mouth with difficulty, because his throat had decided to close.

"No," he said. "I didn't."

"Why not?"

Because I have spent nineteen days not saying it, he thought, because as long as I don't say it he is still walking somewhere behind me, still coming, still about to crest the next rise. Because names are how this war kills people twice.

"He was my brother," Elias said.

* * *

It came out of him then the way blood comes out of a wound when you finally pull the cloth away — all at once, and messier than he'd planned.

"His name was Tomas. Tomas Vann. He was two years younger than me and better at everything, which I resented right up until the day I understood that being good at things was just going to get him killed faster." He was looking at his hands now. They were filthy; everything about him was filthy; he had been filthy for so many weeks that cleanliness had become an abstraction, like peace. "He was conscripted. I bought my exemption — I had clerk's wages, our mother had saved, and we bought the paper that said I was more useful alive with a pen than dead with a rifle. Tomas used to joke about it. He'd write to me from the line and sign off, *your exempt brother, the coward.* He didn't mean it cruelly. He meant it the way soldiers mean things — to survive them."

Coral had turned to face him. She said nothing. She had the particular silence of nurses, he was learning — a silence that wasn't absence but presence, a silence that held you up.

"After Kessel Ford, his letters stopped. The official word came: missing, presumed dead. Presumed. That word does a lot of work, doesn't it? *Presumed.* Like they'd lost track of him the way you lose track of an umbrella." His voice cracked on the last of it and he hated that, hated the crack, hated that grief after all these months still had the power to ambush him like a knife in a doorway. "But then, in the spring, a letter came. Not through any post. Handed to a neighbor, no explanation. In it, instructions — a place in the marshes, a time, a name to ask for. And handwriting I knew."

"Ashe's."

"Ashe's." He nodded. "Tomas had been Ashe's runner. Off the books. Ashe trusted him — or trusted him as much as any captain trusts anyone in an army where trust is a liability. And Ashe, it turned out, was not dead, whatever the dispatches said. He was hiding in the marsh country with a handful of men who'd seen what happened at the ford, men whose names had also been *presumed* away. He'd spent two years writing letters nobody dared send. Mine was the one that finally went, because Tomas volunteered to carry it out. Because Tomas wanted to do one thing in this war that couldn't be filed away in somebody's report."

Elias stopped. The reeds whispered their conspiratorial whisper. Out on the flat water, a heron stood on one leg with the patience of a monument.

"When I found him," Elias said, and here his voice went very quiet, very level, the way a man's voice goes when he is holding something heavy over a long drop and cannot afford to tremble, "he'd been shot two days before. The marsh people had found him and tried. He lasted long enough to talk. He made me swear on our mother — who is dead, so it was the strongest oath he had available — that I'd get the letter to someone who kept records. Not to an officer. Not to a priest. To someone who *wrote things down.*"

"And he knew such a person existed?"

"He hoped. There were rumors. A nurse on the river road who kept names." He looked at her then, really looked, and saw her go very still. "I didn't believe it, honestly. I thought it was camp legend, the kind of story men invent because the truth is unbearable. A nurse who writes down the dead the army pretends never lived. But I was out of legends, so I came anyway."

The silence between them stretched. Elias became aware that he had never told anyone the whole of it before — not the marsh woman who'd hidden him, not the ferryman, no one. Telling it to Coral felt less like confession and more like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying, and discovering the shape it had left against his shoulder.

"Tomas died at dawn," he added, uselessly, because dawn seemed important. "He asked me if I thought anyone would read the letter. I lied and said yes. Then I sat with him until the marsh people made me leave, and I've been sitting with him ever since, in a way. Every checkpoint. Every mile. He's the heaviest thing I carry, and I don't even have to lift him."

Coral reached across the space between them and put her hand over his. Her palm was rough from lye soap and cold water. She left it there for perhaps ten seconds — he counted, because counting was what he did now — and then withdrew it, and the withdrawal was not rejection but discipline, the discipline of a woman who could not afford to hold on to anything, because holding on was how you drowned.

* * *

"My ledger started with a boy called Femi," she said.

They had begun walking again, because resting too long in one place was how people got caught — she'd said this twice already, like a catechism — and now the towpath climbed slightly above the marsh, giving them a view of the drowned fields, silver and endless. She spoke facing forward, which made it easier for him to listen, and easier, he suspected, for her to speak.

"Fifteen years old. He lied about his age to get fed — they all lie about their age, the boys, they lie the way they breathe. He came into our ward at the river hospital with a belly wound that should have killed him in a day and instead took nine days, because he was stubborn. Stubborn and funny and terrified, in rotation, sometimes all three in the same hour." Her voice was steady, but Elias heard the effort of the steadiness, the way you hear the strain in a rope. "He told me his full name on the third night. Femi Adeyemi. He made me repeat it back. He said, *Sister, if I die, don't let me become one of the ones nobody counts.* He'd seen it happen. Boys dying in cots and vanishing — no report, no letter home, no grave with anything true on it. Just gone, like they'd been erased off a slate."

The path curved. Water gleamed below.

"He died on the ninth night. I was holding his hand. And the next week the casualty list came through, and I read every name on it, twice, and his wasn't there. Not misspelled. Not misfiled. Absent. As if he'd never lain in that cot telling me jokes to keep himself from screaming. As if the nine days had happened to no one." She paused. "His sergeant had listed him as a deserter, I learned later. A fifteen-year-old deserter. It cleaned the paperwork. Deserter deaths don't need notifying. Nobody writes to a deserter's mother. Nobody owes a deserter's mother anything."

Elias thought of Tomas signing off as *the coward,* and something turned over in his chest.

"So I took a ledger from stores," Coral went on. "A plain one. And I wrote his name in it. Date, regiment, wound, the truth of how he died. And then I started watching, and once you start watching you see it everywhere — the gaps in the lists, the convenient absences, the deaths that happen off the record because the record would embarrass someone. Officers who lose men to bad decisions report them as fever. Officers who shoot their own report them as enemy fire. Every erasure has a signature behind it, even if the signature is just an empty line where a name should be." She glanced at him. "You understand. You're a clerk. You know what a blank line means in an army ledger."

"It means someone decided," Elias said. "That's what a blank line means. Someone decided, and then made sure no one could trace the deciding."

"Yes." Something moved through her face then, brief and fierce, like lightning inside a cloud. "So I keep the book. I'm not naive about what it is. One nurse's ledger isn't justice. It isn't even evidence, not properly, not yet. But it's a debt register. Every name in it is money owed. And debts," she said, and here her voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost terrible, "debts don't die. They wait. They wait for someone with the standing to call them in. Maybe your letter is that standing. Maybe Ashe's testimony plus my arithmetic equals a bill someone powerful finally has to pay."

They crested the low rise and the marsh opened before them, and for a moment Elias simply looked at it — the flat silver water, the black lines of the channels, the distant smudge of hills where, somewhere beyond, a quarry pass waited with its checkpoint and its conscripts.

"Do you ever count them?" he asked. "The names?"

"Every night. Before I sleep. I read them aloud, quietly, so no one hears. It feels — " she searched for the word — "*necessary.* Like feeding something. Or watering it. If I stop saying them, I'm afraid they'll start fading, and then Halloway wins without ever having to catch us."

"Halloway knows about the ledger?"

"Halloway suspects there's a reason a nurse lies smoothly. Whether he knows what I carry — no. Not yet." She adjusted the strap of her bag. "But he'll know soon. Men like Halloway always find out eventually, because men like Halloway are the ones the blanks were made for."

* * *

They walked until the light began to fail, and then Coral found them a drainage shed — a brick pump-house abandoned so long the ivy had claimed the door — and declared it shelter enough. Inside smelled of rust and pigeon droppings and, faintly, of rain-soaked stone, which was rapidly becoming the smell of Elias's entire life.

He built no fire. She forbade it with a look before he'd finished gathering sticks. So they sat in the blue dusk, side by side against the wall, sharing the second biscuit, and the silence that settled over them was different from the silences of the last days. It was not wary. It had been paid for.

"My brother would have liked you," Elias said into the dark.

"He'd have liked you too," Coral said. "Femi. He talked constantly. Drove the whole ward mad. He'd have talked your ear clean off."

"That sounds like Tomas."

"They would have been insufferable together," she said, and he heard it — the ghost of a laugh, small and cracked, but real, the first real laugh either of them had managed since the bridge. It hung in the pump-house air like smoke.

Later, when her breathing had gone slow and even, Elias took out the letter and unwrapped it just enough to feel the paper, and he thought about what Coral had said — *a debt register* — and he thought about Tomas at dawn, asking if anyone would read the words, and he answered him silently, the way he'd answer every night henceforth:

*Someone's reading. We're taking it to print.*

Then, because counting was what he did, he counted the hours until light. He got to two hundred and six.

And that was when he heard it — far off across the marsh, thin as a needle pushed through fabric: hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast, moving south, moving the way men move when they are not patrolling but hunting.

Beside him, Coral's breathing stopped mid-inhale. She had heard it too.

Her hand found his arm in the dark and gripped it, and neither of them said a word, because out there in the wet black distance, the riders were getting closer.
