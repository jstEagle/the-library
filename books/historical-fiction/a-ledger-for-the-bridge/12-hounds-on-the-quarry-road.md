## 12. Hounds on the Quarry Road

The warning came stitched into a man's chest.

Coral was elbow-deep in the third dressing change of the morning when the wounded driver arrived — a corporal named Finch, brought in from the river road with a splintered collarbone and mud to his eyebrows. She had him stripped and cleaned before she found it: not just shrapnel under the skin of his shoulder, but a scrap of linen sewn there like a second wound, folded small as a communion wafer. Someone had cut him open, tucked it in, and closed him up again with field stitches that were better than they had any right to be.

She knew only one pair of hands that steady.

She read it standing very still, while Finch watched her from the cot with the flat patience of a man who has already decided how much pain he is willing to complain about.

*They came at dawn. Six riders, grey coats, asking for a nurse who writes. I told them nothing because they asked me nothing — they are not here for questions. They are going down the river road. You have until dusk, maybe less. Burn what you cannot carry. — I.*

Sister Imelda. Thirty years of rules, spent in one morning.

Coral folded the note once, twice, until it was small enough to vanish inside her palm, and then she did the thing she had trained herself never to do in two years of war: she looked up from her work and let her eyes go around the tent without seeing any of it. The rows of cots. The smell of carbolic and river damp. The boy in the corner who called for his mother in a language none of them spoke. All of it suddenly arranged itself into a picture of everything she was about to leave.

"Finch," she said. "Who carried you off the road?"

"Fellow in a mule cart. Wouldn't give a name." He grimaced as she tied off the last bandage. "Paid me in tobacco to sit still while he did his needlework. I figured he knew what he was about."

He had. Which meant Imelda had chosen her messenger well too — a man with no name worth giving, no unit worth tracing, and a wound genuine enough to explain any trembling in his hands.

"Dusk," Coral said quietly. "Maybe less."

She went looking for Elias and found him at the far end of the ward, doing the work he'd fallen into without anyone assigning it: hauling water, wringing sheets, holding men steady while their wounds were probed. He had the particular usefulness of someone trying to earn his keep at a debt he couldn't name. When he saw her face he set down the basin at once. He'd learned to read her that fast, in the weeks since the bridge — or perhaps he'd always been able to read people, a clerk's skill, the reading of what is written on a face rather than paper.

"The hospital?" he asked.

"They've been here. They're moving down the river road now." She kept her voice below the moans of the fevered men. "Imelda says dusk."

Elias glanced toward the flap, toward the road beyond it, doing the arithmetic she was doing. The river road curved wide and slow toward the quarry pass; riders would cover by evening what they'd cover on foot in two days. If Halloway's men were already mounted, already moving, then the question wasn't whether they'd be overtaken. It was whether being overtaken happened somewhere open or somewhere they might survive.

"Then we don't take the road," Coral said. "We go across."

"Across," Elias repeated. He said it the way a man repeats a diagnosis.

"The fen path to the old drover's track, then up through the woodlots to the quarry. Imelda drew it for me our first week here — she used to walk it to market before the war ate the roads. She says the fen will take a cart but not a horse at speed." She was already pulling the ledger from its hiding place beneath the false bottom of her medicine trunk, checking the oilcloth wrapping with fingers that wanted to shake and were not permitted to. "It'll take us slowly, if we're careful."

"And if we're not careful?"

"The fen keeps what it takes. That's why nobody hunts in it."

* * *

Leaving was worse than she had rehearsed.

She had rehearsed leaving often, in the way one rehearses a death in the family — knowing it must come someday, telling oneself stories about dignity. But rehearsal had not included the moment when Corporal Ndiaye caught her sleeve as she passed his cot, his hand light as a bird's foot, and said, "You're going, Sister."

"I'm stepping out for an hour."

"Sister." He smiled with the terrible gentleness of the very ill. "I was a fisherman. I know what a boat looks like when it's leaving harbor."

She knelt beside him and held his hand and lied to him, and hated herself with a clean, thorough hatred, and lied anyway, because the truth would have put him in the position of knowing something men had died this week for not knowing. Then she gave Imelda his chart and three others, and stood a moment in the doorway of the dispensary where thirty years of obedience stood wrapped in an apron with flour on it from the morning's bread.

"You stitched a message into a man's shoulder," Coral said.

"I stitched a wound." Imelda didn't look up from the chart. "What was written in it is between you and God."

"Why?"

That made her look up. Her face was the same face it always was, lined and level, but something moved behind it like water under ice. "Because in thirty years I have obeyed every regulation ever handed to me, Miss Okafor, and I have buried every one of the men those regulations failed. I am not able to do much anymore. My knees are gone and my eyes are going." She tapped the chart against her palm, once, sharply. "But I can still choose which book I'm in. Go. And don't come back here. Whatever happens, don't lead them back here."

Coral took her coat from its hook. The ledger lay flat against her ribs inside the lining, sewn there by lamplight over three nights, and when she shrugged the coat on she felt it settle against her heartbeat like a second heart, heavier, and slower, and older.

* * *

The fen took them the way the sea takes swimmers — grudgingly, at the cost of everything.

They went out through the laundry lines at the back of the camp while the rain was still deciding whether to become serious, two figures bent under sacking, and within twenty minutes the world behind them had dissolved into grey. Coral navigated by Imelda's remembered landmarks: a lightning-split alder, a drainage sluice with a rotted plank walk, the place where the ground pretended to be solid for forty yards and then confessed. Elias walked behind her with his arms out slightly from his body, the posture of a man crossing a frozen pond, though it was October and nothing froze.

"How do you know where we are?" he asked after an hour. "Everything looks like everything else."

"That's the point of it." She tested the next step with her staff — a length of alder she'd taken without ceremony from the split tree. "The fen looks like nothing and remembers everything. There's a channel under us running to the river. Old peat cuts. If you step wrong you go down past your waist and the mud holds your boot like a hand." She heard him swallow. "So watch my feet, not the horizon."

They crossed the drowned meadows as the rain finally committed. By midday they were soaked through, and the ledger against her ribs had absorbed enough of her body heat to feel warm, which she tried not to think about — a thing alive against her heart. Once, wading a flooded ditch, she slipped and went down to her shoulder, and Elias hauled her out with a grip that bruised, and for a moment they stood thigh-deep in brown water breathing hard at each other, and neither of them laughed, because laughing felt like something that belonged to other people.

They reached the drover's track at dusk.

It was barely a track — two ruts gone to grass, climbing away from the fen through scrub and gorse toward the black line of the woodlots, and above the woodlots, faint as a scar, the pale terraces of the quarry. From up there, Coral knew, the pass narrowed to a stone throat between cut faces, and the second checkpoint sat in that throat like a cork. Conscripts. Boys. That was the report from the drover who'd come through the hospital in September with a crushed foot: the quarry post was manned by children with old rifles and new officers' boots.

"We sleep here or we walk," Elias said. His voice was hoarse. "If we walk all night we reach the pass by dawn. If we sleep—"

"If we sleep wet, we wake sick, and sick is slow, and slow is dead." She was already walking. "We walk."

He fell in beside her. For a long time there was only the squelch of their boots and the rain on the gorse, and then, without preamble, Elias said, "My brother could walk like this all night. Tomas. He grew up on marshland, further south. He said flat country teaches you patience because there's nowhere to hide except inside yourself."

Coral said nothing. He had begun, lately, to say the brother's name — always sideways, always attached to some practical observation about walking or weather or the quality of boot leather — and she understood it for what it was. A man opening a door a crack at a time so the draft wouldn't kill him.

"He's why you came north," she said. Not a question.

"Tomas and the letter." A pause. "I keep thinking — if I'd answered his last one. He wrote me in the spring, before Kessel Ford. Four pages about a girl in the village and complaints about the rations, and then one line at the bottom: *Something's wrong here, Eli. Ask me in person.*" Elias's laugh was short and awful. "I was busy. Quarter-master's accounts don't balance themselves. I wrote back about the accounts." He walked five steps. Ten. "Whatever he saw at the ford — he tried to tell me first. I made him carry it alone, so he carried it to a deserter's grave instead, and to you, by way of me, too late for all of it."

"It reached me," Coral said.

"Yes."

"And I'm still carrying it. So is he, in a sense. So—" she touched her coat, lightly, where the ledger lay, and saw Elias follow the gesture, "—he's not carrying it alone anymore. That's the arithmetic I do at night. Not the losses. The shares."

He was quiet for the rest of the mile. But his stride changed — lengthened, settled, the stride of a man who has stopped debating whether to arrive.

* * *

They came up out of the trees near midnight and saw the fires.

Not the checkpoint's — those would be screened, disciplined, a cook-smoke smudge behind the quarry wall. These were spread along the valley floor behind them, back the way they'd come, strung out along the river road like a rosary of orange beads. Campfires. A lot of them. Too many for a patrol and far too many for a pursuit that didn't know its quarry.

Elias counted under his breath. "Thirty. Forty." He lowered the borrowed field glasses he'd carried since the bridge in a canvas case, and his face in the dark was the color of ash. "That's not riders chasing a rumor. That's a cordon. They're not following our trail, Coral. They're closing the map."

She took the glasses and looked, though the rain blurred the fires into bleeding stars. Along the river road, and — her stomach tightened — along the fen's eastern edge, the direction they had believed empty. Lanterns there too, moving. Slowly. Methodically. Men walking a line abreast through ground no sensible soldier would bother with, unless he had been told precisely what he was looking for and precisely where she would cross.

"Halloway knows the fen path," she said. "Or he guessed it. Either way—" She lowered the glasses. "Either way he isn't hunting two deserters. Deserters you send a patrol for. This is a net. He knows exactly who we are, and he knows exactly what we're carrying, and he is spending a battalion to make sure neither reaches the port."

"Then the quarry pass—"

"Is the only door left." She said it before fear could edit it. "And he'll have sent word ahead. He'll have told them to look for a nurse and a clerk. Which means we can't be those things anymore by daylight." She stood in the rain a moment longer, watching the fires, feeling the ledger's weight against her ribs as if it had grown heavier with every mile, a stone rolled uphill by Sisyphus with a woman's hands. Below them the net drew tighter by inches. Above them the quarry terraces waited, pale and blind.

"Elias," she said. "How are you at lying?"

He turned to her. Rain ran off his chin. "Badly. You said so yourself. Chapter and verse."

"Good," said Coral Okafor, and began to climb. "Because tomorrow you're not going to lie at all. Tomorrow you're going to tell the truth — badly, exactly as you do — and pray to God it sounds more honest than anything Halloway's men have heard all year."

Above them, at the lip of the quarry, a lantern swung once in the dark and went still.

Someone was awake at the pass.
