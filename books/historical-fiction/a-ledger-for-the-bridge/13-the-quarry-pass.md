## 13. The Quarry Pass

The rain had followed them for two days, thinning to a grey mist that made everything look unfinished, like the world was a sketch someone had abandoned. Coral watched it bead on the limestone walls of the pass as they climbed, and thought of the ward she'd left behind — of how Sister Imelda would be moving down the rows of cots right now with her lamp, checking bandages by feel because the light was never good enough. The thought hurt in a way that had no name, so she folded it away and kept walking.

The quarry road narrowed as it rose. On either side the stone rose in terraced shelves, cut away decades ago for the fortifications that had never held, and now the terraces were colonized by gorse and bramble, green spilling over grey like a wound healing badly. Ahead, maybe four hundred yards, a barricade blocked the road: timber posts, sandbags gone soft and slumped in the wet, and behind them the small movements of men trying to stay warm.

"Three," Elias said beside her, his voice low. He'd been counting since they'd left the treeline. "Maybe four. I can only see three."

Coral counted too. A sentry at the near post, rifle slung, stamping his boots against the cold. Another hunched under an oilcloth awning nailed between two posts. And a third figure sitting on an ammunition crate behind the barricade, younger than the others, holding a tin mug he wasn't drinking from.

"That one," Coral said quietly. "The one on the crate."

Elias glanced at her. "What about him?"

"He's not watching the road. He's watching his hands." She'd spent eighteen months learning to read soldiers the way other people read weather. The ones who frightened you were the ones whose eyes moved constantly, cataloguing, measuring you against orders. The dangerous ones were calm. But every once in a while you found one who hadn't hardened yet — a boy the war had caught before it could teach him what to do with it. "He's thinking about something else. Home, probably. Or the last thing he saw that he didn't want to see."

"And you can tell all that from a mug of tea?"

"I can tell it from the way he's holding it." She adjusted the strap of her satchel — the medical satchel, Imelda's gift, filled with enough genuine supplies to make the disguise breathe. "You wait here. Let me do this part."

"We agreed—"

"We agreed I'd talk first if talking was possible. It's possible." She looked at him then, really looked — at the bruise still yellowing along his jaw from the fen, at the way he stood slightly favoring his left knee, at the ledger's weight sewn into her own coat pressing against her ribs like a second heart. "Elias. If this goes wrong, you take the eastern cut through the rocks. You don't argue, you don't come back for me. You go to Brandt yourself. You know the letter by heart. That has to be worth something even without the ledger."

"It isn't."

"I know. Go anyway."

* * *

She walked up the middle of the road, unhurried, hands visible, the way you approached any checkpoint — the way you approached anything in a war where everyone's trigger finger had been educated by fear. The mud pulled at her boots. The mist beaded on her eyelashes. Forty yards out, the sentry at the post raised his rifle halfway, not aimed but remembered.

"That's far enough."

His voice cracked on the last word. Coral almost smiled at that — almost, because smiling would have been a cruelty. He couldn't have been more than nineteen. Under the mud on his cheeks there were the soft planes of a face that hadn't finished deciding what it was.

"Coral Okafor," she said. "Nurse, Third Field Hospital. I'm escorting a civilian contractor down to the port for medical discharge." She let him see the satchel, the armband, the tiredness. All true things. The best lies were load-bearing walls built entirely out of truth. "Papers were lost when our convoy was strafed on the quarry road yesterday. I'll show you the burn marks on my kit if you want."

The sentry hesitated. Behind him, the man under the oilcloth stirred and came forward — older, a corporal's stripes stitched on crooked, with the particular red-rimmed eyes of someone who drank what he was supposed to guard. "Lost papers," he repeated. "That's a new one. Usually it's burned papers. At least burning takes effort."

"Burning takes effort," Coral agreed mildly. "So does losing. We did neither on purpose, Corporal. The war did it for us."

Something flickered in the corporal's face — not amusement exactly, but the ghost of it, the reflex of a man who'd once laughed easily and now had to be reminded how. "Contractor," he said, jerking his chin toward Elias, who had come up the road behind her at a distance calculated to look obedient rather than suspicious. "What's his trade?"

"Quarrying," Elias said. "Blasting and haulage. Before the war I ran crews at the Kessel works."

The word landed wrong. Coral felt it land, felt the whole scene tilt a degree off its axis. The corporal's eyes sharpened; the young sentry glanced up from his boots.

Kessel. Of course. The quarry district — the whole pass belonged to the same company that had worked the ford country. These boys weren't garrison regulars. They were conscripts raised out of these very valleys, men who might have grown up hearing about Kessel Ford the way children hear about anything their elders refuse to discuss: in silences.

"Kessel," the corporal said. "Lots of our lot went from the works into the line. Whole shifts signed up together." He scratched at his jaw. "Some of them went out to the Ford action, two years back."

"Some did," Coral said carefully.

"Never came home, though. Officially." The corporal spat to the side. "Officially they died of fever. That's what the notice said. Fever." He looked at her with sudden, flat directness, and Coral understood that she had misjudged this man completely — he wasn't careless, he was exhausted, and exhaustion had worn his cynicism clean through to something sharper underneath. "You're a nurse. You'd know. Was there fever out that way? Big enough to take forty men in a week?"

The question hung in the wet air. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the terrace stones with the patience of a clock.

Coral had rehearsed a dozen versions of this conversation on the walk up. None of them had contained this question. Every instinct she had — the same instinct that had kept her ledger hidden through inspections and evacuations and Halloway's charming, precise smile — told her to deflect. To say something bland and professional and move them both through the barrier and away.

Instead she looked past the corporal, at the boy on the ammunition crate, who had set down his untouched mug and was listening with his whole body.

"No," she said. "There wasn't fever."

The corporal went very still.

"There was fever everywhere in that year," Coral continued, and her voice stayed level, clinical, the voice she used for wounds — because if she let it become anything else she would start shaking and not stop. "Men died of dysentery and gangrene and shells and cold, all across this front, and it all went into reports honestly, mostly. But not at Kessel Ford. I've tended men from that engagement. I've heard what they carried out with them besides their wounds. And I will tell you what I know, Corporal, which is not everything — only enough: those men did not die of fever, and the paper that says they did was written by someone who knew better, and I am on my way to the port city because there are people there trying to make that right."

Silence. The mist drifted between them like something alive.

"You're her," the young sentry said suddenly, from the post. His voice had steadied, strangely, as though naming a rumor made it real and therefore manageable. "The nurse who writes names down. There's a story going round the relief columns. A nurse keeps a book. They said it was just a story."

Coral looked at him. "It's not a story."

"They'll hang you," the corporal said. Not a threat — a warning, delivered with the strange tenderness men used when they wanted you to understand the size of the thing you were walking into. "If Halloway's people catch you, they won't even bother with a tribunal. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you're walking toward them anyway." He exhaled through his teeth, long and slow, and Coral watched thirty years of a life she'd never see pass behind his eyes — the quarry shifts, the signing-up parade, whatever had happened to him between then and this wet morning in a pass full of stones. He turned his head and called back, "Denny. Get the gate open. Contractor and nurse, medical transit, papers pending."

The boy on the crate stood up. For a moment Coral thought he was going to say something — ask her to write a name in her book, maybe, or give her something, a token, proof that he had been here and chosen this. Instead he simply shouldered his rifle, walked to the timber posts, and began dragging them aside, his movements quick and businesslike, the way men did things when they wanted them done before their courage changed its mind.

The corporal stepped close to Coral as she passed. Close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and cheap spirits on him.

"The Ford action," he said quietly. "My brother was in B Company."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." He pressed something into her hand — small, folded, grease-stained paper. "Transit chit. It's from a convoy we waved through last month; nobody reconciles these till month's end. It won't survive scrutiny, but scrutiny is a week away and you'll be at the sea by tonight if you keep moving." His jaw worked. "When your people print whatever you're carrying — put the fever lie in it too. Put it in plain words. My mother believed it for two years. She's still got the notice pinned above the stove."

Coral closed her hand around the chit. "I will," she said. "Corporal — your name?"

"Why?"

"So I know whose brother I'm writing about."

He stared at her for a long moment. Then, gruffly, like a man paying out rope he couldn't afford: "Hollis. Corporal Denn Hollis. My brother was Tam Hollis, B Company, Second Battalion." He turned away before she could answer. "Go on, then. Move."

* * *

They didn't speak until the road bent out of sight of the barricade and dropped toward the river valley, where the mist was lifting off the fields in long white banners. Then Elias stopped, leaned against a cut-stone wall, and put both hands over his face.

"I thought he was going to search us," he said into his palms. "When I said Kessel — I thought, that's it, that's how it ends, killed by my own mouth at a checkpoint in the rain."

"But it didn't end there. It ended somewhere better." Coral unfolded the grease-stained chit, checked it, folded it again. "He gave us this. He gave us his brother's name."

Elias lowered his hands. His eyes were wet, whether from the mist or otherwise he didn't say, and she didn't ask. "Why did you tell him the truth? We talked about this. We said partial truths, minimum exposure—"

"Because he asked me if there was fever, and lying to a man standing in front of his brother's grave — even a grave marked with the wrong words — felt like a worse betrayal than any risk." She paused. "And because I've learned something these past weeks, Elias. Halloway counts on people being afraid alone. Every soldier on that barricade believes he's the only one who doubts. The moment one of them finds out he isn't—" she touched the coat, the ledger beneath, "—the whole arithmetic changes."

Elias was quiet for a while. The valley opened below them, the port city still hours away, invisible beyond the river bends.

"Denny," he said finally. "The young one. When he opened the gate, he looked at me. Just for a second." He shook his head slowly. "He had the same face my brother had the night he handed me the letter. Like a man handing you something heavy and hoping you're stronger than he is."

"We are," Coral said. "We have to be."

They started down the road again, faster now, the city pulling at them. Behind them the quarry terraces receded into the mist, and Coral found herself thinking of the chit in her pocket, the name Hollis, the notice pinned above a stove somewhere in these hills — a mother reading lies by lamplight for two years.

Then, ahead, where the road met the valley track, she saw movement. A rider, small with distance, cresting the far rise. Then another. Moving fast. Moving toward the pass they had just left — or circling to get ahead of them.

"Elias," she said softly. "Don't stop walking. But look."

He looked. The riders fanned out along the ridgeline with the easy discipline of men who had done this many times before, and even at that distance, even through the mist, Coral knew the grey coats and the black armbands.

Halloway's men. And they were between the quarry pass and the sea.
