## 14. What Halloway Keeps in His Trunk

Major Halloway had a theory about paper.

Men believed wars were won by artillery and lost by cowardice, but Halloway knew better. Wars were won by whoever controlled the record. A battle that appeared in no report had never been fought; a man absent from the muster rolls had never bled; a village that existed only in a chaplain's diary could be argued into rumor and from rumor into nothing at all. Ink was cheaper than ammunition and infinitely more reliable. He had built his career on this understanding, carefully, patiently, the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, each one set true, none of them ever examined too closely by daylight.

So it was that on the fourth evening after the rains broke, Major Edmund Halloway sat alone in his quarters at Fort Merren with a tin trunk open before him and a single lamp turned low, doing what he did best: managing paperwork.

* * *

The trunk was an ordinary thing—army issue, dented at one corner, its lock long since replaced with one of his own choosing. Any quartermaster who saw it would have assumed it held maps, requisitions, the dull sediment of staff work. That assumption was the point. Halloway had learned early that the safest hiding place for anything dangerous was inside something boring. No one rifled through boredom.

Inside, in ordered layers, lay the war as he preferred it to be remembered.

The top layer was legitimate enough: supply manifests, rotation schedules, correspondence with the Provost Marshal. Beneath that, wrapped in oilcloth, were the files—the real ones, the ones he kept so that no one else ever would. Kessel Ford sat third from the bottom.

He lifted it out and set it on the desk beside the lamp, and for a moment he simply looked at it, the way a man looks at a debt he has long since stopped resenting and simply pays.

The file was thin. That was the first thing anyone noticed about it, if anyone had ever been permitted to notice it at all. A engagement of that scale—a ford crossing, two companies committed, casualties reported as "fever and exposure following river operations"—ought to have generated a sheaf of documents an inch thick. After-action reports. Casualty returns. Burial detail orders. Court of inquiry minutes, had any court ever convened, which none had.

What remained instead was eleven pages, and every one of them was his own work.

He untied the string and laid them out in sequence, because sequence mattered; he had always insisted on sequence. The original patrol report from Captain Rowan Ashe, dated the fourteenth of the month, dry and precise, describing contact with irregulars near the ford and requesting clarification of rules of engagement. Ashe's hand was beautiful—that was the thing Halloway had never forgiven about him, the handwriting, upright and unhurried, the penmanship of a man who believed that what he wrote might someday be read back to him by strangers. It made everything Ashe wrote look like scripture. It had made falsifying around him feel like forgery of relics.

Then Ashe's second report, dated three days later, the one that had never reached division headquarters in its original form. Halloway had intercepted it himself, at the relay station, standing in the rain while the courier waited with his cap in his hands. He remembered reading it by the light of the relay lantern, remembering thinking, with a strange detached admiration: *so this is what honesty costs.* Ashe had written what he'd seen. Not the skirmish with irregulars—the other thing. The villagers. The men who had run the ford with their livestock and their children because they'd been told, wrongly, told by someone whose identity Ashe had traced only partway, that the colonial troops gave no quarter. The shooting that had followed. The hours of it. Ashe had written all of it down in that beautiful upright hand, and he had addressed it to division, bypassing brigade entirely, which meant bypassing General Verey, which meant—

Halloway had stood there in the rain and understood, with total clarity, exactly what he was holding and exactly whom it would kill.

Not Ashe. Letters didn't kill captains; captains died of bullets and fever like everyone else. It would kill Verey. And Verey's fall would not stop at Verey, because Verey's patronage ran upward into the Ministry like roots into soil, and the whole tree would come down, and the war—which was nearly won, which had *cost*, which needed above all things to end cleanly and be forgotten cleanly—would end in tribunals instead of treaties.

He had burned the original report in the relay stove while the courier watched the fire and thought about his supper. Then Halloway had dictated a substitute. *Contact with irregular forces repulsed. Our losses light. Enemy losses significant.* Eleven words where Ashe had written six hundred. He had signed the routing slip himself, as brigade adjutant, because that was his function in the machine: not to order the shooting, but to decide afterward what the shooting would be called.

That was the distinction he clung to on nights like this, when the lamp guttered and the rain came off the river in sheets. He had not ordered Kessel Ford. He had not fired a shot. He had never even visited the ford until six weeks later, when the ground was clean and the reeds had grown back over the places where the digging had been done. What he had done was smaller and quieter and, he suspected, worse: he had decided that the dead were worth less than the ending of the war. Forty-one of them, though the number had taken him months to assemble, because counting them had been nobody's job and he had made it his, secretly, the way Coral Okafor counted her soldiers—except that his ledger existed to keep them buried, and hers, he now understood, existed to dig them up.

* * *

He poured himself two fingers of the cheap brandy the quartermistress sold under the table and drank half of it, and then he did what he always did with this file: he read it through, front to back, looking for loose threads.

There was the casualty return, forged in a clerk's deliberately mediocre hand—he had paid the clerk, a soft young man named Prewitt, and then arranged Prewitt's transfer to a garrison so distant the man would die of gin before he ever thought to talk. There was the burial detail order, signed by a captain since dead of dysentery, thank God for the efficient malice of tropical disease. There was the memorandum from Verey—not signed, Verey never signed anything, Verey's genius was that his instructions arrived as raised eyebrows and half-sentences over cards—but present in the file all the same, in Halloway's own transcription, because Halloway trusted nothing to memory except memory itself.

*"It is regrettable,"* Verey had said, examining his cards, *"that the ford operation was conducted by officers insufficiently acquainted with riverine conditions. Confusion is inevitable in such circumstances. I trust the paperwork reflects the confusion."*

*I trust the paperwork reflects the confusion.* Halloway had transcribed those eight words verbatim within the hour, sitting in his tent with his heart going like a drum, because he understood that he had just been given both an instruction and an insurance policy. If the file ever surfaced, the transcription proved Verey's knowledge. Which meant that destroying it would protect Verey—and hanging him with it would protect Halloway. He kept it for the second reason. Sentiment played no part in it whatsoever.

He turned the pages. Loose thread, loose thread. The deserters.

Three of them, in the weeks after the ford—men from Second Company who had seen the digging or heard about it, and who had gone over the wire rather than carry it. Officially they were listed as missing, presumed drowned, which was the army's favorite epitaph for inconvenient men. Unofficially, Halloway had spent two years waiting for one of them to surface with a story. Two years of quiet. And then, ten days ago, the marsh patrols had reported a deserter taken and shot resisting—unidentified, papers burned on the body per standing order—and a week after that, the first whisper from his informants in the river settlements: a civilian asking questions. A civilian carrying something. Asking after a nurse.

Halloway set down his glass.

The nurse. That was the thread he couldn't stop worrying at, the way a tongue worries a cracked tooth. Every intelligence officer knew about her by reputation—Okafor, the one who wrote names down, the sentimental creature whose little book of grievances was tolerated because sentiment was good for morale and cost nothing. He had met her once, briefly, during his inspection of the field hospital, and found her exactly as advertised: tired, competent, watchful. She had lied to him. He had known it even then, had admired the smoothness of it even as he catalogued it. A woman who lied well to a major on behalf of a stranger was a woman with something worth lying about.

But a ledger of names was grief, not evidence. Grief could be confiscated, mocked, dismissed. Unless it aligned with something real. Unless someone put a document in her hands that turned her private mourning into public arithmetic.

Which brought him to the item at the bottom of the folder—or rather, to where the item had been, because it was no longer in the folder. It was on the desk before him now, separate, weighted down with his glass: four pages, water-stained, copied in a fair hand by one of his marsh agents from an original recovered near the body of the unidentified deserter. The agent had been thorough. The original was already ash in a brazier at the marsh post; this copy had come south in a dispatch case, and Halloway had read it twice with a sensation like walking on river ice.

It was Ashe's hand. Of course it was. Upright, unhurried, unbearably legible. Dated eight days ago. Addressed, in that infuriating fashion of Ashe's, *to whomever keeps honest accounts.*

Alive, then. Alive, imprisoned, and writing—as if the man had spent two years in a cell doing nothing but sharpening his pen. The letter named the ford. Named the numbers. Named, in careful circumlocution that fooled no one who knew how to read it, the general whose eyebrows had issued the order that made it possible. It was, Halloway thought with genuine professional disgust, a masterpiece. Ashe had always written as though the reader were a court. Now he finally had his court.

And somewhere between the marshes and the port, that letter was traveling toward a nurse with a ledger and a tribunal journalist with a printing press, and every hour he sat here admiring the enemy's prose was an hour the ice grew thinner beneath his feet.

* * *

He fed the copy to the lamp.

He did it page by page, holding each sheet until the flame took it properly, because carelessness with burning documents was how men ended up hanged—half-charred paper in a grate, legible corners, the whole catastrophe of evidence. The firelight moved on his face and made the room feel, briefly, warm. *Rowan Ashe,* he thought as the signature curled, *you magnificent, tedious man. You have been dead for two years. I attended to your death personally. It was among the better work I have ever done.*

The problem, he admitted, was that killing Ashe properly required killing the letter, and letters, unlike men, traveled. A man could be locked in a cell at Verhoek and forgotten. A letter moved. A letter passed from hand to hand like infection, and every hand it touched became a carrier. Already it had infected a deserter—dead, that line of transmission closed—and possibly a courier, certainly a nurse, conceivably the journalist Brandt, whose name had begun appearing in his intelligence summaries with a frequency he disliked the way a man dislikes a recurring dream.

He could not burn them all. That was the arithmetic he hated. Paper multiplied; witnesses multiplied; and every act of suppression was itself evidence for anyone clever enough to count suppressions. He had understood this for years, in the abstract. Tonight, watching the last corner of the last page blacken and lift, he understood it concretely: the file in his trunk was no longer a solution. It was a countdown.

Unless he changed the terms.

He sat back and looked at the lamp flame and let the plan arrive, because plans always arrived if you gave them silence. He could not catch the letter—fine. Let the letter travel. But a tribunal needed more than a letter. It needed testimony, corroboration, a living witness willing to swear, and the only living witness whose word carried weight was Ashe himself, locked behind three gates at Verhoek where no journalist's subpoena had ever reached. And the nurse—ah, the nurse was the hinge. Take the nurse, and the letter became a curiosity; take the letter, and the nurse became a crank. Separately, they were manageable. Together, they were a verdict.

His informants placed them on the river road, moving east, which meant the bridge checkpoint, then the quarry pass, then the customs house at Port Sarelle. Three chokepoints. Three opportunities. He would not flood the road with riders—that produced martyrs and rumors, and rumors were the one document he could never burn. He would do it quietly, the way he did everything. Names added to wanted lists. Descriptions circulated with bureaucratic blandness. Orders phrased so that the men executing them would never quite understand what they were carrying out.

And if it came to the worst—if the letter reached print despite him—then the transcription of Verey's card-table sentence was still in the trunk, still dry, still legible. Insurance cut both ways. He had always made sure of that. Whatever happened, Edmund Halloway intended to be the man holding the only complete copy of the truth, and a man who held the complete copy could always negotiate.

He returned the file to its place, third from the bottom, and locked the trunk, and stood a moment with his hand flat on the cold lid.

Then he sat down at the desk, drew a fresh sheet toward himself, and began, in his small exact hand, the order that would put watchers on the customs house at Port Sarelle—pausing once, only once, to glance at the lamp, where the last fleck of Ashe's ink had already gone to smoke.

Outside, the rain kept falling on the river road, on the bridge, on the mud where a cart full of shrouded dead had crossed that morning with a heartbeat hidden among the corpses. Halloway did not know that yet.

But he would know by Friday, and by Friday he intended to be waiting at the water.
