## 15. The Town That Forgot Its Dead

The rain had followed them for two days, thin and gray as dishwater, and by the time the river town of Marlow Bend rose out of the mist — a scatter of slate roofs and chimney smoke on the far side of a stone weir — Coral had stopped believing in dry anywhere.

They came down off the hills on a drover's path slick with clay, Elias limping badly on the left foot he had turned in a rabbit hole the night before, Coral carrying both their satchels because he would not ask and she would not offer twice. The town announced itself first by smell — woodsmoke, river weed, bread — and then by sound: a church bell tolling the hour with a crack in it, so that every stroke ended in a small broken note, like a voice that had been shouting too long.

"Marlow Bend," Elias said, reading the faded sign at the weir crossing. "There'll be an inn. There's always an inn."

"There'll be eyes, too," Coral said. "Towns this close to the river all have eyes."

They had not spoken much since the quarry pass. Something had settled between them in that stone throat — the young corporal's averted face, the held breath, the terrible gift of being let through — and neither of them had found words for it yet. Coral found she did not need them. There was a language in walking side by side through rain, in splitting the last of the hard bread without discussion, in the way Elias now walked on the traffic side of her without ever having been asked. The war had stripped most courtesies down to their bones. What remained was the actual courtesy.

The inn was called the Ferryman's Rest, which struck Coral as either honest or ironic, and she could not yet tell which. The publican was a broad, tired woman named Mrs. Calloway who looked at their mud, their lack of luggage, and their military-adjacent posture, and asked exactly one question: "Coin or credit?"

"Coin," Coral said, and counted out the last of what Imelda's medicine money had left them. The woman's eyes flicked to the coins, to their faces, to the door, and back. She said nothing. In the border country, Coral was learning, saying nothing was its own currency, and everyone was rich in it.

The room they were given was under the eaves, low-ceilinged, with a bed too narrow for two people who were not lovers and a window that looked out over the town's small cobbled square. Coral set her satchel down, checked the ledger — sewn into the false seam of her coat lining, always against her body, always — and allowed herself the luxury of sitting for the first time in a day and a half.

Elias stood at the window, working his boot off with a hiss of pain.

"There's a church," he said. "And a wall behind it. Big one. Newer than the church."

Coral crossed to look. Below, in the graying light, she could see it: a long wall of dressed limestone running along the south side of the churchyard, facing the square, facing the town, facing the river road that every soldier and every coffin and every rumor traveled. Even at this distance she could see it was covered in lettering — rows and rows of it, carved deep, the kind of lettering meant to be read from horseback.

"Memorial," she said.

"For what?"

Coral did not answer. She was already pulling her coat back on.

* * *

The rain had thinned to a mist by the time she reached the square, and the town had come out into it — women with market baskets, two old men playing draughts under the church eaves, children running the gutters in bare feet, a dog shaking itself dry against a post. Ordinary life, going on with the practiced indifference of towns that had learned to keep their heads down. Coral walked with the unhurried gait of a nurse off duty, which was not entirely a lie anymore. She was never off duty. She only changed wards.

The wall was newer than the church by a hundred years and newer than the war by perhaps eight months. The limestone was still pale at the edges where the mason's chisel had left it raw. A low iron rail kept visitors back a respectful pace, and before it, someone had left flowers — real ones, browning now, and a jar of paper ones that would outlast them all.

Coral read the dedication first.

*IN MEMORY OF THE SONS OF MARLOW BEND AND THE SURROUNDING PARISHES WHO GAVE THEIR LIVES IN THE SERVICE OF THE CROWN. THEY DIED OF FEVER IN HONORABLE SERVICE, FAR FROM HOME, AND ARE MOURNED BY A GRATEFUL NATION.*

*Died of fever.*

She read it twice, because the first time her eyes had refused to make sense of it, sliding off the words the way water slides off oiled cloth.

Then she read the names.

There were forty-one of them. She counted without meaning to — counting was a reflex now, the way a surgeon's hands close of their own accord — forty-one names in three columns, carved in a fine, confident, official hand. And she knew them. Not all of them. But enough.

*Private Tomas Wren.* She had not known a Tomas Wren, but she knew the surname — there had been a Wren in her ledger, a corporal, listed missing in the third week of the retreat, in the action at the place the reports called *a minor engagement at the river ford* and everyone else called Kessel Ford.

*Private Aldous Merrick.* Merrick. Her thumb found the name on the stone and pressed against it as if she could check whether it was real. In her ledger, in her own cramped hand, written by lamplight in a flooded tent while a man with a shattered knee screamed two beds away: *Merrick, A., pvt., K.F., not on list.* She had written *K.F.* because writing the full name of the place felt dangerous even in a book no one was supposed to see. The officers' reports had called it an engagement. The casualty lists had called it *died of wounds received in action* for some, *missing* for others, and for most — for most there had been nothing at all. No line. No entry. As if the men had walked into the river fog and the fog had kept them.

And here, in stone, in her own town's grammar of grief, the fog had been given a name: *fever.*

Coral stood before the wall while the mist beaded on her face and her hands slowly curled into fists at her sides.

She thought of the mothers. That was the thing that did it — not the lie itself, she had seen lies by the cartload, she had triaged lies in the same breath as shrapnel wounds — but the mothers. Somewhere behind her, in the lanes of this town, there were women who had come to this wall with flowers. Women who had been told their sons died of fever, and who had grieved a fever, had pictured it — the shivering, the nurse holding the hand, the end coming soft if it had to come at all — because a fever was a death with a shape, and the mind needs a shape. And it was a kindness, of a kind. A merciful lie. Except it was not mercy. It was management. The difference between a fever and what had happened at Kessel Ford was the difference between a death and a disposal.

"Bad business, that ford," said a voice at her elbow.

Coral started. One of the draughts players had wandered over, a stooped old man with a clay pipe and the unbothered air of someone who had survived every government of his lifetime. He nodded at the wall.

"My grandson's name is on it," he said. "Third column. Josiah Pell."

Coral looked. *Josiah Pell.* She did not have him in her ledger. The ledger had only ever held what she could witness or verify — the names that crossed her table, or the names that appeared in the gap between the muster rolls and the burial parties. Josiah Pell had died somewhere she had never been.

"He was a strong boy," the old man said. "Never sick a day in his life. Then they send us a paper saying fever took him, and his mother says, well, if it was fever, why couldn't we have him sent home? Fever's not like — " he stopped, and tapped his pipe out against the rail, and his voice went carefully level. "Fever's not like whatever it was. But you don't say that. You put the flowers down and you say fever, and the priest says fever, and the stone says fever, and after a while the stone's the only one left saying anything, so that must be the truth of it. Stone's patient like that."

"Was anyone here told anything else?" Coral asked. "Any other account. Any survivor's story. A letter."

The old man looked at her then, really looked, and Coral saw the calculation move behind his eyes — the border-country calculation, the weighing of a stranger. "You're not from the newspaper," he said. It was not a question.

"No."

"You're not from the army."

"No."

"Then it doesn't matter what I tell you." He knocked the last ash from his pipe and put it away in his pocket with great care. "There was a man came through, oh, spring it would have been. Said he'd been at the ford. Said it wasn't fever and it wasn't battle neither, not the way battle's supposed to go. Said the order was given wrong and then the wrong was covered, and men who'd put their hands up were — " He stopped again. Down in the square a child shrieked with laughter, and the sound crossed the churchyard like a small bird. "His mother made him stop telling it. Not because she didn't believe him. Because the major's men came round after, asking who'd spoken to him, and a woman with two other sons in the regiment can't afford a story. So we put it away. We're good at putting things away here. It's how the town stays standing."

"Where did the man go? The one who told it?"

"He went downriver," the old man said. "Toward the port. Said there was a journalist there, a woman, taking testimony from anyone who'd stand still long enough. Said names were going to be read out in the capital someday." He shrugged, and the shrug contained thirty years of shrugs. "Nobody here believed him. Believing's expensive."

Coral's heart was beating very hard and very evenly, the way it did during the worst hours of surgery, when her hands had to be calm no matter what the rest of her was doing. A journalist. Downriver. Taking testimony. Elias had said the name like a prayer: *Brandt. Adaeze Brandt. The tribune's office on Cordwainer Street.* She had let herself half-believe it, the way you half-believe a bridge will hold when you have no other way across. Now an old man with a pipe had confirmed the far bank existed.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me." He was already shuffling back toward his draughts board. "I didn't tell you anything. And if anyone asks, you stood here reading the stone like any decent person, and we spoke about the weather." He paused, and without turning: "The fever's been bad this year. Very bad. That's the weather of it, if you follow."

"I follow," Coral said.

* * *

Elias was waiting in the room, foot bandaged with strips of her spare shirt, sitting on the narrow bed with the particular stillness of a man who has been rehearsing bad news. Coral saw it before he spoke.

"There was a rider," he said. "Came through the square an hour ago. Gray coat, no insignia, but the horse is army remount — I'd know the brand anywhere, I did the remount ledgers for two years." He rubbed his face. "He asked at the inn. Mrs. Calloway came up after and told me. Asked had a nurse and a civilian man passed through. Said the nurse might be traveling with a ledger of army property."

"A ledger of army property." Coral laughed, and it came out with no humor in it at all. "That's what he's calling it now. Property."

"He described you. Red-brown skin, nurse's posture, short hair under the cap. He described me worse." Elias's mouth twisted. "Apparently I have a clerk's hands. Halloway remembers his clerks."

Coral sat down beside him on the bed, which creaked like a small ship. Through the eaves window the memorial wall was just visible, pale in the dusk, the iron rail in front of it like the rail of a bed — a bed the size of a town, holding forty-one patients who would never complain of their care.

"Tell me the rest," she said.

"Mrs. Calloway told the rider we hadn't come through. She lied for us." He said it as if he still didn't trust it. "She didn't ask why. She just looked at me and lied. And then she came up and told me she'd lied, and said, 'I've two sons at the front and I'd want someone lying for them,' and went back down to her kitchen." He shook his head slowly. "I don't understand this country, Coral. Halloway can buy an innkeeper in a day — you'll see, someone always sells — and then a woman who's never seen us before risks her license and maybe her neck for nothing."

"It's not nothing," Coral said. "It's the wall."

He looked at her.

She told him then. About the stone, and the fever, and Josiah Pell's grandfather with his pipe and his careful, level voice, and the man from spring who had gone downriver toward a journalist, and the mother who had made him stop telling it because believing was expensive. She told him the way she wrote in the ledger — plainly, in order, leaving nothing out, because a record that flinches is no record at all. Elias listened with his bandaged foot propped on a stool, and by the end his clerk's hands were gripping his own knee hard enough to whiten.

"They carved it," he said. "They actually carved it. Before the war's even over. Before anyone's asked a single question." He stood, wincing, and went to the window as if the wall might have changed in the last hour. "A report can be burned. A report can be *lost*, misfiled, corrected, amended. I know — I filed the amendments. But stone — " He turned back to her, and his face in the fading light was the face of a man watching a door close. "Stone is a verdict. You carve a lie in stone and in fifty years it's not a lie anymore, it's just what happened. It's *history*. Nobody interrogates a wall."

"Then we answer it," Coral said.

She heard her own voice and was surprised by it — not by the words but by the temperature of them. She had spent two years being careful. Care was her profession: careful hands, careful doses, careful silences, the ledger kept hidden, the names written small, the whole architecture of her resistance built on the principle that a witness who is caught is a witness who is useless. She had told herself it was wisdom. Standing in front of that wall, she had understood something else: that care, practiced long enough in the dark, becomes a kind of complicity. That the stone did not fear her careful small book. That Halloway did not fear it either — he feared it *moving*, feared it *spoken*, feared it set against his stone in the one material that could compete with it, which was not more stone but a voice, read aloud, in public, in print, with names attached.

"The ledger isn't evidence," she said, standing now too, pulling her coat on. "I kept telling myself that's what it was — evidence, for some tribunal someday, if tribunals ever happened. But that's not what it is. It's a wall. It's a counter-wall. Forty-one names here carved in fever, and in my coat there are names — his names, our names, the ones they left off every list — and when Brandt prints them, every one of them is a chisel stroke against that limestone. You can't uncarve a wall, Elias. Fine. Then we build one they'll have to look at forever."

Elias was quiet for a moment. "You sound different," he said.

"I'm angry," Coral said. "I've been frightened for two years and it got me a hidden book and forty-one lies in stone. Anger's new. I find I like it better." She checked the ledger's seam with her fingers, that nightly ritual, the seam, the stitch, the flat solid presence of it against her ribs. "We leave at first light. Downriver, on the water if we can get a boat, on the towpath if we can't. Cordwainer Street. Adaeze Brandt."

"And the checkpoints?"

"Two behind us," she said. "One ahead. The customs house." She met his eyes. "Halloway's rider found this town inside two days. He's not searching anymore, Elias. He's herding. Which means the customs house won't be a checkpoint when we reach it. It'll be a trap with a roof."

Elias nodded slowly. "Then we'd best sleep," he said, "while lying is still something other people do for us."

Below the window, the cracked bell tolled the hour, and every stroke broke, and the town let it, because a broken bell that still rang was better than silence, and Marlow Bend had made its peace with better than silence a long time ago. Coral lay down in the dark beside her coat, with the ledger against her ribs, and recited names until she slept — not the stone's forty-one, but hers: Wren, Merrick, the boy with the shattered knee whose name she had never learned and never stopped carrying, all of them, a rosary of the erased, counting her way toward morning.

She did not know — could not have known — that three doors below, Mrs. Calloway was counting something too: the coins a second rider had left on her bar, and the faces of her two sons, and the terrible arithmetic of a woman who had already spent her lie and been paid once for it, and would be asked to sell again by morning.
