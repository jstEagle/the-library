## 16. Betrayal at the Ferry Inn

The Ferry Inn sat where the river bent lazy and wide, its timbers black with a century of floodwater, its sign hanging by one chain so that the painted ferryman seemed forever tipping toward the water. Elias saw it first as a shape, then as salvation. Three days of fen country had taken everything soft out of him — his boots were ruined, his hands cracked from cutting reeds, and Coral walked beside him with the particular silence of someone who has stopped expecting her feet to forgive her.

"We should go around," she said.

"There is no around. There's the inn or there's swimming."

She looked at the river as if considering it. He believed she was.

"An hour," she said. "Food we don't have to chew reeds for. Then gone before anyone's awake enough to remember our faces."

He wanted to tell her it was a mistake. He had learned, in two years as Rowan Ashe's clerk, to read rooms the way other men read weather — the tilt of a barkeep's chin, the number of cups on a table against the number of mouths. But three days of marsh had drowned that instinct along with everything else, and his stomach made the argument for him, loud enough that Coral almost smiled.

* * *

Mrs. Calloway was broad as a door and twice as hard to move, with flour on her forearms and eyes that went over them once, quickly, the way a butcher sizes a carcass. "No papers, no beds," she said.

"We're not asking for beds." Elias set down what remained of Imelda's coin purse — a shamefully small heap now, three silver and a scatter of coppers. "Two meals. A corner by the fire until the rain stops."

Her gaze snagged on something behind him. He turned. Coral had pulled back her hood, and there was mud to her jaw and exhaustion carved under her eyes, but underneath it all she had the look of a woman who had spent years standing upright while men fell down around her, and Mrs. Calloway saw it the way he'd seen it in a flooded hospital tent: instantly, completely.

"You're a nurse," the innkeeper said.

"I was."

"My sister's boy took fever at Kessel Ford." She said it flatly, wiping her hands. "They wrote us he died of ague. Ague." The word came out of her like a splinter. "Sit down. I'll bring stew."

Elias felt the trap close around him even as he thanked her, because this was how it always happened — kindness arriving at exactly the moment vigilance failed. He ate anyway. The stew was mutton and barley and salt, and it was the best thing he had ever tasted, and he hated himself a little for thinking so.

Coral kept the ledger-bundle inside her coat, and when they climbed to the room above the stable — a straw pallet and a window that looked out on the fen, black water and grey sedge to the horizon — she sat with her back to the wall and the bundle across her knees and did not take off her boots.

"Sleep in turns," she said. "You first."

He slept. That was his sin, if you wanted to name one. Not the inn, not the coin, not the story he'd half-told the innkeeper about traveling to his mother's funeral. He slept like a man who had been forgiven, and he dreamed of the marshes, of Tammy kneeling in the black water with the letter pressed between them like a thing being born, saying *take it, Eli, take it, don't let them make me nothing* —

—and woke to Coral's hand on his shoulder and lamplight moving in the yard below.

"Horses," she whispered. "Four of them."

He was at the window before the thought finished forming. Riders in oilskins, army cut, dismounting without hurry, the unhurried way of men who knew the door would be opened for them. One of them knocked. Mrs. Calloway answered. Even through the glass and the drumming rain, Elias could see the shape of the conversation: her arms folded, then unfolded; a nod; a jerk of the head toward the stairs.

"She's already told them," Coral said. Her voice was terrible in its calm. "Or she's telling them now."

"The back stair. Through the stable—"

"Elias."

There was something in the way she said his name that stopped him. He turned. She had the ledger out of her coat and was looking at it, not at him, and in the dark her face was only the pale oval of her eyes.

"If I'm taken, it goes with me into the water. Promise me you'll let it."

"No."

"It's not yours to carry."

"It stopped being mine the day Tammy put it in my hands, and it isn't yours either. It's theirs." He touched the bundle, felt the stiff ridges of pages beneath the oilcloth. "Every name in there belongs to somebody still breathing who said *he died of ague*. We're just the post. You don't shoot the post, Coral, and you don't drown it either."

Below, boots on stairs. More than one pair. Slow, because they were not worried — slow men are the most dangerous kind.

* * *

The back stair was a ladder, really, down into the stable's warm animal dark, and beyond the stable the fen opened like a held breath. Rain needled the surface of every pool. Elias counted the yard — two horses remaining, the others ridden off or stabled elsewhere — and did the arithmetic he was good at: two riders upstairs, four come to the door, which meant at least two more somewhere, probably watching the front road, probably not the rear, because Halloway's men were thorough but they were also tired, and tired men watch doors, not water.

"Coral." He took her shoulders. Through the wet wool he could feel the bird-quick beat of her heart. "Listen to me. They're here for the letter and whoever carries it. They've been chasing *a courier* since the quarry road — Halloway doesn't know your face yet, or he'd have posted it in every town from here to the coast. So I walk out the front, loud, drunk, the deserter they've been promised. I give them a name — any name — and I keep them talking while you go east through the fen. The drainage cut runs to the river road two miles on. You know it better than I do; you mapped it."

"They'll know within an hour you're not their man. Your accent alone—"

"Then I'll have bought you an hour, which is more than most people manage in a lifetime."

"No." Flat. Final. The nurse's voice, the one that ended arguments on wards. "We go together or—"

"Together is what got Tammy killed." It came out harder than he intended, and he watched it land, watched her flinch and then refuse to flinch. He gentled. "He stayed with me because I wouldn't leave him, and the soldiers found two sets of tracks instead of one. Coral. Please. One of us has to arrive. That's the whole war, right there — somebody has to arrive."

She was crying. She didn't wipe her face, didn't acknowledge it, just stood in the dark of the stable with the rain coming through the slats and let it happen, and somehow that undid him worse than sobbing would have.

"The ferry crossing," she said finally. Her voice was wrecked and level at once. "Half a mile south. If the ferryman's still running night traffic, you cross and follow the towpath. Don't stop for anything. Don't come back for me. And Elias—" she pressed the ledger into his hands, and it was heavier than paper had any right to be, "—you carry it now. That's the trade. Your life for my carrying it. I'll draw them north, up the old drover's road, and lose them in the sedge."

"That's not a trade, that's a funeral with extra steps."

"It's arithmetic," she said, and he heard in it every triage decision she had ever made, every boy she'd marked *expectant* and turned away from so she could save the next one. "I've been doing it for three years. Let me do it once for something that matters."

Boots overhead. A floorboard groaned.

He kissed her forehead, quick and clumsy, because there was no time for anything true, and pushed the ledger back inside her coat himself, buttoning it with fingers that shook. "No trades," he said. "You keep it. I'll draw them. If I get clear, I'll find you at Brandt's office — ask for the typesetter, Vann, they'll know."

"Liar," she said softly.

"Yes," he agreed. "Go."

* * *

He went out the front.

It was, in retrospect, the stupidest possible version of the plan, and he committed to it with the whole heart of a man who has nothing left to be careful with. He burst from the inn's front door into the rain shouting — about deserters, about the road south, about a fictional brother waiting at a fictional ferry — and the two riders in the yard came after him exactly as slowly as he'd hoped, one whistling low, one drawing a pistol with the bored economy of a man who had done this many times.

"Well," said the one with the pistol. "Look what the innkeeper baked for us."

"Easy," Elias said, hands up, backing toward the horses. "Easy. I've got information. Kessel Ford. I was there — I can give you names, I can give you—"

He never learned whether the performance would have worked, because the stable door banged open and Coral came out of it running — not north, not toward the fen, but straight across the yard, and she was fast, faster than he'd imagined, and every plan they'd built in the dark came apart in four seconds of rain and lantern light.

"*Run!*" she screamed at him, and the pistol swung toward her, and Elias did the only thing left in the world to do: he threw himself sideways into the gunman, and they went down together into the mud, and the shot went wide and high and took a piece out of the inn's sign, and the painted ferryman tipped a little further toward the water forever.

He got one clean look at her as they wrestled — Coral at the fence line, turning back, her face white and open and full of everything she would not say — and he shouted the last thing he had:

"*East! The drainage cut! Go, you stubborn—*"

Then a rifle stock came down on the back of his skull and the world went to white noise and rain, and the last thing he registered, sinking, was the sound of her footsteps fading into the fen — running, thank God, running east, running away, carrying the dead like a woman carrying fire.

* * *

They put him in the tack room and tied his wrists to a beam, and for a long time he hung there bleeding pleasantly and counting the ways he had failed. Tammy. Coral. The letter. The list.

Mrs. Calloway came in once, in the small hours, with a cloth and a basin. She knelt and cleaned the wound at his temple with hands that were gentle and would not meet his eyes.

"They have my boys," she said quietly. "Both of them. Conscripts — the Major holds their papers. He sent word tonight: my cooperation, or they transfer to the forward line by week's end." Her voice didn't waver. Thirty years of it, maybe, practice at saying unbearable things steadily. "I want you to understand it wasn't the money."

"I do understand," Elias said. And he did. That was the filthiest part. He understood it completely, and understanding changed nothing, the same way knowing the names in the ledger changed nothing until someone read them aloud where the world could hear.

She wrung out the cloth. "The nurse. Did she get clear?"

He said nothing.

"Good," Mrs. Calloway said, and stood, and gathered her basin, and paused at the door with her back to him. "My sister's boy wasn't ague. I always knew it wasn't ague." Then she was gone, and the lamp with her, and Elias hung in the dark doing the only work left to him, which was believing.

Near dawn, boots again, and lamplight, and a voice he had heard described in two towns and feared in neither until now — mild, courteous, almost kind.

"So," said Major Halloway, crouching to bring his face level with Elias's hanging one. He looked like a schoolmaster, grey and neat, with ink-stained fingers and patient eyes. "The clerk. We've been corresponding, in a manner of speaking — you and I. You burned one of my letters in the marsh. I burned one of yours yesterday." He smiled, and it reached nowhere near his eyes. "Now. Where is the nurse?"

And Elias, tasting blood, began — for the first time in his life — to lie well.
