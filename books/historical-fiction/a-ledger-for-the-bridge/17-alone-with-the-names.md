## 17. Alone With the Names

The culvert had been built by engineers who believed in permanence, and the war had proven them liars. Its mouth was half-collapsed at one end, a tumble of brick and root that left just enough gap for a thin woman to slide through sideways, coat scraping stone. Inside it was dry — the only dry thing in the fen — and dark as the inside of a closed fist.

Coral sat with her back against cold brick and listened to the rain work the reeds above her, and she did not move for a long time.

Elias was gone. The word *gone* kept changing shape when she looked at it directly. Not dead — she would have known, wouldn't she? She'd seen enough men die to know how it traveled through the air, that change in pressure, that silence like a held breath let out all at once. What she'd heard from the ferry inn was shouting, then boots on wet boards, then nothing. Nothing was worse than any sound. Nothing meant he might still be alive, which meant every hour she hid here was an hour his captors spent deciding what he was worth.

They would not torture him for information he didn't have. That was the bitter arithmetic of it: Elias knew nothing except that a nurse existed who kept records, and that the records were somewhere in the fens. He knew she'd gone east toward the port road. If Halloway's men were patient, if they simply waited at the culvert mouths and the causeway crossings, they would find her without ever needing to make Elias talk.

So the question was no longer whether she could carry the ledger to Adaeze Brandt.

The question was whether carrying it was worth more than her life — and Elias's.

She opened the coat.

She had sewn the ledger in two nights before leaving the hospital, working by candle stub while Imelda pretended to sleep across the ward, the needle punching through oilcloth and wool and lining in small stubborn stitches. Now her fingers found the seam again, and this time they did not stop at feeling the shape of it. She picked the threads loose one by one, slow, deliberate, the way she'd once pulled shrapnel from a boy's shoulder — carefully, because haste kills, because even when you mean to end something you should do it cleanly.

The ledger came free into her lap, wrapped in its oilcloth, heavier than paper had any right to be.

*Burn it.*

The thought arrived fully formed, wearing someone else's voice — sensible, tired, almost kind. *Burn it, and walk out of this fen tomorrow, and let the war have its erasures. You are one nurse. You were never going to win. Elias is in a cell somewhere because of that book, and every hour you hold it is an hour they hunt you both. Give it up. Give yourself up. Trade it — Halloway offered enough at the quarry to know he wants it badly. A book of names against a man's life.*

It would even work. She believed it would work. That was the horror of the thought — not that it was cowardly but that it was *reasonable*. Major Halloway wanted the ledger destroyed or buried; either served him. If she surrendered it, he had no reason to keep Elias, no reason to waste bullets on a battlefield nurse. Two lives bought for the price of paper. Any quartermaster would call it good trade.

Her hands were already moving toward the little tin of matches in her pocket before she understood what they were doing.

She stopped.

Outside, the rain thickened, drumming the reeds flat, filling the world with white noise. Inside the culvert there was no light at all now; dusk had come down through the collapsed end like water finding a drain. She could see nothing of the ledger. She could feel everything of it.

She thought about the first name.

Not the first chronologically — she couldn't remember anymore which name had come first, and that failure itself was a small wound. But the first name that mattered, the one that had started all of it, the reason there was a ledger at all: *Private Thomas Wren, age seventeen.* The boy from the river crossing, the one with the freckles and the joke about learning to swim too late. He had died at four in the morning on a stretcher under a tarpaulin, holding her hand with a grip that surprised her every time she remembered it, asking her — not his mother, not God, *her* — to tell someone he'd been here. And when she'd checked the casualty report three days later, Thomas Wren wasn't on it. No Wren at all. As if the river had taken him privately, off the books, a transaction between the water and the war that required no witnesses.

She had written his name in pencil on the flyleaf of a supply inventory because she had nothing else to write it on, and the inventory became the ledger, and the ledger became the only church she attended.

*Tell someone I was here.*

If she burned it, no one ever would.

Her thumb found the matchbox. Held it. The cardboard was soft with damp.

"You fool," she said aloud, to herself, to the dark. Her voice sounded strange in the culvert, flattened by brick. "You absolute fool."

Because here was the other truth, the one sitting underneath the noble one like a stone under silt: she was afraid. Not of dying — she had made her peace with that somewhere between the flooded tent and the cart full of shrouds, some bargain struck without ceremony. She was afraid of dying *for nothing*. Of being pulled out of this culvert by Halloway's men, shot at the edge of the fen as a deserter or a spy, and having the ledger confiscated and burned in a brazier outside a customs house while officers warmed their hands at it. Both losses. The names lost *and* the woman who carried them, and the war continuing exactly as it had, its arithmetic unchanged.

That was what burning it would guarantee. Not safety — *erasure*, her own included. Halloway's whole machine ran on the principle that paper could be unmade, that fire was a kind of forgiveness. If she fed the ledger to the flames herself, she would be doing his work for him, finishing his report, signing off on Kessel Ford with her own hand.

And Elias —

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the black.

Elias had told her, on the far bank of the river, in the gray hour after the bridge checkpoint, why he kept walking after everyone told him to stop. His brother had handed him that letter in the marshes with a bullet in him and said something Elias never repeated twice. But he'd said once, quietly, watching the water: "He didn't give it to me because I could deliver it. He gave it to me because somebody had to *hold* it. Carrying is its own kind of witness." She hadn't understood him then. She understood him now, alone in a drainage culvert with a book in her lap: the carrying was the testimony. Even if it never reached print, the fact that it had been carried — through checkpoints, through marshes, through betrayal — was already a fact in the world that could not be unwritten. Halloway could burn paper. He could not burn the having-been-carried.

But oh, he would try.

She struck the match.

Not to burn the ledger. To see it.

The flame stood up small and yellow in the culvert's throat, throwing her own shadow huge against the curved brick, and she opened the oilcloth and turned pages by that trembling light, and she began — without deciding to, the way you begin to pray — to read the names aloud.

"Thomas Wren."

The name went out into the culvert and came back to her off the brick, slightly changed, the way voices change in tombs.

"Corporal Aldous Meek."

Page turn. The match was burning low; she lit another off its stub, cupped the flame.

"Sergeant Ferro Danat. Private Olu Bassek. Private James Corrigan." 

The men lost at Kessel Ford, the ones whose dates lined up in her ledger like a confession the army had never written. She read them steadily, not fast, giving each name its weight, the way Imelda gave each dose of morphine its exact measure — not a grain more than needed, not a grain less.

"Lieutenant Amos Vey. Private Samuel Otieno. Bugler Peter Marsh."

Her voice cracked on Marsh. The bugler. Sixteen, maybe. He'd played reveille badly and everyone loved him for it.

The rain kept up its noise above, and she realized, somewhere around the twentieth name, that she was doing something older than evidence, older than tribunals. This was what people had always done with their dead when the powerful took the bodies away: they spoke the names into whatever dark was available. Into walls. Into wells. Into culverts in fens where nobody could hear. It changed nothing. It changed everything. It was the smallest possible act of refusal and it was inexhaustible, because a name spoken aloud exists in two places afterward — in the air, which forgets, and in the mouth, which doesn't.

"Private Daniel Voss. Corporal Ede Nwosu. Private Henry Lark—"

She stopped.

There, in the margin beside Lark's entry, in her own smaller hand, a note she'd written months ago and half-forgotten: *survived? seen at quarry camp, Oct.?* A question mark. A thread she'd never followed, a man she'd marked dead in ink who might have been alive in pencil. Her chest tightened. This was what the tribunal would do with this book — not just mourn it, *work* it. Check it. Pull every thread. Find the Larks who weren't dead yet and pull them out of the camps before the camps finished their quiet work.

You could not trade that away. You could not buy Elias's life with it, because Halloway would take the ledger and burn it and shoot Elias anyway, or worse, keep him, because a man who knows a nurse keeps records is worth more breathing than dead. She saw it clearly now, the way you see clearly only after you've decided: surrendering the ledger wouldn't save anyone. It would only make her complicit, retroactively, in every erasure that came after.

The second match guttered against her fingers. She didn't light a third right away. She sat in the dark and let the dark sit in her.

Then she took out her pencil — the same stub she'd written Thomas Wren with — and opened the ledger to the last clean page, and by feel, in the black, she wrote:

*Coral Maren Okafor. Field hospital, river bridge. Present.*

Not a casualty. Not yet. Just a name in the book, entered the way she'd enter any soldier's: where stationed, what happened to her. If the book survived and she didn't, whoever read it would know a nurse had carried it this far. And if she survived and the book didn't — well. Then she would be the ledger. She would carry the names in the only place left, recite them to Brandt from memory in a cell, on a scaffold, wherever they put her. Halloway could confiscate paper. He had never figured out how to confiscate a person who had decided.

She closed the book, rewrapped the oilcloth, and sewed it back into her coat with numb fingers and teeth-clenched patience, the needle finding the old holes, the thread biting through damp wool.

Above her, through the collapse of brick and root, she heard a sound that was not rain.

Voices. More than one. Moving along the causeway in the direction of the culvert mouth, unhurried, the unhurriedness itself a threat — men who did not need to rush because they had been told the quarry was closed and the fen was watched and time was on their side. A lantern's glow swung through the gaps in the roots and laid trembling gold bars across the culvert floor, across her knees, across the place where the ledger lay against her ribs like a second heart.

Coral pressed herself flat into the coldest corner of the brick and did not breathe.

One of the voices said something she couldn't catch. Another laughed briefly. The lantern swung closer, close enough that she could smell pipe smoke threading through the rain, and then — past. Receding. Gold bars sliding off her body like water off oilcloth, footsteps dissolving into the drumming reeds, going east, toward the port road.

Toward the customs house.

They weren't searching the fen. They were *waiting ahead of her* — which meant Halloway believed she would come to him, and it meant the third checkpoint was already a trap with her name on it.

Coral lay in the dark a long moment, listening to her heart slow down, and felt something settle in her that was not fear and was not courage but was adjacent to both: clarity. They expected a smuggler. They expected hiding, running, a package to intercept.

Fine. Then she would give them something else entirely. A nurse, walking in the front gate, in daylight, with nothing to hide and nowhere to run — because by then, God willing, the ledger would already be somewhere else, and so would Elias, and everything Halloway counted on would be exactly wrong.

She just needed a way past three rows of bayonets and a bribe she didn't have.

She thought of Imelda's locked cabinet. She thought of what contraband medicine buys in a port town during wartime.

She got up, stiff and soaked, and turned toward the east and the trap waiting there.
