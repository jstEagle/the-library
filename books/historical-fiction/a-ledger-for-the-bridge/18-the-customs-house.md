## 18. The Customs House

The port city announced itself by smell before sight — tar, fish rot, woodsmoke, and under it all the salt-and-iron breath of the sea. Coral had not smelled the ocean in fourteen months. For a moment, standing at the crest of the last hill with the town spread below her, she was a girl again, back before the war, before the ledger, before Kessel Ford became a word that meant a hole in the world.

Then she saw the customs house.

It sat where the harbor road met the quays, a long stone building with a colonnade of pillars and a flag hanging limp in the windless morning. In peacetime it would have been a place of manifests and tariffs, clerks stamping paper while sailors argued about cargo. Now it was something else. She counted the uniforms from the ridge: sentries at each end of the colonnade, a picket table set up on the steps, two officers in Halloway's grey-and-scarlet conferring beneath the eaves, and — her stomach dropped — a mounted patrol circling the block in slow rotation, their horses' hooves striking sparks off the cobbles.

They weren't guarding the customs house. They were waiting for someone.

"They know we're coming," Elias said beside her. His voice came out flattened, scraped raw. Three days since the ferry inn, three days since he'd stepped into the fen path with his arms wide to draw the soldiers after him so she could run, and the men who'd taken him had not been gentle about it. The bruise along his jaw had gone the color of a storm cloud. One eye was still swollen nearly shut. He held his left arm against his ribs when he thought she wasn't looking.

Coral looked at him now anyway. "They know *someone's* coming. They don't know what we look like."

"Halloway knows what I look like." He almost smiled; it hurt him and he stopped. "He interviewed me for six hours. I'm told I made an impression."

"You made an impression because you lied badly."

"I told you. It's my one gift."

She let the silence hold for a breath, then said the thing they had agreed on in the culvert outside town, in the dark, with rain coming through the grate above them: "We don't sneak. Sneaking is what they're watching for."

Elias turned his good eye on her. Even now, even beaten, there was something stubborn and clerkish in his face, a man who believed in the power of correctly filed things. "You're sure."

"No," Coral said. "But I'm sure sneaking will kill us."

---

The plan had come to her in the culvert, somewhere between reading the names aloud and deciding not to burn them — arriving not as inspiration but as arithmetic, which was fitting. Everything in her life had become arithmetic lately.

Halloway's men were hunting a nurse and a fugitive. Very well. Then the customs house would be entered by a nurse escorting a prisoner — openly, in daylight, with papers that were almost right and a manner that was entirely wrong for a fugitive. Audacity as camouflage. The one disguise no one checks is the one worn in plain view.

What they lacked was Elias. As far as Halloway's men knew, Elias Vann sat in a cell awaiting transport. A prisoner could not also be a prisoner's escort.

Which was why Coral had spent the previous evening doing the hardest thing she had done since leaving Imelda's ward: she had gone looking for Sergeant Dree.

Dree commanded a supply detail out of the quarry garrison — the same garrison whose young corporal had looked away at the pass while two liars walked through. Dree himself was older, tired, and owed Coral a debt measured in morphine tablets: over the winter she had treated his fevered son at the field hospital, bending regulations until they screamed, and he had never forgotten it. More importantly, Dree ran contraband. Half the medicines in Imelda's stores had passed through his hands, diverted from officers' private caches, and Imelda had known and allowed it because the alternative was men dying of treatable wounds. Before Coral left the hospital, Imelda had pressed a canvas satchel into her hands — laudanum, sulphur powder, carbolic, a full kit of the kind of goods that could hang a sergeant twice over.

"This is either your death or your passage," Imelda had said. "Choose carefully which hand you put it in."

In the back room of a chandlery near the fish market, Coral had laid the satchel open on a barrel between herself and Dree and named her price: one prisoner, one transfer order in proper form, one hour of the sergeant looking elsewhere.

Dree had stared at the morphine for a long time. "You know what they'll do to me if this unravels."

"Yes," Coral said. "And you know what they did at Kessel Ford if it doesn't." She had learned by then that truth was a blade best used sparingly, but used clean. "I'm not asking you to fight anyone, Sergeant. I'm asking you to misfile something. You've done it your whole career. Do it once more, for a reason."

Dree had rubbed his face with both hands, like a man washing off a long day that hadn't ended yet. Then he'd taken four of the eight packets and pushed the rest back across the barrel. "Half now. Half when I see them walk out of that building alive." And he'd written the transfer order in beautiful clerk's hand: *Prisoner transferred to medical custody, contagious fever ward, port annex. Escort: Sister C. Okafor, Army Nursing Service.* With a stamp he was absolutely not authorized to use.

Now, on the ridge above the city, Coral checked the order one last time and folded it into her coat pocket, where the ledger's stitched bulk pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She'd sewn it into the lining weeks ago; she had stopped noticing its weight the way a person stops noticing their own pulse.

"Say it back to me," she told Elias.

He recited flatly, eyes closed: "You are Sister Okafor. I am Private Willem Ferris, arrested for desertion at the bridge checkpoint ten days ago, remanded to your custody due to prison fever. I do not speak because fever patients are delirious and because you have a cold, commanding manner that discourages conversation. If questioned directly, I mumble and sway." He opened his eye. "I've been practicing the mumble. I think it's my best work yet."

"It's terrible."

"I told you—"

"—you lie badly. Yes." She looked at the customs house again, at the grey-and-scarlet officers beneath the eaves, and felt the fear settle into her bones and go quiet, the way it did before a long surgery. Not gone. Organized. "One thing more. If it goes wrong — if they take the ledger — you run. Not for me. For Brandt. Someone has to tell her office we're coming and what to print first."

"And if they take *you*?"

Coral watched a gull wheel over the harbor, white against the smoke haze. "Then someone should write down my name," she said. "I know a place it can be kept safe."

The colonnade swallowed them at twenty paces out, shadow and stone, and Coral made herself walk at the pace of a woman going to work. Not hurrying. Hurrying was guilt made visible. She had learned that from Imelda, who could cross a ward full of dying men at an unhurried stride and somehow make death itself feel scolded.

Elias shuffled at her shoulder, wrists bound loosely in front of him with cord Dree had supplied — loose enough to slip in three seconds, tight enough to photograph well. He mumbled when addressed and swayed when prodded and, to Coral's astonishment, had discovered within himself a genuine talent for looking wretched. Perhaps it wasn't talent. Perhaps three days in a cell had simply provided the research.

At the picket table on the steps, a corporal with a waxed mustache looked up from his tea.

"Papers."

Coral produced the transfer order and her nursing credentials together, squared to the table's edge, the way documents were meant to be presented. She watched the corporal's eyes do what all soldiers' eyes did when handed a properly stamped page: slide down the lines without reading, snagging only on the stamp.

"Medical custody," he said. "For a deserter?"

"Ferris has prison fever," Coral said. Her voice came out cool and bored, and she marveled distantly at herself — the woman who had never once lied before Halloway walked into her ward now producing falsehoods the way other people produced small change. "The garrison cells are quarantined. Command wants him out of contact before it spreads through the holding pens. Hence the escort. Hence the hurry. Hence, Corporal, why I'd like to stop explaining myself on your steps."

Something flickered behind the mustache — resentment, mostly, but under it the ancient soldierly terror of contagion. He leaned sideways to look past her at Elias, who swayed, mumbled something about water, and sagged against the cord.

"He looks half dead already."

"He'll be fully dead inside a week if I can't get him into isolation," Coral said. "Do you want that in your report? That the fever broke out at your post?"

The corporal straightened as though the tea had scalded him. "Move along, Sister."

They were three steps up, three steps from the doors, when the voice came down the colonnade.

"What have we here?"

An officer in grey-and-scarlet detached himself from the shadows beneath the eaves. Captain Molt, she thought — she had seen him once through a window at the quarry garrison, Halloway's lean shadow, the man who did the major's counting. He crossed the stones unhurried, gloves tucked under one arm, and stopped close enough that she could smell the pomade in his hair.

"Transfer papers," the corporal offered quickly, holding them up like a talisman.

Molt read them. Coral watched his eyes move and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that the next thirty seconds would decide everything, and that there was nothing left to do but stand very still and be exactly who she was pretending to be.

"Okafor," Molt read aloud. "Army Nursing Service." He raised his gaze to her face. "You've come a long way, Sister. We had word a nurse matching your description was traveling south with stolen documents."

"How alarming," Coral said. "Was she carrying a patient?"

"A what?"

"My patient." She gestured at Elias with clinical detachment, the gesture of a woman discussing freight. "Fever, delirium, lice — I'd thank you not to come closer than arm's length. If your traveling nurse had any sense she'd have been carrying a patient too. It's remarkable cover, moving prisoners under medical escort. No one stops you. You might suggest it to whoever wrote that report."

A pause. Somewhere behind the colonnade a horse shifted, harness jingling. Molt studied her, and Coral studied him back with the flat patience she used on doctors who questioned her triage orders, and inside, beneath the calm, she was aware of every heartbeat, every stitch of the ledger against her skin, every name.

Molt smiled thinly. "Open his shirt," he said to the corporal. "Check for marks. The man we want took a beating recently."

The world went very narrow and very bright. Coral felt Elias tense beside her — felt it the way you feel a patient's pulse change under your fingers — and moved before thinking, the way she always moved before thinking in a crisis, which was perhaps her whole character distilled.

"Certainly," she said. "But understand that if he's got lice — and he has, they all do — you'll be carrying them home to your wife tonight. Hold still, Ferris." And she stepped in front of Elias, unbuttoned his collar with brisk efficiency, and turned him toward the light — angling his body so that the swollen eye, the storm-cloud jaw, faced away from Molt and into the shadow of the pillar, while presenting the corporal a chest marked with the rash she had painted onto him that morning with crushed brick dust and tallow. Fever spots. Contagion made visible.

The corporal looked, flinched, and stepped back a full pace.

"That's prison fever, Corporal," Coral said quietly. "Congratulations."

"*Enough*." Molt's voice cracked like a whip, and for one suspended moment Coral saw him weighing it — the stamp, the corporal's flinch, the rash, the sheer implausible audacity of a woman standing in front of him daring to be exactly where she said she was. Suspicion fought paperwork and lost, because suspicion required effort and the morning was hot and the tea was getting cold.

"Get them inside," Molt snapped. "Quarantine corridor, east wing, until the medical officer confirms. And if your patient dies on my floor, Sister, I'm writing your name in the report."

"You'll spell it correctly?" Coral asked. "It's Okafor. O-K-A-F-O-R."

Inside, the customs house smelled of ink and mildew. Clerks worked at long desks under high windows, stamping, filing, ignoring the armed men who moved among them like eels through reeds. A guard directed them down an east corridor to a bare room with a cot, a bucket, and a barred window looking out on the quay — and, crucially, a door that locked from the outside, which meant that for once in her life being locked in was the plan working.

The key turned. Boots receded. Silence.

Coral let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the ridge. Her hands, she noticed, were shaking now that shaking was permitted, and she pressed them flat against the cot until they stilled.

"The rash," Elias said weakly from the cot, and began to laugh — a low, cracked, disbelieving sound. "Brick dust. You painted fever spots on me with *brick dust*. I mumbled at a captain of the Provisional Guard and he backed away from me like I was a lit shell."

"Lie down, Private Ferris. You look delirious."

"I *am* delirious." He lay back, wincing, cradling his ribs. Then, quieter: "That window. It faces the quay."

Coral crossed to it. Through the bars, forty yards of cobblestone away, she could see it: a narrow frontage wedged between a rope-walk and a sailmaker's loft, a green door, and above it a painted sign half faded by salt air —

*THE TRIBUNE — A. BRANDT, CORRESPONDENT.*

Forty yards. One courtyard, one gate, one street. And between here and there, she knew without looking, the mounted patrol still turning its slow circle, and Molt somewhere in the building, and Halloway's net drawn tight around the one door in the world that mattered.

"We're forty yards from print," she said.

"And a hundred miles from free." Elias came to stand beside her, careful of his ribs, and looked out at the green door the way a drowning man looks at a light. "They'll move us at some point. Transfer to the medical officer, you heard Molt. Every move is a chance."

"Every move is a chance to be searched properly, too. Sooner or later someone puts a hand on my coat."

"Then we choose our moment instead of waiting for theirs." He was quiet a moment. "Coral. If it comes to it — if there's only time to save one thing —"

"Don't." She said it sharply, then softened, because she could hear what was underneath it. "I know. You'd give me the ledger and stay behind. And I'd spend the rest of my life writing your name in a book, and I have *had enough* of that book."

Outside, across the quay, the patrol turned at the corner of the rope-walk. And as Coral watched, a figure emerged from the Tribune's green door — a tall woman in a plain dark dress, sleeves rolled, ink-stained fingers pinning a notice to the board outside, unhurried, unguarded, real. Adaeze Brandt stood in the morning light of her own doorstep for perhaps ten seconds, scanning the harbor like a woman who had spent months learning to read the weather.

Then she glanced up — at the customs house, at the barred windows, at nothing in particular — and Coral saw her eyes stop.

Saw them fix on the window. On the two figures behind the bars. Saw the woman go very still, in the way of someone recognizing a face from a description given months ago by a fleeing nurse's allies.

Brandt did not wave. She finished pinning her notice, smoothed it once, and walked back inside her office, closing the green door behind her.

But before it shut, Coral would swear on every name in her ledger that the journalist had looked back once — deliberately, unmistakably — at the clock above the customs house gate, and then at the window again.

*Ten o'clock,* the clock said.

Coral turned from the window, her heart hammering a new rhythm altogether. "Elias," she said. "I think we just received instructions."

Below, in the corridor, boots were approaching — many boots, moving fast — and a key was already turning in their door.
