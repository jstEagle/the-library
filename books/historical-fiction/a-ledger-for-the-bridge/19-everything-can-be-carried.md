## 19. Everything Can Be Carried

The harbor smelled of tar and dead fish and the particular salt-rot of a war port at the end of its rope. Coral stood in the shadow of a net-drying rack, her coat heavy with the ledger sewn into its lining, and looked across two hundred yards of open cobbles at the tribune's office — a narrow stone building with green shutters and a brass plate she could just make out: *Adaeze Brandt, Correspondent*. Lamplight burned behind the shutters. Someone was working late. Someone was waiting.

Two hundred yards. She had crossed worse ground under fire. But there were no ditches here, no smoke to move behind. There was only the dock road, lamplit and empty, and at either end of it, the soldiers.

They had come an hour after dark, six of them in Halloway's gray-and-blue, moving through the waterfront with the unhurried confidence of men who had been told the quarry was already closed. They hadn't rushed. They had posted themselves at the mouth of Fisherman's Stair and at the corner by the chandlery, and then they had simply waited, the way you wait outside a tent where a fever is doing its work. One of them carried a lantern too high, too deliberately, throwing light across every doorway on the quay. Not searching. Illuminating. *Come out*, the light said. *We know.*

Elias crouched beside her behind the drying racks, his wrists still ringed with the bruises from the ferry inn, his breath coming shallow and fast. He had barely spoken since she'd traded Imelda's morphine for him at the customs house — since she'd stood in that marble-floored hall of ledgers and stamps and lied to a captain's face about a prisoner transfer, her heart hammering against the ledger like a second pulse. The gamble had worked for exactly as long as it took them to walk out the far door.

"They're not going to leave," Elias whispered. "They'll hold this quay all night if they have to. Reinforcements will come by morning."

"I know."

"Then we wait for the change of watch. There's always a gap—"

"There isn't." She had been counting the soldiers' movements the way she counted a patient's breathing, and what she'd counted told her the truth: these men were not a patrol. They were a seal drawn tight around the one building on the waterfront that mattered. Halloway knew. Of course he knew. The bribe at the customs house had bought their exit but sold their direction; a man as careful as Halloway did not lose two fugitives at a port without understanding precisely which door they'd been trying to reach.

Coral looked at the lit window again. Behind it, Adaeze Brandt was presumably reading, writing, gathering testimony from the wreckage of the war — testimony that would mean nothing, change nothing, unless the ledger reached her hands. Every name Coral had written in lamp-black over three years of flooded tents and screaming nights sat stitched against her ribs. Sergeant Tull. The boy Pellam. Eleven men of the Kessel Ford column whose names existed nowhere else on earth but in her handwriting and, perhaps, in Rowan Ashe's memory.

She thought of the culvert, two nights ago, when she had held the ledger over a puddle of fen water and genuinely considered letting it drown. She had read the names aloud instead, all forty-one of them, her voice cracking on the ones she'd watched die. *Memory is a debt,* she had thought. *And debts don't drown.*

Now the debt had come due, and the collection point was two hundred yards of open lamplight away.

"Elias," she said quietly. "How fast can you run?"

He turned to look at her, and even in the dark she could see him understand. His whole body went still, the way men go still when they hear the shell coming before they hear it land. "No."

"You're faster than me. You always have been."

"No. We both go. Around the chandlery, along the waterline—"

"—and they'll run us down on open stones, and we'll both be taken, and the ledger goes into Halloway's trunk with everything else he burns." She kept her voice level, clinical, the voice she used for delivering news that could not be softened. It was the only way she knew to say terrible things: accurately. "One of us has to be the wound and one of us has to carry the message. That's triage, Elias. You've seen enough of my work to know how it's done."

"That's not—" His hand found her sleeve. "That's not how it's done. You don't offer yourself up. You said — you told me once, at the bridge, that the whole reason you keep the ledger is so that no one gets to decide a person is worth less than another person. You can't stand there and decide *you're* the one who's expendable."

"I'm not deciding I'm expendable." Coral turned to face him fully. "I'm deciding I'm visible. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." She pulled the coat's seam open with her thumbnail — she had sewn the ledger in with a running stitch meant to be torn out fast — and worked the book free. It was smaller than it felt. It had always felt enormous, heavier than her kit bag, heavier than the stretcher poles. In her hands it was hardly bigger than a psalter. "You take this. You go through the fish sheds, out past the net racks, and you come at the office from the alley behind it. Brandt will have heard of you — Ashe described you in the letter, or she'll believe a mud-covered madman with a bloodstained book because journalists are paid to believe madmen. You knock. You give her the ledger. You tell her everything, badly, the way you tell everything. She'll sort it out."

"And you?"

"I'm going to walk down the middle of the dock road and introduce myself."

Elias stared at her. "They'll arrest you."

"Yes."

"They'll take you to Halloway."

"Yes."

"Coral—"

"Elias." She took his hand off her sleeve and pressed the ledger into it, folding his fingers over the cover the way she'd fold a dying man's grip around a medal or a photograph — making the hand remember, so the mind couldn't refuse. "Listen to me. All those months I hid this book. Under the cot. In the medicine chest. Inside a false bottom in Imelda's stores. I hid it because hiding was what I knew — you keep your head down, you keep your patients alive, you survive the war and let the survivors argue about it after. And do you know what I learned? Hiding keeps things safe. It doesn't make them true. A truth in a hidden ledger is just gossip with good penmanship." Her voice caught and she steadied it. "It becomes true when someone reads it out loud. That's all. That's all this has ever been for."

The soldiers shifted at the end of the quay. The lantern swung, and its light swept the drying racks, close now, twenty yards and closing.

"Give me thirty seconds," Coral said. "Count them. Then run the other way and don't stop until there's a locked door between you and this quay."

"What if they hurt you?"

"They won't kill me. I'm the only witness they want alive." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile real. "And Halloway is a paperwork man, Elias. Paperwork men need the witness to sign something."

* * *

She walked out from behind the net racks the way she walked ward rounds — spine straight, chin level, hands loose at her sides, projecting to every watching eye the simple unremarkable fact that she belonged where she was going. The trick of it, she'd learned long ago, was never to look like someone escaping. Look like someone arriving.

The lamplight took her at ten paces. A soldier turned, startled, hand moving toward his rifle, and she spoke first, loud and clear, pitching her voice the way you pitch it across a noisy ward:

"My name is Coral Okafor. I am a nurse of the Third Field Hospital, and I am the keeper of the casualty ledger for Kessel Ford."

The words went out over the water like a stone skipped across still ice. The soldier froze. Another turned from the chandlery corner. Somewhere behind her she heard Elias move — a soft scuff of boots on stone, already gone, already swallowed by the fish sheds — and she did not turn her head, because turning her head was the one thing she must not do.

"Captain Rowan Ashe is alive," she called, walking forward now, toward the lantern, toward the rifles. "Major Halloway knows it. Your Major Halloway wrote reports saying otherwise. I have spent three years writing down the names your officers erased, and every one of those names is going into the tribunal record, starting tonight."

"Stop there!" The sergeant — young, frightened, holding his lantern up like a shield — planted himself in her path. "Ma'am, you need to—"

"I need to do nothing of the kind. I need you to arrest me, Sergeant, and I need you to do it loudly." She stopped an arm's length from him and held out her wrists. "Because the woman in that office behind you is a journalist for the tribunal, and the moment your major's men come crashing through her door, every newspaper in the capital prints the story you've just handed her. So think carefully. Do you want to be the man who quietly escorted a witness — or the man whose name appears in the dispatches?"

The sergeant looked at her. Then, horribly, he began to laugh — a short, cracked sound — and Coral understood that he wasn't laughing at her. He was laughing at the shape of his own night, at the absurdity of being a conscript from some river town standing between a small fierce woman and the end of a war.

"Hands behind your back, ma'am," he said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Sergeant Oduya's orders are to hold anyone who comes near the tribune's office. He'll want to question you himself."

"Take me to him, then."

They bound her wrists — loosely; she noted, professionally, that the sergeant tied knots the way a man ties knots when he doesn't entirely believe in what he's doing — and marched her down the quay. As they passed the tribune's office, the lamplight behind the green shutters flickered. A shadow crossed it. Paused. Moved away from the window, toward the back of the building.

*Good girl,* Coral thought, though she didn't know if Brandt was a girl or a grandmother, didn't know anything about her except that Elias Vann was at this moment sprinting through the dark with forty-one lives folded against his chest. *Don't look out the front. Take the back door. Take them all in.*

* * *

The room they put her in smelled of ink and cold coffee — a harbormaster's back office requisitioned for the night, a desk, a lamp, a chair. They left her wrists bound and set a soldier inside the door, and then, after twenty minutes that Coral measured by the harbor bells, Major Halloway came in.

He looked tired. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing she would remember longest. All this way, all these riders and hounds and burned marshes, and the man who walked through the door was simply a middle-aged officer with ink-stained cuffs and the posture of someone who had been sitting in a saddle too long. He dismissed the guard, closed the door, and stood looking at her for a moment with an expression that was almost — almost — respect.

"Nurse Okafor."

"Major."

"Sister Imelda speaks highly of you." He drew out the chair and sat, unhurried, arranging his hands on the desk. "Thirty years of service. She says you're the finest triage nurse on the river. She also says, and I quote, 'she keeps two books.'" He tilted his head. "I confess I didn't understand her meaning at the time."

"It means I write down the names you leave out."

"Yes. So I gathered." He sighed, a small, genuine-sounding sound. "You understand my position. There is a document — a ledger, we'll call it — containing fabrications. Unverifiable claims. The testimony of a deserter and a disgraced clerk. If it exists, it is a slander against officers who cannot defend themselves, and it will cost the army credibility it needs for the armistice negotiations. If you tell me where it is, I can promise you — formally, on my word — that no charges will be brought. You'll finish the war tending your patients. You may even keep the ledger, afterward. Burn it yourself, if you like. I'm told that helps."

Coral looked at him across the lamplit desk, at the tired eyes and the reasonable voice, and felt something she had not expected: pity. Not for what he'd done — for what he was. A man who believed the world was made of paper, and that whoever held the file held the truth.

"Major," she said, "do you know what I did tonight, before your sergeant arrested me?"

"You approached a restricted position. You announced—"

"I announced myself. Loudly. On an open quay, within earshot of the tribune's office, I gave my full name, my hospital, and my purpose." She leaned forward as far as the ropes allowed. "Your men heard it. Your sergeant heard it. The harbor watch heard it. By morning, half this port will have repeated it to the other half. You cannot arrest a rumor, Major, and you have just helped me start one."

Something moved behind his composure — the first hairline crack, a flicker in the steady hands. "Where is the ledger?"

"Gone. Beyond your reach, beyond your riders, beyond your trunk and your matches. It's in the hands of a tribunal correspondent who has been collecting Kessel Ford testimony for a month, and my ledger is the piece that makes hers publishable. Dates, Major. Numbers. Names that align with yours." She held his gaze. "You didn't order the massacre. I know that now. You just wrote it away afterward — tidied it, filed it, made it neat. And every name in my book is a line in a sentence you're going to serve for the rest of your life, because the tribunal doesn't hang clerks, Major. It reads them aloud."

For a long moment Halloway said nothing. The harbor bell rang the half-hour. When he finally rose, his composure had returned, mostly, the way a plastered wall returns to looking solid — but Coral had seen the crack, and she would write that down too, someday, somewhere.

"Sergeant," he said, opening the door, "take the prisoner to the military lockup. Separate cell. No visitors."

As they led her out, Coral looked back once — not at Halloway, but at the window of the tribune's office across the quay. The lamp still burned. And as the guards marched her past, she saw, briefly, silhouetted against the glass, two figures bending over a table covered in papers, and one of them — thin, mud-stained, gesturing urgently with both hands — was pointing at something small and dark that lay open between them.

Coral smiled, and walked on into the lamplight, carrying nothing now but herself — which, she understood at last, had always been enough.

Tomorrow, the interrogation would begin. Let him bring his lamp. She had forty-one names, and she intended to spend every one of them.
