## 20. Interrogation by Lamplight

They took her boots.

That was the detail Coral would remember afterward — not the manacles, not the blindfolded ride through the dock district, not even the moment the guards wrenched her arms behind her back and the crowd on the quay watched her go with the flat, incurious faces of people who had learned that watching cost too much. What she remembered was a sergeant kneeling at her feet in the customs house cellar, unlaceing her boots with careful, almost tender fingers, and setting them aside as though they might contain something dangerous. As though a woman's boots could hold a ledger. As though the whole war had narrowed to the question of what could be hidden in the seams of things.

Her feet went numb within the hour. She sat on a wooden chair in a room that smelled of lamp oil and mildew, and she counted what she could carry, because that was what she did, that was who she was: one pair of stockinged feet going blue with cold. One bruised wrist. One voice.

One name already running toward the tribune's office through the rain.

She held to that last item the way a drowning woman holds to a plank, and when Major Halloway finally came down the stairs — unhurried, his footsteps measured on each tread like a metronome keeping time for something patient and terrible — she found that her fear had settled into something almost like calm. Not peace. Never that. But the particular steadiness of a nurse who has done everything that can be done and must now simply watch.

* * *

He was smaller than she expected. She had built him enormous in her mind these past weeks — Halloway the hound, Halloway whose riders shook the quarry road, Halloway who burned anything that remembered. In the flesh he was a compact, grey-templed man with ink stains on his right middle finger and the posture of someone who had spent thirty years sitting at desks deciding the fates of men he would never have to look at. He carried a single lamp. He set it on the table between them, adjusted its wick with two precise turns, and sat down across from her as if they were colleagues about to review supply requisitions.

"Nurse Okafor," he said. "Coral Marguerite Okafor. Born in the river settlements, trained at the Sisters' ward in Harrowgate, posted to the Third Field Hospital fourteen months ago." He folded his hands. "You'll forgive me. I like to know whom I'm speaking with."

"You read my file."

"I wrote your file. In a sense. Every file is written by the war; some of us just hold the pen." He smiled — a warm smile, genuinely warm, and that was the worst thing about him. Sister Imelda had warned her, weeks ago, over the ledger hidden beneath the floorboards of the supply tent: *He'll be kind. That's how you'll know it matters.* "You've been recommended twice for commendation. Once by a Captain Rowan Ashe, as it happens."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Coral kept her face still. Two years of triage taught her that; you learned to hear the worst news of a man's life delivered by a doctor three beds away and keep folding bandages.

"He was grateful," she said. "I tended his men."

"He was grateful," Halloway agreed. "And then he died. Kessel Ford. A tragedy. Fever, mostly, in the official account. The fever has been very busy in this war." He tilted his head. "You were at the bridge hospital when a deserter named Vann came through. Elias Vann. Formerly my clerk. You treated him, fed him, and then the two of you vanished from the evacuation convoy on the night of the ninth. I'd like to know where he is now."

"You know where he is," Coral said. "Your men took him at the Ferry Inn. I saw them."

"I know we *found* him. Whether he's still findable depends very much on this conversation." Halloway leaned back, and the lamplight moved across his face, softening it further, making him look like what he perhaps once intended to be — an administrator, a clerk of empire, a man who believed that order was a form of mercy. "Here is what I'm offering, Nurse Okafor, and I offer it once. Tell me where the ledger is, and you walk out of here tonight. Not released — walked out. There will be no record of your arrest. No tribunal will ever ask why a decorated nurse spent a week in custody. Vann goes free. Your Sister Imelda is never troubled. You go back to your patients, and in eighteen months the war ends, as wars do, and everyone goes home to lie about it."

"And if I don't tell you?"

"Then I burn the ledger wherever it is, and I arrest whoever gave it shelter, and I do it all with paperwork so clean that history will thank me for it." He said it without heat. That was the horror of him — no cruelty in the voice, none needed. "I am not a monster, Nurse. I want you to understand that. Monsters enjoy their work. I merely maintain records. And a record that contradicts the official one isn't truth — it's an error. My entire career has been correcting errors."

* * *

She thought about the culvert. Three nights ago — or four; time had gone soft since Elias was taken — she had crouched in the dark of a drainage pipe with the ledger open on her knees, rain drumming the metal above her, and turned page after page by the light of nothing, reading names by touch and memory because she knew them all by heart anyway. Toma Reyes, who sang off-key. Private Weller, who was seventeen. The boy from the marsh settlements whose name she had written first of all, years ago, in another hospital, in another flood — the boy no report ever carried, the boy who made her start the book.

She had sat there with a matchbox in her hand and understood, finally, what burning the ledger would mean. It would mean the boy died twice. It would mean every name in her keeping died twice, and that the second death — the one administered by silence — was the only death anyone would remember.

She hadn't struck the match. She had read them aloud instead, all forty-one names, into the dark, where no one could hear but the water.

Now she looked at Halloway across the lamplight and felt the strangest pity rise in her, because she could see it plainly, the way you can see a wound under a clean dressing: he believed what he was saying. He truly believed that a record was just paper, that memory was just ink, that the difference between a massacre and a fever outbreak was a matter of which report reached the capital first.

"Major," she said, "may I ask you something?"

"You may ask. I may not answer."

"When you were a boy — did you ever lose someone the reports forgot?"

Something flickered in his eyes. Brief, quickly shuttered, but real. "That is not relevant."

"No," she agreed softly. "It never is. That's how it works."

* * *

He tried again, and she gave him credit for persistence. He offered her freedom twice more, then offered it with threats attached — Imelda's name, the hospital's supplies, the young corporal at the quarry pass whose mercy had let them through (Halloway knew about him; of course he knew; there was nothing in this war the man's pen couldn't reach). He laid it all out with the tidy economy of a man balancing accounts: what she could save, what she could lose, what the numbers demanded.

And Coral sat with her ruined feet on the cold stone floor and listened, and understood that she had been doing this arithmetic her whole life. Every shift, every flood, every night the wounded came faster than the bandages — you counted what you had, you chose where it went, and you lived inside the choosing. Officers thought arithmetic was theirs. Reports, rosters, casualty figures rounded to the nearest convenient number.

But nurses did arithmetic too. They just did it in the other direction. They added men back in.

"Where is the ledger?" Halloway asked, for the sixth time.

"It's beyond your reach."

"Everything is within my reach. That is what my position means."

"Not this." She leaned forward, and the movement made the guard by the door stiffen, but Halloway raised one finger and the man stilled. "You think I sewed it into my coat, or buried it under a floorboard, or left it with a friend you can visit. You're searching every seam in my life right now, Major, and you'll find nothing, because it stopped being mine days ago. It's in hands you can't buy and can't frighten. By tomorrow morning it's being read aloud in a room full of journalists who would *love* nothing more than a major arriving to demand they stop."

His face didn't change. But his hands, folded on the table, tightened — a small motion, the knuckles whitening, gone in a second. She caught it. She was good at catching small motions; it was half her profession.

"You're lying," he said.

"I'm a terrible liar. Ask me anything else and you'll see. But that — that's the truth, and you know it's the truth, because you burned a copy of Ashe's letter in your office and it changed *nothing*, and that frightened you. Paper burns, Major. That's the trouble with governing by paper. Somewhere out there is always one more copy, and one more person willing to carry it."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the customs house floor, and a ship's horn sounded out on the dark water, long and low, like something mourning.

Then Halloway did something she had not expected. He opened the leather folder he'd carried in, drew out a single sheet, and set it before her. A list. Her own handwriting stared up at her — she recognized it instantly, the tight slanted script she used for names, copied from somewhere, photographed or traced or stolen during the raid on the ferry road. Eleven names. Half of one page.

"Recovered material," he said. "We found it in Vann's possession. Or rather, we found *this* in the mud near where he was taken. So you see, the ledger is not beyond my reach. Pieces of it are already in my hand." His voice remained level, but underneath it now ran something new — a thin wire of strain, taut as a suture about to give. "Eleven names. Do you know what eleven names are? Nothing. A rounding error. I could publish these myself with a footnote and bury the rest. Give me the book, Nurse, and these eleven men get proper graves. Refuse, and they stay exactly what they are now — mud."

Coral looked at the page for a long time. Then she began, quietly, to laugh — not mockery, not hysteria, but the exhausted, wondering laugh of someone who has just seen a miracle disguised as an insult.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you poor man. Do you know what you're holding?"

"A fragment of sedition."

"You're holding proof it exists." She tapped the page once, with one finger. "You recovered this from the fen, from mud, from a captured courier — and it survived. That's eleven times now, Major. Eleven times the war tried to erase those men and failed. You can't stop it. You've been trying for two years and you can't stop it, because every time you burn one copy, someone who loved one of those names writes it down again. That's what you've never understood. You fight paper with fire. We fight paper with *people*. And there are always more of us than you, because grief doesn't take orders."

"Enough—"

"Every name in that book is a sentence you'll serve." Her voice didn't rise. That surprised her most of all — it stayed low, steady, the voice she used for dying men, gentle and absolutely immovable. "Not a court's sentence — maybe you'll die rich and safe in some provincial house, maybe your General Verey keeps his medals. I can't help that. But every morning for the rest of your life, some part of you will be listening for a knock at the door, and reading newspapers with your breath held, and flinching at the word *Kessel Ford*. Forty-one names, Major. You'll carry them whether you want to or not. That's what memory does. It doesn't need a tribunal. It just needs one witness who wouldn't shut up."

* * *

And there — she saw it happen.

His composure cracked.

It was not dramatic. Nothing shattered, nothing overturned; the lamp did not topple, the guard did not stir. But the mask of the reasonable administrator — the ink-stained clerk of empire, the man who corrected errors — slipped, and beneath it she saw something raw and terrified and old, and she understood in that instant that Halloway had known, all along, deep down where he kept the things that kept him awake, that the fever story was rot and the graves at Kessel Ford were wrong and the arithmetic had never balanced. He had known, and he had managed it, the way he managed everything, with files and wicks adjusted just so — and now a half-starved nurse with bleeding feet had said it aloud in a cellar, plainly, without raising her voice, and management was suddenly not enough.

"The tribunal won't reach me," he said, and his voice had lost its metronome. "Verey is untouchable. The government needs the army calm. Whatever your journalist prints, in six months it's forgotten, in a year it's a rumor, in ten years it's a footnote nobody reads."

"Maybe," Coral said. "I've heard that speech before. Men at the front say it about the wounded — *he's too far gone, don't waste the bandages.*" She looked at him steadily. "I've watched men everyone gave up on sit up at dawn and ask for water. Truth is like that, Major. It runs late. It comes back from places you sent it to die. But it arrives."

He stood abruptly. The chair scraped. For a moment he stood over her with the lamp in his hand, and the light swung, throwing her shadow huge and broken against the cellar wall — a giant, briefly, made of darkness — and Coral thought, quite calmly: *he could do anything now, and no one would ever file a report.*

But he didn't. He was, in the end, exactly what he claimed to be: a man of records. Even his violence required paperwork.

"Take her back upstairs," he told the guard, and his voice was nearly level again, nearly, except for the hairline fracture running through it that she alone could hear. "And someone bring the woman her boots."

At the stairs he paused, his back to her, one hand on the rail.

"Why?" he asked, without turning. "One nurse. One book. Against the army, against the government, against me. Why?"

Coral flexed her frozen toes and thought of the boy in the marsh, and the flooded ward at the bridge, and Elias standing at a prison gate somewhere in the rain, waiting.

"Because somebody has to be able to say their names," she said. "And apparently it's me."

* * *

They marched her up into the grey beginning of morning. Through a barred window at the end of the corridor she could see the harbor — masts, gulls, the tribune's office a black silhouette four streets away with, she would swear forever after, a light already burning in its upper window at an hour when no honest office kept lights burning.

A printer's hours, Imelda would have called them. The hours of people getting words ready for the world.

Guards put her in a cell and took away the lamp, and the dark came down, and Coral Okafor sat on a cot with no boots and no ledger and no ally within a mile, and smiled in the blackness like a madwoman.

Because down the street, under that light, a compositor was setting type. And somewhere in the columns of lead being locked into place, letter by letter by letter, forty-one names were about to stop being hers to carry alone.
