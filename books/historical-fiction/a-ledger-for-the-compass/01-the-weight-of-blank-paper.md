## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The road down to Vellamere was a gray ribbon stitched through gray country, and Rosalind Brightwater walked it the way he had learned to walk every road: as though he had no reason to hurry and nowhere particular to be. Snow had begun an hour back, fine and dry as sifted flour, settling on his shoulders without melting. He did not brush it off. A man who brushed snow from himself was a man who minded the cold, and a man who minded the cold was a man who intended to arrive somewhere warm, and men who arrived somewhere warm were remembered.

He carried nothing but a leather satchel containing invoices for salt herring and tallow, a shipping clerk's seal worn smooth by someone else's thumb, and — sewn flat against his left side, between the lining and the wool of his coat, thirty-one small rectangles of paper covered in characters that would mean nothing to any man who found them unless that man already knew what they meant — the last true words the republic's coastal network would ever send.

The coat weighed eleven ounces more than it should have. He had weighed it once, on a peddler's scale in a market town whose name he had already let fall out of his memory like water out of a cracked jug. Eleven ounces. He felt them all, every hour, the way a man with a splinter feels the whole hand.

*Never write,* the rules went. *Never linger. Never be remembered.*

He had not written anything in seven years. Not a letter, not a ledger line, not so much as his own name scratched in frost on a windowpane. His name lived in one place only, folded up small in the dark behind his ribs, taken out and looked at rarely, the way a miser looks at the coin he cannot spend. When innkeepers asked it, he gave them Tomas Vey. When the toll-keeper at the river bridge asked it, he gave them Anselm Rook. When a woman in a ferry house had pressed too close and asked too kindly, he had given her a name belonging to a man he'd buried in another life, and then he had walked nine miles out of his way the next morning because kindness made him sloppy, and sloppiness killed couriers.

The town appeared first as a suggestion: a darkening of the horizon where the land gave out, a smudge of chimney smoke leaning eastward on the wind. Then the masts. Vellamere kept a harbor deep enough for sea-going ships, and its masts stood up over the roofline like winter trees, bare and black and patient. Somewhere beyond them, out on the gray water, a ship was supposed to come. He did not know if it would. The dispatches said *wait*; the silence from the capital said everything else.

At the boundary stone where the king's old highway became the town's cobbled street, a sentry stood under an awning, stamping his feet, a carbine slung carelessly enough to suggest long boredom rather than sharp vigilance. Loyalist green at the collar, faded almost to gray. One of Ashe's, then. The garrison still held the town, which meant the garrison still believed in something, which meant the garrison had not yet read the weather.

Rosalind adjusted his face before he adjusted his stride. This was the true craft of it, more than the forged papers or the memorized trade routes: the face. A clerk's face was a tired face, a slightly put-upon face, a face that thought about freight weights and quarrelsome harbormasters and the cost of candle tallow. It never looked directly at soldiers. It flattered them by ignoring them. It was the face of a man so thoroughly unremarkable that the eye slid off him the way rain off oiled canvas.

"Tomas Vey," he told the sentry, "clerk for Merren & Sons, salt and provisions." He produced the satchel's papers with the weary fluency of a man who had done this forty times and resented doing it a forty-first. "Your quartermaster will want me before the tide turns, or he'll complain about it until spring."

The sentry glanced at the invoices, at the seal, at the face that wanted nothing and was nobody, and waved him through with the contempt of the armed for the unarmed. That was the arrangement of the world, and Rosalind had made his peace with it, or something adjacent to peace, or at least a truce that held.

*Never be remembered.* He passed under the town gate and understood, as he always understood, that he was now officially nowhere. No gate ledger would hold him. No watchman would recall him. If he died in Vellamere, they would bury a clerk named Tomas Vey, and somewhere far inland a name would simply stop being spoken, and no one would know it had stopped.

He had stopped minding this years ago. He told himself he had stopped minding it. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

* * *

Vellamere in winter smelled of tar, fish brine, woodsmoke, and cold stone — the smell of a port holding its breath. The streets were narrow and ran crooked down toward the water, built by people who had cared about slopes and drainage more than about sightlines or grandeur. Shutters were half-closed. A woman drew in a bucket from a well and watched him pass with the flat, weighing look of townsfolk everywhere in wartime, cataloguing his boots, his coat, the cut of his hair, filing him away against future questions. He gave her the clerk's nod: brief, preoccupied, gone.

Merren & Sons turned out to be one man named Merren, sixty-odd, deaf in the right ear, with a warehouse near the fish market and a perpetual grievance against the customs house. Rosalind had studied the firm's papers for two days in a river town whose name he had also discarded. He knew their rates, their routes, their rivals, the name of Merren's late wife, the fact that his son had run off to sea and not written in three years. All of this he deployed now in careful doses, letting Merren talk, because the deaf loved to talk and talking men do not examine clerks.

"—and Ferrant at the customs house will tell you the tariff schedule has *changed*, as though paper changes tariffs, as though the King's own assessor didn't set these rates when I was a boy—"

"The King's assessor," Rosalind said mildly, sorting invoices, "is dead, I heard. Along with most things."

"Dead or fled. Half this town is dead or fled and the other half is deciding which to be." Merren snorted, wiped his nose on a knuckle, and looked at Rosalind with sudden shrewdness in his good ear's direction. "You're not from here."

"No one is from anywhere anymore, sir. I'm from wherever the freight manifests are."

That satisfied him. Deaf men trust what they can see, and Rosalind made sure there was nothing to see. He worked through the afternoon in the counting room, entering figures in Merren's books in a neat, characterless hand — a clerk's hand, trained to be legible and to say nothing — and all the while the eleven ounces pressed against his ribs like a second heart, and the questions he had come to ask arranged themselves in order, waiting for evening, waiting for the walk to the garrison.

Because the dispatches were not for Merren. They were for Colonel Edouard Ashe, commander of the last loyalist garrison on this coast, and delivering them was the whole purpose of Rosalind's existence this winter, and after that — after that there was nothing in the instructions. Extract yourself. Disappear again. Resume being no one. Seven years of the same sentence, written in invisible ink.

He wondered, sometimes — in the hollow time before sleep, in the gray time before waking — what shape he would be if he were ever emptied out. Take away the names he wore and the routes he walked and the silences he kept; what remained? He suspected the answer was a very small thing indeed, dry as a seed pod, rattling.

* * *

The garrison occupied the old fort above the harbor, and Rosalind went up to it at dusk with a requisition chit Merren had signed, complaining the entire way about the price of lamp oil. The guard at the fort gate took the chit, looked at the clerk's face, saw nothing, and led him through a courtyard where soldiers were hauling crates and burning documents in a brazier — burning them casually, in armfuls, the ash rising white against the darkening sky. Rosalind's stomach tightened. Burning correspondence was either tidiness or surrender, and tidy men did not burn letters in open courtyards.

Colonel Ashe received him in a room that had once been an office and was becoming a study in defeat: maps weighted down with cups, a cold hearth, a bottle standing open with no glass beside it. Ashe himself was fifty, heavyset, with the ruddy, sagging face of a man who drank steadily and slept badly, and eyes that contradicted everything else about him — quick, clear, appraising eyes that missed the clerk's face entirely and instead read his hands, his boots, the way he stood.

Rosalind had met that kind of officer before. The kind who looked at everyone twice, in case the second look found what the first had missed.

"Requisition for lamp oil and salt pork," Rosalind said, offering the chit with the correct degree of servile weariness. "Mr. Merren's compliments, and his opinion that the army's credit is worth less than the paper it's written on."

"The army agrees with Mr. Merren," Ashe said. He took the chit, signed it against his knee without reading it, and did not dismiss him. "You came up from the south road?"

"This morning. Slow going in the snow."

"Any traffic on it? Soldiers? Deserters?"

"A few deserters, going the other way. No soldiers." He paused, the pause of a civilian deciding whether to gossip. "There's talk at the river crossing that the republican fleet put out from Harlsmouth two weeks ago. Nobody seems to know toward what."

Something moved behind Ashe's clear eyes — not fear exactly; recalculation. "Talk," he repeated. "At a river crossing."

"Rivermen talk, sir. Whether it's worth anything—" Rosalind spread his hands, the universal gesture of the small man disclaiming knowledge.

"It's worth something." Ashe looked at him a moment too long. Then, abruptly, he was the colonel dismissing a supplier: "Tell Merren he'll have his answer on payment within the week. Within the week," he said again, as though repeating it could make it true, and Rosalind bowed and withdrew, having delivered, in plain sight of anyone who might have been watching, the message that mattered most: *the fleet sailed; your ship may be coming; nothing else is certain.*

Thirty-one rectangles of paper remained against his ribs. Those were not for Ashe. Those were for the people the dispatches named — the harbormaster's cousin, the pilot who could take a ship out past the shoals in fog, the dozen quiet loyalists whose names existed nowhere except in code and in the memory of a man who was not supposed to exist. Delivering those would take longer. Delivering those would require him to stay.

And staying broke the second rule. Never linger. Lingering was how couriers died: not in transit but in residence, growing roots, growing habits, growing faces that neighbors recognized and informers described.

But the ship needed a pilot, and the pilot was named in the third rectangle, and the pilot would not sail for a stranger's word alone.

* * *

He lodged that night at the Anchor and Rope, an inn near the harbor steps, taking a room under the eaves because it was cheap and because cheap rooms had thin walls he could listen through and windows he could leave by. He ate bread and smoked herring and drank one glass of thin wine, and he sat where he could see the door and the stairs and, through the fogged window, the harbor — the masts, the dark water, the lanterns of the customs house burning late across the quay.

The customs house interested him. Most buildings closed at nightfall in a town like this; the customs house burned light like a ship at anchor, steady, from a high window facing the water. Someone working late over accounts. He filed it away, the way he filed away everything — patrol schedules, which soldiers drank where, the name of the harbormaster's clerk, the number of guns visible on the fort walls. Not written anywhere. Written in him. He was the archive; that was the whole of what he was.

Later, climbing to his room, he caught his reflection in the dark glass of the stairwell window: a lean man of thirty-four with a clerk's stoop already forming in his shoulders, a face designed to be forgotten, snow-damp hair going gray early at the temples. He looked at it the way one looks at a tool. He tried to remember when he had last seen a face in a mirror and felt anything recognized there.

Seven years ago he had a name people used warmly. Seven years ago he had written letters — real ones, in his own hand — and someone had kept them, or he had believed someone would keep them, and that belief had seemed like enough of a future to build on. Then came the mission he did not think about, and the courier named Ives, and the silence that had saved a family and destroyed a man, and after that the writing stopped, and the stopping had spread outward from ink into everything else, the way damp spreads through a wall until the whole wall is cold.

*Never write. Never linger. Never be remembered.*

He recited them now, silently, in the dark of the narrow bed, the catechism that had replaced prayer. They had kept him alive through checkpoints and purges and two winters worse than this one. They were good rules. They were ironclad. And lying there listening to the harbor water knock against the pilings below the window, he noticed — the way you notice a tooth has begun to ache — that he could no longer quite recall why they had been written, or for whom, or whether the man they protected and the man they hollowed out were even the same man anymore.

Eleven ounces against his ribs. Thirty-one pieces of other people's courage, sewn into a coat belonging to a man who did not exist.

Outside, the customs house light burned on, patient as a lighthouse, illuminating nothing he yet knew he would need.

In the morning, he decided, he would begin finding the names. In the morning he would go down to the harbor, and learn the tide tables, and find the pilot, and be Tomas Vey thoroughly and invisibly, and be gone within the fortnight, God willing, before anyone looked at him twice.

He fell asleep believing it, the way one falls asleep believing one will rise early.

Across the quay, in the lit high window of the customs house, a woman he had not yet met sat late over a great ledger, entering by lamplight the name of every soul in Vellamere — and among the day's arrivals, entered in a neat, characterless hand, *Tomas Vey, clerk, Merren & Sons*, the first record of his existence in seven years, waiting in the book like a trap he could not yet see.
