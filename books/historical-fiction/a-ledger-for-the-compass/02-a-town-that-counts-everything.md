## 2. A Town That Counts Everything

The customs house smelled of tallow, wet stone, and old paper — a smell Rosalind Brightwater found, to his own quiet disgust, almost comforting. He had spent seven years training himself to be comforted by nothing, and here his body was betraying him at the threshold, relaxing into the scent of ledgers the way a dog relaxes at the smell of its own yard.

He corrected himself. He stood straighter in the queue of shivering petitioners and reminded himself what he was: Tomas Ferre, shipping clerk, lately of Marrow Bay, come to Vellamere to reconcile freight accounts for a merchant who did not exist, on behalf of a company that existed only as a name inked on paper that would burn well if it ever needed burning.

*Never write. Never linger. Never be remembered.* The rules sat behind his teeth like pebbles he had swallowed so long ago they had worn smooth. He recited them without thinking, the way other men said prayers, and felt nothing from them anymore — no comfort, no warning. Only the shape of them, the way a man feels the shape of a key he has carried so long he has forgotten which door it opens.

The queue moved slowly because everything in Vellamere moved slowly now, and slowly was the town's way of pretending the war was a rumor from another coast. Outside the tall windows, the harbor lay gray under a sky the color of dishwater, masts standing bare against it like winter trees. Two fishing boats were out; a third was being stripped for scrap by men who worked with the unhurried guilt of people stealing from a house they expected to lose anyway. The loyalist garrison's colors hung above the old fort on the point, and the wind snapped them just often enough to remind everyone they were still, technically, flying.

Rosalind counted the exits. Front doors, two. Service corridor behind the clerks' desks, one, half-hidden by a screen. Windows along the east wall, sashed, painted shut from the look of them — no, not all; the third had been opened recently, the paint scored fresh around the frame. Someone in this building still believed escape might be necessary. That someone was worth knowing, or worth avoiding. He had not yet decided which.

"Name?" said the clerk at the receiving desk without looking up.

"Tomas Ferre. Freight reconciliation, Quillon & Daughters line." He set down a leather folder containing three days of painstakingly invented shipping figures, each number chosen to be dull, forgettable, and verifiable against documents that also did not exist but could plausibly have burned in the warehouse fire at Marrow Bay last spring. He had built the fire into his story himself. It was easier when you chose your own ruins.

The clerk took the folder, stamped something, and jerked a thumb toward the interior offices. "Requisitions office is through there, but the Colonel handles dispatch traffic personally this week. You'll wait."

"I'll wait," Rosalind agreed pleasantly, and waited, and while he waited he did the thing he was best at in all the world: he memorized everything.

It was not a skill he had chosen. It had chosen him, somewhere in the years of courier work, the way scar tissue chooses its own pattern. His eyes went to the notice board first — tide tables, a proclamation about grain allotments, a list of men called to labor duty on the fortifications, forty-one names, and he read them once and had them. Then the wall map of the harbor approaches, soundings marked in faded ink, and he noted which channel markers had been removed since the map was drawn. Then the faces: six soldiers, bored, muskets stacked; a boy sweeping; two women arguing quietly about a seized cargo of salt pork; an old man asleep upright in a corner chair with the serenity of someone who had decided the world would do what it would do.

And then he saw the ledger.

It lay open on the great sloped desk in the center of the hall, a book the size of a paving stone, bound in green baize gone bald at the corners, its spine reinforced with strips of brass that had been polished by decades of hands. Even at twenty feet he could see the density of it — columns ruled in red and black, entries in a hand so regular it looked printed, margins crowded with annotations in a smaller, sharper script. The book was enormous not because the town was large but because the town had been *counted*, thoroughly, obsessively, entry upon entry across years: names, tonnages, tariffs, debts, bonds, sureties, and — his eye caught on a column header and stopped — *standing*, as in allegiance. Standing: Loyal. Standing: Doubtful. Standing: Compromised. Every soul in Vellamere reduced to a line, every line weighted with a word.

Something cold walked up Rosalind's spine on small feet.

He knew what such a book was worth. He knew what it would be worth to the republic's enemies when they came, and they would come — the dispatches sewn into his coat lining said so in cipher, said it plainly enough that even a man exhausted past feeling could read the shape of the sentence between the lines: *the garrison cannot be relieved; prepare for evacuation or surrender; the fleet is lost or will be.* He carried the death warrant of everyone in this room folded flat against his ribs, warm from his own body, and here sat the inventory of the condemned, kept in a fair hand, annotated with care.

A woman came out of the back office carrying a second volume — a continuation, then, there were more than one — and set it on the desk with both arms, the way one sets down a sleeping child. She was perhaps thirty-five, narrow-shouldered, with ink stains worked so deep into her right hand that the fingers looked bruised at the tips. Her hair was pulled back in a knot that had surrendered sometime before noon. She wore a clerk's coat a size too large, sleeves rolled twice, and she surveyed the hall with an expression Rosalind recognized because he wore its twin most days: the expression of a person doing their work properly inside a world that had stopped rewarding propriety.

She caught him looking at her. He looked away one beat too late.

She crossed to him instead of returning to her office, which was not what he wanted, and stopped at a polite distance, which was.

"You've been reading my ledger from over there." Her voice was low, dry, unaccustomed to being raised. "Most people pretend it's furniture. I prefer honesty. Are you a spy?"

The question landed lightly, almost jokingly, but her eyes did not joke. Rosalind gave her the smile he kept for customs officials and landladies, the one that made him slightly stupid and entirely harmless. "Shipping clerk, mistress. Numbers are numbers whether they're freight or fines. Hard not to read them."

"Numbers are never just numbers." She said it without heat, as a fact of her trade. "That column there—" she nodded at the open book across the hall "—is standing. Allegiance. Do you know how I decide it?"

"No."

"Neither do I, half the time. That's the honest answer. A man owes money to a republican sympathizer, is that debt or loyalty? A woman sells fish to both armies, where does she stand? I write down what happened and let the word come after, and some nights the word comes wrong and I rule a line through it and write again." She looked at her stained fingers as though they belonged to someone she employed. "Odile Ferrant. Clerk of the customs house, seventeen years. If your freight is real, I'll have your account squared by Thursday. If it isn't real—" she glanced up, mild as milk "—I'll still square it. The book doesn't care what's true. It only cares what's written."

"And which do you care about?" The question left him before he could kill it, and he hated himself for it — *never linger, never be remembered* — but she answered as if strangers asked her philosophical questions daily.

"The writing," she said. "Truth is a rumor with better manners. Writing stays." And she turned back to her desk, leaving him standing in the tallow-smelling hall with the cold walking up and down his spine, because he had seen, in the moment before she turned, the page lying open on the sloped desk, and near the top of it, under *Standing: Loyal*, a name he had been sent to protect: *Ashe, Col. E., garrison command* — and beneath it, in the smaller sharper hand, an annotation dated three weeks past:

*Ship promised. No confirmation. Hope is not an entry.*

* * *

Colonel Ashe received him in a room that had once been the customs house's map room and had been converted, by degrees Rosalind could trace like geological strata, into a military office, a bedroom, and finally a bottle's apartment. Maps of the harbor overlapped maps of the peninsula overlapped a chart of the whole coast, pinned through one another into a palimpsest of shrinking territory. A cot stood in the corner with a blanket folded on it with parade-ground precision, the single vanity of a man who had let everything else go. The bottle itself was hidden, badly, behind the chart weights.

Ashe was fifty-odd, gray at the temples, with the leathery calm of a man who had stopped expecting good news around the same time he'd stopped expecting sleep. He took the folder Rosalind offered, weighed it in one hand, and said, "Ferre. Marrow Bay. You came up the coastal road?"

"The mill road, sir. Fewer questions."

"There are fewer questions because there are fewer people. The road is empty because everyone sensible has already decided how this ends." Ashe slit the folder's seal with a thumb knife, scanned the false freight figures with the practiced boredom of a man finding the true ones beneath — Rosalind watched him find the cipher key, watched his eyes change — and then set the folder face-down on the desk and poured two glasses of something from a drawer without offering explanation or apology.

"You'll drink," Ashe said. It was not a question either.

Rosalind drank. The spirit was rough enough to strip paint and warm enough to matter. Outside, the wind worried at the window frames, and somewhere below, Odile Ferrant's pen scratched steadily on, keeping the count.

"How bad," Ashe said. Not *how bad is it*. Just: how bad.

Rosalind gave him the verbal countersign, then the substance beneath it, watching the colonel receive each fact the way a seawall receives waves — no motion, only the slow accumulation of damage. Fleet destroyed off the Sable Reaches. No relief. One ship, maybe, out of the northern ports, if it sailed at all, for the garrison and whoever the garrison judged worth saving. After that, nothing. After that, Vellamere would belong to whoever marched in, and the marching would begin within the month.

Ashe listened to all of it without touching the bottle again, which told Rosinald more than the dispatches had. When it was done the colonel sat looking at his own hands for a long moment.

"Seventy-one men fit for duty," he said at last, conversationally. "Four hundred civilians who signed petitions, bought bonds, wrote letters, took wages — four hundred people whose names are sitting downstairs in that woman's books with the word *loyal* next to them, because they thought the words mattered." He looked up. "Do you know what happens to a list when the other side captures it?"

"Yes, sir," Rosalind said. "I do."

"Then you understand why I've asked Mistress Ferrant three times to burn the standing columns." Ashe's mouth twisted. "Three times. She looks at me the way a priest looks at a man asking her to drown a baby. Records, she says. Protection, she says. As if a book were a wall." He rubbed his eyes. "Maybe she's right. I'm too tired to know anymore. Go and eat, clerk. There's a tavern on Rope Walk that hasn't yet learned the war exists. Stay the night; the roads after dark aren't worth the risk, and I've one more thing to send south tomorrow regardless."

"One more thing, sir?"

"A letter to a dead man's family. It can wait for morning. Most things can wait for morning. That's the whole art of command, Ferre — deciding which things can't." He waved a hand, dismissed him, and turned back to his palimpsest of maps, a man alone with the geography of his own ending.

* * *

Odile Ferrant stayed late because she always stayed late, and because tonight the hall emptied early, the soldiers drifting off to their suppers, the petitioners driven home by the dark, until only she and the lamps and the books remained, and the scratching of her pen was the loudest thing in Vellamere.

She opened the second volume to the day's page and began where she always began: reconciling the morning's entries. Salt pork, seized, credited. Harbor dues, waived, annotated. And then she paused, pen lifted, because the shipping clerk was still there — no. Gone. But his entry remained in her mind the way certain entries did, the ones that didn't sit right, the ones that balanced too perfectly. Tomas Ferre. Freight reconciliation. Figures smooth as river stones, every total landing where arithmetic wanted it, no smudges, no corrections, no human hesitation anywhere in the hand.

Seventeen years of reading hands had taught her that living accounts were messy. Dead-perfect accounts were written by people performing bookkeeping rather than doing it. She had reported such things before, in the early years, when reporting meant something. Now she wrote in the margin, in her small sharp hand: *Quillon & Daughters, Marrow Bay — verify house exists.* Because that was the discipline. You did not accuse. You recorded. The record would do whatever the record was going to do.

She understood what the Colonel did not understand, and what the smooth-handed clerk probably did not either, though he had looked at her books the way a man looks at a wound. They all thought she kept the ledger because she loved paper, or feared change, or lacked imagination. Let them. The truth was simpler and harder: she had been twelve years old the year the fever came through the fishing quarter, and the parish rolls had burned in a chimney fire that autumn, and because the rolls were gone, the widows could not prove their husbands had ever lived, and the relief committees paid out to whoever shouted loudest, and her mother — her mother had died owed a pension that died unproven with her. Unrecorded, unpaid, unmourned by any institution because no institution could see her.

Odile had decided at thirteen that she would never again live in a world that could lose a person simply by losing the paper. Names protected people. Not always, not perfectly — she was not a fool, whatever the Colonel thought — but a name in a book was a hook set into history, a claim that could be argued, contested, appealed. Erasure was the only absolute defeat. Everything else was negotiable.

So she wrote the standings, knowing the words were guesses. So she kept the debts, knowing debts outlived their meanings. So she had refused the Colonel three times, gently, immovably, and would refuse him a fourth, and a fifth, until soldiers pried the books from her hands or the world ended, whichever arrived first.

She dipped her pen and entered the evening's last item: lamp oil drawn from stores, customs house, one pint. Then, because the hall was empty and no one would ever know, she added beneath it, in the margin where annotations lived, a private line she permitted herself perhaps once a month:

*Still counting. Still here.*

It was not hope, exactly. She distrusted hope the way sailors distrust a following sea — it comes up behind you quiet and pleasant and drives you onto the rocks. It was more like stubbornness with a long view. The war would end. Armies would come and go. Books would survive, if someone held to the work, and someday — she could not say when or how — the pages would speak for the people who had no other voice. That was all. That was everything.

She closed the volume, snuffed two of the three lamps, and was gathering her coat when she noticed the shipping clerk's folder returned to the receiving desk where the night soldier had left it — stamped, filed, done — and beside it, dropped carelessly, a single leaf of wrapping paper the man had used to blot his pen. He had written on it. Barely anything. A practice flourish, a fragment — half a figure, the tail-end of a word — the kind of unconscious mark every hand leaves when it thinks no one is looking.

Odile picked it up under the last lamp.

She had seventeen years of hands in her memory, and this hand was wrong for a shipping clerk. Shipping clerks wrote fast and shallow, numbers crowding out letters. This hand bore down. This hand had been trained to write rarely and therefore wrote carefully, each letter formed with the deliberation of a man signing something. And the fragment of word — she tilted the paper to the lamplight — was not freight terminology at all.

It read, in a small compressed script: *—evacuat—*

Odile Ferrant stood very still in the empty hall, the lamp guttering, the wind pressing at the windows like a patient hand. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. Somewhere out on the dark water, a ship that might never sail waited for news she had not yet been given.

She folded the scrap of paper precisely, once, twice, and slipped it into the back pocket of the current ledger, where anomalies went to wait.

*Verify the house,* she wrote mentally, and then, with the cold certainty of seventeen years at the desk: *and watch the clerk.*

Outside, the tide was turning, and the town that counted everything slept unaware of what the morning's count would bring.
