## 5. Requisition

The army came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the harbor fog had not yet decided whether to be weather or simply absence.

Rosalind learned of it the way he learned of everything in Vellamere—sideways, through small disturbances that most men would not notice. The fish carts had not come up from the quay by seven. The garrison drummer who usually marked the changing of the watch was silent. And at the corner of Salt Lane, a boy stood motionless at the top of the stairs that led down to the water, staring east with the particular stillness of someone watching something arrive.

Rosalind set down his tally-slate among the bales of hemp he was supposed to be counting and climbed to the loft window of Harrow & Sons' warehouse, where a clerks' cot and a cold stove constituted his entire domestic existence these three weeks past. From the window, above the rooflines, he could see the coast road where it crested the ridge beyond the salt marshes—and along it, a line.

Not a mob. Not a column of raiders. A line, straight as a ruled margin: infantry in the slate-grey of the Provisional Authority, bayonets fixed but sloped, wagons behind them, and at the head, mounted officers moving at a walk. They did not hurry. That was the thing Rosalind understood first, before fear, before calculation—the *unhurriedness* of it. Armies that expected resistance moved fast, hungry for the advantage of speed. This army moved like a landlord arriving to inspect property it already owned.

He counted. Old habit; counting was the one form of writing no one could confiscate. Two thousand foot, perhaps more still hidden behind the ridge. Six guns unlimbered but not positioned—displayed, not aimed. Four wagons with canvas covers, and one longer wagon, box-sided and windowed, that made his stomach tighten before he could say why. He would learn its purpose soon enough.

Below, Vellamere woke into its last ordinary morning. Smoke rose from chimneys. A woman beat a rug over a railing. Somewhere a dog complained about the cold. The town went about its business with the desperate normalcy of people hoping that if they pretended hard enough, history would pass them by like weather. Rosalind had lived seven years inside that pretense. He knew exactly how much it weighed and exactly how little it held.

He should have gone to Colonel Ashe immediately. The dispatches sewn into the lining of his spare coat—the ones already delivered, thank whatever gods remained indifferent—were finished with him, but there were protocols for an occupation, passwords, fallback instructions. His training said: move now, while the streets were empty, while movement itself still passed unnoticed.

Instead he stayed at the window, because something in him needed to see it. Needed to watch, with his own eyes, what happened to a place when the story of it changed hands.

* * *

They entered without a shot fired.

Captain Verel—he learned the name within the hour, as Vellamere learned everything within the hour, through doorways and shop counters and the ferry of women's voices—rode at the head of the column into the Fishmarket Square and stopped his horse before the garrison gate as though arriving for a scheduled appointment. Which, in fairness, he was. The armistice signed at Corvenne eight days ago had ceded the coastal towns on paper; this was merely the paper being enforced.

Colonel Ashe met him at the gate with forty men drawn up in their dress uniforms, the ones they kept for funerals and inspections, both of which this resembled. Rosalind, watching from the warehouse loft with a spyglass he had no official reason to own, saw the two officers speak for perhaps five minutes. He could not hear the words, but he could read the grammar of it: Verel's hand making a small, courteous gesture toward the town; Ashe's shoulders, in their faded blue, going very slightly lower.

Surrender of arms. Parole of the garrison pending evacuation. Continuation of civil administration under military oversight. All of it civilized. All of it bloodless. The republic's last garrison on this coast became, in the space of a morning, a logistical problem to be managed until a ship came.

And that might have been the whole of it—a town changing flags the way a house changes tenants—if it had ended there. But Verel had come with more than soldiers. He had come with the long wagon.

It backed up to the customs house on Quay Street at midday, and men began carrying things out of it: desks, inkstands, boxes of stationery, a portable writing slope of polished mahogany. An office, assembled in the square like a stage set. Rosalind watched a clerk in civilian black—brought in the wagon, pale from days of travel—set out papers with the fussy precision of a man arranging an altar. Then Verel himself walked to the customs house steps, unrolled a document sealed in grey wax, and read it aloud in a voice pitched to carry.

Rosalind caught fragments across the water and the rooftops. *By authority of the Provisional Government... all municipal records... registers of allegiance, debt, trade, and residence... to be surrendered within twenty-four hours... failure to comply to be construed as...*

Construed as what, the wind took. But everyone listening supplied the ending themselves.

* * *

He told himself, in the first hour, that it did not matter. That was the discipline talking, the catechism of seven years: *never write, never linger, never be remembered.* Paper was a trap. Paper was how Ives had died—no, that wasn't true either, and the lie snagged in him like a burr, because paper was how Ives had been *condemned*, a courier list copied and sold and circulated, and Rosalind's silence had not saved him from the record even though Rosalind's silence had been purchased with the promise that records were the enemy, that the invisible man was the safe man.

So: let them take the ledgers. Let them take every scrap. Burning names was a mercy; he had believed it once, professionally, the way a man believes the doctrine that pays his wages.

Then he thought about Odile Ferrant.

He had seen her ledger only twice, and briefly—once when delivering dispatches to Ashe, passing through the customs house while she worked at her high desk with her back straight as a mast, and once, weeks later, when he'd lingered (breaking his own rule) ostensibly to settle a freight query. She kept the town's whole life in that book. Names, trades, debts, allegiances, births registered against shipments, widows' pensions noted in margins beside cargo manifests. Every person in Vellamere existed in those pages twice: once in flesh, once in ink. And Odile Ferrant believed—Ironically, stubbornly, with a faith she probably couldn't defend in argument—that the second existence protected the first. That a man written down could not simply be erased. That the record was a wall between the weak and the strong.

He had thought it naive. Watching the soldiers carry the ledger out, he discovered he had been wrong in a way that was worse than naive. It was not naive. It was accurate, right up until the moment the wall changed hands.

* * *

They came for it at four o'clock in the afternoon, with the light going bronze and the frost already reforming in the shadows.

Two sergeants, six soldiers, and the pale civilian clerk, who carried the requisition order folded in his glove like a man handling a live coal. They entered the customs house without knocking, which required no courage since Odile Ferrant was the only soul inside—dismissed from her post that morning, the town whispered, ordered to surrender her keys, permitted to walk home because the new authority found no charge to press. Yet.

From the loft, three hundred yards away across the roofs, Rosalind watched the windows of the customs house glow with lantern light as the soldiers moved through it. He watched the pale clerk emerge carrying books in stacks against his chest. Manifests first. Then the tax rolls. Then, on the third trip, a volume so large the man had to hug it, a ledger bound in green baize with brass corners gone dull from decades of hands.

Rosalind knew that book. He had stood near it. He had felt, absurdly, the way one feels near a loaded weapon or a sleeping child—the sense of something potent and vulnerable at once.

Every name. That was the thought that would not leave him alone as he watched the clerk stagger down the steps under its weight. *Every name.* Not just the loyalists, not just the garrison's sympathizers—everyone. The republican harbor pilot and the boys who'd smuggled dispatches in herring barrels. The widow Quillon and her son. The debtors whose arrears Odile had quietly annotated *waived—hard winter*. The soldier in the garrison who owed a Vellamere tailor eleven crowns. Seven years of a town's trust, recorded in good faith by a woman who believed ink was a shield, all of it now walking across a square in the arms of an enemy clerk to be sorted, assessed, and—this was the part Rosalind understood with the clarity of a man reading his own death warrant—*used*.

Because that was what victors did with records. They did not burn them. Burning was for losers, for guilty consciences, for men with something to hide. Victors *read* them. Victors cross-referenced. Victors built from a captured ledger something far more efficient than any list of suspects: a complete inventory of a population, pre-sorted by allegiance, by debt, by grievance, by friendship. Names to reward. Names to watch. Names to erase.

He had spent seven years believing invisibility was safety. Now he watched identity itself get confiscated—whole, wholesale, without a single door kicked in—and understood that the opposite of invisible was not *seen*. It was *defined*. To be written down by someone else's hand was to become their sentence about you.

A soldier stumbled on the icy step; the clerk clutched the ledger tighter, and for one absurd moment the great book hung in the air above the frozen cobbles, green baize and brass, the whole remembered weight of Vellamere suspended between one pair of failing hands and the stone.

Rosalind's breath fogged the loft glass and his own reflection ghosted over the scene—his face laid translucently across the square, across the soldiers, across the falling book. He looked at that doubled image and felt something shift in his chest, some timber groaning under a load it had carried too long.

He had memorized pages of that ledger. Weeks ago, standing in the customs house on the pretext of freight queries, running his eyes down columns while Odile Ferrant's pen scratched—names, debts, annotations—committing them to the only archive no requisition could reach. He had done it without letting himself know why. Discipline forbade the knowing. But his hands had known, and his eyes had known, and now, watching the book disappear into the long wagon, he understood that he had been building something in the dark for weeks without admitting it: a copy. A counter-record. A refusal.

The wagon door closed. The lock turned. A soldier chalked a number on the panel, and the wagon rolled away toward the garrison, carrying the town's memory off to be rewritten.

* * *

That night Vellamere went dark early. Occupied towns do; lights invite attention. Rosalind sat in the loft with no lamp, wrapped in his coat, listening to the boots in the street below—patrols now, two men, unhurried, the rhythm of a town that belonged to someone else.

He ran the arithmetic he had been avoiding all day. The ledger contained roughly nine hundred adult names. Of those, perhaps two hundred were compromised—known sympathizers, garrison kin, the smuggling networks, men like himself operating under shipping-clerk skins. Two hundred lives now existing solely at the discretion of Captain Verel's clerks and whatever historian they'd brought in the long wagon to make sense of them. Against that: one man with a partial memory of some pages, a dismissed archivist who knew the book better than its captors ever would, and a widow who, Odile had once mentioned in the tone of a woman cataloguing assets, remembered everything that had happened in Vellamere for thirty years and had buried a husband besides.

It was not enough. It was laughably not enough. Nine hundred names against three people and a chapel vestry.

But he kept returning to the image of the book hanging in the air above the ice, and to the look on the pale clerk's face as he hugged it—not triumph, not malice, just care. Custody. The man had handled the ledger the way you handle something valuable, and valuable things, Rosalind knew, could be stolen back.

Below, a patrol paused beneath the warehouse. One soldier said something; the other laughed, short and muffled in the cold. Then the boots moved on.

Rosalind sat in the dark and did the thing he had trained himself never to do. He wrote—not on paper, nothing so dangerous, but in the disciplined dark of his own mind he wrote a sentence he could not unsay:

*They have taken the record. Therefore the record must be taken back.*

Somewhere across the town, in a narrow house on Rope Walk, a dismissed clerk was presumably sitting in her own darkness, thinking about the same book. In the morning, or the night after, one of them would break silence first.

He found, examining himself with a courier's cold honesty, that he already knew which of them it would be—and that it would not be him, and that some locked room in his chest had known it for weeks.

Three streets away, a light kindled in the customs house that should have been dark, and burned past midnight.
