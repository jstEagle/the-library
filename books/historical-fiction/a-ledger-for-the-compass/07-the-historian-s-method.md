## 7. The Historian's Method

The labor summons came on blue paper, which was how Rosalind knew it was real. The requisitioned government of Vellamere had not yet learned to counterfeit the small economies of bureaucracy—the watermarked stock, the serial numbers stamped in fading violet ink, the clerk's initials in the corner rendered with the weary precision of a man who signed his name four hundred times a day and had stopped pretending any of them meant anything. *R. Brightwater, shipping clerk, to report to the Customs House, Quay Street, seventh hour, for purposes of inventory labor. Failure to attend will be noted.*

*Will be noted.* The phrase did more work than a threat. It assumed a record already existed; it assumed the record mattered; it assumed he existed to be entered into it. Seven years of discipline told him that this was precisely what he should fear most. And seven years of something else—something older than discipline, some residue of the man he had been before invisibility became a vocation—whispered that here was a door left open, and doors left open in occupied towns were never accidents.

He went because Odile Ferrant would have gone. He went because the ledger was in that building now, under other hands, being made into something else.

He dressed the part carefully: the gray coat of a minor functionary, sleeves worn shiny at the cuff, a canvas apron folded under one arm. He rubbed chalk dust into his knuckles until his hands looked like a man's hands who had spent the morning hauling crates. In the glass above the vestry sink—a cracked mirror Marta Quillon had donated without comment—he studied the face looking back at him and found it satisfactory in its dullness. Nobody looks twice at tired eyes in a cold town. Tired eyes are the wallpaper of defeat.

Quay Street smelled of tar and horse and the particular metallic sourness of soldiers' cook-fires lit too close to salt water. The customs house still wore its old face—white pilasters gone green at the base, the republican eagle over the door chiseled shallow enough that someone had already begun chiseling *it* away—but sentries flanked the steps now, and a queue of townsfolk stood shuffling at the side entrance: men summoned like himself for labor, women come to petition for ration corrections, one ancient shipwright arguing with a corporal about a boat the army had taken and was letting rot at its moorings.

Rosalind took his place in the line and practiced being no one. It came back to him the way swimming comes back—the slow exhale, the softening of the gaze, the deliberate arrangement of the body into an attitude of patient grievance. Around him the queue performed the same theater. A whole town learning again, within a single week, the grammar of subjection: eyes down, hands visible, answers short.

Inside, the great hall of the customs house had been transformed. The long counters where merchants had once quarreled over duties were pushed against the walls. The floor was stacked with document cases, and trestle tables ran down the center of the room in two rows like beds in a ward—and at each bed lay a patient, a ledger or register or parish book, open under lamplight, attended by clerks in the gray-green of the victors' administration.

And at the far end, beneath the mutilated eagle, at a table set slightly apart and higher than the rest, sat Maître Corvain.

Rosalind saw him first as everyone in the hall saw him: a compact man in civilian black, silvering at the temples, spectacles perched low, writing with a steadiness that seemed almost insolent amid all that confiscation. He did not look up when the laborers were marched past him. He did not look up when a captain delivered a sealed folio to his table with a click of heels. He wrote. The scratch of his pen carried further than it should have, the way small sounds do in large rooms full of held breath.

Rosalind was assigned to a crew moving document cases from the ground-floor strong room to the second-story archive. It was ideal work: heavy, repetitive, requiring a man to keep his head down and his arms full, passing within ten feet of Corvain's table every fourth trip. By the third hour Rosalind had memorized the rhythm of the hall. By the fifth he had allowed himself, once, twice, a third time, the luxury of actually reading.

What he saw turned his stomach with a slow, professional admiration that frightened him more than horror would have.

Corvain worked through ledgers the way a surgeon works through a list of amputations—without haste, without relish, without mercy. Before each volume he consulted a thin dossier bound in buff card: lists, presumably, of testimony, denunciations, garrison correspondence seized intact. Then the pen moved. Names crossed out—not struck through in anger but ruled out with a single clean horizontal stroke, the way one cancels a misprinted form. In the margin beside each, in a small, exact hand: *disloyal*. Or *absent*. Or *deceased — verify*. Or occasionally, and this was worse, nothing at all, just the stroke, as though the name had simply ceased to be a fact worth annotating.

Beside other names, Corvain wrote *loyal*, and where the original entry recorded a debt, he adjusted the figure downward with a neat caret and a correction in red. Debts forgiven for the faithful. Debts enforced—with interest calculated aloud by a subordinate—for the rest.

Rosalind hauled his case up the stairs and understood, with the clarity that exhaustion sometimes lends, exactly what he was watching. Not vandalism. Architecture. Corvain was not destroying the past; he was rebuilding it, load-bearing lie by load-bearing lie, into a structure that would hold the weight of the new order. In twenty years no one would dig beneath it. In twenty years the crossed-out names would be the only names there had ever been—or rather, the only names that had ever been *disloyal*, since a record does not need to say a man never existed. It needs only to say what kind of man he was, and let time do the rest of the erasing.

On his ninth trip, Rosalind passed close enough to read the page under Corvain's pen, and his blood went cold and then hot in the space of a heartbeat.

*Ferrant, O., custos.* The stroke. The margin: *dismissed. Complicity suspected — file pending.*

And below it, three lines down, a name Rosalind had last seen sewn into his own memory alongside a night of rain and a decision he had rehearsed every day since: *Ives, courier.* Already crossed out years ago in some provincial register, but Corvain was verifying old work now, consolidating. In the margin, in fresh red ink: *executed, year six. Confirmed.*

*Confirmed,* Rosalind thought, climbing the stairs with forty pounds of confiscated paper in his arms. *By whom? Confirmed against what?*

There was no answer in the building except the scratch of pens, dozens of them now, all learning Corvain's hand.

* * *

Maître Corvain had been a historian for thirty-one years, and he had learned in that time that the truth is not a thing one finds. It is a thing one *finishes*.

He rose before dawn out of habit older than the republic whose corpse he was now cataloguing, ate bread and drank watered wine standing at his window overlooking the harbor, and was at his table by the sixth hour while the soldier-clerks still shuffled and coughed and blew on their fingers. He permitted himself this small superiority without guilt. Order begins in punctuality. Every collapse he had ever studied—he had studied nine, counting this one—had announced itself first in the laxity of records: dates unentered, receipts unpreserved, signatures witnessed by no one. Chaos is not mobs in the street. Chaos is an archive nobody trusts.

The woman Ferrant had kept a fine ledger. He acknowledged this the way he acknowledged good masonry in a condemned building. Her hand was regular, her columns true, her cross-references disciplined. It had cost him genuine irritation to strike her name from it. But she had recorded allegiances as though allegiance were a fact observable at the time of entry, and this was precisely the error the old regime had died of: mistaking a snapshot for a verdict. Allegiance is proven only retrospectively, by outcomes. A ledger that pretends otherwise is not history. It is gossip wearing a uniform.

So he corrected. That was the word he used, privately, in the margin of his own mind, and he chose it with care. *Corrected.* Not falsified—falsification implied a prior truth of superior claim, and Corvain had spent a career demonstrating that prior truths are merely earlier drafts. The men across the water who called themselves the government-in-exile would write their own registers someday, if they ever got the chance, and their crossings-out would be no less confident. History was simply the version that survived its author. His task was to survive longer than his.

He was verifying the Ives entry when the laborer passed his table for the ninth time.

Corvain noticed people the way a chess player notices pieces—not individually at first, but structurally. This hall contained, at present, four categories of person: clerks, who feared him; officers, who needed him; petitioners, who resented him; and laborers, who did not see him at all. The gray-coated man with the chalky hands was a laborer of the textbook sort—head down, step economical, face arranged into the blankness of the exhausted. Textbook. That was the difficulty. In nine collapses Corvain had found that the truly invisible are rare, and that most men posing as them overshoot: they make themselves *too* null, too perfectly forgettable, the way a forged coin is always slightly too bright. This one's shoulders were wrong for a porter—too square, too quiet when he set down his loads. And he had looked at the table. Three times. Never at Corvain—that would be fatal stupidity—but at the pages, at the margins, with a flicker of attention briefer than a blink.

A curious man, then. Curiosity in a requisitioned port was weather to be watched, not feared. Corvain made a small mark in the notebook he kept for such observations—*gray coat, labor summons no. 2214, watches margins*—and returned to his verification.

The Ives entry troubled him, and he was honest enough with himself to sit with the trouble rather than bury it. The original provincial register gave execution, year six, on the authority of a tribunal summary that Corvain had requested twice and received once, the copy bearing a filing number that did not match the tribunal's known sequence. Small things. A misfiled number was usually nothing. Usually. But Corvain had built his reputation—in the old academy, in the ministry, in the long gray years when historians were tolerated the way chimneys are tolerated, necessary and soot-producing—on the principle that a record is only as strong as its weakest citation. If he certified *confirmed* on a mismatched number and some future scholar pulled the thread, everything woven around it loosened. One loose name could unravel a chapter. A chapter could unravel a regime's legitimacy. He had seen it happen. He had, in one case in his youth, helped it happen, and had spent decades deciding whether that constituted his greatest sin or his finest hour of scholarship. He had never settled the question. He suspected it was both.

He drew a light pencil circle around *confirmed*—pencil, not pen; pencil is the honest implement of doubt—and wrote in his day-book: *Obtain tribunal original, series K. Also parish death register, Vellamere, years five–six.*

It was this second item that would matter, though he could not yet know it. Because the parish register of Vellamere, years five and six, contained an entry in a curate's careful hand recording the burial of a man matching Ives's description and age, dead of fever in the house of correction—but dated eleven months *after* the tribunal's supposed execution date. And somewhere in the town, in a chapel vestry smelling of candle wax and old wool, a man with chalk on his knuckles was about to learn that the silence he had counted as his life's great sin had purchased nothing at all—because the man he had failed to save had already saved himself, in the only currency left to the captured: he had given up nothing, survived the firing squad that never came, and died holding his tongue.

Corvain knew none of this. He knew only that the number was wrong-shaped, and he was a man who followed wrong shapes the way water follows cracks—patiently, without malice, finding the weakness because finding weaknesses was what he was *for*.

At the eighth hour he dismissed the labor crews, watching from beneath his spectacles as they shuffled out into the gray morning. The gray coat went last, pausing at the door to accept a chit from the sergeant, and for half a second—half a second, no more—the man's posture changed, straightened, became the posture of someone accustomed to walking out of dangerous buildings having gotten exactly what he came for.

Corvain opened his notebook and added one line beneath the first:

*Returns tomorrow. Watch the hands.*

Then he blotted the page, squared it with the others, locked the day-book in the drawer that traveled with him even to meals, and rang the small bell that summoned his clerks to begin the afternoon's correcting—for the past, as he often reminded them, does not rebuild itself.
