## 9. Rules of Copying

The vestry of the Chapel of Saint Aubin smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the ghost of incense that no one had burned since the republic stopped paying its priests. Rosalind stood in the doorway for a long moment before entering, the way a man might stand at the edge of cold water, and told himself—again—that what he was about to do was not courage but arithmetic. The ledger was gone. The names were in enemy hands. If anything was to survive, it would have to be made again, and he was the only man in Vellamere who had spent three weeks memorizing pages he had pretended merely to shelve.

His hands were shaking.

He noticed it with a kind of detached irritation, watching his fingers tremble against the strap of his satchel as though they belonged to someone else, some nervous apprentice who had never learned that the body could be trained out of fear. Seven years of discipline, and his hands betrayed him the moment he picked up a pen. Perhaps that was fitting. The last time he had written anything at all—a name, a date, a single damning line in a courier's log—he had been writing Ives into the ground.

*Not tonight.* He crossed the threshold and closed the door behind him.

Odile Ferrant had already transformed the vestry into something between a scriptorium and a field hospital. She had commandeered the long table where the sacristan once laid out altar linens, scrubbed it down with sand and vinegar, and covered it with the chapel's oldest cloth—a funeral pall, she admitted without shame, because no soldier would think to search under a pall and the wool was thick enough to muffle sound. Candles stood in jars of sand so their light would not show through the shuttered window. Ink pots, goose quills, a bone folder, blotting sand, a razor for trimming nibs: she had assembled all of it from the town's forgotten corners, from the schoolmaster's widow, from a chandler who owed her a debt recorded nowhere now except her memory.

"You're late," she said, not looking up. She was sharpening a quill with the economy of a woman who had cut ten thousand of them.

"Soldiers on the quay road. I went around by the tannery."

"Then you smelled worse than usual." She glanced up then, and whatever she saw in his face softened the edge off her voice. "Sit down, Brightwater. Before you change your mind."

"I'm not going to change my mind."

"People say that," Odile said. "Usually an hour before they change it."

Marta Quillon arrived twenty minutes later, letting herself in through the vestry's back door with the confidence of a woman who had been coming to this chapel since before either of them was born. She carried a basket of bread and hard cheese as if they were meeting for a sewing circle, and perhaps that was the point; a widow carrying food to a church looked like grief, not conspiracy. She set the basket down, unwrapped her hands from her shawl, and said, "Right. Where do we begin?"

"With rules," Rosalind said. "Before anything else. We establish rules, and we keep them even when keeping them feels stupid. Especially then."

He heard his own voice and was surprised by how steady it sounded compared to his hands. He pulled the stool to the table and sat, and began, for the first time in seven years, to speak aloud the things he knew.

* * *

"The first rule," he said, "is ink."

He uncorked the pot Odile had provided and examined it against the candlelight. "What is this?"

"Oak gall and gum arabic, with a little iron sulfate. The old recipe."

"It'll do. But understand why." He turned the pot slowly. "Most ink fails two ways. It smears while wet, or it fades after years. If we are caught with wet pages, we burn them anyway—but if we're *not* caught, if these pages have to live hidden in a wall or a well or inside a mattress for five years, ten, then fading kills them just as surely as a sergeant with a lantern. Oak gall darkens as it ages. What is pale tonight will be black in a month. So we use nothing else. No soot washes, no berry stains, no charcoal. Charcoal is a child's drawing. It wipes away with a thumb."

"And paper?" Marta asked.

"Only rag paper. Nothing with wood pulp in it—it yellows and crumbles. Odile, where did you get yours?"

"A stationer on Cordwainer Lane who died owing me money. His widow sold me his stock. Forty sheets, unruled." Odile laid one on the table and ran her palm across it. "It's good stock. It'll outlive us."

"Then rule two follows from that," Rosalind said. "No page kept twice. Every working page gets copied clean, and the working pages get burned the same night they're used. Not tomorrow. Not when we finish. That night. We don't accumulate drafts. A draft is a confession waiting to be found."

"That seems wasteful," Marta said.

"It is wasteful. Waste is cheap. Discovery is not." He thought of the warehouse loft, of watching soldiers carry the customs ledger out on a handbarrow like a coffin, and felt the memory settle in his chest like swallowed river water. "You have to understand what we're doing. We're not copying a book. We're committing perjury against an empire, one page at a time. Corvain's historians will produce their register within weeks. Ours has to exist somewhere they can't reach—and the only place they can't reach is nowhere. So everything lives in our heads until the final copy, and the final copy exists once, and then the heads that hold it scatter."

"Three heads," Odile said. "Yours, mine, hers. And Tobias, eventually, whether we like it or not."

"We'll come to Tobias." He didn't want to talk about the boy yet. The boy was a wound they'd all agreed not to poke until it scabbed. "Rule three. Memory checked against memory. This is the part that matters most, so listen."

He took a fresh sheet—the first sheet he would ever mark, and he felt the enormity of it in his wrist, in the pulse ticking under his jaw—and dipped the trimmed quill. His hand shook so badly that the first stroke skittered sideways, a hairline crack of black across the rag paper.

He stared at it. Odile and Marta, courteously, looked elsewhere.

*Seven years,* he thought. *Seven years of being no one, and the pen remembers me better than I remember myself.*

He steadied his breathing the way he'd been taught on his first mission—four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm of a man asleep—and tried again. The second line came out straighter. By the fourth, his hand had found something like calm, though he could feel the effort humming in his forearm like a struck string.

At the top of the page, in small, severe capitals, he wrote:

**A LEDGER FOR THE COMPASS — SECOND RECORD**

Below it: **Rule 1. Oak-gall ink only.** **Rule 2. No page kept twice.** **Rule 3. Two memories agree or the entry waits.**

* * *

They worked until the candles had burned down two fingers' width, and what they worked at, mostly, was disagreement.

That was the point, Rosalind kept telling himself. That was the method. He recited from memory the opening folios of the customs ledger—the entries Odile herself had written over nine years of service, the names and debts and allegiances of a town—and she corrected him, and Marta corrected both of them, and the corrections were the whole architecture of the thing.

"The Perrault account," Odile said. "Henri Perrault, rope-walk, owed the customs house eleven crowns four shillings as of last spring."

"Eight crowns," Rosalind said.

"Eleven."

"Eight. Because in March he paid three crowns toward it, in person, at your own window, and you initialed it in the margin. I remember the initials. You write a strange F, more like a seven."

Odile frowned, and he watched her go inward, watched her turn back through her own years the way a clerk turns back through a folio. Then her face cleared. "Three crowns in March. Yes. Yes, he paid after the herring season, and I wrote it in the margin because the main column was already ruled and full. You have a clerk's eye, Brightwater. It's wasted on espionage."

"It wasn't wasted. It got me three weeks of standing in your customs house unremarked."

"Because everyone took you for a dull little shipping clerk," she said, with something dangerously close to affection.

"Dullness is armor," Rosalind said, and wrote the Perrault entry correctly: eight crowns four, payment noted, initial in margin. Then he held the page toward the candle and read it aloud, and Odile repeated it back word for word, and Marta listened for anything that contradicted what she knew of the family—*Perrault's daughter married a fisherman from the north shore, loyalist household, the son signed on with the garrison militia*—and only when all three threads agreed did he set the entry aside as clean.

One name. Twenty minutes. There were hundreds.

"We'll never finish," Marta said quietly, when she understood the pace. "Even with four nights. Even with forty."

"We'll finish the ones that matter most," Rosalind said. "The ones Corvain will get wrong. He doesn't care about debts, Marta. Debts bore him. He cares about allegiance—who served the republic, who informed, who sheltered couriers, who resisted. Those are the entries he'll rewrite, and those are the ones we fight for. We start with the people whose names he'll cross out."

He did not say *the people whose names he'll brand disloyal*. He didn't need to. They all knew the list began with Marta's husband.

She sat very straight for a moment, hands folded on the table like a woman at a funeral—which, in a sense, she was attending—and then said, "Étienne Quillon. Rope-walk, then harbor pilot after his knees went. Ledger lists him under collaboration, entry made the eleventh of October." Her voice did not break, but it went flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice. "He died on the ninth of November, on the mole, with a musket ball in him, fighting the landing boats. I know because I washed the body. I know because the ball came out through his chest and there was powder burn on his coat sleeve, which means he fired too, close quarters, and you do not fire from close quarters at men whose side you're on."

Rosalind wrote every word. He wrote until the quill needed re-trimming, wrote *powder burn on the sleeve* in oak-gall ink that would be black in a month and legible in fifty years, and when he finished he said, "Two memories agree. Yours and the ledger's original entry—I copied it the week I memorized the folios. Étienne Quillon, listed as collaborator, dated the eleventh of October. The date itself proves the error, because—" he paused, hating himself for the arithmetic of it, "—because he was dead by then. Someone entered his name after he couldn't dispute it. Do you know who reported him?"

"No," Marta said. "But I have my suspicions, and my suspicions wear a uniform and eat at the garrison mess."

"Then we note the suspicion separately, marked as suspicion. We never let a guess wear the clothes of a fact. Rule four." He added it to the header list. "Guesses labeled as guesses. Always."

* * *

Near midnight they rested, because Odile insisted—*"A tired memory invents things and believes them"*—and while Marta dozed in the corner pew with her shawl over her head, Odile sat across the table from Rosalind and studied his hands.

"They shake," she observed.

"Yes."

"All night."

"Yes."

"You've written nothing for seven years, haven't you. Not a letter, not a receipt, not so much as your own name."

"Not even my own name." He flexed his fingers and watched the tremor run through them like wind through wheat. "Writing leaves evidence. Evidence leaves a trail. The catechism was simple: never write, never linger, never be remembered. I can recite it faster than my own birthday. For seven years I've been a man made entirely of not-doing, and I told myself that was discipline." He turned the ink pot slowly on the cloth. "Tonight I discovered it might also be a kind of rot. My hand has forgotten how to be a hand. It only knows how to hide."

Odile said nothing for a while. In the corner, Marta breathed in slow, even sleep.

"My husband was the same," she said at last. "Not a courier. A customs officer before me—my predecessor, actually, which is how we met. After the purges started, he stopped signing things. Wouldn't endorse a bill, wouldn't witness a marriage register, wouldn't even sign for a parcel. He'd make me do it. He said a signature was a hook and the world was full of fishermen." She smiled, thin and old. "He died in his bed, safe as a coin in a strongbox, and you know what? Not one person in this town can tell you a single opinion the man ever held. He hid so well that when he was gone, there was nothing left of him to miss. I loved him and I couldn't tell you what he believed. Don't become that, Brightwater. Shaking hands are better than empty ones."

Rosalind looked down at the tremor in his own fingers—at the faint stain already working into the creases of his knuckles, oak gall soaking into skin that hadn't touched ink since he was a boy practicing a copperplate hand under a tutor's disapproving eye—and felt something shift in his chest that was not quite comfort and not quite resolve, but was adjacent to both.

"Rule five," he said softly. "We keep going even when it costs."

"That's not a rule. That's a prayer."

"In my experience they're the same document."

He trimmed a fresh quill, pulled a clean sheet toward him, and began again. The Perraults. The Lavauds. The widow Anselme who had sheltered a wounded republican sergeant in her cellar for six weeks and whose name appeared in the ledger with the annotation *harbors strangers* in Corvain's new, efficient hand. Name after name, checked twice, spoken aloud, agreed upon, set aside. The candles sank. The cold deepened until their breath hung above the table like smoke. Somewhere outside, a patrol passed along the lane, boots ringing on frozen cobbles, and the three of them sat absolutely still until the sound faded, and then, without discussion, went back to work.

It was nearly dawn when Rosalind blotted the night's clean copies, wrapped them in oilcloth, and fed the working pages one by one into the vestry stove. He watched each sheet curl and bloom orange, watch the names vanish upward as heat and ash, and understood—with a clarity that frightened him more than any patrol—that he had just committed the first act in seven years that he could not undo and did not regret.

Odile banked the fire. Marta gathered her basket. At the back door, pausing with her hand on the latch, Marta said, "My son comes home from the north shore fishing grounds Thursday. He saw things on the water the night the fleet burned. Things the fleet's survivors won't say aloud yet." Her eyes found Rosalind's in the grey light. "If we're building a true record, boy, you'll want to hear what he saw."

The door closed behind her. Rosalind stood alone in the vestry among the ash-smell and the guttering candles, his ink-stained hands finally, blessedly still, and counted the hours until Thursday.
