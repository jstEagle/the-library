## 10. Colonel Ashe's Bargain

The garrison smelled of cold iron and spilled brandy.

Rosalind had been summoned by a boy in a corporal's coat who didn't know how to salute and didn't pretend to, and he climbed the outer stair to the commandant's quarters with his collar up against the wind coming off the harbor. The wind carried the smell of the requisitioned town with it—tar, wet wool, woodsmoke from cook-fires that burned green because even the army was running out of seasoned fuel—and beneath all of it, faint as a watermark, the sea itself going rotten where the fleet lay broken on the far shoals.

Colonel Ashe received him alone.

That alone was strange. A month ago the colonel's rooms had been a hive of adjutants and signalmen, maps pinned three deep, runners coming up the stairs two at a time. Now the outer office stood empty, its desks pushed back against the wall like furniture in a house prepared for mourning, and the inner door hung ajar on a room lit by one lamp and one fire, both of them losing their argument with the dark.

"Sit down, Brightwater." Ashe did not look up from the glass in his hand. "Or stand. I've stopped pretending it matters."

The room had been stripped of everything official. No maps. No dispatch box. Only a writing desk bare as an altar, a cot made with parade-ground corners, and bottles—four of them, three empty, arranged on the sill with a drunkard's accidental precision. The fire was real enough, though, and Rosalind took the chair near it because his hands wanted the warmth and because refusing small comforts had become a habit he could no longer afford.

"You sent for me," he said.

"I sent for a shipping clerk." Ashe finally looked at him, and Rosalind saw what the lamp showed and the rank concealed: a man aging in fast-forward, the flesh of his face loosening around the bone as if the structure underneath had quietly resigned. "That's what your papers say. That's what my quartermaster says you are—the man who can find tonnage in a harbor full of nothing. So let us conduct ourselves accordingly, clerk, and you may go on being whatever else you are some other evening."

Rosalind said nothing. In seven years he had learned that silence was not only a defense; it was an invitation. People filled it. They always filled it.

Ashe filled it now. He rose, went to the window, and stood with his back to the fire, which meant his shadow fell across Rosalind like a second, larger man seated in judgment.

"Do you know how many souls I have left?" the colonel said. "Two hundred and eleven. Two hundred and eleven men under arms and another forty camp-followers, wives, a chaplain who has taken to drink so thoroughly I've considered putting him on the ration roll as ammunition. The ship comes in four days. *If* she comes—she's promised, and promises have been worth exactly the paper they're written on since the fleet burned. And when she comes, she takes two hundred and fifty people, no more, because that is what her boats will hold in a winter swell, and every name above two hundred and fifty is a man I must look in the eye and tell to stay."

He turned. The lamplight caught the brandy in his glass and made a small amber country of it.

"So I count," Ashe said. "I have been counting for six weeks. It is the only arithmetic left to me. And into this arithmetic, Brightwater—or whatever your true name is; don't trouble yourself denying it, I've commanded garrisons too long to believe anyone is named after a river—into this arithmetic, certain parties propose to add *names*."

Rosalind kept his face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, the way ice turns over in a river before it goes under.

"The customs ledger is gone," he said carefully. "Requisitioned. Sealed."

"Yes. Gone. Sealed. Carried off to be read by historians." Ashe said the word the way other men said *rats*. "And yet names have a way of propagating, don't they? Like damp. You seal one book and open a drawer somewhere and there they are again, breeding in the margins. There's a widow on Salt Lane telling anyone who'll buy her bread that her husband died a patriot while the paper calls him a collaborator. There's a boy—I'm told a boy—running messages between houses at hours that would interest Captain Verel's provosts. There is a dismissed archivist who walks the sea-wall every evening at the same minute, regular as tide-tables, as though discretion were a garment one could put on over one's habits." He drank. "And there is a shipping clerk who has spent three weeks asking careful questions about sailcloth."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, a sentry's boots rang on stone, pacing out his post, pacing out the last days of the republic in this town.

"Colonel," Rosalind began, "if you have concerns about—"

"I have one concern." Ashe set down the glass with exaggerated gentleness, the way a man handles anything when he is afraid of what his hands will do otherwise. "I have two hundred and fifty berths. Everything else is weather."

* * *

He came away from the window then, and sat opposite Rosalind, and for a moment the performance of command dropped entirely and Rosalind saw the man beneath: tired, intelligent, and honest in the particular way of officers who have lost an army and intend to lose it correctly.

"When I was a major, at Corvenne," Ashe said, "we held the bridge for nine days. On the ninth day the order came to withdraw, and the withdrawal list was written by a staff captain who had never seen the men. He listed them by regimental number. Neat columns. Beautiful penmanship. And because the columns were neat, nobody questioned them, and because nobody questioned them, we left forty-one wounded behind who were on no list at all—not the dead-list, not the missing-list, not any list. They simply ceased to exist between one line and the next." He looked into the fire. "A fisherman found two of them in the spring. The other thirty-nine are still, officially, deserters. Their wives draw no pension. Their children carry the word *deserter* in their mouths like a stone."

"That was a bad list," Rosalind said quietly. "Not a reason for none."

"It is a reason for fewer lists!" Ashe's voice cracked out of him, then steadied. "Listen to me. I am not Verel. I am not Corvain with his little annotations. I do not want the past rewritten—I want my people *out*. And here is what twenty-six years of service has taught me that your historians and your archivists will never learn: **names are ballast**." He leaned forward. "Every name you carry, you carry in your body. Every name you've memorized, sworn to, hidden in a lining or a memory—it weighs. And when the soldiers stop you on a quay—and they will stop you, they stop everyone now—that weight is what they find. Men survive by being light, Brightwater. By being *no one*, carrying *nothing*, answerable to *no story*. You want to save these people? Then be empty. Board that ship with clean pockets and a blank face, and live, and let the dead sort out their own paperwork in the spring."

"And if the record dies instead?"

"Then it dies." Ashe said it without flinching, and Rosalind understood that the flinching had happened years ago, somewhere, and had used itself up. "Paper burns. Memory rots. Towns are renamed. I have watched it happen four times in my career and the world has gone on turning, and the children of the forgotten have grown up and learned to be careful, which is the whole of what survival ever taught anybody." He reached into his coat. "But I am not a philosopher. I am a man with a manifest."

He laid a folded paper on the table between them and did not push it forward. Just laid it there, in the lamplight, where it sat like something living.

"One berth," Ashe said. "Under a name I will enter myself, in my own hand, on a list signed by my own authority—which, until that ship casts off, is still law in this town, whatever Verel's proclamations say. A good name. A dull name. A clerk's name." A thin smile moved through his exhaustion. "You seem suited to those."

Rosalind did not touch the paper.

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, the questions stop. The sailcloth stops. Whatever is being copied in that chapel vestry at night—yes, I know about the vestry; I have two hundred and eleven men and nothing to do but know things—the copying stops. No more lists. No more testimony. No more names above two hundred and fifty." He picked up his glass again, and his hand, for the first time, was not quite steady. "You give me a light ship, Brightwater. That's all. Give me a ship that arrives with nothing in her hold for Verel's provosts to hunt, and I will spend the last coin of my authority getting you onto her."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and Rosalind became aware that the fire had burned down to a red argument among the coals, and that the lamp needed trimming, and that outside the window the harbor lay black and patient, waiting for a tide four days distant.

He thought about Ives.

He thought about Ives the way he thought about him every night now, which was differently than before. For seven years Ives had been a closed account—a debt entered, sealed, carried. But Odile Ferrant had begun pulling threads through the old record, and Marta Quillon's grief had proven that memory survived in the people the paper forgot, and lately—lately Rosalind had allowed himself to wonder whether the account was truly closed, whether somewhere in some fragment, some margin, some surviving scrap, there might be news of what Ives's silence had actually bought. He had not dared finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was writing, and writing was visibility, and visibility was death—he recited the catechism nightly, and nightly it held less.

And here was Colonel Ashe offering him the catechism bound in leather. Be no one. Carry nothing. Live.

It was offered kindly. That was the devil of it. Verel would have bought him with threat; Corvain would have bought him with logic. Ashe bought him with a wounded man's arithmetic and forty-one names at Corvenne that no column had ever held, and he meant every word of it, and because he meant it, the offer went into Rosalind like a splinter rather than a blade—blade you could refuse; a splinter worked its way in on its own.

Because part of him wanted it. God, part of him wanted it the way a drowned man wants air. Four nights of writing until his fingers bled, four nights of Odile checking figures against figures and Marta dictating through her grief and Tobias slipping through streets where informers breathed—one misstep, one search, one sergeant in a bad temper, and it all ended in a courtyard wall with the copies burning in a brazier. Versus a berth. A dull name in a steady hand. Morning tide, and the town shrinking behind him, and the long gray safety of being no one again.

Seven years of careful silence. Were they survival? Or were they complicity wearing survival's coat?

"The widow's husband," Rosalind said at last. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Quillon. The ledger brands him a collaborator. He died holding the north gate against the first company that entered—you know that. Your own gate-sergeant filed a report saying so."

"I know it."

"Then you know what happens to a record when honest men decline to keep it. It doesn't die, Colonel. It gets *kept by someone else*. By Corvain. And Corvain keeps it wrong, and his version is the one with the seal on it, and Marta Quillon's son goes hungry under a traitor's name while the truth sits in your mouth unspoken because speaking it would be ballast." He looked up. "You fought twenty-six years for a republic whose entire idea was that a man's name meant something the state couldn't take. I watched you salute that idea every morning of this siege. Don't ask me to help you bury it in the harbor on your last day."

Ashe was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, the anger had gone out of him, leaving something worse: agreement, exhausted and defeated.

"You sound like my wife," he said. "She died at Corvenne. Not on any list either." He rose, crossed to the sill, and stood looking at the four bottles as if counting them again, the only arithmetic left. "Keep the paper," he said, without turning. "It's valid until the tide turns. I am an old soldier, Brightwater, and I have learned that men rarely change their course by argument—they change by wrecking. Go and wreck yourself if you must. But understand what I am telling you, man to man, officer to whatever you are."

Now he did turn, and the lamplight showed his eyes clear and sober, the brandy notwithstanding, and Rosalind understood that the drinking was camouflage, that the colonel had been perfectly lucid through every word.

"There is a provost captain named Duret," Ashe said. "Verel's ferret. He has been asking after a shipping clerk for three days. Not suspiciously—*routinely*. Which is worse. Routine searches find things that clever ones miss. Three days, Brightwater. After that, the berth stays valid but the man walking to it will be followed." He picked the folded paper back up off the table—Rosalind had not touched it—and pressed it into Rosalind's hand, closing the fingers over it himself, like a father pressing a coin into a child's palm. "Four days. Decide by the third, or the decision will be made for you."

Rosalind walked back down through the empty garrison with the paper in his breast pocket, feeling it there like a coal wrapped in cloth—warmth and danger in the same small weight. Outside, snow had begun to fall again, fine and dry, hissing on the lantern-glass. The sea-wall path would take him back toward the town, toward the vestry, toward the lamp Odile kept burning past midnight.

He was halfway along the wall when a shape detached itself from the shadow of the powder magazine and came toward him quickly, hooded, breath smoking—and even before the figure spoke he knew the walk, knew the urgent, clipped stride of a woman who had spent her life being exact about time.

"Brightwater." Odile's voice was low and shaking, and it was not fear shaking it. "Come now. Come quickly. I've found something—Ives. There's a fragment. He lived, Brightwater. He lived, and there's a page in his hand, and you need to see what he wrote before—" 

She caught herself, glanced back at the dark garrison windows, and pulled her hood lower.

"Before they find it first."
