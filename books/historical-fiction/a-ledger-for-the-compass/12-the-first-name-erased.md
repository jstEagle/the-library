## 12. The First Name Erased

The proclamation went up on the customs house door before the fish stalls had finished being cleared from the square, and Marta Quillon read it twice because the first time her eyes refused the words their meaning.

*By order of Captain Verel, commanding the forces now in occupation of Vellamere, and by authority of Maître Corvain, historiographer appointed to this district: the Provisional Register of Allegiance is hereby published and shall stand as the official record of conduct during the late rebellion. All ration entitlements, labor assignments, and matters of civil standing shall be governed by said Register.*

Below the formal language came the lists themselves, pasted sheet upon sheet down the door's oak panels, columns of names in a clerk's careful hand—some marked with a small circled *L* for *loyal*, some left blank, which everyone would quickly learn meant *unproven*, and some struck through with two firm horizontal strokes, like a man drawing a line through a debt he refused to carry any longer.

She found Anselme's name on the fourth sheet.

*QUILLON, ANSELME — collaborator; furnished provisions and intelligence to rebel forces; deceased.* The word *deceased* was added in a smaller hand, almost an afterthought, as though the clerk had paused over it and decided that even the dead must be properly filed.

Marta stood in the cold with her basket on her arm and did not move for what felt like a long time. Around her, other townsfolk drifted up to the sheets, reading, flinching, murmuring, drifting away again with their collars pulled high. A woman she had known all her life—Céline Aubert, who had once wept into Marta's kitchen cloth over her husband's debts—read the name *Quillon* beneath its double stroke and stepped back half a pace, and then, catching herself, stepped forward again and said, too loudly, "Terrible ink they use these days," and hurried off toward nothing at all.

That was how it began. Not with soldiers. With half a step backward on frozen cobbles.

Marta walked home by the long way, along the sea wall, because she needed the wind. It came off the water hard enough to make walking a kind of argument, and she let it push at her the whole distance, thinking: *He died for them. He died under their guns on the harbor steps with a boat hook in his hands, and they have written him down as a man who fed the enemy.* She thought about the night of the rising, eleven months gone now—the bonfires on the mole, the republican drums, Anselme coming out of the house in his good coat because he said someone had to stand where the town could see a gray-haired shipwright refusing to bow. She had begged him not to go. She remembered that clearly, and she hated herself for remembering it, because it made the memory of him less than it should have been, reduced him to a man who might have been argued out of his courage. He had kissed her forehead and said, "Names outlast us, Marta. Make sure mine is a good one." And then he had gone down to the water, and the water had taken him, or rather the muskets beside the water had, and they had brought him home across the threshold feet first as the old custom demanded, and she had buried him in the churchyard above the tide line where he could keep arguing with the sea forever.

And now a historian she had never met had dipped a pen and unmade all of it in four words.

Her son was waiting when she got home. Pieter, nineteen, tall as his father and built like him too, all shoulders and stubbornness, sat at the table with the morning's labor chit in front of him and a look on his face she recognized from the day they had buried Anselme—that terrible stillness young men put on when feeling would otherwise drown them.

"They wouldn't take it," he said.

"Wouldn't take what?"

"My chit. At the yard." He turned the paper over as if the answer might be printed on the reverse. "The foreman—Baptiste, you know him, he taught me caulking—he took me aside, off the planks, so no one would hear. He said the new regulations came down yesterday. Rationed work goes by the Register now. He said—" Pieter stopped. His jaw worked. "He said he was sorry. He said there was a stroke through our name."

"There is," Marta said. "I've seen it."

"Then what do we eat?"

It was such a simple question. Such a boy's question, though he was hardly a boy anymore, and it cut her deeper than the proclamation had, because the proclamation was an abstraction done in ink and this was real: the cupboard in the corner held flour enough for five days if she baked thin, salt cod enough for three, and nothing else worth counting. The occupation army paid in scrip for labor, and labor was distributed by the Register, and the Register said the Quillons were collaborators, and collaborators' sons did not get work rations. She could see the whole machinery of it turning in her mind, gear after gear, each one reasonable on its own, each one just a clerk following a list, until at the end of the chain a family starved in a town full of fish and grain and no one at all had decided to starve them. That was the horror of it, she thought. No one had decided. A decision had merely accumulated.

"We eat," she said aloud, "because I am going to fix this."

"Mama—"

"I am going to fix this, Pieter, and you are going to let me, because if you go down to that yard and shout at Baptiste they will add your name to a different column, do you understand me? They will write *son of a collaborator, seditious disposition*, and then it will be two names ruined instead of one lie told." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, Anselme's voice, the voice he used on deck in weather, and she saw Pieter recognize it too, and subside.

But fixing it—she had said it to steady him, and only when the door had closed behind him, gone to walk off his fury on the sea wall as she herself had done, did Marta sit down at the table and ask herself what *fixing* could possibly mean against an official register posted on a government door.

* * *

The answer, when it came to her, came in Odile Ferrant's voice, though Odile was nowhere in the room.

They had spoken twice since the requisition. The first time had been in the market, brief and dangerous, Odile passing her a parcel of dried beans and saying under the noise of the crowd, "Your husband's name was in my ledger. I wrote it myself, three years ago, from the harbor tax rolls. What I wrote was that he supplied cordage to the garrison at cost. That is what the paper says, Marta. Whatever they have written now, I know what I wrote." The second time had been outside the chapel, at dusk, Odile pausing beside her on the step as though they were two acquaintances who happened to share a devotion, and saying, "There are people working on something. People who remember. If you want your husband remembered rightly—not consoled, *remembered*—come to the vestry when the bell rings for None. Come alone. Bring every date you can hold in your head."

Now Marta sat at her table and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that this was the fight. Not the posting on the door—paper could be torn down, reprinted, corrected—but the thing underneath the paper. Corvain's register claimed to be the record. If it stood unchallenged, then in ten years it would simply *be* the truth; schoolchildren would learn that Anselme Quillon had sold out his neighbors, and Pieter would grow old under the weight of it, and eventually even Marta would begin to doubt her own nights, her own ears, her own grief, because a written lie repeated long enough becomes load-bearing. She had seen it happen with smaller things. A neighbor accused of hoarding who never fully lived down the accusation even after it was proven empty. A rumor about a priest that outlived the priest. Ink was patient in a way flesh was not.

So she would bring them dates.

That evening she cleared the table, swept the floor, banked the fire low, and sat down with the household account book—her own ledger, kept in her own hand for twenty-two years—and began to build her testimony the way a woman builds a case for a lawsuit, except that no court existed yet that would hear it, and might never exist, and she would have to build it anyway.

Anselme had been meticulous with money, which meant he was meticulous with dates. She knew the week he had signed the cordage contract with the garrison quartermaster because it was the same week they had paid off the loan on the *Ardent*, their little coaster, and she remembered the payment because Anselme had burned the mortgage note in the stove and they had drunk the good brandy and he had laughed harder than she'd heard him laugh in years. She knew the date the rebels had come to the yard demanding timber, because it was two days after the Feast of Saint Erasmus, patron of sailors, and Pieter had wanted to attend the feast and Anselme had made him stay and sand hull planks instead, and Pieter had sulked for a week—which meant, God forgive her, that her son's sulking was now evidence. She knew that Anselme had refused the rebels. She knew it the way she knew the shape of her own hands. He had come home white-faced and furious and said, "They wanted the *Ardent* and forty barrels of pitch. I told them to take it from the sea." And the next morning the *Ardent* had still been at her mooring, untouched, because Anselme had scuttled her himself in the night, in the shallow water off the yard, rather than let either side commandeer her—a fact recorded nowhere, known to perhaps four living people, and recoverable the moment anyone thought to drag the shallows and find her ribs still lying in the mud exactly where he sank her.

*A sunken ship,* Marta thought, staring at her account book. *A whole ship, lying in plain sight under six feet of freezing water, testifying to his loyalty, and no one has thought to ask it a single question.* That was the thing about the victors' history, she realized. It interviewed only the living who agreed with it. It never interrogated the mud.

* * *

She went to the vestry when the bell rang for None.

The chapel smelled of tallow and cold stone, and inside, in the little room behind the sacristy where vestments were kept, she found them: Odile Ferrant, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up with what looked suspiciously like a compass stylus; a lean gray man she did not know at first, seated at a plank table with a stub of pencil moving across scrap paper, and it took her a moment—because she had only ever seen him hunched and hurrying, a shipping clerk's shuffle—to recognize Rosalind Brightwater from the chandlery, the quiet one, the one whose face slid off the memory like wet slate. And a third figure by the brazier, feeding it splinters of broken pew, a young man with a bruised wariness about the eyes whom she placed after a moment as Tobias Renk, the Renks' boy, whose mother was sick and whose father had drowned years ago.

"You got my message," Odile said.

"I brought dates." Marta set her account book on the table between the candle and the pencil-man, and found her hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against the wood until they stilled. "I want it understood before I begin that I am not here to beg. I am not asking anyone's charity for my son's bread. I am here because your register"—she looked at Brightwater, because somehow he seemed the one to say it to—"your register has committed theft. It has stolen my husband's death. He died on the harbor steps fighting the republic's drums, and a man with a pen who never saw him alive has decided he died a traitor instead, and I am here to take it back."

Brightwater set down his pencil. For a moment he only looked at her, and there was something in his face—something tired and old and recognizing, as if her fury were a country he had lived in once. "Then tell us everything," he said. "Slowly. Dates first. We'll check each one against what we already hold, and anything that agrees twice, we write."

"And what happens to what you write?" Marta asked. "Where does it go? Because I have seen what happens to writing in this town lately. Soldiers carry it out the front door while the owner watches."

"What we write goes into memory first and silk afterward," Odile said quietly. "And someday, Marta—maybe soon, maybe years—it goes onto a record that can't be requisitioned. But tonight it goes nowhere but true."

So Marta began. She gave them the week of the cordage contract and the burning of the mortgage note. She gave them Saint Erasmus's feast and the refused demand for the *Ardent* and the pitch. She gave them the scuttling—hesitated, glanced at Tobias Renk, and decided that a boy who had already traded names once needed to see what loyalty actually cost—and watched Brightwater write it down and stop, pencil hovering. "The *Ardent*," he said. "She can be raised. Her keel is evidence, Marta. A hull doesn't perjure itself."

"She's under six feet of water off the yard slip," Marta said, "and the harbor is patrolled."

"Everything in this town is patrolled," said Tobias Renk from the brazier, unexpectedly. "Doesn't mean everything in it is guarded."

They worked past dark. Marta talked until her throat was raw, dredging twenty-two years of ordinary domestic paper—receipts, tithes, the christening fee for Pieter, the funeral bill for a daughter lost in infancy—and watched these strangers treat each trivial figure as if it were scripture, cross-checking, reconciling, Odile reciting entries from a ledger that existed now only inside her skull, Brightwater transcribing in a cramped hand that trembled slightly and never stopped. Once, near midnight, she caught him looking at Anselme's name as he wrote it, and there was grief in his face that had no business being there, the grief of a man at another man's grave, and she understood without understanding that this quiet clerk carried his own stroke-through names somewhere behind his eyes.

Near the end, Odile reached across and touched her wrist. "One more thing. The harbor steps. Anselme's death. Do you have witnesses? Names of anyone who saw him fall?"

"Half the town saw it. Half the town will deny it by spring." Marta thought. "Old Yannick Voss was mending nets on the mole. He saw everything that night—he told me so at the burial. He said, 'Your husband went down facing forward, Marta. Remember that.' But Yannick is frightened, and Yannick is old, and the soldiers drink at his daughter's tavern now."

"Frightened is manageable," Odile said. "Dead is not. We'll take him while he's only frightened."

Marta gathered her account book, her dates surrendered now into other hands, and stood in the doorway of the vestry looking back at the candlelit table, the bowed heads, the scratch of pencil on salvaged paper—a whole illegal nation in miniature, population five, treasury of memories. On the table lay a page with Anselme's name at its head, and beneath the name, in Brightwater's trembling hand, the beginnings of a paragraph that ended with the phrase *died in armed resistance on the harbor steps, 14 October, witnessed by multiple residents*.

Fourteen words against four. She thought of Corvain's double stroke through her husband's name, and she thought: *You drew your line in ink, monsieur. We are drawing ours in witness. Let us see which one the water rises to erase.*

She was halfway home through the frozen streets, breath smoking, when she heard boots behind her and did not turn fast enough, and a soldier's lantern swung up and a voice said, "Woman. You're out past curfew. Name and quarter."

She gave them her name.

The soldier checked a list by lantern light, and Marta stood in the cold watching his finger slide down the column to find her, and watched it stop, and watched his face change by one small degree—nothing dramatic, nothing cruel, just the faint closing of a door—as he read whatever the provisional register said about the wife of Anselme Quillon.

"Move along," he said. "Next time, be home before the drum."

It was nothing. It was a curfew warning every widow in Vellamere had received. It was also the first time in her life the state had looked at her and seen an enemy, and she walked the last streets home with her heart hammering and her resolve annealing into something that would no longer break, because she finally understood the true arithmetic of the thing, the arithmetic Odile and the trembling clerk already lived by: they had erased a name today, and erasure was not an ending but a beginning, because every name they struck made one more person who had nothing left to lose by telling the truth.

Behind her, across the sleeping town, the customs house windows showed one light still burning—Corvain's office, the historiographer working late over his clean and murderous pages—and Marta Quillon promised him silently, from the dark side of the street, that he should enjoy the quiet while it lasted. By tomorrow's bell, she intended to bring him a witness.
