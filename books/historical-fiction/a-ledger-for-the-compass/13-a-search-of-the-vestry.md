## 13. A Search of the Vestry

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Rosalind had learned, in seven years of listening to doors, that every hour was the right hour for some kind of visit and the wrong hour for all others. Midnight belonged to lovers and debt collectors. The hour before dawn belonged to sickness and death. But this knock—three blows, measured, unhurried, with the flat authority of a fist that expected obedience rather than answer—came in the gray interval between the last bell of compline and the first light on the harbor ice. It was the hour when a town's watch changed over, when sentries were yawning and sergeants were counting their men twice because they could not count them once correctly.

It was also the hour when three people were bent over a trestle table in the chapel vestry, copying names by candlelight onto paper that smelled of tallow and fear.

Odile's head came up first. She had the instincts of a woman who had spent thirty years hearing footsteps in corridors—the particular tread of someone coming to check her work—and she set down her pen without a sound, as if even the click of it against the inkwell might be entered into evidence.

"Three knocks," Marta whispered. "Not four."

"Four would be the provost," Odile murmured. "Three is a search party. They knock three times so the third can be heard over the door being broken if you're slow."

Rosalind was already moving. His body knew this choreography better than his mind did; it had rehearsed it in a dozen towns he had never been formally present in. The working copies went into the iron brazier—not the good pages, not yet, only the drafts, the reconciliations, the sheets where Marta's testimony crossed Odile's figures and left the small honest discrepancies of memory doing its best. He fed them in one at a time, pressing each down with the poker so it burned whole rather than lifting on a gust and sailing across the vestry like a burning bird, an accusation with wings.

The paper caught reluctantly. Winter-damp paper always did. He watched the ink curl brown, then black, then nothing, and thought: *Ives's name was on that sheet.* And then, with the strange arithmetic grief performs: *It is still in me. That is the only place it cannot be seized.*

"Out through the sacristy?" Marta breathed.

"No." Odile was standing now, smoothing her coat, transforming herself before their eyes from a conspirator into something else—a functionary. It was remarkable to watch. Her spine straightened, her chin found an angle of mild administrative offense, and her hands folded themselves into the posture of a woman who had been interrupted during important work and intended to say so. "No one runs. Running is confession. We are three loyal subjects of the Republic who have been using the chapel for evening prayers, and I am the former chief clerk of the customs house, and I will be *heard*."

"You were dismissed," Marta said.

"I was dismissed from my desk, not from my rank. There is a difference, and Sergeant Whoever-He-Is is about to learn it." She glanced at Rosalind, and for one unguarded instant the armor slipped and he saw what was underneath: terror, old and well-managed, the terror of a woman who had already lost everything once and knew precisely how it felt. "The papers under your coat. The silk. If they take it—"

"They won't take it."

"Don't promise. Promising is for priests."

The third knock landed like a verdict, and then the door itself opened without waiting for permission, because doors in Vellamere had stopped having opinions of their own.

* * *

The sergeant who came in was young for his rank, which usually meant either talent or patronage, and his face suggested neither—only the dogged patience of a man promoted because he had not yet learned to refuse anything. Behind him came six soldiers with lanterns, and behind them, filling the doorway like weather, the cold.

"Sergeant Brac," the sergeant said, producing a paper with the ceremony of a man who had practiced the gesture. "By order of Captain Verel, acting under the authority of the Provisional Commission, these premises are to be searched for seditious documents, unauthorized records, and correspondence prejudicial to the restored order."

"At this hour," Odile said.

Sergeant Brac blinked. "Madam?"

"At *this hour*. Your captain's own proclamation sets the curfew review at dawn and permits searches between first light and vespers, excepting houses of worship, which are to be entered only with the consent of the officiating priest or, in his absence, the senior lay officer of the parish." She said it fluently, without a flicker, and Rosalind—who had read Verel's proclamation four times and remembered no such clause—understood that she was inventing it, and inventing it in the accent of absolute certainty, which is the only accent law has ever spoken in. "I am the senior lay officer. My name is Ferrant. You may verify it in your own requisition register, entry forty-one, since your historians have been so thorough with our books."

There was a silence in which Rosalind could hear the brazier settling, the last sheet collapsing into ash. He stood slightly apart, near the vestment chest, wearing the expression of a man who had wandered in from the cold and was regretting it. His heart was going like a drum in a procession, but his face—he had spent seven years teaching his face to be furniture, and furniture did not sweat.

"The proclamation," Sergeant Brac began, "states—"

"The proclamation states several things, Sergeant, most of them in the passive voice, which is how cowards write. What matters is what it does not state, and it does not state that the army may enter consecrated ground in the dark on the word of—" she paused, delicately, "—an informer. Because it *is* an informer's word, isn't it? Searches at this hour always are. No honest man reports a crime at the hour of compline's end. Honest men are asleep. Only informers keep those hours, and only because shame keeps them awake."

One of the soldiers behind Brac made a sound that might have been a cough and might have been a laugh, and strangled it.

Brac flushed. Rosalind watched him wrestle with it—the order in his hand, the woman in front of him, the dawning suspicion that whatever he did here would be wrong in someone's ledger. It was the oldest trap in any occupation, and Odile had built it out of nothing but posture and grammar.

"You may search," she said, before he could decide, which was her real mastery—she never let a man reach his own decision while she could offer him one. "With my attendance. And I will note in writing, for Captain Verel's attention, the hour, the circumstances, and the conduct of the search. I kept the customs ledger of this port for eleven years, Sergeant. I know how records are made, and therefore I know exactly how they are *unmade*, and so, I suspect, do you."

She fetched paper. She actually fetched paper, sat down at the trestle table beside the cooling brazier, dipped a pen, and wrote the date and hour in a fair round hand while soldiers shifted uncomfortably in the lantern light, suddenly aware that everything they touched was being transcribed.

Rosalind moved, unhurried, toward the shadowed end of the vestry, and put his hand on the vestment chest, and waited.

* * *

They searched badly, because they searched frightened.

That was the thing about men carrying lanterns into a room where a clerk was writing down their names: they became gentle. They lifted the altar linens as though the cloth might bruise. They opened the hymnal cabinet with two fingers. One of them held a candlestick up to the light as if hoping it would explain itself.

But there was one who searched properly. He was older than the others, thirty perhaps, with a farm-broad back and the careful hands of a man who had handled things that could break. He worked down the wall opposite Rosalind, methodical, tapping floorboards, running fingers along shelf joints, and he was coming, board by board, toward the place where Rosalind stood with the oiled silk sewn flat against his ribs beneath his coat, riding the rise and fall of his breathing like a second heartbeat.

Rosalind catalogued his options with the cold bookkeeping he had taught himself long ago. The window behind him was narrow and barred. Marta stood between him and the door, an old woman whose presence would stay no hand. Odile's theater had bought minutes, not safety. If the broad-backed soldier reached him and asked him to open his coat, there was no sentence in any language that would save him, because the truth was a death warrant and every lie was worse.

He thought, absurdly, of Ives. Of the catechism. *Never be remembered.* He had followed every rule, and here he stood in a chapel at the end of the world, about to be remembered in the worst possible way, by the worst possible witness.

The soldier reached the last board before him. Tapped it. Straightened. And looked at Rosalind.

For a moment neither of them moved. The lantern threw the soldier's face into half-light, and Rosalind saw it properly for the first time—a weathered face, a scar through one eyebrow, eyes that were tired in the specific way of a man who had been doing arithmetic lately and not liking the sums. The soldier's gaze went from Rosalind's face to his coat, to the shape of it, the slight unnatural stiffness where silk should not be, and Rosalind watched him see it.

Watched him understand it.

And then the soldier turned away. He rapped his knuckles along the final stretch of wall, said, "Clear," in a voice pitched for the sergeant, and moved on to the pulpit with the diligence of a man who intended to find nothing anywhere else either.

Rosalind's breath went out of him slowly, through his nose, without sound.

He did not know the man's name. But some deep drawer in him, the one where seven years of other people's lives were filed, slid open, and he saw it: a page of the customs ledger, autumn of the year before the war ended, a fisherman named—*Aurel. Aurel Senn.* Fined for under-declaring his catch, the penalty set to strip his boat, his nets, his family's winter. And a shipping clerk named Corvel—Rosalind's mask of masks—who had reclassified the catch as mixed freight at a lower duty, quietly, in an afternoon, costing the revenue office eleven crowns and saving a boat. He had never met the man. He had never seen his face. He had done it because the man's daughter was ill and the fine was a drowning, and because in that season Rosalind had still believed that small mercies were private things, free of consequence, seeds dropped down a well.

Seven years down the well. And tonight a hand had come back up out of it, and turned away, and said *clear.*

Small mercies compound. Nobody tells you that. Nobody warns you that kindness is not spent but *invested*, that it sits in the dark drawing interest in ways you will never audit, until the day it pays out your life in a freezing vestry while a sergeant reads your handwriting upside down.

* * *

Odile let them go with exquisite reluctance, the reluctance of a hostess seeing off guests who had broken a cup. She walked them to the door, corrected the sergeant's citation of the proclamation article number (inventing the correction as smoothly as the original), and promised—promised—to forward her account of the search to Captain Verel personally, with a copy to the Commission, since accuracy mattered in dark hours.

Sergeant Brac, holding his useless warrant, mumbled something about duty and withdrew his men into the blue-black morning. The last one out was the broad-backed soldier, and at the threshold he paused, half-turned, and looked back—not at Odile, not at Marta, but at Rosalind, one second, two, his face unreadable as closed water.

Then he was gone, and boots faded down the frozen lane, and the three of them stood in the wrecked quiet of the vestry among the ash-smell and the scattered linens.

Marta sat down abruptly on the vestment chest, as if her bones had filed their resignation. "The informer," she said. "There's an informer. There has to be. They didn't come here for nothing."

"No," Odile said. Her voice had shed its armor the moment the door shut; it came out thin. "Someone told them. Someone who knows we meet, knows the hour, knows the room." She looked at the brazier, at the ash that had been a week of work. "We've been careful. Careful isn't a wall anymore. This town has started selling itself, one name at a time."

Rosalind barely heard them. He was watching the door.

"We burn faster now," he said. "Nothing stays overnight. We write, we reconcile, we commit it to memory, and the paper goes into the fire the same night. From tomorrow, the vestry holds nothing but God and dust."

"And the silk?" Marta asked.

"The silk stays where it is." He pressed his hand flat against his ribs, against the faint crackle of oiled cloth. "As long as it's on my body, it's a man, not a document. Men get searched. Documents get confiscated. I'll take my chances with the searching."

Odile studied him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not gratitude, exactly; reckoning. "You knew that soldier," she said. "The big one. When he looked at you, that wasn't a stranger deciding to be lazy. That was a man making a choice."

"I don't know him," Rosalind said. And then, because lying to Odile Ferrant had begun to feel like defacing a page: "I know his name. Aurel Senn. He doesn't know mine. Three years ago I altered a duty classification that saved his boat, and I never told anyone, least of all him, because telling would have undone it." He looked at his hands, at the ink stains that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift now, the stains of a man becoming visible whether he willed it or not. "I thought mercy was safest when it was silent. Tonight a silent mercy stood in a doorway and lied to a sergeant for me."

"Then you'll want to know," Odile said quietly, "that Sergeant Brac will file his report by noon. And a report of a fruitless search means the informer gets pressed to try again—or to try somewhere closer to us." She gathered the scattered hymnals, restoring order with the compulsive tenderness of her profession. "Whoever sold us knows this room. Which means by tomorrow night, this room belongs to them. We'll need a new place, a new hour, and above all—"

She stopped. Through the frosted pane of the vestry's high window, gray light was beginning, and with it, faintly, the sound of the town waking: carts, gulls, the garrison drum. And threaded through it, from the direction of the customs house, a bell ringing fast and irregular—the alarm pattern, the one that meant not fire, not flood, but *arrest*.

Marta was on her feet. "That's the quay ward," she whispered. "That's—"

Rosalind was already moving to the window, pressing his eye to the frost-feathered glass, and below, in the lane, he could see torchlight still dissolving in dawn, and figures moving toward the harbor, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading a ledger he already knows is balanced against him, that the search had not failed at all.

It had succeeded exactly as designed. The vestry was the decoy. Someone had wanted the soldiers looking at candles and ash while elsewhere, in the dark, they took what they had really come for.

"Odile," he said, without turning from the glass. "Where does Tobias Renk sleep?"
