## 14. The Informer

Tobias Renk had learned to sleep in the shallow way soldiers slept, one ear open for boots on stairs, and so he heard them coming long before they reached his mother's door — two sets of footsteps in the lane outside the tenement on Cordwainer's Row, unhurried, the walk of men who were not hunting but collecting. He lay in the dark of the room he shared with his brother and counted their steps past the window, and when they stopped at the door below, he was already out of bed and dressed.

His mother coughed in her corner. The medicine bottle stood empty on the sill where he'd left it that morning, upside down to prove to himself it was truly finished. Three days since the apothecary had turned him away with a look he would remember longer than any sermon: not cruelty, exactly, but arithmetic. A boy with no coin and no work ration was a sum that came to nothing.

He went down the back stair, through the yard where the wash froze in gray ropes, and over the wall into the tanner's lane, and by the time the knock fell on his mother's door he was three streets away, walking fast, breath smoking, telling himself the thing he had been telling himself for six days now, which was that it didn't count. That names weren't wounds. That he had given them nothing they could use against anyone who mattered.

The sergeant had been almost gentle, which was the worst part. Not the cell, not the threat — those he had braced for. It was the gentleness. Sergeant Brack had sat across from him in a room that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and had not raised his voice once, and had said, *We know you run messages, boy. We know your mother's lungs are full of water. We know what quinine costs now.* And then he had simply waited, folding his hands, while Tobias understood that everything the man said was true and that truth was the weapon being used on him.

Six names. He had given six. The widow Quillon's street. The chapel vestry's hours. The clerk who still came and went from the Ferrant house at odd times. He had tried to give them small ones, useless ones, but the sergeant had known how to ask, the way water knows how to find the crack in a wall, and Tobias had learned in that room that he did not have the shape of a hero anywhere in him.

Now he walked toward the harbor because the harbor was where people went when they had nowhere, and because his feet knew the route even when his mind refused to lead.

* * *

They found each other in the fish market, which at that hour was only ice and gulls and the smell of a town holding its breath. Rosalind Brightwater stepped out from between two gutting tables like a man who had been standing there all morning and perhaps had been; there was something about him that made you uncertain whether he had arrived or simply always been present, unremarked, like damp.

"I'm not going to run," Tobias said, because it seemed better to say it first.

"No," Rosalind agreed. "You wouldn't get far, and we both know it."

"You've come to turn me in." Tobias made his voice flat. "Or worse."

"I've come to ask you a question." Rosalind moved closer, and up close his face was thinner than rumor made it, gray under the eyes, the face of a man who had been writing all night with his coat collar up against the cold. "When they searched the vestry, they found ash and candle stubs and nothing else. But the same night, a patrol walked Cordwainer's Row and the Saltgate, and they searched two houses that no informer had mentioned to me. They searched rooms where I had been. Rooms where pages had been." He paused. "You told them more than six names, Tobias. You told them our habits. Where we worked. How we moved the paper."

Tobias felt the cold go through him, deeper than the wind off the water. "I never gave them the vestry," he said. "I swear it. I gave them the vestry hours, yes, the times the lamps burned, but I never—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "They took my mother's medicine debt as surety. They said if anything happened to the patrol, if anyone was hurt, the debt would be called and she'd be put out on the street in winter. I didn't have a choice."

"Everyone says that," Rosalind said. "Most of the time it's true. That's the trick of it. That's why it works."

A gull screamed somewhere above the ice. Tobias looked at his own hands, chapped red, a boy's hands, and thought of the sergeant's folded fingers, patient as a trap.

"What happens now?" he asked.

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. In that quiet, Tobias saw something pass over the man's face — not anger, though he had expected anger, and not pity either, which would have been worse. It was recognition. As if Rosalind were looking at a version of himself that had taken a different road at some crossroads years ago, and was measuring the distance between the roads.

"Seven years ago," Rosalind said, "there was a courier named Ives. When the soldiers closed on us, I stayed silent. I let him be taken rather than compromise myself and others. My rules said it was correct. Never write, never linger, never be remembered — and above all, never trade yourself for one name." He looked out at the frozen harbor, at the masts of the requisitioned ships standing like winter trees. "I told myself for seven years that silence was discipline. Last week I learned Ives survived capture and gave up nothing before they killed him. He was braver in a cell than I was ever required to be, and my silence bought nothing except that I lived to hear about it." He turned back. "So I am done with rules that only ever cost other people."

"What does that mean?" Tobias whispered. "What are you going to do?"

"It means," Rosalind said, "that I am going to do the stupidest thing available to me, which is to trust you. And you are going to earn it, because you're going to help us, and here is why you will: not because I threaten your mother, though I could name her address right now and we both know it. Because the men who bought your six names will spend them badly. They'll write them down wrong. Corvain's register already has half this town marked as traitors who died fighting them, and Marta Quillon's son can't draw rations because of ink. Your mother's medicine doesn't exist in their books, Tobias. Her debt exists. Their records don't keep people alive. Ours might."

"Their records got me the medicine," Tobias said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded like an excuse, and it was.

"And they'll take it away the day you stop being useful," Rosalind said, without heat. "You know that. You've known it since you walked out of that room. That's why you ran instead of going home to tell her the good news."

Tobias stared at him. It was true, and he had not let himself think it, and now it stood between them in the cold air like a third person. The sergeant had promised a weekly supply. The sergeant had also smiled at the end, a small satisfied smile, and Tobias had carried that smile home in his chest like a swallowed coal.

"They search the same rooms first," Tobias said finally. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "Every time. The Saltgate houses, because they're big enough to hide stores. The vestry, because everyone trusts churches. The Ferrant house, because Odile Ferrant is famous. They never search the ropewalk, because the foreman drinks with the quartermaster. They never search below the customs house, the old bonded cellar, because Corvain thinks it's beneath the dignity of the archive. And they never, ever search their own quarters — the requisitioned rooms at the Mermaid. If you wanted paper to disappear into this town, that's where I'd put it."

Rosalind did not move, but something in the quality of his stillness changed, the stillness of a man recalculating everything at speed.

"You understand what you just did," he said quietly. "You've just become worth more to us than the six names were worth to them. And they will find that out eventually, and when they do—"

"I know," Tobias said. "I did the arithmetic already." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, old on a young face. "Everyone in this town is doing arithmetic now, sir. That's all Vellamere is anymore. A ledger with frostbite."

"Then let's give them numbers they can't balance," Rosalind said. "Come with me. There are people you should meet, and pages you should learn to carry, and after tonight you'll know the whole of what we're building. And Tobias —" he paused, and for a moment the shipping-clerk blankness dropped away entirely, and what looked out at the boy was seven years of accumulated silence learning, at last, to speak — "what you did, you did out of love, which is more than the sergeant can say. Remember that when it gets hard. Love isn't the crime. What they make us trade it for — that's the crime."

* * *

That night, in the chapel vestry, Tobias Renk sat at a table between a disgraced archivist and a widow who talked to her dead husband while she dictated, and he watched a man with bleeding fingers write names onto oiled silk by the light of a single shielded lamp, and he understood that he had crossed some line from which there was no walking back.

It should have frightened him. Instead he felt, for the first time since the sergeant's room, clean.

He told them everything. Which patrols walked which streets at which hours, and which sergeants took bribes and which took pride, and the rotation of the guard at the customs house, and — most valuable of all, Odile said, her eyes bright and terrible over the ledger she was rebuilding from memory — the schedule of Corvain's verification work. Because Tobias had been in the archive twice more since his betrayal, running the sergeant's errands, and he had seen the historian working late with parish books stacked around him like fortifications, and had heard him say to his assistant, in the dry voice of a man arguing with himself:

"If two testimonies disagree, one of them is a lie. Find me which, before I certify another page."

Odile's pen stopped. She looked at Rosalind across the lamplight.

"He's checking," she said softly. "Corvain is actually checking."

And Tobias, who had sold six names to buy a bottle of medicine, sat very still in the warm dark of the vestry and understood that he had just heard the sound of the official history beginning, quietly, to come apart at its own seams.
