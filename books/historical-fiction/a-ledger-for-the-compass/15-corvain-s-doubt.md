## 15. Corvain's Doubt

The lamp in Maître Corvain's office burned a thin, tallow-yellow light, and beneath it the provisional register lay open to its third page, where two testimonies had begun, quietly and without malice, to contradict each other.

He had found it the way he found most things: not by looking for it, but by refusing to stop reading after he had finished reading. It was a habit from his years at the archive in Carresse, before the wars, when his work had been the arrangement of other men's certainties into shelves. A document read once is a document believed. A document read twice is a document questioned. He had built a career, and then a reputation, and then—when the republic fell and the victors needed someone who could make their past for them in orderly ink—a position, on the simple principle that he never stopped at once.

The first testimony belonged to Sergeant Hallam, recorded on the fourth day of occupation, regarding the disposition of the town militia at the surrender of the northern gate. The second belonged to a fishwife named Perrin Goudet, recorded the same week in the ordinary sweep of civilian depositions, in which she had mentioned, almost as an aside while cataloguing her grievances about requisitioned salt, that she had seen the militia assembled at the *harbour* gate that morning, not the northern one.

A small thing. The kind of small thing Corvain had annotated a hundred times without losing sleep: faulty memory, faulty vantage, the natural blur of frightened witnesses. He had even written the marginal note already—*discrepancy, immaterial*—in his small, upright hand.

But he could not stop looking at it.

Because it was not only the location. Hallam's testimony placed Captain Delorme of the militia at the northern gate, surrendering his sword personally to Colonel Vasse's aide. Goudet's testimony placed Delorme at the harbour gate, arguing—with what vehemence she did not say, though her phrasing suggested she had enjoyed reporting it—against the surrender altogether.

And Corvain remembered, now, with the particular discomfort of a man catching himself in an error of craft, that there was a third document. A requisition order, countersigned by Delorme himself, dated three days *after* the capitulation, authorizing the transfer of militia stores from the armoury near the harbour. A man who surrendered his sword at the northern gate did not, as a rule, spend the following days signing paperwork with any authority at all. Unless he had surrendered nothing. Unless he had walked away from one gate and gone to another, still armed, still commissioned, still—Corvain turned the word over carefully, the way one turns over a coin of doubtful mintage—resisting.

He set down his pen.

The office around him was quiet in the way that requisitioned buildings were quiet: not peaceful, but emptied, the silence of rooms whose owners had been subtracted rather than absent. The customs house smelled of cold iron and old paper and, faintly, of the sea, which came up through the floorboards on windy nights like a tenant refusing eviction. On the wall behind him hung the map of Vellamere with its districts shaded according to reliability—green for compliant, yellow for watchful, red for the harbour streets where the army posted double patrols—and Corvain had drawn those shadings himself, and had believed them when he drew them, and was not certain, tonight, sitting very still with his hands flat on the desk, that he believed them now.

It was the word *immaterial* that bothered him. He had written it perhaps four hundred times since taking up this post. Discrepancies of date, discrepancies of name, discrepancies of number—all immaterial, all smoothed over in the fair copy, because the register was not an archive in the old sense. The register was an instrument. Its purpose was not to preserve the past but to settle it, the way a magistrate settles a dispute: with a verdict that closes the matter. General Verel's staff had been quite clear. The town had resisted or it had submitted; its men had been loyal to the republic or loyal to themselves; every name required a category, because categories were how order was maintained, and order was the only thing, Corvain had told himself for eleven months now, standing between Vellamere and the kind of chaos that got people shot against walls.

But a man who signed papers three days after surrendering his sword did not fit in any category. And categories that shed light on nine hundred names should not be able to shed darkness on the nine hundred and tenth without the whole lantern flickering.

Corvain took up his pen again, crossed out *immaterial*, and wrote instead: *to be verified.*

Then he sat for a long time, looking at what he had written, feeling something move in him that he did not immediately recognize, because it had been so long since he had felt it that he had nearly forgotten its shape.

It was curiosity. Professional, disciplined, dangerous curiosity—the kind that had made him a good archivist once, back when being a good archivist meant following a question wherever it led rather than deciding in advance where it was permitted to arrive.

* * *

The parish books were kept in the vestry of the chapel of Saint Aldric, which presented a difficulty, and the difficulty had a name: Père Anselme, who had refused the oath, refused the summons, refused everything except his own stubborn continuance, and who answered Corvain's formal letter of request with a verbal reply delivered by a choirboy: *the books may be consulted in the presence of God, which is to say, in my presence, at hours when I am awake, which are fewer every year.*

So Corvain went himself, on a grey Thursday morning with sleet ticking against the cobbles, wearing his official coat with its chain of office and carrying his warrant, and found the old priest exactly where tradition said a stubborn man would be: in the way.

The vestry was low-ceilinged and smelled of candle wax and damp stone. Shelves lined two walls, holding the parish registers in their cracked leather spines—baptisms going back a hundred and forty years, marriages, burials, and, in a separate case that Corvain noticed at once and pretended not to, a collection of loose papers, affidavits, and private memoranda that no parish had any strict business keeping.

"Maître Corvain." Anselme's voice was dry as August grass. "The historian. Come to correct our memories?"

"Come to consult your records," Corvain said. "Which are, whatever else may be said of them, accurate."

"Accurate." The priest lowered himself into his chair with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "We record who was born, who was wed, who died. We do not record who was loyal. Perhaps that is our failing."

"It is your virtue," Corvain said, before he could decide whether he meant it as courtesy or confession. "It is precisely why I need you. Your books do not take sides. That makes them useful for checking books that do."

Anselme looked at him for a long moment, and something passed across the old face—not warmth, Corvain thought, but a kind of wary professional recognition, the look of one craftsman examining another's tools. "You want to verify testimony," the priest said.

"I want to verify everything."

"Everything is a great deal of verifying."

"I am aware."

Another silence. Then Anselme rose, groaning, and fetched the registers himself, setting them on the table with the deliberation of a man granting access to something he could not actually refuse to grant. "I will sit here while you read," he said. "Not because I distrust you. Because I promised these families, when I wrote their names down, that the names would be safe. I intend to keep an eye on the promise."

"You cannot protect names with your eyes, Père."

"No," the priest agreed placidly. "But I can remember what they cost."

Corvain began with the burials, because death was the parish's most reliable transaction. He was looking for Delorme, expecting nothing—men who vanished into the war did not usually die conveniently inside parish boundaries—but the discipline of method demanded he start somewhere, and starting with the dead had always steadied him. Names, dates, causes, officiants. The ink changed hands several times across the decades; a good archivist learned to read the clerks as well as the entries. There had been a hand here, decades ago, small and precise and slightly forward-leaning, that had recorded deaths with unusual care—noting next to several entries during the famine years not merely *died* but *died of hunger*, which no regulation required and which some supervisor had surely frowned upon, and which Corvain found himself touching with one fingertip, lightly, the way one touches a scar.

He worked through the morning. By noon he had verified Hallam wrong and Goudet right: Delorme had indeed been at the harbour gate, and more than that—he found the marriage record of a militiaman named Aubec, whose deposition Corvain had also taken, and discovered that Aubec's wedding had taken place on a date that Aubec's own testimony had placed a full year later, which meant either that the man had lied about his marriage or that he had lied about something else and used the marriage as cover, and Corvain wrote both possibilities down because writing down both possibilities was what honest verification looked like.

And then, turning pages toward the present, he came upon the entry that stopped him.

A burial, dated eighteen months ago. Name: Ives Calloran. Cause of death, in the small forward-leaning modern hand: *died in custody.* Officiant: Anselme. And beside the entry, squeezed into the margin in different ink, added later, in a hand Corvain did not know—a woman's hand, quick and angry:

*He gave them nothing.*

Corvain sat back.

He knew the name. Of course he knew the name; the name was in the seized republican correspondence, flagged months ago, filed under intelligence matters that had crossed his desk and departed it, because Calloran had been a courier, and couriers were the army's business, not the historians'. Calloran had been captured, interrogated by the provosts, and—according to the file Corvain half-remembered—had given up a route, a contact, something useful enough to close the matter. Standard. Ordinary. The machinery working as designed.

Except that a parish priest had buried him and a woman had written in the margin that he had given them *nothing*, and the two statements could not both be true, and Corvain had just spent an entire morning learning which of two conflicting statements to trust.

"Père Anselme," he said quietly. "Calloran. Ives Calloran. You buried him."

The priest did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was careful in a new way. "I did."

"The cause of death says custody. The margin says—" Corvain turned the book so the old man could see his own margin, or the margin that was not quite his. "Who wrote this?"

"A friend of his." Anselme's eyes were steady and entirely unhelpful. "Afterward."

"That is not an answer to my question."

"It is the only answer I have that does no harm." The priest folded his hands. "Maître, you have been sitting in my vestry all morning reading our dead like an auditor. I let you, because you asked honestly and because honesty has grown rare in this town. So I will give you one honest thing in return, gratis. Whatever you believe happened to that young man, ask yourself who benefits from your believing it. Then ask who benefits from the truth. The answers will not be the same people. They never are."

Corvain closed the register with exaggerated gentleness, the gentleness of a man restraining something. "You understand that withholding information from the historical commission is itself an offence."

"I understand," Anselme said, "that you will not arrest a priest in a chapel on the strength of a marginal note, because you are not that sort of man. If I am wrong about what sort of man you are, we will both find out shortly, and one of us will be surprised."

* * *

He did not arrest anyone. He walked back through the sleet with his collar up and his mind moving too fast for the weather, and by the time he reached the customs house he had assembled, in the ordered interior landscape of his thoughts, a list.

It was not a long list. But lists, like ledgers, have a way of growing once the first entry is made.

One: Hallam's testimony regarding Delorme was false, and false in a direction that flattered the army's account of an orderly submission. False in the direction the register wanted. Two: Aubec had misstated his marriage date in a manner that concealed activity during the interregnum weeks—activity the register currently categorized as collaboration, and which might be nothing of the kind. Three: the Calloran file, which he would re-read tonight, contained an interrogation outcome that a burial margin flatly contradicted, and if the margin was true—if the courier had broken nothing, revealed nothing—then the intelligence that justified three arrests last spring had come from somewhere else, or nowhere at all, and three arrests had been made on the strength of a story the machinery had preferred to the facts.

Corvain stood at his window, watching sleet silver the roofs of the harbour quarter, the red-shaded quarter, the unreliable quarter, and understood with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like vertigo, that he had been annotating the town's memory for eleven months without once checking it against the town.

He had trusted documents. It was his religion, if he had one: paper did not tremble, paper did not grieve, paper did not lie to save its mother's medicine or its own neck. But paper was written by trembling grieving frightened hands, and worse—it was written by *his* hands now, by the commission's hands, by hands with orders—and a record compiled under compulsion was not a record at all. It was a second conquest, fought in ink.

There was a knock. A clerk, apologetic, bearing the day's intake: fresh depositions to transcribe, the ongoing labor of categorizing a population. And atop the stack, a proclamation draft from the general's staff requiring the commission's seal—the announcement of the evacuation sailing, final departure on the morning tide four days hence, approved persons only, all remaining records to be crated for transport with the commission's effects.

Four days. Corvain looked at the draft, then at his own marginal note—*to be verified*—then out at the red-shaded harbour where the masts of the last ship stood black against the grey water.

Eleven months of work, and in one morning a priest's margin and a fishwife's grievance had put a hairline crack down the middle of it. He thought of what his old master at Carresse used to say, tapping a flawed folio: *a history with one false page is not mostly true, boy. It is wholly untrusted.* He had nodded then, obedient. He had forgotten it thoroughly, and recently, and profitably.

Corvain took up his pen, and instead of sealing the proclamation, he pulled the Calloran file from the intelligence shelf, unlocked it, and laid it open beside the parish register's memory in his mind—one account that said the courier talked, and one margin that said he hadn't—and between them, waiting to be read, a third document he had not yet imagined, in a hand he was beginning to suspect he had already seen somewhere, writing names the ledger said were erased.

Outside, the tide was turning. Four days, said the proclamation. Corvain dipped his pen, and began—to the alarm of no one, witnessed by no one, in the small upright hand of a careful man—to check.
