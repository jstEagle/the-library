## 16. The Ship Announced

The proclamation went up at first light, pasted to the customs house door with a paste that froze before the clerk could smooth it flat, so that the paper stood away from the wood in a stiff curl, like a lip curling back from a tooth. Rosalind read it twice in the gray cold, his breath fogging the vellum, while behind him the queue of ration-seekers shuffled and muttered and did not read it at all, because most of them could not.

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL ADMINISTRATION OF VELLAMERE, it began, and then the words he had been counting toward for eleven days: *the vessel* Resolute *shall depart upon the morning tide of the twenty-third instant. Passage shall be granted only to those whose names appear upon the approved register. The register shall be posted three days prior to sailing. No appeals will be entertained.*

No appeals will be entertained. He almost admired it. Six words, and every argument in the town extinguished like a pinched wick.

He turned away before anyone could mark how long he had looked, and walked, unhurried, clerk-paced, toward the fish market, where the ice was being chipped off the stalls and no one glanced twice at a man with ink-stained fingers and a ledger-keeper's stoop. But inside him something had begun to run — not panic, nothing so useful as panic, but a kind of arithmetic that would not stop. Four nights. Four nights minus the hours Odile spent sleeping, which she refused to surrender more than four of; minus the hour each evening when Tobias came and went through the vestry window with his reports; minus Marta's coughing fits, which were worse now, deep and wet, though she waved them off with the hand that wasn't holding her husband's ring on its cord around her neck. Four nights, and one hundred and six names still unreconciled against the parish fragments, and forty-three of those with testimonies so thin they would tear under Corvain's fingernail like wet paper.

He caught himself counting names the way other men counted coins, and understood that this was what Ashe had meant when he'd said, weeks ago, over the last decent bottle in the garrison cellar: *Names are ballast, Brightwater. Ballast keeps you steady right up until it drags you under.* He had thought it a cynical thing then. Now it sounded like arithmetic too, and the two arithmetics were at war in him, and there was no neutral ground between them.

* * *

Ashe sent for him at midday, which was itself unusual — the colonel did not send, he summoned, or rather he sat drinking in the empty officers' mess until people found their way to him like moths who had given up on the moon. The garrison had been stripped to its bones by requisition; the mess hall's long table was bare except for a candle stub, a bottle, and two glasses, one of which was already half gone.

"Sit," Ashe said, and poured without asking, because asking implied the possibility of refusal and Ashe had stopped entertaining possibilities some time around the destruction of the fleet. "You've seen the proclamation."

"I've seen it."

"Then you know you're on the list."

Rosalind took the glass but did not drink. "Am I?"

"Under your shipping-clerk name, yes. Verel's staff processed the garrison auxiliaries yesterday. Your papers came through clean." Ashe slid a folded packet across the table — heavy stock, waxed against damp, stamped with the Provisional Administration's seal, which was a sheaf of wheat over crossed anchors, an emblem of harvest and harbor that had never once described the country it governed. "Passage aboard the Resolute. Berth in the after hold, which is the warmest place on the ship and therefore the place I intend to put myself. We sail on the morning tide four days from now, and after that, Mr. Clerk, none of this—" he gestured with his glass at the bare walls, the frozen harbor, the town beyond the frosted windows where smoke rose thin and desperate from chimneys that were running out of things to burn— "none of this will have happened to you."

"You're assuming I want it un-happened."

"I'm assuming nothing." Ashe drank, set the glass down with the precision of a man who was managing his hands very carefully. "I'm telling you what I tell everyone whose name I signed onto that list. Which is forty-one people, Brightwater. Forty-one out of nine hundred. Do you know what I did with the rest? I ranked them. By usefulness, by vulnerability, by whether they had children, by whether they had enemies among Verel's officers. I sat up three nights doing it, and do you know what I discovered?" He laughed, short and terrible. "That it's the same work your friend Corvain does. Crossing out. Deciding whose name weighs enough to keep. The only difference is that my list saves them and his damns them, and both of us call it order."

Rosalind turned the passage papers over in his hands. The wax seal was flawless. The ink was black and certain. Somewhere in Verel's offices a clerk had written *approved* beside a name that was a fiction Rosalind had worn for seven years, and the fiction had never once failed him, and here it was offering to carry him out of the story entirely.

"The Ferrant woman isn't on the list," Ashe said.

It was not a question, and Rosalind did not answer it.

"And the widow Quillon. And the Renk boy, if he's mixed up in whatever it is the three of you are doing in that chapel — and don't look at me like that, Brightwater, I command a doomed garrison in a captured port, I know everything that happens in this town by Tuesday." He refilled his glass. "I'm not asking what you're doing. I'm asking you to stop doing it. Whatever record you're building, whatever truth you're stitching together in the dark — it won't survive. Corvain's register will be printed, distributed, archived in the capital, and in ten years it will be the only version anyone remembers, because the victors write with better ink and longer memory. What you're doing is carving a second inscription on a stone that's about to be thrown into the sea."

"And if the stone washes ashore?"

Ashe looked at him for a long moment, and Rosalind saw, beneath the drink-flush and the exhaustion, something that was not contempt. It might even have been hope, worn down to a nub but still there, the way a nail head remains after the board has rotted away around it.

"Then it washes ashore," Ashe said finally. "And someone reads it. And it changes nothing for any of us, because we'll be dead or scattered or renamed by then." He pushed the bottle across the table. "Take the papers. Sail on the tide. If you must leave something behind, leave it finished — finish it tonight, tomorrow night, and then stop. A record that gets its author arrested is not a record, it's a confession with extra steps."

"That," said Rosalind, standing, "is very nearly poetry, Colonel."

"It's gin," said Ashe. "Go away."

* * *

The papers weighed almost nothing. That was the thing he kept returning to as he crossed the town in the falling dusk — the whole weight of escape, of survival, of seven years of invisibility rewarded at last with a berth in a warm hold and a life un-happened, all of it fitting into a breast pocket without changing the line of his coat. He had carried dispatches heavier than this sewn into his lining. He had carried Ives's silence heavier than either.

At the corner of the ropewalk he stopped, and took the papers out, and stood holding them under the last light, reading his own false name — *Aldous Merrick, shipping clerk* — written in a stranger's confident hand. Aldous Merrick had served him well. Aldous Merrick had never lingered, never written, never been remembered. Aldous Merrick could walk aboard the Resolute in four days and dissolve into the world's general indifference, and no one in Vellamere would ever be able to say what had become of him, which was, by the old catechism, the highest achievement available to a man in his trade.

*Never write. Never linger. Never be remembered.*

He recited it the way he always did, silently, walking, the syllables keeping time with his boots on the frozen cobbles. And heard, as he had heard increasingly these past weeks, how much of it was liturgy and how little was law. A catechism, Odile had called it once, watching him check the vestry window latch for the third time. *You say it like prayers, Rosalind. Prayers aren't rules. They're wishes.*

The chapel was cold when he let himself in, and Odile was already at the trestle table with her shawl over her shoulders and two candles burning, which was extravagant by their economy and meant she had news. She looked up, and he saw before she spoke that the news was bad, or enormous, or both — her face did the same thing for each.

"The proclamation," she said.

"I've seen it. Four days."

"Three and a half. The tide comes early on the twenty-third; the harbormaster told me before they dismissed him. And there's more." She drew a paper from beneath her ledger-reconstruction — the working copy, the one they burned page by page as it was superseded — and smoothed it flat. "Tobias copied this from the notice board an hour ago. The approved register posts tomorrow morning. Not in three days. Tomorrow." Her finger tapped the line. "*Posted three days prior to sailing,* it says, but Verel amended it this afternoon. The list goes up tomorrow, and anyone not on it has until the tide to present themselves for review before Captain Verel personally. Present themselves, Rosalind. Voluntarily. With their papers."

"To be catalogued."

"To be catalogued, questioned, and quite possibly detained pending review — which is what happened to the Lammermoor family when they tried it in Harrowgate, according to a fisherman who came through last month. You don't stand in front of Verel unless you're already safe, and nobody in this town is already safe." She sat back, and the candlelight moved on her face, and he saw how tired she was — tired in the marrow, the way the whole town was tired, the way a wall is tired that has taken water into its mortar all winter and is waiting, without complaint, to come down. "So. Tomorrow our names — yours, mine, Marta's, Tobias's, and every soul in the hundred and six — become visible on a public board, and the army begins matching faces to lists. After tomorrow, every trip we make across this town is a wager."

Rosalind put the passage papers on the table between them.

Odile looked at them a long time. Then at him.

"Ashe's," she said. It wasn't a question either. She seemed to deal mostly in statements that wanted no answer; it was one of the things about her that had rearranged something in him over these weeks, the way she asked nothing and noticed everything.

"One berth. After hold. Warmest place on the ship."

"There'll be others. He wouldn't waste one on you alone."

"He offered it to buy me stopping. I haven't decided whether I've accepted."

"You've decided." She said it without heat, the way she reconciled figures — this column does not balance with that one, therefore one of them is wrong. "You brought them to show me, which means you'd already decided not to use them, because a man who means to leave doesn't spend his last evening explaining himself to a dismissed archivist in a freezing chapel."

Rosalind sat down across from her. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the door he hadn't fully shut, steadied, leaned again. Out in the churchyard the wind moved through the bare elms with a sound like a crowd remembering something.

"Forty-three names with thin testimony," he said. "One hundred and six unreconciled. Marta can dictate perhaps two hours a night before the coughing takes her. Tobias can't run messages in daylight anymore — there are patrols on the ropewalk now. And we've got three and a half nights, minus sleep, minus error, minus the simple fact that my hands—" he held one up, and it was steady, mostly, in the way of a rope that holds only because everything pulling it holds— "my hands shake after midnight, and shaking hands smear oiled silk worse than paper."

"So we don't subtract," Odile said. "We allocate." And she pulled the working copy toward her, and uncapped the ink, and drew a grid on the back of a burned page's replacement with the swift economy of a woman who had spent thirty years making chaos legible. "Marta knows sixty-one of the hundred and six personally — she told me yesterday she danced at half their weddings and buried a third of them, which makes her testimony worth more than any parish book. We take her in strict order: the forty-three thin ones first, because those are the names Corvain will strike hardest. Tobias carries questions to the households we can't visit and brings back answers — he's invisible in the way boys are, nobody interrogates a boy carrying his mother's marketing basket. I reconcile. You write." She looked up. "And on the third night, we begin the silk."

"The silk takes a full night by itself. The transfer, the sewing—"

"Two nights, then. We'll make two into one, the way we've made everything else." She wrote the numbers down, small and certain, and for a moment the grid on the burned page looked less like a plan than like a fortification — walls laid out against a siege, drawn by someone who knew exactly how long the food would last. "There's one thing more," she said, quieter now. "The register posts tomorrow. Our names on a board, in the open, where Tobias's mother walks every morning to draw rations." She met his eyes. "If Tobias Renk sees what I suspect is on that list — that his own confession bought his mother a place on it, or cost her one — we may lose him. Or worse. We should decide tonight what we tell him."

Rosalind looked at the candle flames, at the grid, at the folded papers with their flawless seal sitting on the scarred wood like a coin someone had left in payment for a debt far larger than coins measure.

"We tell him the truth," he said. "Whatever's on that list. He traded names once because he was frightened and his mother was dying, and we gave him mercy instead of exposure, and I am not going to repay that by deciding what he can bear. He's earned the truth the hard way. Most of us earn it the easy way and never notice."

Odile studied him for a moment, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a small thing, quickly gone, but real, the first he had seen from her since the fleet burned.

"You've changed your catechism," she said.

"No," Rosalind said, reaching for the pen, feeling the familiar tremor start in his fingers and letting it start, because the writing mattered more than the steadiness now. "I've started writing a new one."

Outside, the wind rose, and somewhere down toward the harbor a bell rang the hour — five bells, ninety-one hours until the tide — and in the vestry of a frozen chapel, three names that were not yet on any approved list bent together over the names of a hundred and six, while across the sleeping town, in the requisitioned customs house, a single lamp still burned above Maître Corvain's desk, where the provisional register lay finished under a paperweight, waiting only for dawn to make its crossings law.
