## 17. Four Nights

The first night began with a candle stub and ended with blood.

Rosalind had not known his hands could fail him. Seven years of carrying nothing heavier than a ledger's worth of silence in his skull, seven years of keeping them folded, idle, unremembered—and now they shook over the vestry table like an old man's, refusing the first stroke of the pen as though the ink itself were something they'd forgotten how to touch.

*Begin,* Odile said, or did not say—she only set the page before him square to the table's edge, the way she must once have squared ledgers for a living, and waited. Marta Quillon sat with her hands knotted in her lap, her widow's black gone grey at the seams, and looked at the blank paper the way other people look at open graves.

"Alaric Quillon," she said. "A-l-a-r-i-c. The ledger spells it Alarick, with the k, because the man who wrote it never listened when my husband spelled his own name. He was a cooper. He made barrels for the salt trade. He died on the fourteenth of the Frost Month, at the harbor gate, with a fish knife in his hand, because that is what was near, because that is what he had." Her voice did not waver. It was a voice that had been rehearsing itself alone in a cold house for two years. "The ledger says collaborator. I am telling you what it should say."

Rosalind wrote it. The letters came out crooked, half-forgotten, like a language spoken in childhood. *He was a cooper.* He had not written a true sentence in seven years and the truth came out of his pen ugly and trembling and alive.

By midnight the candle was a puddle and his first page held eleven names, each one dictated, argued over, checked against Odile's memory of figures and Marta's memory of faces. When Marta finally rose to go—Tobias Renk waiting in the doorway to walk her home through the frozen lanes—she paused beside Rosalind's chair.

"You write like a man praying," she said. "Don't stop."

Then she was gone, and the vestry was cold, and Rosalind sat looking down at his own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger he was only beginning to forgive.

* * *

The second night, Tobias brought the town with him.

The boy came in smelling of woodsmoke and river ice, cheeks cracked red from running, and emptied his pockets onto the table: a scrap of sail twine, a button, a twist of paper, and a child's slate no bigger than a psalter, on which he had written in chalk, in a hand that lurched but did not hesitate: *Mme. Aubert remembers her brother's debt was paid in full, autumn before last, 40 crowns, witness the priest at Saint-Veran.*

"You told her," Rosalind said. Not accusing. Astonished.

"I told her I was collecting memories for my mother," Tobias said. "It's even true. She likes talking about before. Everyone does, if you let them start." He shrugged, fourteen years old and already fluent in the grammar of survival. "People don't remember what you ask for. They remember what they're sad about. You just have to stand still long enough."

Odile took the slate and read it twice, and Rosalind watched something move across her face—the expression of a clerk encountering a figure that reconciles. "Forty crowns," she murmured. "That matches the margin note in the third register, the one Corvain's men didn't take because it was misfiled under tallow shipments." She looked up, and for the first time since Rosalind had known her there was fire behind the stubbornness instead of only iron. "We can check memory against memory against *paper*, Rosalind. Not just against each other. There are fragments everywhere. Misfiled. Overlooked. In attics. In hymnals. This town counted everything for forty years; nobody counted the counters."

So the second night became a net. Tobias ran messages by day and by night, from the widow Aubert to the old pilot Gorse to the sacristan who kept baptismal records in his head because his eyes had failed; and what came back were scraps, dates, sums, grudges, funeral bread debts, and every one of them a thread. Rosalind sat at the table and wrote until the candle drowned in its own grease, and lit another, and wrote. His fingers had stopped shaking somewhere past midnight. They had begun instead to ache, a deep hot ache in the joints, and he welcomed it. Pain was proof of use. For seven years these hands had been kept like unloaded weapons; now they were doing the one thing they had always been shaped for.

Near dawn, copying out the testimony of the pilot Gorse—who had seen Ives taken, who had seen the wagon, who had seen the road it went down—Rosalind's pen slipped and tore the page, and he stared at the ruin of it and found he could not make himself throw it away. He smoothed it flat instead, and copied the torn passage carefully around the wound in the paper, and thought: *Ives would laugh at this. A record with scars in it.*

He wrote until his middle finger split along the crease where the pen pressed, and the blood came thin and dark and mixed with the ink so that he could not tell which was which, and he decided that this too was honest, and kept writing.

* * *

The third night, they fought.

It began small—everything in Vellamere that winter began small, damp working through walls—and it was about a sum. Odile insisted that the grain levy of two winters past had been twelve hundred sacks; Rosalind's reconstruction of the requisition order said nine hundred.

"Twelve hundred came through the customs gate," she said, flat as a plank. "I entered them. My own hand."

"And nine hundred were recorded as delivered to the garrison stores. Which means three hundred went *somewhere*, and if we write the number we cannot prove, Corvain will find the gap and use the gap to discredit everything around it."

"So we write what is true and let him choke on it."

"We write what is *provable*—" Rosalind heard his voice climb, heard seven years of discipline cracking along its seam, and hated himself for it, and could not stop. "Odile, one soft number and he unravels us. He will take the weakest thread and pull until the whole cloth—"

"The whole cloth?" She stood up then, and the candlelight made her shadow enormous against the vestry wall, taller than any soldier. "You speak to me of cloth. I kept that ledger for nineteen years. Nineteen years of watching clerks fudge and magistrates round down and merchants bribe, and I wrote the *true* figure every time, in my own hand, knowing it would cost me, because a record that bends is not a record—it is a rumor with good posture. If we put nine hundred in this book because nine hundred is safe, then we have built Corvain's house with our own timber, and I will not sign it."

Silence. Somewhere above them, wind worried the chapel roof, and a loose slate ticked like a clock.

Rosalind looked down at the disputed line. And understood—not suddenly, but the way ice understands spring—that she was right, and that she had been right all along, and that this was the thing he had spent seven years failing to learn: that a record kept safe was worthless, and a record kept *true* was worth anything, including them.

"We write twelve hundred," he said. "And we footnote the discrepancy, and we name the storekeeper who signed for nine hundred, and let the reader see the three hundred missing. We show the wound. Like the torn page."

Odile sat back down. She did not smile. But she pulled the page toward herself and wrote the figure in her own hand, larger than the rest, defiant, and beneath it added: *Entered by O. Ferrant, who counted them.*

They worked until the fourth bell. When Rosalind finally blew out the candle, his finger had soaked its rag wrapping clean through, and Marta's dictation lay finished in fair copy, and Tobias slept sitting up in the corner with his mother's medicine bottle cradled in both hands like something rescued from a fire, and Rosalind thought: *this is what communion is. Not bread. Not wine. Four people and a lie's opposite, kept warm together through the night.*

* * *

The fourth night was for the silk.

Odile produced it from the bottom of her market basket, folded around dried figs: oiled silk, sailmaker's grade, pale as skin and slick as ice, bought—she would not say how, and Rosalind did not ask—from the loft of the chandlery where the requisitioned canvas was stored for the evacuation ship's repairs. Three yards of it. Enough.

They worked by two candles now, burning at both ends of the table like a ship's watch changed mid-passage. The fair copy lay complete beside them: sixty-one pages, two hundred and six names restored or defended, each with its witnesses, its sums, its wounds shown honestly. Marta had gone home to sleep at last, truly sleep, after dictating the final entry—a list of the harbor-gate dead, nine names, read aloud slowly while Rosalind's bleeding finger left a faint rust-colored ghost on the margin, a stain he chose not to scrape away. Now it was only the three of them and the silk.

Ink would not hold on oilcloth. They had learned this the night before, wasting an hour and a page on experiments, and so they used what Odile called the sailmaker's trick: a blunt stylus, a bone folder from her old desk, pressed hard enough to score the silk, then rubbed with lampblack mixed into tallow. The letters came up grey and permanent, readable by touch, readable by candlelight held at an angle, invisible to anyone who did not know to look. Writing on silk was slow as carving. It did not matter. They had calculated the hours, and the hours were enough, barely, if no one slept more than two at a time, and if nothing went wrong.

Nothing went wrong. That was the miracle none of them named aloud. Tobias came and went with his pockets full of the town's remembered griefs and glories; Odile reconciled, entry against entry, her lips moving silently over figures like a woman at prayer in a language only clerks spoke; and Rosalind scored the silk, letter by letter, name by name, until his shoulders burned and his eyes swam and the bone folder wore a groove into his thumb that he suspected would outlive the winter.

Somewhere past the deepest hour, copying the entry for Alaric Quillon—*cooper; died resisting at the harbor gate; falsely recorded*—his hand simply stopped. Not cramped. Stopped. He sat looking at the half-scored name, and Odile looked up, and neither spoke, because both of them were thinking of Ives, whose name stood finished three lines above, restored at last with the fragment Odile had found: *died in custody at Carrec; gave up nothing; witnessed by the pilot Gorse.*

Seven years Rosalind had carried that silence like a stone sewn into his coat lining, heavier than any dispatch. Tonight the stone came out. It did not feel like lightness. It felt like the ache of a limb remembering circulation.

"He's in there," Rosalind said quietly. "Whatever else happens. He's in there now."

"He was always in there," Odile said. "You were just the only one who knew. That's the cruelest thing a record can do to a person—keep them without letting anyone read them." She bent again to her figures. After a moment she added, not looking up: "When this sails, he gets read."

They finished as the sky behind the east windows turned the grey of dirty pewter. Two hundred and six names on three yards of silk, rolled tight as a wick, wrapped in a strip of plain linen, and sewn—Odile's needle, Rosalind's bleeding hands holding the cloth steady—into a length of sailcloth patch, ordinary, salt-stiffened, indistinguishable from a hundred others bound for the chandlery loft and the repair of the ship that would carry the garrison out on the morning tide after next.

Tobias held the finished patch up to the greying window, turning it, squinting, testing it against everything he knew of searching soldiers' eyes. Then he nodded once, tucked it inside his coat, and was gone into the blue dark, footsteps crunching away toward the harbor.

Rosalind stood alone in the vestry among the candle smoke and the scattered fair-copy pages, and flexed his ruined hands, and felt the strangest thing: not relief, not fear, but a kind of terrible clarity, as if the four nights had been a long walk up a hill and he had only now reached the top and could see the whole country at once.

The copy was ready. The copy would sail.

And standing there in the cold, watching the tide-light strengthen in the east, Rosalind understood—with the same calm that had come over him at the harbor gate stories, at the moment of the fish knife—that a copy smuggled away in rigging was a message in a bottle, and bottles do not testify. The names would be saved, perhaps, someday, somewhere. But here, in this town, under Corvain's official hand, the lie would stand unchallenged, and Odile would face her hearing alone, and every erased name would stay erased for everyone who remained.

There was one record left unwritten. It could not be sewn into silk. It could only be spoken, aloud, in front of the historian himself—and signed.

Down by the harbor, faint on the frozen air, came the sound of the evacuation ship's bells being tested for the morning tide after next.
