## 1. What Can Be Carried

The letters came to Freya Crane in two stacks, and he read both.

This was not a habit anyone had given him permission for. It was simply the way the work had grown, the way a path grows across a field — by being walked. Edith Marlowe wrote her correspondence at the long table under the window of the house on Quay Street, and when she finished a letter she would hold it out without looking up, and Freya would take it, read it front to back, fold it along its own creases, seal it, and carry it wherever it needed to go. She had never once asked him to do this. She had also never once asked him not to.

He understood why she allowed it. A woman who could not vote had to trust someone to carry her words through a town where words were currency and counterfeiting was a civic pastime. Freya was twenty-six, quiet, good with horses, better with locks, and best of all with paper. He could read a letter in the time it took him to walk from the table to the door, and he did, every time, standing in the hall with his hat already on, his eyes moving down the page while Edith's pen scratched on behind him like a small persistent insect.

*Dear Mrs. Aldous,* today's first letter began, *we are grateful for your subscription of one pound, and we will be gratefuler still if you can persuade your husband that the cause is not a disease but a cure.* Freya almost smiled. Edith wrote the way she spoke, which was to say the way she threw things: accurately and with follow-through.

He sealed it. He read the second — an appeal to a shipping merchant named Corliss for the use of a hall on the fourteenth — sealed it too, and went out into the morning.

* * *

Harbor Town, though nobody called it that except people who had never lived anywhere else, was built on the idea that everything could be weighed. The herring came in by the ton. The timber came in by the cord. The coal came by the barge-load, and the men who unloaded it were paid by the basket, and the baskets were counted twice because the tally-clerks trusted no one, least of all themselves. Even the fog seemed priced here, sold off in damp sheets to whoever would take it.

Freya had learned early that most things worth having fit in two hands. His father, a rigger who fell from the *Maiden's Reward* when Freya was nine, had left behind a sea chest, three coils of rope, and a saying: *A man can carry what a man can carry, and the rest is somebody else's luggage.* It was not a profound philosophy. It had kept the family fed for eleven years anyway.

What Freya carried now, on this particular morning, was: two letters, a parcel of pamphlets wrapped in brown paper and labeled as hat patterns, a borrowed cart-horse named Duchess who believed all hurry was a moral failing, and — inside his coat, against his ribs — a ledger.

The ledger was leather-bound, soft-cornered, smaller than a hymnal. He had bought it four years ago from a stationer's clearance bin for threepence, and it had become, without his quite deciding, the true record of the harbor. Not the customs house version, with its official columns and its official lies, but the other account: who owed what to whom, who had promised what, which debts ran in money and which ran in the coin that actually bought things.

He entered names in the evenings, by lamplight, in a hand so small and even that Edith had once remarked it looked less like writing than like knitting. *Mrs. Okafor, midwife — delivered the Vane brothers' mother, never paid, will not ask; owed a favor of any kind.* *Anton Weiss, shipwright — lent tools to repair Duchess's harness, refused payment; owed passage, protection, or work.* *Constable Denny — looked away from the pamphlet cart on the tenth of March; owed silence about his sister's situation, which is none of my business and therefore priceless.*

That last entry was the closest Freya ever came to a joke on paper.

He did not think of himself as keeping accounts out of sentiment. Sentiment was ballast you hadn't asked aboard. But there was a satisfaction in it that ran deeper than sentiment — the satisfaction of a man who counts the nails before building. In a town where every institution kept its books in ink that could be erased by whoever held the pen, Freya kept his in pencil, and kept the pencil himself.

* * *

The first delivery took him along the fish quay, where the morning catch lay silvering in boxes and the gulls conducted their usual parliament overhead. He left Mrs. Aldous's letter with a girl at a gate, watched the girl run it inside, and noted — automatically, the way other men noted the weather — that the Aldouses' hedge needed cutting and their gate hinge wanted oil, which meant money was tighter than Mrs. Aldous's generous pound suggested. He filed it away. Everything got filed away. It was the one filing system in Harbor Town that never lost anything.

At Corliss Shipping he found trouble waiting, as trouble usually was, in the shape of a man behind a desk.

"Miss Marlowe wants my hall." Corliss said it the way other men announced a death in the family. He was a wide, careful man with hands like mooring posts, and he turned Freya's letter over as though the back might contain an escape clause.

"Fourteenth," Freya said. "Evening. She'll pay the lamp-oil herself."

"She'll fill my hall with women asking for the vote, and the Admiralty has an office two streets over."

"The Admiralty," Freya said, "has never once noticed what happens two streets over."

Corliss looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, short and unwilling, the laugh of a man conceding a point in a game he'd rather not be playing. "Tell her she can have it. Tell her if any window gets broken, I'm charging the movement double."

"I'll tell her you said windows break themselves around causes."

"Tell her nothing of the sort. Go on, get out."

Freya went. In the doorway he paused, because Anton Weiss was coming up the street from the shipyard with sawdust on his sleeves and his daughter Greta walking beside him with the gravity of a much older person. Greta was twelve and counted everything — steps, gulls, soldiers, days — and she counted them aloud sometimes in a voice like a small clerk's.

"Morning, Herr Weiss," Freya said.

"Anton." The shipwright corrected him every time, patiently, as though the honorific were a splinter. "You still driving for the lady?"

"Still driving."

"And she still thinks England will listen to reason?"

"She thinks England will listen to anything said loudly enough and often enough."

Anton smiled. He was a big man gone quietly grey, German-born thirty years since, with hands that could fair a hull by touch alone, and he carried his foreignness the way he carried his tools — openly, usefully, without apology. Half the boats in the harbor had his fingerprints somewhere in their bones. Nobody thought of him as anything but the best shipwright on the river.

Nobody, Freya would remember later, thought about it at all. That was the thing about peacetime. It let you believe that what everyone ignored was the same as what everyone accepted.

"Come by the yard sometime," Anton said. "There's a ketch going together in the loft. Good lines. You'd like her."

"I'd like her," Freya agreed, and meant it, and put it in the ledger that night the way he put everything: *A. Weiss — offered to show me the ketch in the loft. A kindness. Owed.*

He was, he would think much later, always writing debts down. He just never imagined he'd have to pay them all at once.

* * *

The afternoon's deliveries took him out past the gasworks to the terraced streets where the suffragist money actually lived — small sums from small houses, half-crowns pressed into envelopes by women who heated one room in winter to afford the subscription. Freya carried receipts back to them personally because Edith insisted every penny be witnessed, and because he liked seeing the rooms where the movement was actually made: kitchens, mostly, with a pamphlet hidden under the bread tin.

At number eleven Corder Street a woman named Ruth Pell gave him tea he didn't want and news he did.

"They're saying Serbia's answered the ultimatum," she said, pouring. "My brother's wife has cousins in Vienna. There's talk of mobilization."

"People talk about mobilization every summer," Freya said. "It's the continental hobby. Like bullfighting, only with paperwork."

"My brother-in-law says this time is different."

"Your brother-in-law sells funeral insurance. Differentness is his trade."

But he drank the tea, and he thanked her, and riding home through the long gold light he found himself doing something he despised in other men: listening to the harbor. Not its sounds — its silences. And the silences were wrong. Too many men at the quay wall, not enough at work. Too many knots of conversation that opened and closed like fists when he passed.

Duchess, indifferent to history as always, stopped twice to consider fences.

* * *

Edith Marlowe met the evening news standing up, because she met everything standing up.

Freya brought it to her himself, having heard it confirmed at the telegraph office by a boy whose hands were shaking too badly to make change. He came into the house on Quay Street without knocking, which he never did, and Edith knew at once. She was at her table, pen moving, and she set the pen down with the deliberation of a woman putting a weapon within reach rather than away.

"Well," she said.

"Austria's declared war on Serbia," Freya said. "Russia's mobilizing. The wire office has a crowd outside it and the crowd isn't talking."

For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere out on the water a ship's bell rang the hour, unhurried, ignorant, faithful.

"If Germany comes in," Edith said at last, "and Russia stands with Serbia, then France—" She stopped herself, the way she stopped herself from very few sentences. "Then everything after that is arithmetic."

"It's not our arithmetic."

"Everything," Edith said quietly, "is our arithmetic. That's rather the whole argument of my life."

She stood. She was fifty-one years old and built like a question nobody wanted asked in polite company, and she crossed to the window where the harbor showed itself between the rooftops — masts, cranes, smoke, the whole patient machinery of a town that lived by moving things.

"There will be requisition orders," she said. "There will be lists. There will be men with forms who have never done an honest day's weighing in their lives, telling us who we are." She turned. "And every woman in this town who can't vote will find out exactly how much her opinion weighs, which is nothing, officially, and everything, actually. We should have finished the job while there was peace to finish it in."

"You'll keep speaking."

"I'll keep speaking until they stop me, and then I'll speak from whatever they stop me in." She picked the pen back up. "Take the evening post. All of it. Tonight."

It was seven letters. Freya read every one in the hallway, standing in his hat, while downstairs Edith began writing the speech she would give on the fourteenth if there was a fourteenth, if there was a hall, if there was an England to give speeches in.

The last letter was a short one, to a printer, confirming the placards. *THE WOMAN'S ARGUMENT IS THE PEOPLE'S ARGUMENT,* it said. Freya read it twice, folded it along its creases, sealed it, and put it in his coat against the ledger, against his ribs, where all the important cargo rode.

Outside, the lamps were being lit along the quay, one by one, by a man on a ladder who worked the same way every night of his life and would work that way tomorrow, and the day after, unless the world ended first. Down at the yards, late though it was, lights still burned in the shipwrights' lofts. Men finishing boats. Men believing in keels and caulking and the spring season, in next year as a place you could actually sail to.

Freya walked home the long way, along the water, and took out the ledger, and by the light of the last lamp added the day's final line:

*War declared east of us. Nothing yet ours. Watch the lists.*

He did not know, closing the book, how soon the watching would begin, or how many names he would write before winter, or that some of those names would need to be carried farther than any road in England reached.

Two days later, Germany declared war on France, and the harbor woke to find the twentieth century standing on its doorstep with a requisition form in one hand and a list in the other.
