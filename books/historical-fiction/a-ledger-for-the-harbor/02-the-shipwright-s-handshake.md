## 2. The Shipwright's Handshake

The letter in my coat pocket smelled of cedar shavings and pipe smoke, which is how I knew it was from Anton Weiss before I read a word of it. I had delivered enough of his correspondence over three years to know the man by his envelopes — always sealed with too much wax, always addressed in that careful shipwright's hand, each letter formed like it was being fitted to a joint.

But this chapter begins earlier, and I find I can't tell it any other way. Memory doesn't file itself in order. It files itself by weight, and some things weigh more now than they did when they happened.

* * *

Three years ago, then. The summer of 1911, when the harbor still belonged to itself and not to His Majesty's Army, and the biggest question on the quay was whether the herring run would come early.

I was twenty-four and driving for Edith Marlowe four days a week, which meant I spent a good deal of time waiting. Edith spoke long. Committees ran longer. And a man who waits learns every corner of a town the way water learns a basin — thoroughly, and without anyone noticing.

So I waited at the shipyard.

It wasn't sentiment, not at first. The yard had benches in the sun and a clear view down to the water, and Anton Weiss never once asked me why I was there or what I was carrying or who I carried it for. He'd nod, go on with his work, and after an hour or so he'd sit down beside me with two cups of coffee so thick you could have caulked a seam with it, and we'd say nothing for a while, and it was the best conversation I had most weeks.

He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like mooring lines and hands that seemed too fine for their size until you watched them work. German-born, came over as a boy of nine with his father, who had laid the keel of half the fishing fleet before his heart quit him on a Tuesday. Anton inherited the yard the way you inherit a debt and a love in the same envelope — no choice about either, and no wish to be rid of either.

His daughter Greta was seven that summer, all elbows and questions, forever underfoot among the wood curls. She'd interrogate me while her father planed. Where do the letters go? Do you know everybody's name? Is your car older than Papa's boat?

"Older than most things," I told her.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate and decided I was acceptable. That was Greta. She audited everyone and issued few licenses.

* * *

The day of the handshake was hot enough that the pitch in the seams went soft and the whole yard smelled like a tarred rope left in the sun. Anton was fitting a garboard plank on the *Kestrel*, one of the Vanes' boats, though I didn't know the Vane brothers then except as names men said quietly, the way you say the name of weather coming.

He called me over to hold the other end of the plank. No asking whether I knew anything about boats. He just handed me the wood and said, "Hold. Don't push. Wood tells you where it wants to go; your job is to disagree politely."

I held. We disagreed politely for forty minutes. When the plank sat true and he drove the last fastening, he stood back, wiped his hands on his apron, and looked at me with an expression I'd learn later to read as satisfaction rationed out carefully, because a shipwright who praises his own work invites the sea to contradict him.

"You've got patience," he said. "That's rarer than skill."

"My mother would be astonished to hear it."

"Where is she, your mother?"

"Gone eight years." I didn't elaborate. He didn't ask. That was another thing about Anton — he understood that some cargo you don't open unless the owner asks you to.

We sat with our coffee. Greta was asleep in the loft above the shed, curled in a hammock of sacking between two ribs of an unfinished dinghy, and the light came through the gaps in the boards and striped her like she was part of the timber.

Then Anton said, without preamble, the way he started everything: "My father told me something once. He said, in this country, a man should keep his papers dry and his friends closer than his papers."

"All right," I said.

"I'm telling you what my father told me. You don't have to make sense of it yet." He turned his cup in his hands. "I came here at nine years old. I have built forty-one boats in this harbor. I know every draft mark on every pier from here to the bend. And every few years somebody reminds me — kindly, usually, which is somehow worse — that I was born on the wrong side of a line drawn through a field in Prussia."

I remember thinking it was the heat talking. I remember thinking a great many convenient things, because I was twenty-four and peacetime makes philosophers lazy.

"So if things ever turn sour here," Anton went on, "if it ever comes to lists and papers and men counting who belongs — I'll need to move fast. Faster than a man can arrange honestly. Do you understand me?"

I did understand him. Understanding is cheap. What he wanted next cost more, and I gave it the way young men give things — lightly, with both hands, without weighing it.

"If it ever comes to that," I said, "you come to me. I drive for Edith Marlowe. I know roads the army doesn't, and I know people who owe me favors in three counties. You and Greta get to me, and I'll get you north. Passage, protection, whatever it takes. My word on it."

And we shook on it. That was the whole ceremony — a handshake in a hot loft smelling of pitch, witnessed by a sleeping child and forty-one boats' worth of sawdust.

Anton held the grip a moment longer than the words required, and something crossed his face — relief, maybe, or the look of a man who has checked a lifeboat's davits and found them sound but hopes never to need them. Then he let go and said, "Good. Now hand me that batten, we're not done disagreeing with this plank."

That was it. That was the promise entire. Made in an afternoon, filed away under *someday*, and I walked back to Edith's that evening feeling lighter, the way you feel after giving directions to a stranger — pleased with yourself for nothing much.

* * *

Here is what I didn't weigh that day, because I measured everything in those days by what fit in two hands and a handshake fits easily: promises are cargo too. And cargo has weight that changes with the weather.

A promise made in peacetime is ballast. It sits low in the hold, invisible, doing its quiet work keeping you steady. You forget it's there. But when the storm comes — and I write this now with the war eleven days old and the harbor already filling with grey uniforms — ballast is the first thing you feel, dragging at you, and the last thing you can afford to throw overboard.

Eleven days since the news came up from the telegraph office. Eleven days since the boy ran along the quay shouting it and the town stopped mid-stride, all of us together, like one animal hearing a sound in the grass. Eleven days, and already the word *enemy* has begun its work in men's mouths — spoken softly at first, apologetically, the way you test ice with one foot.

This morning I delivered a letter to Anton from his brother-in-law in Hamburg. I read it before sealing it, as I read everything — that habit Edith calls shameful and I call honest. It was family news, a christening, a price for pig iron. Nothing. Everything. Because the censor's stamp will come for letters like that soon, and men like Anton will be the reason given.

When I handed it to him at the yard gate, he took it and looked past me at the harbor, where a naval ensign flew from the customs house that had flown nothing but trade flags for sixty years.

"Well," he said. "Sour."

One word. He'd remembered the exact shape of our old conversation, and he'd chosen the exact word from it, and he handed me back my own promise across that gate like returning a tool borrowed three summers ago.

I told him it wouldn't come to lists. I heard myself say it and heard how it sounded — a man whistling past a graveyard of his own making.

Greta came running up then, ten years old now, taller, still auditing. "Is it true there's to be soldiers?"

"There are always soldiers somewhere," I said.

"Papa says this time it's near."

Her father put his hand on her head, and I watched the gesture carry more weight than any handshake ever made.

I drove back along the river road with the light going copper on the water, and I could feel it — the ballast of that afternoon three years ago shifting in my hold, settling, becoming real. A promise is a small thing until the world conspires to collect it.

Tomorrow Edith speaks at the rally on the square. She asked me tonight, in her office that smells of ink and stubbornness, whether I thought the crowd would come.

"Crowds are weather," I told her.

"And what are you, Freya?"

"A man with a full ledger," I said, "and a growing sense that some entries are about to come due."
