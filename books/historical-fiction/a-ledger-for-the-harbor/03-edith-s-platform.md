## 3. Edith's Platform

Edith Marlowe had learned to read a crowd the way sailors read weather, and the crowd assembling in Harbor Square that Saturday was reading like a falling barometer.

She stood at the edge of the square with her back to the bandstand, watching them come in. Not enough of them. That was the first thing. A year ago she could count on two hundred faces for a spring rally; today, twenty minutes before she was due to speak, she counted perhaps eighty, and half of those were the faithful — the mill girls from Calloway's, the two schoolmistresses who never missed a meeting, old Mrs. Prentiss with her banner rolled under one arm like an umbrella.

The other half were the curious, and the curious could be won or lost in the space of a single sentence.

"They'll come," said Ruth Calloway, arriving at her elbow with a stack of handbills she would not need. Ruth was sixty and had been organizing since before Edith was born, and she delivered reassurance the way other people delivered bad news — flatly, without believing it.

"Some of them will." Edith smoothed the front of her coat. "The rest are at the recruiting table by the ferry office."

"Let them be. Boys sign up to march and shoot; we're asking them to think. Thinking has always been the harder sale."

"It isn't the boys I'm worried about." Edith nodded across the square, where three men in army uniforms stood outside the customs house, talking easily, not looking at the bandstand at all — which was itself a kind of looking. "It's the men with forms."

Ruth followed her glance. "Rumors," she said. "Half the town says there's going to be requisition orders, half says it's just inventory for the war effort. Warehouses, records, that sort of thing."

"And when they requisition the warehouses, will they requisition the women who work in them?"

"They'll requisition whatever's written down." Ruth smiled without warmth. "Which is why I've stopped writing things down myself."

Edith laughed despite herself. It was one of the small ironies of their movement that its most careful organizer had become, in middle age, deliberately careless on paper. There were names in this town that existed nowhere but in Ruth's memory, and Edith suspected that by winter that would be true of half the harbor.

Freya Crane came across the square with his collar up against the river wind, carrying her lectern notes in the leather satchel he always carried. He moved through a crowd the way water moves through a hull — quietly, filling every low place, noticed only when he was gone. She had hired him eighteen months ago because he drove well and asked no questions, and had kept him because he read everything she gave him faster than she did and remembered it better.

"Mrs. Marlowe." He handed up the satchel. "There's a man from the *Gazette* by the fountain. He'll take notes if you say anything quotable, so decide now whether you want to be quoted."

"I always want to be quoted. It's the misquoting I object to."

"Then you may want to soften the part about the government being a boarding house run by lodgers who refuse to pay rent."

Edith looked at him. He had the particular stillness of a man who has already read the speech and found the dangerous parts. "You've been reading my notes again."

"You leave them on the seat of the motorcar. I drive; I get bored at red lamps."

"If the *Gazette* prints that line, Mr. Crane, I will tell them my driver wrote it."

"He'd deny it. He can't afford sedition any more than you can." Freya glanced once, briefly, toward the uniforms by the customs house — the same glance everyone in the square kept making and pretending not to — and then back to her. "Crowd's thin."

"Crowd's thin," Edith agreed.

"War does that." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said everything, and then he touched the brim of his cap and went to stand at the edge of things, where he always stood, where she had privately begun to think of as *his* ground — near the exits, near the shadows, near whoever might need help leaving quickly.

* * *

She took the platform at two o'clock precisely, because precision was a weapon and she intended to use every one she owned.

The bandstand smelled of fresh paint — someone had painted it gray, military gray, within the last week. The bunting that usually draped the railing had been taken down. In its place hung a single flag, and Edith noted, with a clarity that felt almost like cruelty, how much easier it was to speak beneath a flag than beneath a banner reading VOTES FOR WOMEN, and how much she resented that ease.

"My friends," she began, and waited for the shuffling to stop. It did not entirely stop. Somewhere near the fountain a child was crying, and somewhere near the recruiting table — she could hear it even here — a drummer was practicing a march rhythm, patient as a clock.

"We gather today to speak of citizenship." Her voice carried well; she had trained it in drafty halls against indifferent acoustics and hostile hecklers, and a little wind off the river was nothing. "For fifty years the women of this country have asked a question so simple that children understand it before they understand arithmetic: if I am governed, why may I not govern? If I pay, why may I not vote? If I work, why may I not be counted?"

Eighty faces. Maybe ninety; a few more had drifted in. Mrs. Prentiss unfurled her banner along the bandstand rail, and two of the mill girls helped her tie it, and for a moment the square looked almost like the squares of last spring, and Edith felt the old lift in her chest, the feeling that history was a tide and they were standing in it.

Then she saw the young man in the second row check his watch, look over his shoulder toward the drums, and begin, quietly, apologetically, to move away.

She watched him go. She did not stop speaking. But something in her registered it the way a captain registers the first list of a ship — not panic, just data. And once she had seen it, she saw it everywhere. A woman near the back gathered her children. Two clerks exchanged a look and drifted toward the ferry office. The crowd was thinning not in protest, not in boredom, but in the way a crowd thins when a larger procession is forming elsewhere — patriotism had put on its uniform and started marching, and reform, which wore plain clothes and asked questions, could not compete with drums.

Her fury arrived the way it always did: not hot but precise, a cold bright instrument. She had spent eleven years building audiences out of nothing — out of kitchen meetings and church basements, out of women who had to ask their husbands' permission to attend. She knew exactly what each face in this square had cost. And now the country had found a fire to stare at, and every hard-won gathering in England would gutter while everyone watched the flames.

*No*, she thought. *Not while I'm standing.*

"Last week," she continued, letting her voice drop so that the crowd leaned in — the oldest trick, and it still worked — "I received a letter from a woman in this very town. I will not give her name, because she asked me not to, and because giving it might cost her her position. She works fourteen hours a day during the packing season. She pays taxes on her own earnings. Last month she was told that the question of whether women should have the vote must wait until the present crisis is resolved."

She let that sit.

"Gentlemen — and ladies — I have news for you. The crisis is always present. There has not been a year since this country began when some crisis was not offered as the reason why justice must wait. War, famine, trade, plague, the price of coal, the health of the King — the excuse changes costume, but underneath it is always the same figure, saying the same thing: *not yet, not yet, not yet.*"

A murmur of agreement from the mill girls. One of the soldiers by the customs house turned his head, finally, and looked at the bandstand.

"Well," Edith said, "I am done waiting. And I will tell you something else. This morning I heard a man say that asking for the vote now was unpatriotic. Unpatriotic! As though patriotism were a silence. As though loving your country meant never telling it the truth." Her voice rose now, and she let it. "I love this country the way I loved my mother — fiercely, and without illusions, and with a firm intention of arguing with her until one of us dies."

That got a laugh — a real one, startled out of people — and she rode it.

"They tell us to hold our tongues for the duration. I ask you: the duration of what? Of the war? Wars end. But the duration of injustice has already lasted longer than any war in memory, and it will outlast this one unless we end it ourselves, here, in squares like this one, with voices like yours—"

"*Traitor!*"

The word came from the edge of the crowd, from a man in a dockworker's cap whom Edith did not know, red-faced, veins standing at his throat. "*My boy signed up Monday and you stand there running down the country he's fighting for!*"

The square went still in that peculiar way squares do — eighty people becoming one animal, holding its breath.

Edith had been heckled before. She had a whole repertoire. But she looked at this man — his hands, his fear dressed as anger, the photograph she would have bet anything was in his breast pocket — and she set the repertoire aside.

"Sir," she said, "what is your son's name?"

The question wrong-footed him. "...What?"

"His name. The boy who signed up Monday. Tell me his name, and I will carry it in my pocket every day this war lasts, and when the vote comes — and it *will* come — I will tell the House that your son fought for a country that finally counted his mother. What is his name?"

The man stared at her. His mouth worked. Around him, people had stopped drifting away; they had turned back, drawn by something no drum could make.

"...Albert," he said finally, grudgingly, as if surrendering ammunition. "Albert Finch."

"Albert Finch." Edith repeated it clearly, for the crowd, for the *Gazette* man scribbling by the fountain, for herself. "Then let me say this, Mr. Finch, and you may judge whether it is treason. If Albert can fight for this country, then Albert's mother has earned the right to speak for it. I do not want your son's sacrifice wasted on a country too cowardly to trust the women who raised him. That is my patriotism. You are free to hate it. But you will not call it silence."

For a long moment the man looked at her — and Edith saw the exact moment the anger lost its footing and something more complicated took its place. He didn't apologize. He pulled his cap down and shoved through the crowd and was gone. But three people who had been leaving came back, and a woman Edith didn't know took a handbill from Ruth's stack, and another took two.

*Fury braided with resolve,* she thought, absurdly, in the language of her own old diary. *Fine. Let it be rope. Rope holds.*

* * *

She spoke for forty minutes. When she finished, the applause was real but thinner than it should have been, and the crowd dispersed quickly, folding back into the streets like water into sand. Ruth began collecting banners. Freya appeared at the foot of the bandstand steps with her coat over his arm.

"The *Gazette* man wants a statement about the requisition rumors," Freya said.

"Does he. And what have you heard, driving all day through this town? You hear more than the *Gazette* does."

He hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Freya Crane did not hesitate, and so she noticed it the way she noticed the gray paint and the missing bunting.

"There are officers at the customs house with lists," he said quietly. "Long ones. They requisitioned the municipal records office this afternoon — every register, every roll, everything. The clerks weren't even allowed to keep copies."

"Records." Edith frowned, pulling on her coat. "Whatever for? What does a warehouse full of birth registers win anybody?"

"I don't know." He said it evenly. Too evenly. "But they went through the shipping manifests first, and they were asking specifically after men born in Germany. And Austria. Names, addresses, employers." He met her eyes. "Anton Weiss employs eleven men at his yard. Half the harbor was built by German-born shipwrights. If they start pulling men off the slips because of where they were born—"

"—then this town loses the very hands that built it." Edith finished, and felt the cold arrive under the fury, a new cold, civic and specific. "And loses something worse than hands. It learns that a neighbor's name can be turned into a charge."

Freya said nothing. But he shifted the satchel on his shoulder, and Edith saw — she was certain she saw — that his hand stayed on it a moment longer than it needed to, the way a man touches something he means to protect.

"Well," she said briskly, because despair was a luxury she had never been able to afford and did not intend to learn the price of now. "If they are keeping lists, Mr. Crane, then we shall simply have to become harder to write down. Come along. Mrs. Prentiss has the motorcar warmed, and I intend to be in Millbrook by six to give the same speech to a room full of women who haven't yet heard there's a war on."

They walked toward the car together, past the recruiting drums, past the customs house where the officers were carrying boxes now — actual boxes, sealed and stenciled, going into a van.

Edith did not look away from them. She wanted the image fixed: the records of a free town, carried off under army seals, in daylight, while everyone watched and no one objected.

*Someone should object*, she thought. *Someone always should.*

She did not yet know that the objection would cost her nearly everything she had. She only knew that the crowd had thinned, the ice of ordinary life had begun, very quietly, to crack — and that Albert Finch's name was in her pocket, and she intended to keep it there.
