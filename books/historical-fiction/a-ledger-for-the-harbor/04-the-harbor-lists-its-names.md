## 4. The Harbor Lists Its Names

The officers arrived on a Tuesday, which felt wrong somehow. Catastrophes in my experience came on Sundays or in the small hours, arriving like weather. This came on a Tuesday morning with the milk carts, two army lorries grinding up Harbor Street while the baker was still pulling his first loaves, and a lieutenant stepped down onto cobblestones still wet from the night's fog and asked a boy of perhaps ten where he might find the customs house.

The boy pointed. The lieutenant thanked him. That was the whole of it, and I think that politeness frightened people more than shouting would have. You can brace against cruelty. Courtesy gives you nothing to hold.

I had been delivering Edith's letters since six — three to the mill offices, one to the widow Prentiss on Rope Walk, one sealed and addressed to a man in the capital whose name I read twice because I would see it again, though I didn't yet know how. By eight I was back at the Marlowe house to collect the afternoon's bundle, and that was when we both heard the lorries, and Edith set down her pen mid-word and said, "There it is," the way you say *there's the storm* after days of watching the sky go green.

"There what is?" I asked, though I knew.

"The lists." She stood and went to the window. "Every country does it. I've read about it being done to us in other men's wars, and thought it barbaric, and here it comes on a Tuesday with the milk."

* * *

By noon there were forms. The forms were everywhere — pasted to the customs house door, stacked on trestle tables outside the post office, thrust into the hands of anyone who paused too long on the pavement. REGISTRATION OF PERSONS OF ENEMY ORIGIN, they said, in letters meant to be official and instead looking faintly apologetic, like a doctor's handwriting on bad news. Every resident born in enemy territory or descended from such residents was required to present themselves within fourteen days: name, birthplace, occupation, property, dependents, associations.

*Associations.* That word did more damage than all the others combined.

I stood at the trestle table for an hour that first day, ostensibly waiting for the post, actually watching. A sergeant sat behind the table with a ledger of his own — everyone keeps ledgers; mine is simply better bound — and beside him a corporal who stamped things. The line began short and grew long as word moved through the town the way word always moves through a harbor town, faster than any printed notice could carry it.

Herr Brandt the clockmaker came first, alone, hat in his hands. He had been repairing the customs house clocks for thirty years. He gave his name — Wilhelm Brandt, born Lübeck, fifty-nine years resident — and answered each question in careful, accented English, and when the sergeant asked about *associations*, Brandt said, "I repair clocks," and the sergeant wrote something anyway.

Then the Kessler brothers, who smoke the herring that half the town eats without thinking about whose hands cured it. Then old Marta Lund, who is Swedish and therefore required no registration at all but stood in the line regardless because her late husband was German and she seemed to believe the debt transferred. Then Anton Weiss.

I had known it was coming since August. Knowing did not help.

Anton stood in that line the way he stands anywhere — square, patient, hands loose at his sides, the hands of a man who has spent forty years persuading wood into shapes it didn't know it wanted. Greta was at school, thank God; school had not yet closed. When his turn came he gave his name clearly. Born Bremen. Shipwright, self-employed. Property: the yard on Tannin Slip, the loft above it, tools. Dependents: one daughter, Greta Weiss, twelve years.

"And your associations?"

"I build boats," Anton said. "The harbor builds them with me. Half these boats have my keels."

"Organizations, clubs, societies."

"I belong to the shipwrights' guild. It is on the form, third box."

The sergeant looked up then, and I saw something pass between them — not hostility exactly. Weariness meeting weariness. The sergeant was maybe forty, with the face of a man who had spent twenty years doing clerical work and had been told last month it was now war work. He wrote *shipwrights' guild* in the margin, stamped the page, and said, "You'll be issued a registration card. Carry it at all times. Report any change of address within forty-eight hours."

"A change of address," Anton repeated.

"That is correct, sir."

"To whom do I report it?"

"The customs house. Or any military post."

Anton nodded once, took the carbon copy they handed him, folded it, and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, the way a man carries something he means to keep. He walked past me without seeing me, which was unlike him, and I understood that he had not failed to see me. He had chosen not to. There are courtesies even deeper than greeting, and one of them is pretending not to witness a man's humiliation.

I waited until he'd gone, then collected my post — two letters for Edith, neither urgent — and walked home the long way along the quay so I could think.

* * *

That evening I opened my ledger.

I should explain what the ledger is, because the army will eventually explain it wrong. It is calfskin, pocket-sized, holds maybe two hundred pages, and I have kept it for six years. It began as what it sounds like: accounts. Who owed Edith's office postage, who owed me carriage fees, who had promised what to whom. But a harbor runs on debts that aren't money — favors, warnings, silences, names spoken at the right moment to the right ears — and a man who drives for the suffragists learns quickly that the movement survives on exactly such debts. So the ledger became a map of obligations. Frau Ostergaard, two pounds for printing, forgiven. Constable Daley, one warning re: meeting on the common, not yet repaid. The Vane brothers, passage north for two, price unnamed. And under all of it, in my own private shorthand, the true accounts: who could be trusted, who could be bought, who was afraid, and why.

Now I turned to a fresh section and ruled columns, and at the top I wrote: *Registered.* Because someone should keep the other list. The list with the whole names in it.

Wilhelm Brandt, I wrote. Not *enemy alien Brandt*, not *category B*. Wilhelm Brandt, who once repaired Edith's mantel clock for nothing because she argued with him about votes for women for two hours in his shop and he said afterward that any cause worth arguing two hours for deserved a working clock. Born Lübeck 1855. Came here at nineteen with a kit of tools and a brother who died at sea the following spring and never went back, not once, not in thirty-five years.

Lars and Piet Kessler, who send money every month to a sister in Hamburg they have not mentioned to anyone since August, because mentioning her now would be like painting a target.

Marta Lund, who registered voluntarily out of grief, which may be the most terrible entry on any list ever made.

Anton Weiss, born Bremen 1868. Shipwright. Dependents: one daughter, Greta, twelve. Associations: the guild, the harbor, and me.

I wrote until the lamp burned low. Forty-one names by the time I finished, and I knew it was incomplete — there would be families upriver, families who worked the seasonal boats, who hadn't heard or hadn't believed. Forty-one people who walked to a trestle table and let strangers write them down smaller than they are.

Because that is what a form does. I am a courier; I read documents for a living; I know their grammar. A form takes a man and subtracts everything the boxes cannot hold. It asks your birthplace and not your grief. Your occupation and not your craft. Your associations and not your friendships. By the time the sergeant's pen lifts, Wilhelm Brandt has become a category, and categories can be moved, and counted, and — the word nobody said aloud that Tuesday, but which sat under every stamp like water under ice — *collected*.

So I kept the other list. Full names, full histories, written in my own hand, which is small and neat and would mean nothing to a stranger. If the state intended to reduce my neighbors to entries, then I would keep the fuller entries, the truer ones, and hide them better. It was not much. I want to be honest about that: it was not much. It was a man writing names in a book by lamplight while the river fog came up the street. But defiance begins somewhere, and for most of us it begins as bookkeeping.

* * *

The second day, the line was longer, and the mood of the town had begun to curdle.

It curdles fast, fear. By Wednesday there were boys — our boys, harbor boys — following the registrants away from the table, walking behind them at a distance, saying nothing, just walking, which is worse than throwing stones because it can't be punished. Old Sørensen came off the trestle table pale as paper and found his shop window soaped overnight, the word painted across the glass in letters a child would know how to make: HUN. His grandfather built that shop. His grandchildren were born in this parish. The soap cost nothing and washed off by Thursday; the word took longer.

Edith watched it happen from windows and street corners, and I drove her past Sørensen's shop on Thursday and she made me stop.

"He registered?" she asked.

"Tuesday. First day. He's Danish, technically — his mother was Danish — but his father wasn't, and he wouldn't split the family over a form."

"No," she said softly. "Of course he wouldn't." She looked at the glass a long moment, at the fading letters. "Freya, I want you to understand what's happening. This is not a spasm. Spasms pass. This is administrative. They've brought forms, and forms don't leave. Whatever this town was before Tuesday, it is now a place where a man's grandmother's birthplace determines whether he sleeps in his own bed. And the terrible thing—" she pressed her gloved hand flat against the cold window, "—the terrible thing is that most of them will comply, and compliance will work, and they'll learn that being small is survivable, and they will teach their children to be small."

"And the ones who won't?"

She turned and looked at me directly. Edith Marlowe is sixty-one years old and has been shouted down in halls better than any in this county, and she can hold a look like a keel holds water. "The ones who won't will need drivers," she said, "and clerks, and friends with steady handwriting. I'm not asking anything today. I'm telling you what's coming so you can decide before it arrives rather than during."

I decided, as it happens, that evening, with the lamp and the calfskin and forty-two new names. But I didn't tell her that. Some decisions you make better in silence, and some promises you keep better unspoken, because a spoken promise becomes a record, and records, I was beginning to understand, are the war's real ammunition.

* * *

On Friday I delivered a letter to Anton at the yard.

He was up in the loft, and Greta opened the door — she'd been sent home from school early, or kept home; nobody said which — and she looked up at me with those grave dark eyes of hers and said, "Papa says we're not to talk about the papers," which is a twelve-year-old's way of telling you the papers are all anyone talks about.

Upstairs, Anton was working. Of course he was working. There was a hull taking shape under the loft's low beams, a ketch, maybe twenty-six feet, planks steamed and bent to curves that existed only in his head and, apparently, in his hands. He didn't stop planing when I came in. We had known each other nine years by then, and we had a language that didn't require stopping.

"You registered," I said.

"I registered." Shavings curled off the plank and fell. "And you've been standing at that table watching everyone, friend, which I also noticed."

"Somebody should watch."

"Somebody should do more than watch." He set the plane down and looked at me, and for a moment the loft was very quiet — the river sounds below, the gulls, the creak of timber, and beneath it all the far-off grind of army lorries on streets built for carts. "When I came here, this harbor was mud and pilings. I helped make it what it is. Every warehouse, half the wharves, the cradles under half the fishing fleet — my hands. Now there is a card in my pocket that says what I am, and the card is thinner than one of my shavings." He picked up the plane again, turned it over, set it down. "Greta asked me yesterday if our name is a bad word now."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her names don't change meaning. Only liars change meanings." He almost smiled. "She didn't believe me. She's too clever to believe comforting things, which she gets from her mother, God rest her." He crossed to the window that overlooks the slip. Below, two soldiers were measuring the warehouse district with a surveyor's chain, unhurried, thorough. "They're measuring everything, Freya. Do you understand? Not inspecting. Measuring. You measure a thing when you intend to move it or use it. Warehouses, cranes, boats, records—"

"Records?"

"All of it. The harbormaster told me yesterday the military wants copies of every registry, every bill of lading, every license going back ten years. Everything the town has ever written down about itself." He said it lightly, the way you report weather, and only afterward, years afterward, would I understand he was handing me the shape of the whole catastrophe in one sentence, the way he could hand me a curved plank and I couldn't see the boat in it until the boat existed.

"Well," I said. "Good thing nobody writes anything down anymore."

He laughed at that — a real laugh, brief and low, the laugh of a tired man finding one coin in an empty pocket. "No," he said. "Thank God for illiterates and smugglers. The two honest professions left." Then, quieter, not laughing: "You take care with whatever you carry, friend. Paper travels badly in wartime. It develops weight."

I left by way of the rope ladder rather than the yard stairs, because the soldiers below had drifted toward the slip gate, and I didn't want my name in anyone's notebook connected with that loft. Nine years I'd been coming and going from that yard openly, hauling planks, carrying messages, sitting on the porch eating Greta's terrible biscuits. Now I went down a ladder like a thief, and the shame of it sat in my chest all the way home, sharp and specific, and I have learned to trust shame when it's specific — vague shame is just fear wearing a costume, but specific shame points at something true. What it pointed at that Friday was this: the world had redrawn its lines, and I was already on the wrong side of one of them, sneaking across.

* * *

Saturday night I sat with the ledger and added the week's entries. The registrations, all of them, with details no form would hold: Sørensen's soaped window; the Kesslers' Hamburg sister; Brandt's two-hour argument; Greta's question. Then, at the bottom of the page, after a long pause, I wrote the sentence that would matter more than all the rest, though I wrote it as a mere note, a courier's memo to himself:

*Military requisitioning ALL municipal records — registries, licenses, bills of lading, ten years back. Customs house vault. Harbormaster confirms.*

I underlined *all*.

Then I closed the book and sat holding it, feeling its weight — two pounds, maybe less, calfskin and paper and ink, light enough to forget on a shelf. Ten years of this town's memory was about to go into a soldiers' vault, every debt and name and license and promise the harbor had ever committed to paper, sorted by men with stamps. And somewhere in that flood of confiscated paper, sooner or later, would be a leather ledger full of suffragist correspondence routes and smugglers' debts and the names of forty-two neighbors recorded in a hand the authorities would find interesting indeed.

I had always believed a ledger was a safe. That was the assumption underneath six years of keeping it — that writing a thing down fixed it, protected it, held it against forgetting and against lying alike. Sitting there Saturday night with the fog pressing against the glass, I understood for the first time that a ledger is also a confession, and that I had written mine in advance, and that the state had just announced it would be collecting confessions at the customs house.

Outside, far downriver, the first army searchlight swept the water, testing itself, a white blade dragged slowly across the black. I put the ledger in the flour tin behind the loose brick in my kitchen wall, where it had never needed to live before, and lay awake listening to the river.

Fourteen days, the notices said, for registration to complete.

I wondered how many days after that before the sweep came for the paper.
