## 5. Requisition

They came on a Tuesday, which surprised me. I had imagined the army arriving the way weather arrives — a slow darkening, a smell in the air, time to shutter the windows and count what might be lost. Instead it was a bright morning with a hard wind off the water, and the first I knew of it was the sound of boots on the customs house stairs, twenty pairs at least, keeping time.

I was in Edith's motor, parked outside the post office with a bundle of letters on the seat beside me, when Sergeant Bramley came down those stairs at a pace no clerk had ever managed and said, without looking at me, "Go home, Crane. Everything's closed."

"Closed by whose order?"

"By theirs." He jerked his chin upriver, and I followed the gesture to where a gray launch was tying off at the coal wharf, soldiers stacking down its gangplank like sand poured from a sack.

I want to record here that my first thought was for the letters. That is true, and I am not proud of it, but honesty is the one inventory I have never falsified: my first thought was that Mrs. Halloran's letter to her son held three pounds folded inside the envelope, and if that letter went into a government sack, the money would go with it, and Mrs. Halloran would not eat well until spring. My second thought — slower, heavier, like a crate slipping its sling — was of the ledger in the drawer beneath my bed, four years of names and debts and promises in my own hand.

* * *

They worked with a method that frightened me more than any shouting could have. There was no shouting. A captain with a red-tabbed uniform and a voice like a man reading a shopping list stood at the foot of Harbor Street with a clipboard, and companies of men peeled off him the way lines peel off a chart: one detail to the grain warehouse, one to the salt shed, one to the chandlery, one to the customs house itself, where poor Mr. Quill the revenue officer stood on his own steps holding his own keys out to them like a man offering bread to a robber because he cannot think what else a body does with keys.

I drove Edith past the waterfront at half nine, because she insisted on seeing it, and because Edith Marlowe had never once in her life looked away from a thing on the grounds that looking might hurt her.

"They're chalking numbers on the doors," she said.

They were. Every warehouse, every office, every net loft and cooperage had received a white figure on its planking — a number, a date, sometimes two letters I did not recognize and would come to dread. The harbor had been a place made of names. The Kestrel Yard. Weiss & Sons, Boatwrights, though there were no sons, only Anton and Greta. Okafor's Chandlery. The Salt House. Now it was 14, 15, 16, C-2, C-3, a sequence some lieutenant could read aloud in a tent forty miles inland and know everything he believed he needed to know about us.

"Stop here," Edith said, at the corner by the fish market, and I stopped, and we sat with the engine ticking while a sergeant directed two privates carrying a filing cabinet down the customs house steps between them, swaying like pallbearers.

"All the records," Edith said quietly. She wasn't asking. She'd understood it before I had, or perhaps she'd simply been expecting it longer; women who fight governments learn to expect governments to behave like governments. "Every deed, every license, every register of births and marriages and deaths. Every list of who owes what and who came from where." She turned to look at me, and her face in that light was very plain and very hard, the face she wore when she was about to say something true. "Freya. They haven't come for the warehouses. Warehouses don't frighten anyone. They've come for the paper. A town is only its paper once you've decided to rule it instead of live in it."

"And the people?"

"The people are what the paper says they are. That's the trick of it. You'll see."

* * *

I saw it within the hour.

The order had gone out overnight, printed on the requisition forms themselves, tacked to the post office door with four nails: ALL PERSONS OF ENEMY ALIEN ORIGIN RESIDING WITHIN THE HARBOR DISTRICT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION AT THE CUSTOMS HOUSE NOT LATER THAN FRIDAY THE TWENTY-FIRST. FAILURE TO REGISTER CONSTITUTES AN OFFENCE PUNISHABLE BY DETENTION.

Enemy alien origin. Six words, and underneath them a line to sign your name on, and beside that a space for your place of birth, and beside that a box to be marked with an X by a soldier who would then carry your name away in a cabinet on a stretcher.

I stood in the queue on Friday because I wanted to see it done properly, the way you watch a surgeon work on someone you love. The queue ran from the customs house door along the quay and doubled back at the bollards, and it moved slowly, because the clerks — our own Mr. Quill among them, working now under a soldier's eye, his collar grey with humiliation — were required to ask each person the full schedule of questions and write each answer in a fair hand.

Anton Weiss was six places ahead of me. I watched him the way you watch a boat you built take her first water.

He had dressed for it. That was the thing that went through me like a nail: he had put on his good coat, the one with the brass buttons he wore to launches and funerals, as though the registration were a ceremony he meant to honor rather than endure. He stood straight in the queue with his hat in his hands and answered the questions in his careful English, each word placed like a plank laid true.

Name? Anton Josef Weiss.

Place of birth? Bremen.

Occupation? Shipwright.

He paused there. I saw his jaw move, and then he added, clearly enough for the whole queue to hear, "Twenty-two years at this harbor. I built half the boats you can see from where you're standing."

The soldier did not look up. The pen scratched. Somewhere behind me a gull screamed, and a woman began quietly to cry, and nobody turned around, because turning around would mean admitting you had heard, and admitting you had heard would mean deciding what to do about it.

When Anton stepped aside, finished, registered, reduced to an entry, our eyes met for perhaps a second. There was no message in it. That was the worst part. Three years earlier, on the deck of a half-finished ketch, this man had shaken my hand and I had given him my word — *if it ever turns sour, you come to me, and you and yours will have passage, whatever it costs* — and now we stood ten feet apart in a government queue and could not even nod, because a nod between a registered enemy alien and the driver of a known agitator might itself go into somebody's file.

He put on his hat and walked home along the quay, and I watched his back all the way to the bend by the Salt House, and I thought: *a promise is a debt, and I keep accounts.*

* * *

That night I took out the ledger.

I want to be exact about what it was, because after this week I may be the only one who knows. It was a leather book, eight inches by five, bound originally for a shipping agent's daybook and bought secondhand by me for sixpence from Okafor's shelf of odds. Four years of entries in my hand, small and close, because paper was dear. Names. What was owed and to whom. Mrs. Halloran, three pounds against winter fuel. Tom Vane, one favor unspecified, called in twice, never refused. Dr. Prasad, who signed the certificate that kept old Marta Lindqvist out of the workhouse, owed a year of free driving. And the other kind of entry too, the ones that had no column heading: *A.W. — passage and protection, promised, June 1911.* *E.M. — letters carried unread, never.* *Greta W. — school fees through Michaelmas, paid, tell no one.*

It was, if I am honest, the truest history of this harbor anyone had written. The parish register recorded baptisms. The customs house recorded tonnage. My ledger recorded what people actually did for one another when no institution was watching — and, since the war came, it recorded the other ledger too, the shadow one I had begun keeping in September: names of the German-born, their addresses, their registration numbers, the dates the soldiers photographed their shops. I had told myself I was keeping it to help them. Standing there with the book open on my knees, lamplight on the pages, I understood for the first time what else it was.

It was evidence. It was the whole case, bound in calfskin, indexed in my own unmistakable hand.

Every name I had entered to protect was now a name the authorities wanted. Every connection I had recorded as kindness — Freya drove so-and-so, Freya paid such-and-such, Freya met the Vanes at the reeds on Sunday — read, in a military courtroom, as conspiracy. I had spent four years writing down the truth about my neighbors, believing that truth was a shield, that a thing written down honestly could not be twisted. But the army did not read the way I read. They read for patterns, and patterns are easy to find in any honest account of human decency, because decency, organized, looks exactly like sedition.

And the handwriting. God, the handwriting. Any clerk in the district could identify it. I had written letters for half the town — wills, complaints, petitions, Edith's speeches copied fair — my hand was as familiar around the harbor as the church bell. If the ledger fell into the right drawer, it would not merely implicate me. It would hang half the town, and it would do it in characters so distinctive that no denial, no lawyer, no silence could unpick them.

I sat with it for a long while, weighing what to do, and I found I could not burn it. I tell you plainly: I tried to make myself able to, and I failed. Every page was a person. Burning it felt like burning them, and I was not yet the man who could do that arithmetic — though I would be, later, and the learning of it would cost more than I can set down here.

So I wrapped it in oilcloth, as one wraps anything one means to keep dry, and I hid it in the false bottom of my document case among Edith's blank petition forms, and I told myself it was safe, and I went to sleep believing that a thing hidden in a private room belongs to the person who hid it.

This is what I did not yet understand about armies: they do not search rooms. They search *everything*, on principle, the way a flood does not choose which cellars to fill.

* * *

The sweep came Monday, and it came for paper.

I was at the Marlowe house, mending a tire, when the lorry drew up at the gate — not a requisition lorry but a records lorry, canvas-topped, with a corporal and four men and a stack of empty crates stenciled M.I.5, which meant nothing to me then and everything to me now. The corporal had a printed warrant and the good manners never to raise his voice, and he informed Mrs. Marlowe's housekeeper that the premises were to be searched for documents relevant to national security, that this was routine, and that any interference would be noted.

Routine. That word again. They had made an industry of it in eleven days.

They went through the house the way water goes through a wreck. Edith's correspondence — baskets of it, years of it, the whole circulatory system of the suffrage movement in this county — was carried out in armfuls. Her speeches. Her subscription lists. Her diary, which she surrendered from the desk in the sitting room herself, standing very straight, saying only, "You will find nothing seditious in it, Corporal, but you will find better prose than you're used to," which earned her a flush on the young man's neck and, I think, something dangerously near respect.

Then the corporal came into the yard where I stood with the tire iron in my hand, and he looked at the document case on the motor's running board, and he said, "That too. All papers, sir. Motor vehicles included."

I could have lied. I have replayed that moment perhaps a thousand times, and every version in which I lie ends the same way: they open the case anyway, they find the false bottom anyway, and my lie goes into the report beside the ledger, and the lie hangs me faster than the truth would have. So I stood aside, and I watched a nineteen-year-old corporal lift out four years of my neighbors' lives wrapped in oilcloth, weigh it in his hand like a parcel of meat, and drop it into a crate marked with a chalk number.

"What happens to it?" I asked. My voice came out steady, which surprised me.

"Inventory and storage," he said. "Requisitioned materials, security category. It'll be assessed."

Assessed. Assessed by whom, at what desk, under what lamp, I did not know, and not knowing has a weight of its own — I felt it settle on my shoulders like wet canvas and stay there.

Edith appeared at the door as the lorry pulled away. She watched it go with an expression I had seen on her once before, at a rally in the rain when the constables moved in on the platform. Not fear. Recalculation.

"They have your book," she said. Not a question; she knew what I carried as well as I knew what she wrote.

"They have everything."

"No," she said, and she took my arm, and her grip was stronger than a woman her size had any right to. "They have paper. Paper is only dangerous if it's the last copy. Is it the last copy, Freya?"

I thought of the pages. Four years, hundreds of names, my hand on every line. And I thought of the copies that existed nowhere, of the things people had told me aloud that I had never written down, of the debts and promises that lived only in the space between one person and another — and I understood that she was not asking about the ledger. She was asking whether I believed a town could survive being reduced to what the army carried away in crates.

"I don't know," I said.

"Find out," said Edith Marlowe. "Before they finish assessing."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Toward dawn I dressed and walked down to the harbor, because I could not bear the walls of my room, and the tide was low and the mudflats gave off their cold iron smell, and the whole waterfront sat there chalked with numbers in the early light like a graveyard where the stones had been numbered instead of named.

At the Kestrel Yard, the gates wore a fresh board: CLOSED BY MILITARY ORDER. ENTRY FORBIDDEN. Through the fence I could see Anton's loft above the boat shed, and a thin thread of smoke rising from its stove pipe, which it should not have been doing, because the yard was closed and no one was supposed to be there — and I stood in the grey morning looking at that impossible smoke and understanding that Anton Weiss had decided, in his quiet shipwright's way, not to comply, and that somewhere in a government drawer my own handwriting was already telling the world exactly who his friends were.
