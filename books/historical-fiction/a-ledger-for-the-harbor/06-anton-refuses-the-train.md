## 6. Anton Refuses the Train

The notice came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet his eyes.

Anton Weiss stood in the doorway of the shipyard office and read it twice, because the first time his hands were shaking too badly to hold the paper steady. *By order of the military authority: all persons of enemy alien birth resident within the harbor district shall present themselves at the railway station on Friday next, for transport to an internment camp inland. Failure to comply will be treated as an offense against the state.*

Enemy alien birth. He had been born in Kiel forty-nine years ago, which was true in the way that a scar was true — a fact about him, but not the fact of him. The fact of him was this yard, these ribs of oak and pine rising out of the mud like whale bones, the smell of pitch and cedar shavings that had soaked into his skin so deep he doubted any border could scrub it out.

He had built half the boats in this harbor. Now the boats were requisitioned property, and so was he.

"Father?" Greta stood behind him in the office door, her school satchel still on her shoulder though school had been closed three weeks now. She had learned to read upside down faster than most adults read right side up. "What does it say?"

He folded the paper along its crease, twice, until it was small enough to disappear into his coat pocket, into the same pocket where he kept his pipe and the stub of pencil he used for marking timber. "It says we're going on a train," he said. "An adventure."

Greta was twelve. She had stopped believing in adventures when her mother died, which was four years ago, and she did not believe in them now. She looked at him with Anna's gray eyes and said nothing, and the nothing said everything.

* * *

That night he walked the length of the shipyard, as he did every night after locking the gate, but this time he counted what would be left behind.

Three hulls under construction. The *Marta*, a cod trawler, planked to the waterline, her frames fair as a woman's shoulder. The two pilot skiffs, half-decked, waiting on their keelsons. And in the loft above the sail room, hidden under canvas where the army surveyors hadn't thought to look, the little ketch — fourteen feet on the waterline, larch on oak, begun last winter for a man who had since decided to buy a motorboat instead and told Anton to keep it, finish it whenever, sell it if he liked.

He had been working on it evenings ever since. Not for money. For the pleasure of a boat no one had ordered, no one owned, no government form described. A boat that existed only because his hands wanted it to.

Now his hands belonged to the King's enemies.

He climbed the ladder to the loft and pulled back the canvas. In the dark the ketch smelled of fresh shavings and linseed oil, and touching her gunwale was like touching something alive, something warm-blooded. He had named her already, in his head, though names went unspoken these days unless you wanted them written down somewhere by someone who wasn't your friend. He'd name her when she floated. Names needed water.

A boat unfinished was a promise unkept. He had made promises all his life — to customers, to crews, to God, to Anna before she died, to Freya Crane that afternoon three years ago on this very quay — and every one of them except the last had been kept, and he was not going to let a piece of paper with a stamp on it break the streak.

They could take his shop sign down. They had already done that; some clerk had pasted over it a printed card reading ENEMY-OWNED PREMISES, SEALED. They could take his tools — they'd taken inventory of his tools, itemized his adzes and augers in a ledger with columns for quantity and condition, and the clerk had written *good* beside each one in a hand like a schoolboy's. They could take his house, his bank account, his rights, his name turned into a number.

But they had not yet found the ketch. And a man who could still build could still refuse.

* * *

"You can't hide here," Freya Crane said on Wednesday night, standing in the loft with his cap in his hands, looking like a man delivering news he wished he could eat instead. "The army surveyed this building once already. They'll come back. They always come back."

"They didn't find her."

"They weren't looking hard. Next time they will be." Freya moved to the ketch and ran his palm along the sheer strake, and Anton watched the younger man do it and understood that Freya loved boats the way Anton loved letters — as vessels, as things that carried what mattered across distances that would otherwise be uncrossable. "Anton. Listen to me. If they find you here, you won't go to a camp. You'll go somewhere worse. Men who evade registration get treated as spies."

"I registered." He tapped his chest. "I'm a category. I have a number. I stood in line like a good enemy and gave my fingerprints to a corporal who couldn't spell *shipwright*. I've done everything their papers asked." He heard his own voice rise and brought it down. "What I won't do is stand on a platform Friday morning and let them load me into a cattle car while my boats sit here half-built, waiting for some soldier who doesn't know a frame from a futtock to knock them apart for firewood."

"The boats are wood. You're flesh."

"The boats are thirty years of my life." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Freya. When a man builds a boat, he puts time into the timber. Every plank is an hour of his life nailed to another hour. You pull those boats apart, you're pulling apart days of me. Days I worked when Anna was sick and I couldn't sit with her, so I came here and planed cedar until my arms ached too much to feel anything else. Days after she died. Days teaching myself to be alone in a country that was starting to forget I'd helped build its harbor." He looked down at his hands, the knuckles gone big and knotted, the left index finger short-tipped from a chisel accident twenty years ago. "I'm fifty years old. How many more boats do you think I have in me? Three? Four? Those are mine. Nobody counts them but me."

Freya was silent a long moment. Then: "And Greta?"

There it was. There it always was. Anton closed his eyes. "Greta comes with me."

"Into hiding? In a drafty loft in November?"

"Better a drafty loft with her father than a camp without him. Have you seen the camps, Freya? Have you talked to anyone who's come back from Knutsford or the Isle of Man? Barbed wire and boredom and men going quietly mad, and a twelve-year-old girl—" He stopped. Breathed. "No. Whatever else happens, she stays where I can put my arm around her at night. That's the whole of my politics now. One arm around one child."

"And if they search the loft?"

"Then I'll have a few minutes' warning, and I know this building better than any soldier born in Birmingham knows anything. There's a crawl space under the floor of the paint locker that opens onto the slipway. I put it there myself in 1909, when the customs men got curious about certain cargo. It's held ever since." He almost smiled. "This harbor runs on secrets, boy. You of all people know that. You drive a car full of them."

Freya looked at him for a long time, and Anton watched the younger man weigh something — not whether Anton was right, but how much trouble he himself was willing to carry. That was the thing about Freya Crane: he measured everything, weighed everything, kept accounts of everything in that leather book he thought nobody knew about. Anton had never read it. He didn't need to. He knew that somewhere in it was a line about a promise made on this quay, three summers ago, over beer and the smell of tar: *If things ever turn sour, Anton, you come to me. I'll get you out.*

Things had turned sour.

"I'll bring food," Freya said finally. "Bread, tinned meat, whatever I can carry without questions. And candles. But Anton — this buys days. Not weeks. The ice will break soon, and when it breaks, the navy owns the river, and then even I can't—"

"I know about the ice." Everyone in the harbor knew about the ice; it was all anyone talked about anymore, the way farmers talk about rain. "Days are what I need. The *Marta* needs six hours' work to be sound enough to float, and floating is surviving. A boat at a dock can be confiscated. A boat sunk in the reeds is just a rumor." He laid his hand flat on the ketch's deck, feeling the grain through the fresh varnish. "And her — she's fourteen feet. Small enough to handle alone. Big enough for two passengers and a sea bag apiece, if the weather's kind."

"Passengers," Freya repeated carefully.

"If it comes to it." Anton met his eyes. "I'm not saying anything. I'm building a boat. A man is allowed to build a boat."

"A German-born man is allowed very little just now."

"Then I'll build it anyway. Let them add it to my file."

* * *

Friday morning, Anton stood at the gap in the shipyard fence where he could see the railway station clock, and watched the train come in.

They came down the hill in twos and threes, men he had known all his life: Brandt the baker, whose rolls had fed half the town's children for twenty years. Old Herr Lindqvist, who was Swedish, actually, but had a German wife, and had chosen to go with her rather than be separated — or perhaps hadn't chosen, perhaps the forms had decided for him, the way forms did. The Fischer brothers from the net works. Young Emil Sauer, who had been born in this town, actually born here, in a house two streets from the station, but whose father was Kiel-born, and so Emil went too, because blood apparently traveled better than birth certificates.

Greta stood beside him at the fence gap, small and still, watching. She had been quiet lately — not silent, not yet, but quieter, asking fewer questions, answering in shorter sentences, as though she were rationing her words against a winter she could see coming. She counted things now, he'd noticed. Soldiers. Gulls. Steps. He didn't know why and was afraid to ask.

"That's Mr. Brandt," she said. "His bread burned yesterday. Somebody threw a brick through his window and the oven went cold and nobody bought from him after, and now he's getting on a train."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Anton searched for an answer that wasn't a lie and wasn't a wound. "Because some men need other men to be smaller than they are. It makes them feel bigger."

"That's stupid."

"It is entirely stupid." He put his arm around her shoulders. She was growing; the arm fit differently than it had a year ago. "Remember that word. Stupid. When they teach you in school someday that this was necessary, that it was regrettable but unavoidable — you remember that you saw Mr. Brandt get on a train because of a brick through a window, and you remember the word."

Below them, whistles blew. The train exhaled steam. The men filed up the steps with their cardboard suitcases, and someone — Brandt, he thought — waved at the crowd of onlookers, and hardly anyone waved back, and that was the part Anton couldn't stop seeing afterward: not the train, not the barbed wire visible in the third car's windows, but the wave that fell into silence like a stone into deep water.

Greta's hand found his. "Papa. Will they come for us?"

"Not today."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe tomorrow. But tonight we're here, and the ketch needs her garboard seam caulked, and caulking waits for no empire." He squeezed her shoulder. "Come. You can hold the mallet. Your mother's hands were steadier than mine, and you have her hands."

She looked up at him then, and for a moment the fear in her face rearranged itself into something else — curiosity, maybe, or the first thin edge of pride. "Is it true we're hiding?"

"It's true we're staying. Hiding is just staying where other people aren't invited."

* * *

The loft became a country.

They lived in it the way gulls live on a ledge — lightly, watchfully, provisioned against weather. Freya came every second evening with a basket disguised as a carpenter's kit: bread under rags, tins inside a toolbox, a bottle of milk nested in wood shavings. He never stayed long. He'd sit on a sawhorse, eat half a roll, report what the town was saying — the requisition lists growing longer, Edith Marlowe's rally planned for Sunday, soldiers drilling on the customs quay — and then leave before full dark, walking his bicycle downhill with the lamp unlit so as not to draw the eye of patrols.

Anton worked. That was the whole discipline of it: work from dawn light through the gap in the north-facing boards until the light failed, then rest, then work again by candle stub on the ketch while Greta slept in the nest of sails he'd made her in the corner. His body knew the motions too well to betray him; his hands could fair a batten in the dark. Only the noise was dangerous, and so he learned a new craft — silent joinery, pressing seams together with his thumbs instead of tapping them home, clamping instead of nailing, doing in four hours what his hammer would have done in one.

Some nights the fear came for him. It arrived around two in the morning, usually, when the river fog pressed against the roof boards and somewhere down the quay a sentry's boots rang on iron, and it whispered: *they will find you, they will find her, you have traded a numbered camp for a cell, you have done this to your daughter out of vanity, out of love for dead trees.* On those nights he would climb down the ladder and stand in the cold among the frames of the *Marta*, laying his palms against the oak like a man checking a fevered child, and say to the timber, silently: *not yet. Not yet. Hold together a little longer, and so will I.*

Timber holds together better than men. That was the whole reason he'd become a shipwright instead of a priest.

One night Greta woke while he was below and came down the ladder in her blanket, and found him there in the dark with his hands on the hull.

"Papa. Are you talking to the boat?"

"Listening."

"What's it saying?"

He considered lying, decided against it. "It's saying it isn't finished. Neither am I."

She stood beside him a moment, small and grave, and then she reached up and laid her own hand on the plank, exactly where his lay, as though taking an oath. "Neither is she," Greta agreed, and went back up the ladder, and Anton stood in the dark a long while with his heart too large for his ribs.

* * *

On the ninth night, Freya came later than usual, and came up the ladder fast, and did not sit down.

"There was a search today," he said. "Not here. Two streets over — the Sauer house. They took what was left of Emil's family's papers, and his brother-in-law. He argued. They took him rough." Freya's breath was short; he'd ridden hard. "Rourke's men are making a list of properties they haven't inspected closely. This yard is on it. Anton — I saw the list. You have days. Maybe less."

Anton set down the plane he'd been holding and looked at the ketch, lit yellow by the candle stub. Planked to the rub rail. Deck beams in. The mast, a beautiful stick of Sitka spruce, lay along the wall waiting for its partners.

"How long to make her floatable?" Freya asked.

Anton did the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic — in hours of work, in ounces of caulking cotton, in the distance between the tide he needed and the tide he had. "If I do nothing else. If the weather holds. If the seam compound doesn't freeze in the pot." He looked at Freya Crane, at the young man's face trying to be calm and mostly succeeding. "Four nights."

Freya nodded slowly. "Then I'd better find you a boat that floats sooner," he said, "because I don't think four nights is a number we're going to be given."

Down at the station, far off, a whistle blew — only a freight, probably, only the eleven o'clock goods train grinding north with requisitioned wool. Probably. Anton blew out the candle stub between finger and thumb, and in the dark he and Freya listened to the sound fade upriver, each of them counting, in his own ledger, what remained.
