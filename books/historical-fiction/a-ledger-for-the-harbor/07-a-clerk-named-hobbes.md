## 7. A Clerk Named Hobbes

The customs house had a smell now. Three weeks of army occupation and the building that once smelled of tar, ink, and wet rope smelled instead of boot polish and cold tea. I noticed it every time I carried Edith's correspondence past the doors — letters to lawyers in the capital, appeals nobody answered — because I had spent half my life learning buildings by their smells, the way other men learned them by their floorboards.

You learn a lot carrying letters. You learn who burns them unread, who keeps them in hatboxes tied with ribbon, who reads them twice and answers in pencil because they mean to say more later and never do. The customs house under occupation read everything twice and answered nothing.

That was where I first took proper notice of Corporal Hobbes.

* * *

He sat at the second-floor window desk with the best light in the building and the worst job in it, cataloguing requisitioned property on inventory sheets ruled so fine his handwriting shrank to fit. He was perhaps twenty-two, soft through the middle, with a mustache he was clearly growing into rather than having grown. His cap sat too far back. His boots were regulation but his socks were not — I could see the stripe of civilian red when he crossed his legs, which he did often, because sitting still was evidently a form of suffering for him.

I had delivered a letter to the adjutant's office and was waiting to be signed out, which took as long as it took, which was long. The clerk at the front desk made me stand. So I stood, and listened, and what I heard from upstairs was a man humming.

Not martial music. Music-hall. Something about a girl from Kew, hummed badly, with feeling.

When the sergeant finally stamped my chit, I climbed one extra flight of stairs on the pretext of finding the washroom, and passed the open door of the records room, and looked in the way anyone looks into an open door — briefly, incuriously, a man with a full bladder and no interest in paperwork.

Corporal Hobbes looked up from his inventory sheets with the gratitude of a drowning man thrown an oar.

"Bit of a queue down there," I said.

"There's always a queue." He said it like a grievance. "Everyone wants something certified, verified, or confiscated. Nobody just—" he gestured vaguely, "—talks."

I gave him thirty seconds of talk. Thirty seconds is the correct dose. Enough to be remembered as pleasant, not enough to be remembered at all. I told him the harbor fog had come in early this year and he told me he was from a town with no harbor at all and I said that must be restful and he laughed like I'd said something clever, which is the surest sign you've found a lonely man.

Then I found the washroom, washed hands I hadn't dirtied, and went home to think.

* * *

Here is what my ledger would have said, if my ledger had been available to consult, which it was not, because it was sitting in a crate somewhere in this very building with a string-tied label and somebody's initials on it, catalogued among the sewing machines and the harbor charts as *miscellaneous bound volumes, 1*.

I had gone three weeks without it. Three weeks of keeping accounts in my head, which is no way to keep accounts. A debt in your head grows strange shapes at night. It inflates. It attaches itself to the wrong people. By the third week I could no longer swear to myself whether Mrs. Aldous owed me two shillings or I owed her, and that frightened me more than the soldiers did, if I am honest, because the soldiers were a known weather and my own memory was a leaking boat.

I wanted my ledger back. That was the whole of it, at first. Not strategy. Not resistance. A man wants his own handwriting back the way he wants his own teeth.

But wanting something behind an army's lock is a poor plan unless you can make the army hand it to you smiling, and the smiling hand belonged, as far as I could see, to Corporal Hobbes.

* * *

Cigarettes are the small currency of every occupied town. I had learned this watching the last war's veterans — no, there had been no last war; I meant the older men, the ones who'd garrisoned distant places and come back with habits. Tobacco opens doors that rank cannot. A man who will not take money — and Hobbes struck me as a man who'd refuse money, out of some dim instinct for the shape of corruption even while swimming in it — will take a cigarette, because a cigarette isn't anything. It's a moment. Everyone understands moments between friends.

I started with moments.

Tuesday: I came up with a reason about a misfiled receipt for Edith's legal fund — plausible, checkable, harmless — and stood in his doorway while he hunted for it, and when he found nothing I offered him a cigarette for his trouble. He took it. His fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle.

"You write all day?" I asked.

"Till my eyes swim." He held the cigarette unlit, saving it, I supposed, or savoring the having of it. "Four hundred and six line items this week. Do you know what a line item is?"

"A thing someone else owned."

He laughed, then looked slightly ashamed of laughing, then laughed again anyway. Lonely men will pay a great deal for laughter.

Thursday: matches, since he seemed to be hoarding the cigarette. And a question I'd prepared until it sounded offhand: how does a fellow get assigned records duty, anyway? Because it sounded restful compared to standing sentry in the rain, and wasn't he lucky, and didn't the officers dump everything on him?

They did. Everything. The officers dumped everything. Within ten minutes I knew the vault was on the ground floor behind the old strong-room door; that Captain Rourke's intelligence section took whatever they liked on Thursdays without signing for it, which offended Hobbes more than any cruelty could have; and that the requisitioned goods were being moved to a central depot in batches, alphabetically by confiscating unit, which meant the harbor's property would leave town piecemeal and unrecoverable.

"Like the tide taking furniture," Hobbes said, pleased with the phrase, and I thought: yes. Exactly that. Except the tide doesn't keep lists.

* * *

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I have left to trade, and I did not feel clean during those weeks. Every smile I gave that boy was a tool. When he showed me the photograph of his sweetheart — a round-faced girl squinting into sun outside a bakery — I said she had kind eyes, and I did not know if she did, and the words went out of me smooth as coins from a practiced hand.

There is a particular shame in currying favor with an occupier. The men at the docks watched me go in and out of the customs house and I could feel them deciding things. Old Brandt — no. Not Brandt. Brandt had been taken inland already, on the train Anton refused. The men who remained watched me and said nothing, which in a harbor town is the loudest thing a man can hear. One of them, Larsen, spat neatly into the water as I passed the fish house, and it might have been habit, and it might not have been, and I walked on with my collar up and my face arranged into nothing.

I told myself it was for Anton. Some of the time I believed it. The rest of the time I understood that I liked it — the access, the small power, the feeling of moving through the enemy's rooms as though I belonged there. Power is a drug even in teaspoon doses, and I have never been good at leaving a glass unfinished.

* * *

The fifth visit, I brought two cigarettes and stayed forty minutes.

Hobbes had been left alone over a Sunday with the inventory of Box lots — the sealed cases from the customs vault, contents listed but not yet transferred — and he was bored in the way that only a young man surrounded by paper and forbidden to burn any of it can be bored. He read items aloud to amuse himself. "One brass telescope, marine, inscribed. One case preserved eggs, condition doubtful. One stuffed cormorant, glass eyes, provenance unknown."

"The cormorant's from the yacht club," I said. "It was shot in 'ninety-one by a drunk admiral. It's worth more than the telescope."

"How do you know all this?"

"I grew up on this harbor. Half of what you're cataloguing, I've carried, repaired, or sold." I let that settle, then added lightly, "You should have me audit it. I'd find your errors."

"I don't make errors." But he said it the way a man says it when he has made several, and we both knew it, and the shared knowledge was another coin in the till.

He turned back to his sheets. I looked out the window at the gray water and did not look at the sheets, because looking is a thing men notice, and I had trained myself years ago to read sideways, upside down, in mirrors, in reflections off teacups — the courier's whole education, learned from Edith Marlowe, who reads a room the way scholars read dead languages.

Still. Sideways reading has its limits. And so it happened honestly, more or less, that I saw the entry.

He had the B-lots on top. Bound volumes, boxed in the sweep of the municipal offices. I caught a column of entries upside down across the desk, and my eye — trained on thousands of envelopes, sorting addresses in a glance — snagged on a familiar shape before my mind caught up with it.

*Miscellaneous bound volumes, 1.*

And beneath, in the description column, in Hobbes's shrinking hand: *leather ledger, hand-ruled columns, approx. 200 ff., private accounts &c., owner unstated.*

Owner unstated. They hadn't known whose it was. Of course they hadn't. My name was nowhere in it — I had built the thing the way I built everything, careful, deniable, names coded, sums disguised as shipping figures. It had been seized in the general swallow of records, tossed into a box with the harbor's dead weight of paper.

It was mine. It was mine and it was twenty feet away from me in a locked strong-room and it contained, in my own patient hand, the true names of every German-born family on this waterfront, the debts they owed and were owed, the favors banked against bad weather, the promises — including one promise, entered three years ago, dated, witnessed by no one, that read in my shorthand: *A.W. — passage & protection, if ever needed. Given.*

Given. In peacetime, a light thing, a kindness between friends over a finished hull. Now it was evidence. Now, in the hands of Captain Rourke's intelligence section, it was a map of exactly the network the army believed it was hunting — except the network didn't exist yet, and the map would build it simply by being read. Names. Connections. A pattern of trust, drawn in my own hand, ready-made for a prosecutor.

Half the harbor could hang from those pages. Anton first. Greta after, though they wouldn't care about a child except as leverage, and they would use her, because armies use whatever floats.

"Interesting stuff, some of it," Hobbes said. He had turned a page. "Mostly it's duller than church. Who keeps a ledger for twenty years, eh? There's a whole page of — some cipher, looks like. Little figures. Sums, maybe. A bookkeeper with secrets." He chuckled. "Everyone's got secrets in a war. Even the stationery."

I smiled. I felt my face make the smile from very far away, the way you watch your own hand move in a dream.

"Probably a grocer," I said. "Grocers count everything."

"Mm." He flipped past it. Flipped *past* it. My heart resumed.

* * *

I walked home along the quay with the fog coming in off the river, and the shame I had been carrying all week changed its weight entirely.

It is a strange arithmetic. For five visits I had despised myself moderately, steadily, the way you despise a limp — a constant, manageable humiliation. Trading smiles for scraps. Playing the fool's friend to the fool. And then one glimpse of my own handwriting described in a stranger's voice — *hand-ruled columns, owner unstated* — and the shame burned off like morning fog, and what remained underneath was cold and clear and entirely without self-pity.

Purpose. That's the word, though it sounds grander than it felt. It felt like a hand closing around a rail in rough water.

Because here is what I knew now, and knowing it changed everything: the ledger existed. It had not been burned, lost, or buried in a depot crate on a train south. It sat in Box Forty-One — I had seen the lot number at the column's head, I was nearly certain, nearly — in the ground-floor vault, catalogued, accessible to a corporal who was left alone with it on Sundays and bored enough to read aloud.

And here is what I also knew: I could not steal it. Not yet, not crudely. A missing item in an army inventory is a wound that gets examined. It would need to be done so it was never missed, or missed so late and so diffusely that no one could say when or how. That meant time. That meant Hobbes deeper in my pocket. That meant learning the vault's hours, the relief of its sentry, Rourke's Thursday appetite. It meant becoming, in earnest, the thing Larsen's spit had accused me of being — the friendly traitor, the collaborator's smile — except pointed the other way, and the difference between those two things would be invisible from the quay, and might be invisible even to me on a bad night.

I climbed the hill to my rooms. In the window of the shipyard below, one candle stub burned late — Anton working on whatever he was working on up there in the loft, refusing with his hands what he refused with his mouth. I could see the shadow of him pass across the glass, steady as a metronome. A man building boats in a country that had decided he was ballast.

I thought of the entry in my own hand. *A.W. — passage & protection, if ever needed.* Given. I had written it down because writing things down is how I keep faith with myself; a promise in the head is a wish, a promise in ink is a debt. I had never imagined the ink itself would become the danger, that the record of my honor would be the weapon against it.

Well. Then I would have to go and get it back.

Two cigarettes a week. A laugh at the right moments. Kind eyes for a girl in a photograph. It would take weeks, maybe, and the ice was coming, and somewhere south of the vault a train schedule was being written that did not include the Weiss family.

I took out my notebook — the thin public one, the one with grocery sums a search could safely read — and wrote, in plain figures a grocer might keep:

*Owed: 1 ledger. Interest accruing.*

Underneath, after a moment: *2 cartons, best quality. And patience.*

The candle in the shipyard loft went out. The fog closed over the water like a page turning, and I sat in the dark doing the only arithmetic that mattered: how many Sundays were left, and how many cigarettes it takes to buy a man's blindness, and whether I would recognize my own soul at the end of the transaction or whether it too would end up in a box, catalogued, *owner unstated*.
