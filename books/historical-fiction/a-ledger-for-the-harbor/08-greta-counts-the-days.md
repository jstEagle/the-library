## 8. Greta Counts the Days

Greta kept her numbers in the back of her spelling book, because nobody checked a spelling book twice.

She wrote them small, in pencil, in a column that ran down the margin beside the word *necessary*, which she had spelled wrong on a test three weeks before the soldiers came and which Miss Aldrich had corrected in red ink: *one c, two s's*. Greta could still see the red letters when she closed her eyes. Red ink had been the worst color in the world once. She knew better now.

The column went like this:

**Days since school closed: 19.**
**Soldiers counted from the loft window this morning: 11 (2 new).**
**Gulls on the customs house roof: 34.**
**Times Papa has said *soon* since Tuesday: 6.**

She did not write down everything. Some things she kept in her head, where they couldn't be found and read by anyone. The things in her head were the biggest numbers anyway.

*Days since Herr Brandt was taken from his shop: 4.*

That one lived behind her eyes.

* * *

The loft above the boat shed smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil and, underneath it, the river — that green iron smell that got into everything in their town, into clothes and hair and bread left out too long. Greta knew every plank of the loft floor by its sound. The third one from the ladder creaked even if you stepped soft. The one under the cot didn't creak at all but dipped, like a held breath.

She lay on the cot now with the spelling book propped on her knees, adding to the column by the gray light through the gable window. Morning arithmetic. It steadied her the way counting stitches had steadied her mother, back when there had been a mother to steady. You put a number on a thing and the thing stopped being the whole world and became only itself. Eleven soldiers was not an army. Eleven soldiers was eleven men who needed boots and bread and someone to shout at them in the morning, and shouting carried across water, and that was how you knew they were coming before you saw them.

Thirty-four gulls meant the fishing boats hadn't gone out. Gulls followed boats out and came back with them; thirty-four sitting birds was a harbor going nowhere, a harbor holding its breath along with everyone else.

Nineteen days since school closed. That number hurt worse than any of them, though she'd have said the soldier one if you asked her. School had closed the way a door closes — not slammed, just quietly shut, and then a notice pasted up saying enemy-language instruction was suspended pending review, and then no school at all, and then the review became a requisition and the requisition became men in khaki carrying out the desks four at a time, stacked like firewood, and Greta had stood at the fence and watched her own desk go, the one with her initials carved in the corner where Papa had done it with his smallest chisel, GW, and she had thought: *that is my name leaving without me.*

Six times Papa had said *soon*. Soon the boat would be finished. Soon they would have word. Soon, soon, soon — and each time he said it he looked at the window instead of at her, so Greta had started writing the word down instead of believing it. Six *soons* since Tuesday. If he got to ten she decided she would ask him what soon meant in actual days, because she was twelve years old and arithmetic was the one language nobody had taken from her yet.

Below her, the sound of the plane. Not an airplane — a hand plane, shushing along a plank of the ketch's hull, pause, shush again. Papa working by daylight now instead of candle stub, which meant the yard watch had changed and the new sentries were lazy ones, the kind who walked their post twice and then sat in the shed doorway with their collars open. Lazy sentries were worth maybe two hours of planing. Greta had learned to tell them apart by their whistles. One of them whistled a music-hall tune with all the wrong notes, and when she heard that tune she knew she could come down and fetch water without hiding.

She added to the column: **Lazy sentry today: yes.**

Under it, smaller: **Hull planks left: 9.**

Papa had told her that number himself, last night, in the dark, when she couldn't sleep and neither could he and the river outside made its long groaning sound like something enormous turning over in bed. Nine planks and then caulking and then paint if there was paint, and then — he had stopped there. He always stopped there. The sentence after *and then* was the one that mattered and the one he wouldn't say, and Greta had lain in the dark doing the sums herself: nine planks at two a day was five days, caulking two days, seven days, and seven days was not very many days at all, and after that came the part of the sum where you needed a boat crewed and a river open and luck, and luck was the one quantity that had never appeared anywhere in her book.

* * *

At noon she came down for water and found her father standing at the workbench with a letter in his hands.

Not a new letter. An old one, softened at the folds, the paper gone the color of weak tea. He held it the way he held his best chisels — careful, both hands, as though it might roll off the bench and be damaged beyond mending.

"Who's it from?" Greta asked, setting the pail down.

"A friend." He folded it away inside his shirt, quick, like a man pocketing evidence. "A good friend."

"The driver?" She had seen him only twice from the loft window, the man who came to the yard in the evenings before everything changed, the one who drove the motorcar for the suffrage lady. Freya Crane. Everyone knew the name; the Cranes had been on the river longer than the Weisses. "Is he bringing word about the boat? About going?"

Papa looked at her then, and she watched him decide something. She was getting good at watching people decide things. The sentries decided whether to bother walking their post. Frau Keller next door — or the woman who used to be next door, before the soldiers billeted there — decided each morning whether to hang washing where it could be seen. Decisions were everywhere now, happening behind faces, and Greta had learned to read them the way she read the tide marks on the pilings.

"He is trying," Papa said. "That is what I can tell you. He is a man who keeps his promises."

"You promised me we'd go." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out flat and hard, like a dropped tool. "You promised Mama's sister would take us. You promised before—" She stopped. Before was a country they didn't visit.

"I promised." He knelt so they were level, and up close she could see the sawdust in his eyebrows and the new lines around his eyes that hadn't been there in summer. Summer belonged to some other family. "Greta. Listen to me. A promise is like a keel. You don't see it under the water. But it is what keeps the boat upright when the wind comes sideways."

"That isn't an answer."

"No." Almost a smile. "It isn't. Answers are coming. First the planks."

He went back to the hull and she filled the pail at the rain barrel and climbed the ladder, and halfway up she stopped, because through the loft's other window, the one facing the road, she could see the square.

She could see the bakery. Or where the bakery had been.

The fire had been eleven days ago — she knew the number exactly, because she'd written it down that night with a shaking hand and the pencil had torn the paper: **Bakery burned: 1.** The Kessler brothers had run it, and their father had run it before them, and their German was the old village German full of words nobody else used, and someone had painted a word on the door first, a single word, and the next night there had been a smell like burnt sugar all down the street and then all night a light like a bad sunrise, and in the morning the Kesslers were gone and the word on the door was scorched black and still legible, and grown-ups had stood in the street looking at it the way people look at a grave.

Greta had looked at it too, from a distance, from the fence, doing what she always did: counting. One burned building. Two brothers missing. Zero people punished. That sum didn't balance. She had checked it four times and it never balanced, and she understood, with the cold clarity that arithmetic gives a child and spares an adult, that the sum was not supposed to balance. That was the point of it.

* * *

The question had been building in her for days, the way weather builds. She felt it in her chest like pressure before rain.

It came out on the fourth evening, in the dark of the loft, with the candle stub pinched out and the river groaning below and Papa's breathing not yet slowed into sleep.

"Papa."

"Mm."

"Is our name a bad word now?"

The breathing stopped. In the dark she heard him turn over, heard the cot rope creak, heard the long silence in which he was deciding — she listened to him decide, and this time the deciding took forever.

"Weiss," he said finally. "Anton Weiss. Your grandfather's name, and his father's before him, from a village with more apple trees than houses. It built half the boats in this harbor. It is not a bad word."

"But it's on the lists."

"Yes."

"On the bad lists. With the X's."

"Yes, Greta."

She stared into the dark where his voice was. "Frau Keller says names are just labels. Miss Aldrich said a noun is a person, place, or thing. So our name is a thing now. Things get seized." Her voice was doing something strange, staying level while everything under it shook, and she hated it and kept going, because the sum had to come out. "The warehouses got seized. The records got seized. Even Mr. Crane's book got seized, I heard Papa— I heard people say. So if our name is a thing on a list, they'll seize us too. That's the pattern. I've been counting the pattern."

Silence. Then the strike of a match, sudden gold, and Papa's face lit above the candle stub he wasn't supposed to burn during sentry hours. He cupped the flame and looked at her across it, and his eyes were wet in the light, which frightened her more than anything the soldiers had done, because fathers were not supposed to do that.

"Greta." His voice came out rough, like a plane catching against grain. "Listen very carefully, because this is the most important sum you will ever do. They can list your name. They can copy it into their books and stamp it and file it in their boxes. Do you know what they cannot do?"

She shook her head.

"They cannot count what you are. There is no number for it. Not for the way you keep tally better than any clerk in that customs house. Not for how you've kept watch over this loft like a little lighthouse keeper. Not for—" his voice caught, steadied, "—not for anything that matters. Their lists are small books, mein Schatz. You are not a small thing. Never let anyone's small book make you a small thing."

Greta nodded, because nodding was what was wanted, but inside she turned the words over like coins, testing their weight. She wanted to believe them the way you want a sum to balance. And mostly she did. Mostly.

But she kept her spelling book under the loose plank, and the next morning she added a line at the bottom of the column, pressing hard so it would last:

**Our name: 5 letters. Bad word: no.**

And below that, because honesty was the whole discipline of arithmetic:

**But the ice on the river: getting loud.**

* * *

It was true. The ice had begun to talk.

All that week it spoke in its sleep — clicks and sighs, a sound like a giant dragging furniture — and Greta lay awake cataloguing the noises, assigning each one a number of severity, until she had a scale of her own devising: one click, harmless; a groan, notable; the long tearing shriek that woke the whole loft on Thursday night, severe, unprecedented, requiring her to sit bolt upright with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

In the morning the river wore a new look. The ice that had lain solid and white from bank to bank for weeks now showed a dark seam down the middle, a thread of open water stitched through the white, widening hour by hour. Steam rose off it. The gulls were frantic over it, screaming, thirty-four of them wheeling like torn paper.

Papa stood at the big window a long time, plane forgotten in his hand, watching the seam.

"What does it mean?" Greta asked, though she already knew. She always already knew. Knowing was the easy part. Saying it aloud was the part that cost.

"It means winter is losing its grip." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white on the plane's handle. "It means the river will open."

"And when the river opens..."

"When the river opens, the navy's boats come up from the mouth. Patrols. Guns." He set the plane down with great care, as though putting away something dangerous. "And nothing goes up or down this water that they don't allow."

Greta took out her spelling book and opened it to the column. Her pencil hovered. Then she wrote, in her smallest, hardest letters, the number that had been waiting for her all week, the one all the other numbers had been walking toward:

**Days left: fewer than planks.**

Below the loft window, far down the quay, a woman in a dark coat stood at the rail looking at the broken ice — the suffrage lady, Edith Marlowe, the one whose voice could carry across a square without a megaphone. She stood there a long time, and then she turned and looked directly up at the shipyard, straight at the window, as if she could see Greta counting.

Then she began to walk toward the boat shed. Fast.
