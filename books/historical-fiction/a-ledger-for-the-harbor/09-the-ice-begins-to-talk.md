## 9. The Ice Begins to Talk

Edith Marlowe had learned to read weather the way other women read letters — by what it cost the sender. The sky over the harbor that morning was the color of unbleached linen, flat and patient, and the wind came off the water with a smell like iron filings and old rope. She stood at her study window with a cup of tea going cold in her hand and watched the river do something it had not done all autumn.

It groaned.

Not the small complaints of tide against piling, which she had heard every morning of her forty-nine years, but a long, structural groan, deep in the channel, as though the river were a floor under too much furniture. Out past the customs house, where the current slowed around the shoal, the water had gone the color of pewter and moved with a new reluctance, folding itself into plates rather than waves.

"Ice," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud had been her profession for twenty years and she saw no reason to retire from it in her own kitchen.

The first ice always arrived like this — talking before it showed itself. The old fishermen said the river spoke for three days before it froze, telling everyone who would listen what it intended to take away. Edith had heard it every winter of her life and had never once paid it any attention beyond pulling her coat closer. This year she listened the way you listen to a doctor repeating himself.

She set down the cup. On her desk lay the draft of a speech she would not give, because there was no platform left to give it from — the drill hall had cancelled, the market square permit had been "withdrawn pending review," and the Methodist hall's trustees had discovered, with remarkable synchronicity, a roof in urgent need of repair. She read it anyway, standing, because a speech unread rots faster than one delivered. It was a good speech. It was about citizenship, and about how a country that asked its women to sew bandages must sooner or later ask them what they thought about the war those bandages would prolong. It had lines in it she was proud of. It might never be spoken anywhere but this room, to an audience of one chair.

The groan came again, longer this time, and she heard something else beneath it: engines. Not fishing boats. The navy had taken to running launches upriver on patrol since November, gray hulls with a machine gun bolted to the foredeck, and their wakes broke the young ice into shards that rang against each other like dropped crockery. The river could not freeze properly because the war kept stirring it, and yet it kept trying, and the sound of that failure — ice forming and being broken, forming and being broken — was worse than silence.

Freya Crane was due at eleven. He still drove for her, though there was little driving left to do; the motor car sat in the coach house under a tarpaulin because petrol was requisitioned now, along with everything else, and Freya came on foot instead, carrying whatever needed carrying, reading whatever needed reading. She had noticed, over the years, that Freya never simply delivered a letter. He absorbed them. He knew the contents of every envelope that passed through his hands, not by opening them — or so she had chosen to believe — but by some faculty of his that made paper legible through the very grain of it. She had decided long ago that this was a useful quality in a man and none of her business otherwise.

He arrived at eleven exactly, stamping frost off his boots in the hallway, bringing cold air and the smell of coal smoke with him.

"You felt it too," he said, before he'd got his coat off. "Everyone's talking about it. The ice."

"The ice talks before it freezes. It always has."

"Not like this." He came into the study without being invited, which was one of the liberties she permitted him, and went to the window. "Marta Keller says her father used to say a hard early freeze is God closing a door. The men at the yard are watching the shallows. They say if it locks up solid by Christmas—"

"Then it locks up," Edith said. "And in spring it opens again."

"And when it opens again," Freya said, without turning around, "the navy runs the traffic."

She let the silence sit between them, because some truths are better heard than said, and then she said it anyway, because she had spent two decades learning that most people will not hear a thing until it is said twice.

"Listen to me, Freya. When the ice goes out — not freezes, goes out, breaks up, whenever that comes — the river reopens. And when the river reopens, every boat on it moves under naval control. Papers, manifests, inspection at the boom below the point. No one leaves this harbor by water after breakup unless the Admiralty wants them to. And no one leaves by land without papers either, because the roads have checkpoints now, and they're adding more. Whatever anyone in this town intends to do about the situation—" she meant the registrations, the internment orders, the names on the lists, and they both knew it— "they must do it while the ice holds. While everything is frozen and slow and half-asleep. Winter is the only door we get. Weeks, Freya. Not months."

He turned from the window. In the flat light his face looked older than his years, and she remembered suddenly that he was not so very old — younger than her youngest brother had been at Ypres, if her brother had lived to be anything at all.

"Weeks," he repeated. He said it the way a man repeats a figure of debt.

"Sit down. I want to show you something."

She took from her desk drawer a folder — plain, unmarked, the kind of folder that existed in every office in the country now, multiplying like mice. Inside were copies: the registration lists for the district, which she had obtained through a woman on the town council whose sister owed Edith a favor dating back to the mill strike. Names, ages, places of birth. She laid the pages out on the desk and watched Freya read them the way he read everything, fast and completely, his lips barely moving.

"Forty-one men," she said. "Registered enemy aliens in this harbor alone. Shipwrights, caulkers, riggers, three bakers, a piano tuner. Men who built half this waterfront. And the order stands that unmarried registrants report for transport to the camps by the new year. The married ones get 'reviewed' — which means the same thing, later, with more paperwork." She tapped the page. "Anton Weiss is on this list. So is his brother-in-law. So is the boy who swept your mother's step when you were small, if I remember the name right."

"Kessler," Freya said quietly. "August Kessler."

"August Kessler." She closed the folder. "I am telling you this not to frighten you but to put numbers on the fear. Forty-one names. Some will go quietly, because going quietly is all they've ever been allowed. A few will run, badly, and be caught at the road checkpoints and made examples of. And perhaps — perhaps — a handful can be moved properly, with planning, with boats, before the ice breaks and takes the option off the table. That is the arithmetic. I've done it three times since Tuesday."

Freya sat very still. She had seen him frightened before, once, during the dock strike, when a foreman's men had followed his car; he had driven calmly and talked calmly and afterward his hands shook for an hour where no one could see. His hands were steady now. That frightened her more.

"What are you planning?" he asked.

"I'm planning nothing. Planning is a word that gets women hanged." She smiled to soften it, and the smile did not work on either of them. "I'm noticing things. I notice that the Vane brothers still run eels downriver on moonless nights and nobody stops them, because the navy cannot search every reed bank between here and the estuary. I notice that Corporal whoever-he-is at the customs house is nineteen and bored and cold. I notice that the ice usually locks in the second week of January and breaks in March, and that this year the old men are saying it will lock early and break early, because the autumn ran cold and dry." She met his eyes. "I notice that you keep a ledger, Freya Crane, and that you know what everyone in this town owes everyone else, and that debts of gratitude can be called in faster than debts of money."

Something crossed his face — quickly, but not quickly enough. Guilt, or its cousin. She filed it away, as she filed everything, and pressed on.

"Whatever you're thinking of doing," she said, "don't do it alone, and don't do it stupidly, and above all don't do it slowly. The ice is the clock now. Every day it thickens, we gain cover; every day after that, we lose time. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you."

"You don't. Nobody does until it's too late, and then they write memoirs about it." She stood, went to the window beside him. Below, the street ran empty toward the harbor, and beyond the rooftops the river lay pewter-flat, groaning to itself. A navy launch chugged upstream against the current, breaking the young ice, and the shards rang and ground and refroze behind it in a wake that healed too slowly.

"Look at it," Edith said. "That's what they've done to everything. Break it up so it can't freeze, break it up so it can't flow. Keep the whole country in that state between ice and water where nothing holds and nothing moves. And they'll do it indefinitely. They have the coal for it. We don't." She turned. "Which is why the only honest question left in this town is: who do we carry across before the door shuts? Not whether. Who."

* * *

She walked out with him as far as the corner, wrapped in her good coat, because she had found lately that she could not bear rooms for more than a few hours at a stretch — the walls had begun to feel like the inside of a filing cabinet. Frost furred the pump handles. A boy swept snow off the chapel steps with great ceremony and no results.

At the corner they stopped, and Edith said, "There's one more thing, and I'll say it plainly because plainness is all I have left that works."

Freya waited.

"When they come for me — and they will come for me, Freya, the warrant may already exist in some captain's drawer, I've been told my file is 'active,' which is the nicest threat I've ever received — when they come, whatever plans are moving must not stop. You must promise me that. If I'm standing in front of a magistrate answering questions about sedition, somewhere north of here there should be a boat making water that wouldn't otherwise be making water. My arrest buys attention. Attention buys time. Time buys crossings. That's the only use I am now, and I intend to be useful."

"That's a horrible way to talk."

"It's the only way that's ever worked. Ask any general." She pulled her gloves tighter. "Promise me."

"I'm not promising that."

"Freya."

"I said I'm not promising it." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "You've spent twenty years teaching this town that promises are contracts. Well. I make contracts carefully."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence with its low complaint. Then a sound came from down the hill — boots, many, in step — and they both turned to watch a file of soldiers come along the quay road, twelve of them, breath steaming, rifles slung, escorting a cart toward the customs house. The cart carried document boxes, stacked and roped, and Edith understood with a cold drop in her stomach that she was looking at records — municipal records, shipping registers, perhaps more — being consolidated where the army could hold them. Paper flowing uphill. Everything the town was, being carried into a building with sentries at the door.

"They're collecting it," Freya said softly. "All of it."

"They collect everything eventually. It's the modern method." She kept her voice level, but her heart was going like a girl's. "You see why the ice matters now. It isn't just boats. It's that every week that passes, they know more and we can move less. The equation only runs one direction. Weeks, Freya. I'd give us eight. Six if the old men are right about the cold."

They watched the cart reach the customs house steps. Sentries took the boxes inside, one by one, unhurried, thorough, the way ants move a colony.

When the last box disappeared, Edith exhaled and turned back to him. "Go home. Think about who. Not whether — who. And Freya—" she caught his sleeve, and the frost on it crackled under her glove— "whatever you decide, decide it soon. The river's already started talking. It won't talk forever. After that it just closes its mouth, and everything on it belongs to them."

He nodded once, and went down the hill toward the harbor, and she stood a moment longer in the cold, listening to the ice groan in the channel — that long, structural sound, patient and enormous, like a door turning on hinges the size of the world.

Six weeks, she thought. Perhaps fewer.

She turned back up the street, and behind her the launch came downriver again, breaking the ice it had broken an hour before, keeping the river awake, keeping everything awake, forever.
