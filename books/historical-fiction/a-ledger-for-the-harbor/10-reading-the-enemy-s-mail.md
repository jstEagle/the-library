## 10. Reading the Enemy's Mail

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in army blue who didn't wait for a reply. *F. Crane to report to the customs house, 8 o'clock, Monday next. By order of the Provost Marshal. Bring no papers.*

Bring no papers. As if Freya Crane could be separated from papers the way you could separate a man from his hands.

He stood in the yard behind Edith's house and read the order three times, letting the words settle like silt. Then he folded it small and put it in his coat pocket, which was not bringing papers anywhere so much as carrying them home, where they belonged.

Edith found him there, still holding the pocket as though it might warm.

"You'll go," she said. Not a question. She had a way of stating futures that made them difficult to argue with.

"I'll go."

"Good." She came down the steps with her shawl over her shoulders even though it was August and the harbor was breathing heat off the water. "A man inside the machine is worth ten outside it. That's not philosophy, Freya, that's arithmetic."

"It's also treason," he said. "Depending which side of the desk I'm sitting at."

"Most things are." She looked out toward the river, where a gray patrol boat sat at anchor now, its gun crew visible even from this distance as two smudges of darker gray. "They'll want you because you can read. Remember that. They want your eyes, not your soul. Keep one for yourself."

* * *

Monday morning the customs house smelled different. Freya noticed it before he was through the door — the old smell of tar and ink and river damp had been buried under something sharper: carbolic soap, boot polish, the particular staleness of men who slept in shifts. The building hadn't changed hands so much as been digested.

Corporal Hobbes was waiting for him in what had been the harbormaster's office. Freya knew him by sight only — a narrow young man with spectacles that were always slightly too clean, the kind of clerk who filed the world alphabetically to keep from having to feel it. He had a stack of mail in front of him bound in twine, and he regarded it the way other men regard weather.

"Crane." He consulted a paper without much interest. "You're the one who drives for Marlowe."

"I'm the one who can read," Freya said. "That's what the order says."

Hobbes looked up then, and for a moment there was something almost human in his face — the flicker of a man who recognized another man being used. "It's sorting work. Censorship duty. Letters intercepted at the ferry landing and the rail junction, mostly. You read them, you classify them, you flag anything with military significance." He said the phrase *military significance* as if reading it off an interior placard. "Names of officers, movement of troops, prices of goods, complaints about rations. Anything about boats leaving or arriving. You understand?"

Freya understood. He understood perfectly, and the understanding sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. Every letter about boats leaving would pass through his hands. Every name of every man trying to move quietly through this occupied town would land on his desk first.

"When do I start?" he said.

"Now."

They gave him a table in the back room — the records room, formerly, where the town's deeds and shipping manifests had lived until the army crated them all and hauled them away. Now it held mail sacks and a rubber stamp and a ledger of classifications, and Freya sat down at a table where he had once helped Anton Weiss copy a bill of lading, and began to read the enemy's mail.

* * *

The letters came in bundles of forty or fifty, tied with twine, each envelope already opened by a blade that never quite cut straight. Most of them were nothing. A fishwife writing her sister about the price of herring. A boy at the training camp inland telling his mother the boots chafed and the sergeant was a brute and the food was worse than either. An old woman complaining that her lodger had joined up and left her his cat.

Freya read them all. That was the job. He read them the way he read everything — completely, hungrily, the habit of years — and he classified them in the army's categories with the rubber stamp feeling heavier each hour. *No military significance.* *No military significance.* *No military significance.*

Except that everything had significance. That was the vertigo of it. The fishwife's letter told him herring was up three cents, which meant the boats were running short crews, which meant which skippers had gone to war and which stayed. The boy's letter named his regiment and his train schedule and the fact that his mother kept money under a loose brick. The old woman's letter told him the lodger's cat was called Kaiser, and that she refused to change its name, and that the neighbors had stopped speaking to her for it — a whole war in eleven lines, fought over a cat.

By Wednesday he had read four hundred letters and learned more about the town than five years of driving for Edith had taught him. And by Thursday he understood the second thing, the thing that made his hands go cold on the twine:

The army was reading him too.

Not his mail — he got none — but his habits. Hobbes appeared at intervals with the bland regularity of a clock, watching how fast Freya worked, watching what he lingered over. There was a man at the door who pretended to be checking the stove but checked Freya instead. When Freya flagged a letter — a chandler's note mentioning that a certain wharf had been repaired, which was genuinely useful intelligence — Hobbes took it with both hands and a small nod, as if receiving a tithe.

"Good eye," Hobbes said. "Captain Rourke likes a good eye."

Rourke. The name went into Freya's memory, into the place where he kept names, which was the same place where he kept debts.

* * *

Friday afternoon, alone in the records room while Hobbes took his supper, Freya came upon a bundle that wasn't mail.

It had been misfiled — or not misfiled; simply left, the way busy men leave things they consider beneath notice. It was internal correspondence: memoranda, requisition follow-ups, a circular on fuel rationing for naval launches. Nothing marked secret. Nothing interesting, except —

Except that Freya had spent fifteen years reading letters, and a man who reads letters learns to read everything: the hand behind the handwriting, the pressure of the pen, what is written around the edges. And these memoranda were written by men who believed no one outside their own kind would ever see them. Their prose was naked. It listed, in passing, in the flat bureaucratic voice of men doing their jobs:

— the transfer of two informants from the ferry detail to the shipyard watch;

— the name of the boarding-house keeper on Marsh Street who reported "foreign-sounding gatherings";

— the fact that the Vane brothers' fuel purchases had been noted and were "under observation";

— and, on the last page, a routine acknowledgment that Box 41 of the customs vault remained sealed pending inventory review, per Captain Rourke's instruction.

Box 41. The number meant nothing and everything. Freya's confiscated ledger had gone into the vault with the municipal records; he'd watched the crates carried in himself. Somewhere in that building, twenty feet from where he sat, his own handwriting waited in the dark — every name, every debt, every promise he'd recorded in three years of quiet accounting. Half the town's secrets, catalogued in a book with his initials tooled into the cover.

He copied nothing that night. His hands wouldn't have obeyed anyway. But he went home along the water, past the shuttered shipyard where Anton Weiss's unfinished hulls sat under canvas like animals put out to winter, and he stood in the dark and did arithmetic of his own.

The army wanted his literacy. Fine. His literacy was his. What it read, it read for them; what it remembered, it remembered for himself.

* * *

It became a discipline, over the following week. Freya sorted mail by day and harvested it by night — not on paper, never yet on paper, but in the ledger behind his eyes. Names of men the army trusted. Names of men the army watched. The boarding-house keeper on Marsh Street, whose reports were quoted verbatim and were lies dressed as concern. The corporal at the rail junction who sold passes. The schedule of the fuel launch, regular as tides, Tuesdays and Fridays.

And once, folded inside a memorandum about dock labor quotas, a list. Twelve names. Men born elsewhere — Hamburg, Bremen, Danzig — employed at the yards, slated for "registration review." Anton Weiss was seventh on the list. The ink was fresh. The review was set for the week after next.

Freya sat very still for a long time after he read it, listening to the stove tick and the sentry shift his weight at the door. Then he did the most dangerous thing he had done since the war began, more dangerous than any letter he might carry: he tore a strip from the bottom of a discarded classification sheet, and with the army's own pencil, on the army's own paper, he wrote seven words:

*A. Weiss. Review. Week after next. Tell E.M.*

He folded it into the seam of his coat lining, where a man keeps what cannot be carried openly, and he stamped the day's remaining letters *no military significance* until the ink pad ran dry.

Outside, the light was going gold on the river, and somewhere north the ice was still months from forming, and Freya walked home through streets full of people who did not know that the quietest man in town was now the best-read man in it.

He had always measured the world by what fit in two hands. He was beginning to learn that the heaviest things weigh nothing at all.
