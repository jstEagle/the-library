## 11. The Ledger in Box Forty-One

The requisition inventory ran to four hundred pages, and Corporal Hobbes guarded it the way a dog guards a bone it has no intention of chewing.

Freya learned this on his third visit to the records annex, which had been the harbor master's office for sixty years and was now a room full of army filing cabinets and one bored man from a regiment whose insignia Freya didn't recognize and suspected Hobbes barely did either.

"Cigarettes again," Hobbes said, not looking up. He said it the way other men said *good morning*.

"Two packs this time." Freya set them on the desk beside a cold cup of tea and a half-finished crossword from a newspaper three weeks old. "The good ones."

"The good ones." Hobbes peeled the paper back, counted with his eyes, and nodded like a banker accepting a deposit. "You know I can't show you anything."

"I know."

"You're not even supposed to be in here. You're supposed to be sorting mail at the signal station."

"I'm here to collect a signed manifest for intercepted correspondence. Captain Rourke's orders." This was true, which was the best kind of lie: one that required no maintenance. Rourke had indeed ordered him to chase down a manifest. The fact that the manifest would take him past the inventory shelf, past Hobbes's desk, past every locked drawer in the building — that was simply the shape of the errand.

Hobbes sighed the sigh of a man who has decided, in advance, that his conscience will cost him nothing today. He pulled the inventory ledger across the desk, opened it to a ribbon bookmark he kept there for exactly this purpose, and turned it ninety degrees so Freya could read it upside down.

"Customs house seizure," Hobbes said, tapping a column. "Shipping registers, bond papers, pilot logs, tariff schedules. All of it boxed and numbered. They want it catalogued before spring, when the naval people arrive to pick through it."

Freya read. He read the way he'd been taught by Edith Marlowe without her ever knowing she taught him: fast, whole lines at once, nothing wasted. Box numbers, contents, condition notes. Box 12, shipping registers 1898–1904, damp-stained. Box 19, alien registration forms, partial. Box 23, correspondence of the Harbor Improvement Society —

His eye stopped.

Box 41. *Miscellaneous private papers, seized in sweep of Marlowe premises and associated persons.* And beneath it, in the clerk's careful hand, a fuller description added later, dated nine days ago: *One leather-bound notebook, handwritten, approx. 180 pages. Names, sums, dates. Referred to intelligence.*

*Names, sums, dates.*

Three years of his own handwriting, described in six words by someone who had never lived a single one of them.

"Anything you want?" Hobbes asked.

"No," Freya said. His voice came out level, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of being proud. "Nothing I need."

He took his manifest, signed the carbon copy, and walked out into weather that couldn't decide between rain and sleet. Behind him the door closed on its pneumatic hinge with a sound like a held breath released.

*Box 41.* He stood on the customs steps and let the number settle into him the way a stone settles into a coat pocket — small, specific, dragging everything down.

They had it. Of course they had it. He had watched them carry crates out of Edith's office, had watched a lieutenant flip through the town's municipal records with cotton gloves and an expression of mild distaste, and somewhere in all that watching he had let himself believe his ledger was too small to matter. A driver's notebook. Debts and favors. Who owed whom a sack of coal, a night's lodging, a word with the right man at the ferry office. It had never seemed like evidence. It had seemed like a memory he could hold in two hands.

But to a captain with a filing system, a list of names was a list of names. And half the names in his ledger belonged to men who were, as of September, enemies of the Crown by accident of their mothers' tongues.

*Approximately 180 pages.* Which meant they hadn't copied it yet, or the description would have said so. It sat in a box in a vault under a new lock, waiting to become testimony against everyone Freya had ever tried to help.

He went back to the signal station, sorted forty-one letters — he noticed the number, and hated noticing — and told no one anything.

* * *

That evening he walked out to the point where the breakwater met the shore, because it was the only place in town where a man could speak aloud without an audience, and even there he only mouthed the words.

Treason.

Not smuggling, which the Vanes treated as a family trade with better margins than fishing. Not forgery, which was a clerk's crime, punished with years. Treason — aiding the enemy in time of war — carried a wall and a firing squad and a name in the newspaper spelled correctly for once. He had seen the notices posted since autumn. He had driven Edith past them. She had read them aloud in a flat voice, once, and then said, *"Notice how they print the sentence but never the reason,"* and Freya had understood that she meant to give them a reason eventually, and that he loved her for it, and that loving her for it was going to get people killed.

Stealing a document from military custody. In wartime. From a vault.

He tried to make the arithmetic come out differently. If the ledger stayed where it was, intelligence officers would work through it page by page, matching names to faces, faces to addresses. Anton Weiss appeared in it eleven times — coal advanced against wages, a hull repair paid in installments, a note from two winters ago: *A.W. — passage north when needed. Promised.* Eleven entries. Each one a thread, and threads, pulled, unraveled outward. Greta's name was in it twice. The Vane brothers, thirty times. Edith herself, in entries Freya had written with the careless confidence of peacetime: *E.M. — £4 to Mrs. Okafor's rent, to be repaid when the fund comes through.* Charity, written down, now indistinguishable from conspiracy.

If it stayed, it hanged half the town slowly.

If he took it — if he got it out, burned it, scattered the ash in the river the way he'd once promised himself he'd never do to any record — then the crime was his alone. One man's neck instead of forty.

It wasn't a hard sum. That was what frightened him. Every honest part of him wanted it to be hard.

He stood until the light went off the water, then walked back into town with his collar up, past the sentry at the customs corner who nodded to him now, who knew his face, who thought of him — Freya was almost sure — as *the reader*, the useful literate one, harmless as furniture.

Let him think it, Freya thought. Let all of them think it. Furniture doesn't get shot.

* * *

Edith saw it in him before he said a word. She always did. Three days later, in the kitchen of the house on Marsh Lane where she now received visitors under the fiction of a sewing circle, she set down her cup and said, "You've found something."

"I've found where they keep it."

She waited. Edith's great talent, Freya had long ago concluded, was that she could wait without making it a weapon.

"The customs vault. Under the annex. My ledger is in a box with a number on it, Edith. Forty-one." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as a struck match. "Forty-one. I sorted forty-one letters the day I found out. I keep doing arithmetic like that lately. Like the numbers are trying to tell me something."

"What does it contain?" she asked, though she knew. She had watched him write in it across two years of committee meetings, had teased him once — *you keep books like a Scottish factor, Freya, we are revolutionaries, we are supposed to lose receipts* — and he had said, *a promise is a debt, and debts should be written down or they evaporate,* and she had gone quiet in a way he hadn't understood then.

"Everything," he said. "Everyone. Anton. The Vanes. Half the women in your circle. Me."

"And you intend to take it back."

"I intend to try."

Edith turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the chip in the rim with the spout, some old habit of order in a disordered season. When she looked up her face had the expression he privately called the platform face — calm, lit from inside, terrible.

"Do you know what they do to men who steal from the army?" she asked.

"I've read the notices."

"They don't post the ones about spies." She said it without drama, a woman reporting weather. "Those are quieter. A wall at dawn, a paragraph on page seven if the censor is feeling generous. I need you to understand that I am asking you not to do this."

"You're asking?"

"I am asking." She folded her hands. "And then I'm going to tell you why you'll do it anyway, and why you must. Because that book is the only honest account anyone keeps in this town. Because Anton Weiss built half these wharves and his name is in that box filed under *enemy*, and because if we let them define us by what they confiscate, then they have already won the only argument that matters." She paused. "Also, because I have no one else. You are the only person I know who reads everything and forgets nothing and is trusted by smugglers and suffragists and soldiers alike. That combination doesn't occur twice in a generation."

"That's not an argument, Edith. That's an inventory."

"It's both. Everything true is both." She reached across the table and put her hand over his, brief and hard, a handshake disguised as comfort. "Be careful. Be slow. Be boring. Boring men survive wars."

"Boring men also die of old age without having done anything," Freya said.

"Yes," Edith agreed. "Isn't it dreadful. Go home, Freya. Sleep. Start planning tomorrow, with a clear head, and don't tell me details — I'd rather be able to say honestly that I don't know, if it comes to that." Her mouth twisted. "I've discovered I'm a coward about one thing only, and it isn't the things you'd expect."

Walking home through streets gone dark under the blackout rules — half-observed, half-enforced, the town still learning to be afraid on schedule — Freya turned the problem over and over, and every road through it passed the same gate: the vault, the lock, the hour.

He knew the vault. Everyone who'd grown up on the waterfront knew the customs vault, because everyone's father had told them about it: poured concrete, iron door, floor below the high-water line so that even flood couldn't reach the bond papers. In peacetime it had held tobacco and spirits and the customs officer's private brandy. Now it held a town's paperwork and one leather notebook with a promise in it that Freya had made three years ago on a sunlit deck, lightly, the way promises are made when they seem like courtesies.

*A.W. — passage north when needed. Promised.*

He'd written it because writing it felt like honoring it. Now the sentence might hang Anton instead of save him. Words were ballast; you never knew which way they'd shift.

* * *

On Friday he went back to the annex, manifest in hand, cigarettes in pocket, and found Hobbes in an unusual state: upright, actually working, stamping folders with the grim satisfaction of a man granted a purpose after months of drought.

"Big day," Hobbes said. "Intelligence is coming Monday. Captain Rourke himself. They're pulling selected boxes for review." He lowered his voice with the pleasure of a man lowering it. "There's a list. Priority items."

Freya's mouth was dry. "Which boxes?"

"Can't say." Hobbes stamped a folder. Stamped another. Then, because two packs of good cigarettes buy a certain loyalty, and because boredom is the hungriest thing in any army: "But the vault's being reorganized Sunday night. New shelving plan. Everything comes out, gets renumbered, goes back in. Door stays open most of the evening while the transport corporal runs boxes up and down." He glanced up, and for a moment the dullness behind his eyes sharpened into something almost like warning. "Lot of traffic. Lot of confusion. You'd want to be nowhere near here Sunday night, Mr. Crane. Being a sensible fellow."

"Very sensible," Freya said.

"Monday," Hobbes went on, resuming his stamping, "everything's locked down proper again. New padlock on the vault besides the combination — Rourke's idea. After that, well." He shrugged, a small complete philosophy. "After that it's soldiers' business."

Sunday night. An open vault, a confused transport corporal, one evening of chaos before the new lock went on. After Monday, Box 41 would pass into Rourke's hands, and whatever pages Rourke read, he would read carefully.

Freya walked out of the annex into a thin bright cold, and the harbor lay before him grey and obedient, cranes stilled, ships at anchor under navy pennants, the whole waterfront holding its breath the way it had since September. Somewhere in that stone room beneath his feet sat three years of his own hand, waiting to be read by a man whose job was reading.

Two nights. One open door. And on the far side of the theft, if it succeeded: a fire, a river, ashes — and the knowledge that he would spend the rest of the war as the sort of man who could be shot for what he was about to do tomorrow.

He bought a second pack of cigarettes on the way home, and a third, and told the shopkeeper he'd taken up a filthy habit lately, and the shopkeeper said hadn't everyone.

Then he went home, cleared his kitchen table, and began, in pencil, on scrap paper that could be eaten or burned in seconds, to draw the vault.
