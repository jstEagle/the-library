## 12. Edith's Arrest Warrant

The file on Edith Marlowe opened, as all such files did, with a typewritten error.

She learned of it the way she learned most things in this town — sideways, through someone who shouldn't have told her and did anyway. Mrs. Aldous from the bakery, whose nephew clerked at the customs house, came in on a Tuesday morning with a wrapped loaf she hadn't ordered and a face like a closed door. She set the loaf on Edith's kitchen table, unwrapped it, and said nothing while Edith cut two slices. Then she smoothed the brown paper flat on the table and turned it over, and there, where the printing ink had bled through from the stack it had been separated from, was a fragment of a page: *Marlowe, E. — seditious association — rally of the 14th — witness statements pending.*

"That's my bread paper," Mrs. Aldous said. "It came back from the customs house wrapped round somebody else's forms."

Edith read the words three times. The ink had smeared where a wet hand had touched it, so that *seditious* looked half-drowned, which struck her as about right for the word itself — dragged out into daylight before it had finished becoming whatever it was going to be.

"Who signed the statement?" she asked.

"I couldn't say." Mrs. Aldous folded her hands. "Nephew says the file's thin. Says the captain over there wants more before he moves. But he's collecting, Miss Marlowe. That's what he does now, he collects. Names go in and they don't come out."

"Thank you for the bread."

"Thank you for nothing," Mrs. Aldous said, which between the two of them meant a great deal, and she let herself out through the kitchen door without looking back, because looking back was a habit that got people remembered, and being remembered just now was a dangerous thing to be.

Edith sat with the bread paper for a long while after the door shut. The stove needed wood. The kettle wanted filling. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something on the quay, and the harbor went on doing what harbors do — creaking, loading, lying about its weights — as though the world had not tilted six weeks ago and kept tilting since.

She should have felt afraid. She catalogued herself honestly, the way she catalogued everything: no fear. What she felt instead was a cold, clarifying anger, the kind that comes when a thing you predicted arrives on schedule and you find you still resent it. She had stood on that platform on the fourteenth and told four hundred people — no, she corrected herself, three hundred and forty, because patriotism had eaten sixty of them between the first announcement and her last word — that the vote was not a gift a government might withhold in wartime but a right that wartime made urgent. She had said that women were keeping the town fed while its men drilled with broom handles, and that a country which could trust women with hunger could learn to trust them with ballots.

She had watched faces turn away from her even as they nodded. That was the part she had not put into any speech: the nodding. They agreed with her and were ashamed of agreeing, and shame curdles fast into denunciation when an officer is taking notes.

Now the notes existed. Somewhere in the customs house, behind a lock installed by men who had never before needed one, a folder with her name on it grew fatter by the week, fed by witness statements from neighbors who bought her bread and smiled at her on the street.

She put the kettle on. While it heated, she took down the box where she kept the movement's papers — subscription lists, minute books, the treasurers' tallies — and began, methodically, to decide what a fire would eat and what a search would never find. This was not panic. Panic was for people who believed the state's power was unlimited, and Edith had spent thirty years learning precisely how limited it was. The state was a machine built of clerks, and clerks slept, and misfiled, and could be bribed with cigarettes and flattery and occasionally principle. But a machine did not need to be perfect to crush a single life. It only needed to be pointed.

The kettle sang. She made tea she didn't drink and waited for Freya Crane, because she knew he would come, and by noon his motorcar — requisitioned in all but name, painted the gray the army liked, driven now on army business and Edith's errands both, since Freya had never seen a reason to distinguish — pulled up outside with its usual sound of a large animal clearing its throat.

He came straight to the kitchen, coat still buttoned, snow melting off his shoulders, and he was holding the brown paper before she'd finished pouring the second cup. He read it the way he read everything, once, completely.

"When did this come?"

"This morning. Via Mrs. Aldous's nephew and a loaf."

"Witness statements pending." He set the paper down carefully, as if it might detonate. "That's Rourke. It has to be. He was at the rally; I saw him at the edge of the crowd writing."

"Everyone writes at rallies now. Half my audience was journalists until August. Now they're witnesses."

Freya sat down across from her. She watched him arrange his face into the expression he used for delivering bad news gently, and she interrupted before he could begin.

"You're going to tell me to leave."

"I'm going to tell you what I know," he said, "and then yes, I'm going to tell you to leave."

* * *

He laid it out with a driver's economy — distances, schedules, the geography of escape reduced to what fit in two hands, which was how Freya measured everything. There was a train Thursday morning if one could stomach the station and its new uniformed readers of manifests. There were the Vane brothers, who moved things north along the reeds for prices that rose weekly. There was, worst case, the coast road and a bicycle, though Edith noted he said *bicycle* the way other men said *amputation.*

"They haven't arrested you," Freya said. "Not yet. Which means you have days, possibly a week, in which leaving looks like prudence instead of flight. After the warrant, every hour you stay becomes evidence against you and against everyone who helped you."

"And everyone who helps me leave," Edith said, "becomes evidence too. You've thought of that. You've thought of everything except the actual argument, so I'll make it for you."

"Edith—"

"If I run," she said, "I confirm every word in that file. A woman who flees arrest for seditious association has confessed to sedition, and worse, she has confirmed that the movement is criminal — that it hides, that it scatters, that its leaders are fugitives rather than citizens. Do you know what happens to the subscription list if I vanish? Half of them tear up their cards by Friday. Not from cowardice — from arithmetic. They conclude that the price has gone up beyond what they can pay, and they're mostly right. A movement is not an idea. An idea survives anything. A movement is a hundred women deciding each week whether to keep paying, and the leader running away is how the payments stop."

"The leader imprisoned stops them faster."

"No," she said. "A leader imprisoned becomes a question asked aloud in every kitchen in this town. Why is she held? What did she say? People who never came to a rally start reading the speeches. I have been trying for fifteen years to get this town to listen to me. Captain Rourke is about to accomplish it for me, at public expense." She allowed herself the smile; it cost her something to spend it. "They can hold me without charge for a while under these new orders. Fine. Every day they hold me uncharged, the order looks less like law and more like fear. Fear is the one argument I've always known how to beat."

Freya turned his cup slowly on its saucer. She knew his silences; she'd taught half of them to him, or so she flattered herself, back when he was a dock boy with quick hands and a habit of reading letters that weren't addressed to him, which she had decided long ago was not theft but appetite. Now the silence went on too long.

"There's something else," he said finally. "You won't like it."

"Tell me anyway."

"They're confiscating correspondence. When they take someone, they take the letters. All of them." He met her eyes. "Everything anyone ever wrote you is going into an evidence room, Edith. Including things you may have forgotten you kept."

She thought about her boxes. The decade of correspondence. Letters from women in three counties, plans, grievances, money matters discussed with a frankness the tax man never saw because reform movements kept honest books but indiscreet sentences. And other letters. Older ones. She felt heat climb her neck and despised it.

"Burn them," she said. "Tonight. Everything personal. The organizational papers go to—" she stopped, recalculated. "No. The organizational papers burn too. We'll rebuild the lists from memory; memory doesn't get subpoenaed."

"I'll do it myself," Freya said. "You shouldn't be seen buying lamp oil twice in one month."

"Freya." She reached across and stilled his hand on the table. His fingers were cold from the drive. "Listen to me, because I'll only argue this once and then I intend to never discuss it again. If I go north, the movement here dies quietly, and quiet deaths are the kind that don't get avenged or even noticed. If I'm taken, the movement becomes loud. Loud is survivable. I have spent my whole life making noise, and I am very good at it. Let me be useful the only way left."

"You're talking about yourself like ballast," he said. "Something to be jettisoned so the rest of us float."

"I'm talking about myself like a platform," Edith said. "Platforms exist to be stood on. And someone will need to stand somewhere, Freya, when this war finishes teaching everyone what it thinks of the people it calls its own. When they come for the dockworkers next — and they will, the registration lists are already printed, I've heard — someone will need to have established, publicly, expensively, that this town contains at least one person who cannot be frightened into silence. Otherwise there'll be nobody left willing to say a word on anyone's behalf."

He looked at her then with an expression she couldn't immediately name, and it took her a moment to recognize it, because she had last seen it on Anton Weiss's face, down in the shipyard, three years ago, when a promise was being made that seemed cheap at the time. It was the look of a person watching a debt get larger.

"You promised passage once," he said quietly. "To a shipwright. Peacetime promise. And now look what keeping promises costs."

"Then stop making them you can't afford," Edith said, and squeezed his hand, and let him laugh, briefly, terribly, at both of them.

* * *

He stayed through the afternoon. They burned papers in the kitchen stove in careful batches — subscription lists first, then minutes, then a decade of letters — and Edith found she could watch her own history curl and blacken with less grief than she'd expected, because most of what mattered had never been written down. It lived in a hundred kitchens, in women who now knew how to run a meeting and would not unlearn it because a folder in a customs house grew thick.

Near dark, Freya loaded the ashes and carried them out to the garden himself, and when he came back he stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands, not leaving.

"Say it," Edith said.

"Thursday's train," he said. "If you change your mind, be on it. I'll drive you to the station myself and call it an army errand."

"I won't change my mind."

"I know." He put his cap on. At the door he paused. "There's a woman in the records office," he said, in the tone of a man deciding to spend a secret. "Under the new administration. Young, frightened, and I think reachable. Someone has to keep track of what goes into those files — names, dates, what they actually have versus what they invent. If it's going to happen, we should know it's happening."

"A spy."

"A reader," Freya said. "We need a reader on the inside."

Edith looked at him for a long moment — this young man who carried letters and read them and kept accounts of favors in a leather book, this boy she had armed with rhetoric and a motorcar and, apparently, a taste for dangerous work.

"Be careful," she said, meaning: *be indispensable, be invisible, be alive.* All the things she had just refused to be.

He nodded, and went out, and the motorcar cleared its throat and faded down toward the harbor.

Alone, Edith banked the stove and sat in the cooling kitchen. On the table lay the brown bread paper with its drowned, half-legible accusation. She smoothed it once more, then wrote across the top of it, in her clear campaign hand, the date and a single line: *Witness statements pending. So is mine.*

Then she took down the good bottle from the high shelf, poured one measure, and drank to the file with her name on it — to its thinness, to its growth, to the day it would be read aloud in a courtroom where she intended, God willing and clerks permitting, to be the loudest thing in the room.

Outside, down by the water, she could hear the river ice beginning to talk.
