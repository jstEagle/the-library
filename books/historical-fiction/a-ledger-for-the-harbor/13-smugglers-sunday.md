## 13. Smugglers' Sunday

The Vanes kept their Sunday like a religion with only one commandment: nobody asks where the money came from.

I had known that about them for years without ever needing to say it aloud. The whole town knew it and the whole town said nothing, because half the town owed them something — a crate of uncustomed tobacco in a lean winter, a bolt of cloth that arrived before it was ordered, a barrel of brandy that fell off no particular ship into no particular skiff. The customs men knew it too, and were paid to forget it on a schedule as regular as the tides. The harbor ran on shipping, and shipping had always run a little on the dark side of the ledger. The Vanes were not an aberration. They were ballast.

But that was before the army came, and the army did not take its cut and look away. The army wrote things down.

So when I walked out to the salt marsh road past the ruined bakery on Sunday morning, I was not going to see old acquaintances. I was going to see men who might hang me, or save me, depending on how the wind sat, and I could not control the wind.

* * *

The bakery was still burned. It had been burned since September, when a mob with more fear than malice decided that Herr Brandt's shop was a German shop and therefore fair kindling, never mind that Brandt himself had been born three streets from the church and had baked bread for every one of those men who stood watching it go up. Nobody had been charged. Nobody would be. The shell of the place stood at the bend in the marsh road with its chimney pointing at the sky like a finger asking a question no one wanted to answer.

I passed it the way you pass a grave you don't have time to visit properly — hat off, eyes down, moving.

Beyond it the reeds began. They ran for a mile along the river's slack side, gold-brown and taller than a man, and somewhere inside them lay the channel the charts didn't show. The navy's patrol boats drew six feet. The Vane boats drew eighteen inches when they needed to, which was whenever the navy happened to be looking. The brothers had spent thirty years learning what the reeds knew, poling flat-bottomed skiffs through water that appeared on no survey, and the army's arrival had changed nothing about their knowledge and everything about its price.

Their yard sat on a hummock where the marsh firmed up: two sheds, a slipway gone gray with weather, and four hulls pulled up on the mud like sleeping animals. Smoke came from behind the larger shed, which meant the stove was lit, which meant they were home.

I stopped at the edge of the reeds and made sure my hands were visible and empty. You didn't approach the Vane yard any other way, even in peacetime. Especially now.

"Sunday," said a voice from the reeds to my left, close enough that I flinched. "Church is in town."

"Church is occupied," I said. "I'm looking for Silas."

"Silas is looking for nothing today. Nothing finds him on Sunday."

"It found him anyway," I said. "It's standing right here."

A long silence. Then a laugh I recognized — low, unhurried, the laugh of a man who has never once been late and never once been early — and Tobias Vane rose out of the reeds ten feet away with a shotgun broken open over his arm, purely ceremonial, the way a bishop carries a crozier. He was fifty and built like a bollard, with his father's red beard going to iron and his mother's pale eyes that took your measure and entered it nowhere.

"Freya Crane," he said. "The letter man." He looked past me down the empty road, then at my hands, then at my face, doing all three slowly enough that I understood he'd done them twice already before I saw him do them once. "You're carrying something heavier than letters."

"I need passage north. Two people. Before the ice breaks."

Tobias didn't move. Somewhere in the reeds another shape shifted — Silas, then, the younger by four years and quieter by forty. "Two people," Tobias said. "Names?"

"You know one of them already. Anton Weiss. The shipwright."

"I know him. Good hands. Built my father's last boat, may she rest wherever the customs men scuttled her." His face gave back nothing. "And the other?"

"His daughter. Greta. She's twelve."

"And why does a shipwright and a child need to go north in November on a boat that isn't mine?" he asked, mild as milk, and I understood this was the whole negotiation happening in miniature: he wasn't asking for information, he was asking whether I trusted him with it, and my answer would price everything after.

"They're registered enemy aliens," I said. "Anton got his order. Camp inland, indefinite. He's hiding in the shipyard loft. When the requisition sweep reaches the shipyard — and it will, there are government contracts on those hulls — they'll find him. After the ice breaks, the river runs under naval control and there's no way out until next winter. By next winter anything could have happened to him. To her."

"Anything usually does," Tobias agreed. He walked past me toward the sheds, gesturing me along beside him, which was either trust or a way to keep me where he could see me. With the Vanes it had always been both. "Walk. Talk. What's the army paying informers these days? Asking for a friend."

"Nothing. I don't inform."

"Everyone informs now. That's the new economy. Half my neighbors would sell my moorings for a ration card and think themselves patriots for the discount." He kicked open the shed door and the warmth rolled out, coal smoke and tar and the sweet smell of fresh-cut pine. Silas stood at the stove with his back to us, a thinner copy of his brother with the same eyes and none of the patience. On the workbench between them lay a chart of the river, weighted at the corners with shell casings, marked up in pencil.

"Tell him," Silas said, without turning around. "Tell him what happens to smugglers who carry spies."

"He's not a spy," Tobias said.

"He wants to move an internment-order man across water the navy patrols. That's smuggling a spy by any name the tribunal uses."

"The tribunal can use any name it likes," Tobias said. "That's rather the point of tribunals." He poured something brown into two tin cups and handed me one, and I drank it because refusing would have cost more than the rotgut. "Here's where we stand, Crane, and I'll be plain because plainness saves time and time is the one cargo we can't run. We'll take passengers north. We've done it twice since September."

I hadn't known that. I let it show on my face, because with these men pretending not to be surprised was a form of lying and they priced lies higher than anything else.

"Twice," Tobias confirmed, enjoying it. "A family of three in October, out through the eastern reeds on a night so black we steered by the smell of the water. And before that, a customs officer who'd gotten ambitious about honesty and needed to be elsewhere quickly. Both arrived. Both paid." He held up one thick finger. "Paid. That's the word that matters. This isn't charity, and it isn't politics. I don't care about the war. I care that the war has made my business stupid. Every boat we own gets searched now. Every cargo gets weighed against some clerk's list. The reeds still know us but the revenue men have gunboats, and gunboats don't read tide tables, they just sit on top of the channel and dare you. So whatever we do now costs triple and risks tenfold, and the price reflects it."

"How much?"

He told me.

I put the cup down carefully, because my hand had developed opinions. The number was enormous — not extortionate, that was the terrible part; I could see the arithmetic in it, fuel, risk, bribes at the northern landings, the replacement value of a boat if she was taken. It was honest piracy. And I could not pay a tenth of it.

"That's not a price," I said. "That's a ransom."

"It's a market," Tobias said. "You came to it freely."

"I came to you because there's no one else."

"Yes," he said, and for a moment the pale eyes were almost kind. "That's also the market."

* * *

Silas turned from the stove at last. He'd been listening with his whole body the way he always did, and now he crossed the shed and stood over the chart, and I watched the brothers conduct one of their silent conversations — a flick of the eyes, a tilt of the head, thirty years of shared nights on the water compressed into a second.

"Ask him what he actually has," Silas said. "Because he's got something. Look at him. Men with nothing beg. He's calculating."

He had me dead to rights. I had been calculating, the way I calculated everything, turning over what I carried and what it weighed. My ledger was in Box 41 in the customs vault and I couldn't spend a page of it. Edith was under watch, her money frozen with her movement. What I had was a job sorting other people's secrets, a promise to a shipwright, and a conscience that had lately begun to feel like ballast I couldn't jettison without capsizing.

"I have access," I said slowly. "To information. Not to sell—" Silas snorted — "but to trade. I see the army's mail. Intercepts. Patrol schedules, sometimes. Cargo manifests."

Silas looked at Tobias. Tobias looked at the ceiling as though consulting it.

"Patrol schedules," Tobias repeated. "Now that's currency."

"Tobias." Silas's voice had an edge I hadn't heard from him before. "That's treason with a capital T. Moving a family across the river, a magistrate might call that humanitarian, might fine us, might even wink. Selling the navy's schedule to whoever asks? That's a cell on Dartmoor."

"I'm not selling it to whoever asks," I said. "I'm selling it once. One schedule, one window, one crossing. You use it and it's used. There's nothing left to trace back to me except the absence of a page."

"The absence of a page," Silas said, "is exactly what they look for."

The stove ticked. Outside, a gull worked the wind over the marsh, crying its one opinion over and over. I thought of Anton in the loft with his candle stubs, planing wood by ear in the dark because a lit lamp at the wrong hour would bring soldiers. I thought of Greta counting days. I thought of Edith saying, *the movement dies if its voices scatter*, and how she'd refused to scatter, and how everyone I loved seemed constitutionally unable to run while there was still something to stand in front of.

"I'll get you the schedule," I said. "Not today. A few days. In exchange—"

"In exchange for nothing," Tobias said. "In exchange for the difference between your number and zero, which is most of it. But hear the terms, Crane, because the terms are iron. First: payment on delivery, before my keel touches northern mud. If your information is stale or false, we turn around and you owe the full freight anyway. Second: we take them from a point and time of our choosing, not yours, and if they miss the window we don't wait. Third—" and here he leaned forward and the mildness went out of his voice entirely, leaving something older and colder, something from the deep water under all his manners — "if any of this comes back on us, if your name appears in any file next to mine, I will deny you so thoroughly and so loudly that they'll believe me, and I will do it while you're being questioned, and I will sleep afterward. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am not your friend. I am a man with a boat. Friendship sank in August with the passenger ships."

"I understand," I said. And I did. It was, in its way, the most honest thing anyone had said to me since the war began.

"Then we're agreed in principle," Tobias said, and shook my hand, and his grip was dry and hard as wharf timber. "Bring me something worth reading and we'll talk numbers again. Real ones."

I walked back along the marsh road with the wind at my back and the burned bakery ahead of me, and I felt the shape of what I'd just done settle onto my shoulders like wet canvas. I had traded one duty for another. The army paid me to read its mail and I intended to rob it — not of silver, worse, of certainty, of the comfortable belief that its paper walls had no windows. And I hated it cleanly, the way you hate a necessary amputation, knowing the wound is clean and knowing you will feel the missing piece in cold weather for the rest of your life.

The chimney of the bakery pointed its question at the sky. I put my hat on and kept walking, because the answer was behind me in a shed, waiting on a patrol schedule that existed somewhere in a file I was permitted to touch and forbidden to want.

Four days, I told myself. Four days to find it, copy it, and get it to the reeds before the ice finished talking itself into breaking.

I didn't know yet that I had four days for everything else, too.
