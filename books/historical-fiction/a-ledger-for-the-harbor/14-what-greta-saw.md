## 14. What Greta Saw

Greta Weiss had a system for counting things, and the system was this: everything could be counted if you were patient enough.

She counted the soldiers on the corner by the customs house — four at dawn, six by noon, and one who spat sunflower shells into the gutter and never once looked up when she passed. She counted gulls: forty-one on the breakwater Tuesday morning, seventeen after the navy boat went by, which meant thirty-one had somewhere better to be. She counted days since school closed — eleven, then twelve, then a number she kept in her head like a stone in her pocket because Papa said not to write things down anymore, *not even sums, Liebling*, and so her arithmetic lived in her skull now, all of it, whole columns of it, marching in rows behind her eyes like the soldiers marched on the quay.

She was good at numbers. Herr Brandt had said so, back in September, before September meant anything. *You have a head for figures, Fräulein Weiss.* He'd said it in front of the whole class and she had gone red as brick and loved him for it.

Herr Brandt's shop was the chandlery two doors from the bakery. He sold rope and tar and lamp oil and the little brass fittings that made Papa's boats sing, and he let Greta wind the clock on his counter because no one else had the patience to do it slow, the way the old mechanism liked, three turns and a rest, three turns and a rest.

On Thursday — day thirteen since school closed, forty-two steps from their loft door to the bakery, six gulls on the awning — Greta went to buy bread because Papa was hiding in the shipyard loft and couldn't be seen buying anything, and Mama was gone to her sister's in the valley where the trains didn't stop for soldiers, and someone had to carry coins through the town and come back with bread, and that someone was Greta, twelve years old, with a head for figures.

The bakery was warm and smelled the way it always smelled, like yeast and scorched sugar, and Frau Lindqvist gave her the end piece without being asked because Frau Lindqvist believed children standing in line deserved the crusty part. Greta was counting the coins into her palm — seven pfennig, eight with the bent one that nobody refused — when the bell over the door rang wrong.

Not the shop bell. A boot against glass.

Two soldiers came in, and behind them came a man Greta knew in the way you know everyone in a harbor town, by face and trade and the smell of their work: Herr Aldous Meyer, the watchmaker, whose shop sat between the bakery and the chandlery, whose windows held forty clocks all ticking slightly out of step so that walking past felt like being inside a heart that couldn't decide its rhythm.

He wasn't supposed to be here. That was the first thing Greta understood, before she understood anything else. Herr Meyer belonged behind glass, among clocks. He was a thin man made of elbows and spectacles, and his hands were always gray with metal polish, and he had once fixed Papa's chronometer for free because Papa had helped him carry a grandfather clock up three stairs and complained about it cheerfully the whole way.

One of the soldiers had Herr Meyer by the collar of his coat. The coat was tearing at the shoulder seam and making a small sound, thread by thread, a sound Greta would hear later whenever she tried to sleep, a sound smaller than shouting and worse.

"*Ausweis*," the soldier said. Papers. Show papers.

"I have shown my papers." Herr Meyer's voice was high and careful, a voice trying to be reasonable inside a situation that had eaten reason. "I registered. I have the card. It is in my shop, I can fetch—"

"Fetch nothing. You're wanted for questioning."

"For questioning," Herr Meyer repeated, and Greta watched him do arithmetic of his own, the terrible kind, the kind where every sum comes out wrong. "What — what is there to question? I repair clocks. I have repaired clocks in this town for nineteen years. I wound the church tower clock, ask anyone, ask Pastor Ehrlich, ask—"

"Move."

The second soldier took his other arm. And Herr Meyer, the man of clocks and elbows, did the thing that Greta would count afterward, again and again, because counting was how she kept things from dissolving: he reached out one hand toward the counter, toward Frau Lindqvist, toward anyone, and his hand closed on air, and his fingers spread open like something letting go of a branch.

Frau Lindqvist said, "He's a watchmaker, for God's sake. Look at him. Look at his hands."

"He's a German," the first soldier said, in the flat tone of a man reading a form aloud, and Greta understood that the word had changed shape. It used to mean Herr Meyer's accent, his stollen at Christmas, the way he said *gut, gut* when a repair pleased him. Now it meant a category on a list, and the list was in the soldier's pocket, and the pocket was stronger than nineteen years.

They walked him out. The bell rang properly this time, a small bright sound, obscene.

Greta stood with her bread and her coins. Frau Lindqvist put both hands flat on the counter and looked down at them. Nobody spoke. The ovens ticked as they cooled, and outside, forty clocks kept ticking too, out of step, out of step, and somewhere among them the church tower clock that Herr Meyer climbed a ladder every Sunday to wind was running now on time already spent, unwoundable, going down.

Greta counted her way home. Forty-two steps. She got to nine and stopped.

* * *

Papa saw it in her face before she got the loft ladder halfway up.

"Greta." He came down off his stool, wood shavings clinging to his forearms, candle stubs lined up on the bench like little soldiers of their own. "Liebling. What happened."

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

It wasn't that words weren't there. Words were there, crowded, pushing — *they took Herr Meyer, they tore his coat, his hand closed on nothing, the bread cost eight, the bread is still in my hand* — but they jammed in her throat like a fouled line, like the winch rope that one time on Papa's ketch, knotted where no knot should be, and Papa had said don't pull, don't ever pull against a foul, you'll only make it fast; you have to find where it caught and work it loose gently or cut it clean.

She didn't know where hers had caught. She didn't know how to cut it.

So she stood at the bottom of the ladder holding bread, and her mouth stayed open, and no sound came, and Papa crossed the loft floor in three strides and took her by the shoulders, and whatever he saw in her face made him go the color of the raw planks stacked against the wall.

"Who?" he said. "Greta. Who did they take?"

She pointed. Not with her finger — with her whole body, a stiff turn toward the harbor side, toward the row where the shops stood. Toward the chandlery, which was next to the bakery, next to the watchmaker's.

Papa closed his eyes. "Meyer," he said, very quietly, in the voice he used for naming the dead.

Then he knelt down in the shavings and held her, bread and all, and she pressed her face into his shoulder and breathed the smells of his work — pine, pitch, linseed — and waited for herself to start crying, because this seemed like a crying thing, this was clearly what crying was for, and it would not come. There was just the knot in her throat and the numbers behind her eyes, marching.

That night she lay on the pallet under the window and listened to the town. She had always been able to fall asleep to the harbor sounds — halyards clinking, the foghorn clearing its throat, drunk sailors arguing musically about cards. But now the sounds had categories too. Boots were a category. Every set of boots on the cobbles below came with a question her body answered before her mind could: ours, or theirs. Ours was loose and staggering, singing half a song. Theirs was even, four beats, four beats, four beats, metronomes, and she thought suddenly and horribly of Herr Meyer's forty clocks, all those pendulums keeping time for a man who could no longer keep any.

She did not speak on Friday. Or Saturday.

Papa didn't push. That was the thing about Papa — everyone in town thought he was stubborn because of the boats, because he'd argue tide tables with the harbormaster until grown men surrendered, but with her he was patient as seasoning wood. He talked to her instead, an easy stream, filling the silence the way you'd fill a crack with pitch: about the planks he was steaming, about how a keel was a promise a boat makes to the water, about how her mother used to eat the heel of the loaf first because she claimed it was the best part and Papa had married her partly out of suspicion of that claim. He left spaces in the talking. Spaces shaped exactly like answers, where she could have set a word down if she'd had one.

She had none. She had numbers.

By Monday the count was: days since school closed, eighteen. Days since Herr Meyer, five. Words spoken aloud by Greta Weiss, zero.

* * *

They had to go past the bakery to reach the chandlery, and they had to go to the chandlery, because Papa was building a boat in secret in a loft and a boat wants oil and rope and brass, and there was nowhere else in the town to get them, and there was nowhere in the world Papa would rather not go than that street, and he went anyway, carrying her on his shoulders.

"Piggyback tax," he called it, from when she was small. She was too big for it now, really, nearly twelve, all arms and knees, but neither of them mentioned that. Her chin rested on top of his head. From up there she could see farther than anyone: the masts, the cranes, the gray water, the roofs.

The bakery came into view.

Or the place where the bakery had been.

It was burned. Not ruined — burned, gutted, the windows black holes, the awning a charred fringe, the smell of it still live in the air, wet ash and burnt sugar fused into something that would never fully leave the street. Someone had thrown a lamp through the glass two nights ago. Everyone knew who, and everyone knew why — because Frau Lindqvist had said *look at his hands* to a soldier, because she'd given the crusty end pieces to children regardless of surname, because her late husband had been born in Lübeck and dead for eleven years and none of that mattered either now. The fire brigade had come, people said. They had come slowly.

A notice was nailed over the door. Greta read it upside down from Papa's shoulders, her head for figures finding the letters easily enough: CLOSED BY ORDER. PENDING INVESTIGATION. The ink was official. The ash underneath it was not.

Papa stopped walking. Just for a moment — two seconds, three. She felt it in his spine, the pause traveling up through his shoulders into her chest, the way she could feel a boat hesitate when it touched bottom. Then he walked on, steady, past the burned windows where the display shelves had melted into shapes like drowned things, past the notice, past the place where Frau Lindqvist had handed out crusts to whoever queued, which had apparently been a crime all along that only now got enforced.

Halfway down the block, an old woman was scrubbing the pavement in front of the chandlery. Not cleaning it — it wasn't dirty — scrubbing it anyway, on her knees, with a brush and a bucket, working at cobblestones the way you'd work at a stain in a family name. She was one of the neighbors, one of the German ones, and she scrubbed and scrubbed and did not look up at them, and Greta understood, in the wordless part of her that was doing most of her understanding lately, that the woman was trying to wash the street clean of the morning Herr Meyer had been dragged across it, and that she knew it couldn't be done, and that she was doing it anyway, and that this was the whole country now, kneeling on its own stones, scrubbing.

Papa shifted her weight and murmured, without turning his head, "Don't look long, Liebling."

But she had looked. Looking was done. It was in her now with all the other counted things: four soldiers at dawn, six by noon, forty-one gulls become seventeen, thirteen days, five days, zero words, one hand closing on air, one coat tearing thread by thread, one bakery breathing smoke into a sky that hadn't asked for it.

In the chandlery, the new owner — not new, Mr. Collier, English, who had bought the stock cheap last month when the requisition orders started — wrapped Papa's rope and lamp oil in silence. His eyes flicked to Papa's face, then away, then to the window, then to the door. Everyone's eyes did that now. Everyone was practicing being furniture.

Papa paid. He carried the parcel under one arm and Greta on his shoulders, and they went home the long way, around by the water, past the breakwater where the gulls were — she counted without meaning to, twenty-three — and when they got to the foot of the loft ladder he lifted her down and turned her around and crouched to her level, and his hands were shaking, she noticed. His hands, which could fair a curve by eye and drive a nail flush in the dark. Shaking.

"You don't have to talk," he told her. "You hear me? Not to me, not to anyone, not until the words are ready. Silence isn't broken, Greta. Silence is a plank. You hold it until you're ready to lay it, and then you lay it true."

She looked at him. Behind his eyes something was moving fast, some arithmetic of his own — days left, ice coming, a boat half-built, a daughter gone quiet as ballast — and she watched him finish the sum and hide the answer, the way parents do, badly.

"But if you see anything," he said, softer, "anything at all — soldiers near the yard, men asking questions, boots where boots shouldn't be—" His thumb moved along her jaw. "You tell me anyway. Any way you can. Knock twice. Write it. Point. Scream the roof down if you must. Can you do that for me?"

She nodded.

He kissed the top of her head, where her chin usually rested, and went up the ladder, and she stood below in the dark of the boathouse smelling pitch and cold water, holding her silence around her like the coat they'd torn off Herr Meyer, except hers was intact, hers was hers, and no one could take it unless she chose to give it.

Three nights later — days since school closed, twenty-one; days since Herr Meyer, eight; words spoken, zero — Greta woke to boots.

Not on the cobbles. In the yard. Even, four beats, four beats, more than one pair, and lantern light swinging through the slats of the loft wall in yellow bars that crawled across the ceiling above her pallet like searchlights looking for something.

Below the loft, in the boathouse, the unfinished ketch stood under its tarpaulin. In the loft, her father lay frozen on his cot, and she could hear him not breathing from across the room. On the bench beside the door sat the ledger-thought of everything they were: the candle stubs, the tools, the boat, the name *Weiss* stitched into her one spare coat hanging on its nail.

And the lantern light stopped moving, and a voice said, low, to another voice: "Someone's been working in here."

Greta sat up in the dark. Her throat was full of knots, a whole winter of them, fouled lines everywhere — and she thought of Papa saying *don't pull, work it loose or cut it clean*, and she thought of Herr Meyer reaching for a hand that wasn't there, and she thought: *not Papa. Not the boat.*

She opened her mouth, and out of the knot, past the fear, up from wherever the words had been waiting all these weeks, came her voice — rusty, cracked, too loud in the dark — speaking her first sentence in eight days, and it was not her own name, and it was not a number.

It was a warning.
