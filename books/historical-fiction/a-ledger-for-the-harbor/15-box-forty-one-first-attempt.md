## 15. Box Forty-One, First Attempt

The volunteering was the easy part. That was the thing nobody understood about treason — it didn't announce itself with trumpets. It came in through the ordinary door, wearing an ordinary face, and it sounded like this: *I'll take the night shift, Corporal. You look half dead.*

Hobbes had looked half dead for a week. The requisitioned customs house ran on cigarettes and fear now, and Hobbes was running low on both. When Freya offered — casually, the way you'd offer to carry a crate — the corporal's relief arrived on his face before his suspicion could catch it.

"You want to file? At night? Voluntarily?"

"I can't sleep," Freya said. "Might as well be useful."

It wasn't even a lie, which was the best kind of cover. He hadn't slept properly since he'd seen the inventory list, since he'd run his finger down that typed column and stopped at line fourteen like a man stopping at a grave.

*Box 41. Ledger, leather-bound, approx. 200 ff., handwritten. Contents: names, sums, correspondence fragments. Seized from Crane, F., driver.*

His own handwriting, catalogued by clerks who could read it. Two hundred pages of debts and favors and promises — half the harbor's quiet economy, written down by a man who had once believed that keeping accounts was a virtue. Every dockworker who owed him a kindness. Every favor Edith had asked. Every name he'd recorded the day the registration forms came out, when he'd copied down the real names of men the army had turned into categories — their birthplaces, their boats, their daughters' ages.

If Rourke's intelligence people read it the way Freya had written it — honestly, thoroughly, in the habit of a lifetime — then Anton Weiss was already a name on a transport manifest, and Greta was a line item, and Edith Marlowe was the hub of a wheel drawn in Freya Crane's own careful hand.

So: night filing duty. One door between him and Box 41.

* * *

The customs house at night was a different building. By day it was all boots and barked orders, soldiers hauling crates of seized records down corridors built for merchants' polite paperwork. At night it settled into itself like an old ship at anchor. Gas lamps hissed at intervals. Somewhere above, rain worked at the slate roof with patient fingers.

Freya had filed for three hours before he let himself move toward the vault corridor. Three hours of alphabetizing intercepted letters he'd already read copies of, sliding folders into drawers marked in a hand that belonged to a government that hadn't existed in peacetime. His fingers did the work. The rest of him stood in the dark corridor of his own skull, looking at a box.

The vault door was at the end of the west passage, behind the room where they kept the customs ledgers going back forty years — the town's commercial memory, now technically contraband. There was a desk outside the vault where the night clerk sat, except tonight there was no night clerk; Hobbes had arranged that too, with a shrug and a mumbled story about a man down with fever. One less pair of eyes. Freya hadn't even had to pay for it. He suspected Hobbes would eventually present a bill, and he suspected he would hate what it cost.

He passed the desk. He walked the last thirty feet of corridor.

And stopped.

The lock was new.

He knew locks the way Anton knew wood — not professionally, but intimately, the way a man knows a thing he's depended on. The old vault lock had been municipal, elderly, a brass-faced thing installed when the customs house was built, the kind of lock a competent man could persuade with patience and a bent wire. Freya had stood in front of it twice this week, memorizing it, letting his hands learn its geography in advance the way you learn a route you mean to travel blind.

This one was military. Black iron, squat, a lever mechanism behind a shielded keyhole. Stamped on the escutcheon, small and smug: a maker's mark from a Birmingham foundry. War manufacture. The kind of lock designed by men whose entire profession was assuming someone like Freya was coming.

He put his hand flat against the cold iron anyway, as if touch might tell him something sight hadn't. It told him only that the metal was colder than the air, which meant the vault beyond held the night's chill like a hold full of ballast. Behind that door: forty boxes of confiscated lives. Box 41 sat among them with his handwriting inside it, patient as a loaded gun.

*Patience,* he told himself. *A lock is only a delay. Come back with time. Come back with tools.*

But even as he thought it, he heard the footsteps.

They came up the east passage with the uneven rhythm of a man who had been drinking — not falling-down drunk, but drunk the way sentries got when the nights were long and nothing ever happened. Freya had three seconds. He used them badly. Instead of retreating down the dark corridor toward the filing room, he stepped sideways into the alcove beside the vault door, pressed himself flat against the stone, and stood very still, which is what a guilty man does, and which is why a guilty man gets caught.

The sentry rounded the corner carrying a lantern he didn't need — the corridor lamps were lit — and a bottle he did. Young. Infantry greatcoat two sizes too big. A boy from somewhere flat and far away, stationed in a harbor town full of enemies he couldn't identify and secrets he couldn't read, and so he drank, because that was the one order every army understands.

He saw Freya immediately. There was no skill to the seeing; there was just light, and a man standing in it.

"What are you doing there?"

"Filing." Freya let tiredness into his voice, let it do the work his composure was doing. "Night duty. Corporal Hobbes assigned me."

"At the vault?" The boy squinted. The lantern wavered. "Filing's in the east wing."

"The east wing drawers are full. I was told to bring overflow here for the morning clerk." Freya gestured vaguely at the ledger under his arm — he'd brought one, a genuine requisition folder, blank inside but heavy enough to look official. Preparation, at least, had gone well. "I've been at it three hours. I haven't opened anything. The lock's new anyway, isn't it? I couldn't if I wanted to."

That was either the smartest sentence or the stupidest he'd ever spoken. It named the lock — which a man with honest business might notice, true enough — but it also told the sentry that Freya had been thinking about the lock, which a man with honest business wouldn't have needed to say.

The boy considered this with the slow gravity of alcohol. "You're the one who reads the mail."

"I'm the one who sorts it."

"Same thing." He said it without malice, which made it worse. Then he lifted the bottle and drank, watching Freya over the neck of it, and something in the watching sharpened — not suspicion exactly, but the animal unease of a guard dog smelling a smell it has no word for. "You shouldn't stand at the vault. Orders are, nobody stands at the vault."

"Then I'll go back to my files." Freya stepped out of the alcove slowly, hands visible, the picture of a tired civilian. "Good night, soldier."

"Night." But as Freya passed him, the boy grabbed his sleeve — gently, almost apologetically, the grip of a man steadying himself more than detaining anyone — and said, close enough that Freya could smell the gin under the lantern oil: "You looked at the door a long time. Before I come round. I seen you from the passage."

Freya held the boy's eyes. This was the moment, and he knew it was the moment, and his heart behaved itself out of sheer spite. "Son," he said, "I've been staring at doors all week because I'm trying to stay awake. If staring at a door were a crime, half this garrison would hang."

A beat. Then the boy laughed — a short, surprised bark — and let go of the sleeve. "Aye. Fair enough." He raised the bottle in something like salute. "Go file your papers."

Freya walked back down the corridor at a clerk's pace. Not fast. Fast was flight. He reached the filing room, sat at the desk under the hissing gas lamp, and put the blank folder in the drawer where it belonged. His hands were steady until he tried to pick up the next letter, and then they shook so badly the paper chattered against the desk like teeth.

He finished his shift. Of course he finished his shift. Leaving early would be a signature on a confession. He sorted letters for another hour while sweat dried cold between his shoulder blades, and when the relief clerk came at dawn, gray-faced and yawning, Freya thanked him for the company of an empty room and walked home through streets still blue with night.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He lay on his bed in the room above the old chandlery, coat still on, and conducted the court-martial of himself with a thoroughness no captain could match.

Charge one: he had approached the vault without a workable plan. Verdict: guilty. Charge two: he had stood in plain light at the one place in the building where standing was itself suspicious. Guilty. Charge three: he had left —

Here the trial stopped, because charge three was the one that mattered, and it took him a long moment to make himself think it.

Before the sentry came, in those few seconds when he'd stood with his palm against the new lock — the vault door had worn a fine skin of dust along its face and frame. Military locks get dust like everything else; the cleaning details attended to rifles and floors, not doorframes. And his hand, pressing flat against that iron, had left a mark. Not a print, maybe. Maybe just a smear, a pale disturbance in the gray.

Or maybe a print. The pad of a thumb. The whorls of four fingers. He had pressed hard — he remembered pressing, remembered wanting the metal to tell him something — and dust remembers pressure. Somewhere in that black-iron face, in the gray film that a sergeant with a lamp and a grudge might examine on any given morning, was the record of Freya Crane's hand, laid against the vault door at two in the morning like a signature on a confession.

He got up and lit the lamp and tried to reason with himself the way he reasoned with everything: as arithmetic. How likely was anyone to dust that door? Low. The garrison barely trusted its own clerks; nobody was investigating the vault because nothing was missing. How identifiable was a partial print in dust, days old, weathered by a drafty corridor? Barely. And even found, even identified — a print proved a man had touched a door. Doors get touched. He was in that building every day. He had a dozen innocent reasons and only one guilty one, and innocence was, after all, the officially recorded fact about Freya Crane.

The reasoning was sound. He built it brick by brick, and it held weight, and he lay back down and stared at the ceiling and believed none of it.

Because reasoning wasn't the problem. The problem was the image that had lodged behind his eyes and refused eviction: a sergeant's lamp held close to black iron, a breath blown across gray dust, and a thumbprint rising out of it like a drowned man surfacing. The problem was that he had spent three years writing down other people's small dangerous facts in a leather book, and now he understood — with the particular cruelty of experience — how little space there is between a fact and a verdict.

He thought about Anton in the shipyard loft, working by candle stub on a boat that existed nowhere on paper. He thought about Greta's tally of days. He thought about Edith in her cell, or whatever room they were keeping her in, composing herself the way she composed speeches — for delivery. All of them balanced, in ways they didn't fully know, on whether a leather book in a steel box stayed shut.

And he had gone and put his hand on the lid.

Near dawn he gave up on sleep and sat at the table instead, and did the only thing his nature allowed: he made a plan for the failure. If the print was found, there would be questions. He would answer them with the truth minus its meaning — yes, he'd walked to the vault, yes, he'd touched the door, the overflow drawers were full, he'd wondered if the new lock meant new procedures, curiosity, idleness, the fidgets of a sleepless man. Boring. Boredom was the best armor; no one interrogates a boring man for long. And meanwhile the real work had to accelerate, because every night that door stayed locked was a night closer to someone reading two hundred pages of his honesty.

Which meant he needed help he didn't trust and couldn't afford. The Vanes had said *come back when you have something worth our risk*, and he had nothing but a civil servant's wage and a conscience. Unless —

Unless he counted what he read. The intercepted mail crossed his desk by the sackful. Fuel movements. Patrol schedules. Navy business, wrapped in the army's paper, passing through the hands of a man they'd decided was furniture.

He sat in the gray light with the lamp burning uselessly against the dawn, and let himself think the thought all the way through for the first time: that he knew where the navy kept its fuel cache upriver, because he'd read it in a captain's letter to his wife, of all things, tucked between complaints about the cook and a paragraph about their son's measles.

Coordinates. Dates. A sitting duck with a number.

That was currency. That was the Vanes' price, or near enough to start the bargaining. And using it meant handing smugglers a target in wartime, which meant — his mind supplied the word without being asked — treason, actual treason, the kind they shoot you for, as opposed to the kind you commit nightly in your own head.

Outside, the gulls started up over the water, and the first patrol boat coughed its way past the quay, and somewhere across the river the ice groaned like an old man turning over.

Freya pinched out the lamp, though the daylight made it pointless — a small useless secrecy, a habit forming — and went to find clean cuffs and a calm face.

One hour's work in the mailroom, he figured, and he'd have what he needed.

He tried not to hear how much it sounded like a plan, and failed.
