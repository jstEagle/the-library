## 16. The Price of Passage

The Vane brothers kept their office in a hulk.

That was the word for it — a hulk — a coal barge gone soft with rot, beached above the tideline on the mudflats south of the shipyard, where the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a man walking upright. Everyone in town knew the Vanes ran things out there. Nobody said so, because saying so was how you ended up being discussed in their office yourself.

Freya went at low tide, on a Sunday, wearing his army pass and carrying nothing that could be read as evidence except the thoughts in his head.

The plank gangway creaked under him like an old man's knee. At the top, a boy no older than Greta sat on a crate with a slingshot across his lap, watching him with the flat patience of a heron. The boy didn't ask questions. He jerked his chin toward the stern cabin and went back to studying gulls.

Inside, the cabin smelled of lamp oil, tobacco, and tar. Two men sat at a table scarred by decades of knives and coins. Silas Vane was the elder by four years and looked older than that by twenty — gray shot through his beard, hands like dock rope. Tobias, the younger, had a face that seemed assembled from spare parts: heavy brow, crooked nose broken twice at least, eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

They'd been playing cards with nobody. The deck lay facedown between them, abandoned, which told Freya they'd been waiting.

"Driver," Silas said. Not a greeting. An identification.

"Vane."

"Sit, then." Tobias kicked a stool out from under the table. "You've been down here twice before, both times for other people. This time's for you, I think."

"It's for Anton Weiss."

Silas turned a card over, considered it, turned it back. "Shipwright. German."

"German-born. Thirty years building boats in this harbor. His keels are under half the fleet that feeds this town."

"Half the fleet the navy's eating right now." Silas laid both big hands flat on the table. "You want us to move two passengers north, past patrol stations, upriver to the fishing villages, before the ice breaks. In winter. With the river full of navy pickets who've been ordered to shoot first and file reports second."

"Yes."

"And what do we get?"

Freya had come with money — most of what he had, sewn into his coat lining since the requisition, because paper currency in a bank could be seized and a coat could not. He put the roll on the table.

Tobias picked it up, weighed it, set it back down without opening it. It was the not-opening that told Freya everything.

"Decent," Tobias said. "For one passenger. In summer."

"Two passengers," Silas said. "Winter water. Navy patrols. And a cargo that hangs everybody involved if it goes wrong." He leaned back, chair groaning. "You're asking us to bet our boats, our route, our necks. Money doesn't cover that bet anymore, Crane. Money's just paper the government can trace. We need something the government can't trace."

"What, then?"

Silas and Tobias exchanged a look. It lasted half a second, but Freya had spent years reading faces across counters and desks, and he caught the whole conversation in it: *He'll either have it or he won't.*

"The fuel cache," Tobias said.

Freya kept his face still. Inside, something dropped through him like a stone through dark water.

*The fuel cache.* Three weeks ago, sorting intercepted correspondence in the customs house annex, he had opened a naval dispatch — misrouted, marked for a different department, sitting in the wrong tray because clerks were overworked and war made everyone sloppy. A fuel depot. Upriver, above the narrows, disguised as a civilian coal yard. Coordinates, guard rotations, tonnage figures. He'd read it twice, sealed it properly, filed it where it belonged, and told himself he hadn't memorized anything.

He'd memorized everything. That was the curse of a man who kept ledgers. Nothing passed through his hands without leaving a copy behind in his head.

"I don't know anything about fuel caches," he said.

"No," Tobias agreed pleasantly. "But you read the mail."

* * *

Freya stood. Too fast; the cabin tilted. He put a hand on the table and found both Vane brothers watching him with the calm of men who had already won and were simply waiting for him to finish losing.

"How do you know what I do?"

"Everyone knows what you do," Silas said. "You drive for Edith Marlowe, you carry letters, you sort the army's post because your reading is better than theirs. Half the harbor's secrets go through you, and the other half goes through us, and the two halves meet at your desk more often than you'd like to believe." He tapped the table once. "We don't know what's in the dispatches. We only know that things get moved when you've been near them. So we're not asking you to confirm anything. We're asking you to bring us what you find. If you find nothing, walk away, and we were never talking about fuel."

It was a gift, of a kind. A door left open so he could pretend he'd never walked through it.

"You know what they do to men who give the navy's positions to smugglers," Freya said. "In wartime. It's not prison. It's a wall and a rifle squad at dawn."

"We know exactly what they do," Tobias said. "We also know what they're doing to Anton Weiss and every man like him, and none of them got a wall and a rifle squad. They got cattle cars and a camp with a number instead of a name. You want to talk about what people deserve, driver? Sit back down."

Freya sat back down.

Because that was the thing — the thing he'd been turning over every night since Edith's arrest warrant was drawn, since Hobbes showed him Box 41 listed in the inventory like a dead man listed in a casualty report. The state had decided that some lives were categories and some categories were disposable, and it did this with paperwork, calmly, in triplicate. And here were two smugglers pointing out that the paperwork cut both ways: if the state could make a man disappear with a form, then a man could make the state's forms lie.

He thought about Greta counting soldiers from the loft window. About Anton's hands, which had built the harbor and now weren't allowed to touch it. About Edith in a cell somewhere, composing speeches she couldn't deliver.

About himself, copying names out of the army's own files at night, telling himself it was resistance, knowing it was also theft, knowing theft was becoming a language he spoke fluently.

"One cache," Freya said. "One time. And after Weiss is north, this never happened, and I never come down here again."

"Never's a long word," Silas said. "But sure. Never." He extended his hand across the table, and Freya took it, and the handshake was dry and hard and final, like signing something in blood without any blood.

"Passage price stands at what you brought plus the coordinates," Tobias said. "Boat ready within a night of your signal. We use the reeds, we run dark, we don't take passengers who panic. Can the shipwright keep quiet?"

"He builds boats in silence. Silence is his trade."

"And the girl?"

Freya felt his jaw tighten. "She's twelve."

"Twelve-year-olds scream," Tobias said. "It's not an insult. It's ballast weight."

"She counts things," Freya said. "Numbers calm her. Put her on the compass heading and let her count the miles, and she'll be quieter than the boat itself."

Something moved behind Tobias's ruined face — not quite warmth, but the memory of it. "A counter," he said. "Good. Counters are useful on dark water."

* * *

He walked back along the flats with the tide beginning to turn, mud sucking at his boots, and he made himself do the arithmetic honestly, because lying to himself was the one debt he'd never allowed in the ledger.

Here was what he would be doing. He would be taking information entrusted to the army — taken from the mails, intercepted under the authority of a war the country was fighting — and handing it to civilians who would use it however they pleased. If the Vanes robbed the cache, guards might die. If the navy traced the leak, they would trace it to the man who sorted the mail, and from him outward: to Hobbes, who lost keys; to Edith, whose file was already thick; to every name in a ledger sitting in Box 41. One page of coordinates could burn the whole network he'd spent months weaving out of suffragists and clerks and men who knew the reeds.

And here was what he would be buying. Two lives. A girl's future. A promise made three years ago over a handshake in a warm shipyard, back when promises were light as sawdust.

He tried the old scale — the one he'd used all his life, weighing what fits in two hands — and found the scale wouldn't balance. Some things couldn't be weighed against each other; they could only be chosen between. Duty or mercy. The state's trust or a friend's life. And the terrible clarity of it, the part he would remember years later, was that it wasn't even hard. He hated how easy it was. A good man should struggle more than this.

He reached the seawall as the gas lamps came on, and he climbed to the road, and he walked past the customs house annex with its new sentries and its blacked-out windows, and he let himself in with the evening shift because the evening shift expected him.

The trays waited. The war's mail waited — thousands of letters, each one a small life opened and flattened by strangers. Freya put on his cuffs, took up his knife for slitting envelopes, and began.

An hour in, he found it again. Or rather, he found its sibling: a follow-up dispatch, routine, restating depot strength at the coal yard above the narrows, confirming the winter guard rotation. The same coordinates he'd memorized weeks ago, printed now in fresh ink, as if the war itself were handing him permission.

He copied them onto a scrap of blotting paper in figures so small they looked like dust. Grid references. Tonnage. Guard change at dusk and dawn. Then he fed the blotting scrap into his cigarette tin, beneath a layer of honest tobacco, and slit the next envelope, and the next, his hands steady, his face the blank professional mask the clerks around him wore like uniforms.

That was the worst part, he realized. Not the stealing. The steadiness. Somewhere along the line — sorting letters, forging favors, keeping accounts of other people's debts — he had become a man who could commit treason between one envelope and the next without spilling anything on the page. He searched himself for horror and found only purpose, and purpose frightened him more than horror ever had.

At midnight he walked out past the sentry, who nodded, who trusted him, who would shoot him in six months if the trail ever led back here — and Freya nodded back and touched his cap and meant every ounce of the respect in it, which somehow made it worse.

On the quay, the river talked to its ice in the dark, patient, working loose. Above the narrows, invisible in the black, men slept beside a coal pile that wasn't coal, guarding a secret Freya now carried in a cigarette tin against his heart.

He lit one, and the smoke went up straight in the still air, and he watched it go, and he hated himself — cleanly, thoroughly, without excuse or appeal, the way you hate a debt you intend to pay in full.

Then he ground the cigarette under his heel and went to tell the Vanes the war was for sale.

Tomorrow, Hobbes owed him an answer about Box 41. And the ice, tonight, had begun to sing.
