## 17. Edith Taken

Edith Marlowe had spoken to smaller crowds and larger ones, and she had learned long ago that the number of faces told you nothing about the number of minds. Forty people in a fish-packing shed on a Tuesday evening in November could change a town. Four hundred in a summer square could change nothing at all.

Tonight there were sixty-one. She counted them out of habit while she waited for the noise of settling to die — sixty-one, and she knew most of their names, which was itself a kind of arithmetic she no longer trusted herself to do. Three years ago every face would have been a stranger or a sympathizer. Now she looked across the shed and saw wives whose husbands worked the requisitioned warehouses, a customs clerk who kept his cap pulled low as though attendance were a crime, old Mrs. Fenwick who had never missed a meeting since 1908 and who now sat nearest the door, an instinct Edith did not comment on because commenting would have made it true.

The army had taken the town hall. That was where they had held the harvest fair, the wedding receptions, the meetings of the harbor board. The army had also taken the customs house, the grain stores, the school annex for officers' billets, and — as of last week — the printing works that produced her pamphlets, on the grounds that paper was a war material. So tonight they met in a shed that smelled of herring and creosote, standing between stacked crates, and Edith thought: let them see us here. Let them see what we look like when you take everything with a roof and a lock.

She climbed onto a crate. It bowed under her but held.

"I'll be brief," she began, and someone laughed, kindly, because Edith Marlowe had never once been brief in eleven years. "No — truly. Brief, because there is less to say than there was. Not because the cause has shrunk. Because the argument has been settled by events."

She had planned the speech on the walk over, composing it the way she composed everything, in cadences she tested against her own breath. The war, she said, had been sold to the women of this country as a temporary arrangement — give us your sons, your flour, your patience, and when it is over you will have your vote as thanks. But she had watched what temporary arrangements became. She had watched men of German birth who had built these very docks be handed forms and called enemies. She had watched a town's records vanish into crates stamped with numbers, as if a place's memory were contraband.

"You cannot requisition a conscience," she said. "Believe me, I have watched them try. They have taken the hall, the presses, the registers. They have not yet found the room in any of us where we keep our own names."

A murmur ran through the shed. Near the back, the customs clerk with the low cap had stopped looking at the door.

"They tell us that asking for the vote now is disloyalty. That a woman who wants a ballot wants her country to lose." She let that sit. "I want my country to win so thoroughly that it can afford to keep its promises. There is no version of victory worth having that requires half its people to stand outside the counting-house forever, holding the ledger open with one hand and being forbidden to write in it with the other."

The phrase pleased her — she heard it land — and she noted, with the small cold clerk that lived in her head alongside everything else, that even now, even here, part of her was drafting. Part of her had always been drafting. It was how she survived rooms like the ones she expected to survive her.

She was four sentences from the close — she had counted those too — when the doors at both ends of the shed opened at once.

Later, she would reconstruct the moment the way one reconstructs any catastrophe: not as a blur but as a series of clean, separate facts. Two constables and a man in army uniform she did not know. The lamplight swinging as the door moved, shadows tilting across the crate stacks. Mrs. Fenwick's hand going to her mouth. The silence arriving before the order did.

"Edith Marlowe," the soldier said. He was young, and he read from a paper, and his voice cracked on the second word, which she noticed and stored away — a boy doing a man's errand, frightened of her, which meant he had been told things about her. "You are to come with us. For questioning."

"For questioning," Edith repeated. "Under what charge?"

"No charge, ma'am. Just questioning."

"Just questioning," she said again, and turned back to the crowd, because she understood — everyone understood, it was in the air like weather — that whatever happened next would happen quickly and would happen without paperwork, and that the only record that might survive was whatever sixty-one people carried out of this shed in their heads. "You heard him. No charge. Remember that. Write it down tonight, all of you, while your ink still works."

The constables started forward. Someone in the crowd — she thought it was the cooper's wife, big-armed Marta, who had come straight from the packing line — shifted her weight in a way that was either nothing or the beginning of something, and Edith lifted one hand, flat, the gesture she used to stop a heckler mid-breath.

"No," she said quietly. "Not one of you moves. If they take me from a riot, I am a rioter. If they take me from a meeting, I am a citizen. Sit down, Marta."

Marta sat down. Edith stepped off the crate.

They walked her through the shed and she did what she had trained other women to do and had never expected to perform herself: she kept her chin level, she looked at faces, she made each person meet her eyes for one full second so that they would remember being seen. At the door she paused. The young soldier put his hand near her elbow and then, visibly deciding against it, withdrew it.

"Mrs. Marlowe," he said, low, almost apologetic. "There's a vehicle."

"There usually is," she said.

The last thing she saw of the shed was Mrs. Fenwick's face, and the last thing she heard was the cooper's wife beginning, softly, to say the rosary, which was ecumenical of her, given the room, and which Edith chose to take as a good omen.

* * *

The vehicle took her not to the police station but to the customs house, which the army had swallowed whole, and that told her more than the arrest had. A municipal charge went to a magistrate. This was going somewhere else.

They put her in a room that had once been the harbor master's office — she knew it well, had argued tariff policy in it across a desk from a man named Osgood who is probably dead or worse now — with the charts stripped off the walls and a single electric bulb burning in a cage. They left her alone for forty minutes, which was technique, not neglect. She used the time to inventory herself: coat buttons intact, boots laced, hair pinned. Composure, she had learned, was mostly wardrobe and posture maintained past the point where the body wanted either.

Then two men came in. One was the young soldier from the shed. The other was older, civilian clothes, soft hands, the look of a man who processed information rather than gathered it. He introduced himself as Captain Rourke, intelligence section, and set a leather folder on the harbor master's desk, and Edith thought: there it is. The folder. Every life reduced to a folder eventually; hers had simply arrived early.

"Your meeting tonight," Rourke said. "Seditious association. Obstruction of recruitment. Speaking against His Majesty's forces."

"Which of those is a crime, Captain? You've listed three things, and I'd like to know which one exists."

He smiled. He had the smile of a man who enjoyed this and was honest enough not to pretend otherwise. "You've been watched for some time, Mrs. Marlowe."

"I should hope so. I've been public for eleven years. Watching me is the least anyone could do."

"We're taking your correspondence."

And there — there it was, the thing she had braced for without knowing she had braced for it, because she had prepared herself for the cell, the questions, even the transport north that rumor said awaited women like her, and she had not prepared herself for the drawers.

Her correspondence lived at home in a walnut writing desk: eleven years of letters. Letters from organizers in six counties. Letters from women in prison, smuggled out in laundry, describing hunger strikes in handwriting that got weaker down the page. Letters from her sister in Toronto. Letters — she felt her stomach go cold and her face, thank God, do nothing at all —

Letters from Freya Crane. Undelivered. Letters she had written and never sent and kept anyway, because she had never learned how to destroy anything she'd written, and because destroying them would have been admitting what they were, and admitting what they were was a door she had decided, years ago, to keep shut from the inside.

"Of course," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Take whatever you like. My correspondence is political, Captain, and politics is the only crime I've committed."

"That remains to be seen," Rourke said, and nodded to the soldier, and the boy — his name, she caught it as Rourke spoke it, was Hobbes, Corporal Hobbes — went out to fetch the evidence bags.

It was Hobbes who actually collected the letters. She watched through the open door as he stood at her desk with cotton gloves and lifted the bundles out drawer by drawer, and she watched him hesitate — she saw it clearly, the pause of a man who reads, who knows what a bundle of unsent letters means to a woman, who is following orders and dislikes it — and then he packed them anyway, because he was a corporal and she was a prisoner and the world had arranged itself accordingly.

When they brought the last box past her, tied with string, she asked to see it. Rourke, amused or careless, let her.

Eleven years. Reduced to three boxes. And on top, out of alphabetical order because she had never filed them properly, because she had kept them loose the way you keep a wound loose, the bundle addressed in her own hand to Freya Crane, marked *not to be sent* in the margin, five letters thick.

Her composure held through the customs house. It held through the ride. It held through the processing, the photograph, the surrendering of her hatpins — they confiscated her hatpins, all seven of them, as instruments, and she nearly laughed at that, at the idea that she was dangerous at the scalp. It held through the first night in a cell with a cot and a bucket and a window too high to see out of.

It broke at four in the morning, silently, lying flat on the cot with her eyes open, when she finally let herself think about what was inside those five letters. Not the movement letters — those were coded, careful, deniable; she had written them for exactly the room she was now in. But the others. The others were not careful. The others contained sentences like *I think of you driving through the rain with the lamp lit and I cannot make myself mean anything smaller.* Sentences about a woman named after a river in another country. Sentences she had written at twenty-six and thirty-one and thirty-four, addressed to a man who delivered other people's mail for a living and had never known he was the subject of hers.

They would read them. Men like Rourke would read them aloud, perhaps, to each other, for entertainment or leverage, and they would learn something true about her that had nothing to do with sedition, and that truth would be in a file, in a box, in a building, belonging to the state.

Edith Marlowe lay in the dark and did not cry, because crying would have meant believing the situation was hopeless rather than merely bad, and she refused the confusion. But her composure cracked, cleanly, along a seam no one had ever seen, and she lay there holding the two halves together by will alone until the gulls started up outside the high window, and morning came, and she sat up and braided her hair and was ready when the bolts went back.

* * *

Freya heard about it at nine o'clock, from Marta the cooper's wife, who found him at the livery where he still kept the motorcar nobody could get petrol for anymore and grabbed his sleeve hard enough to bruise.

"They took her. Last night. From the meeting. No charge — she made everyone hear it, no charge — and they drove her to the customs house and she went like it was a Sunday visit, and Freya—" Marta's voice broke and reassembled itself, working-class and efficient about it. "They took her papers. Everything off her desk. Boxes of it. I saw the boy carry them out."

He was moving before she finished. He did not remember deciding to move. He remembered the smell of the livery, the horse that was left, the cold coming off the river, and then he was walking, fast, toward the customs house, telling himself the entire way: *you cannot help her by getting arrested beside her.* Telling himself and not believing it, because the alternative to walking toward the building was standing still, and standing still had become, sometime in the last month, unbearable.

He did not go in. He had no pretext, no pass, no reason a chauffeur had business in an intelligence office, and the new sentries at the door were not the bored boys of October; they were regulars with rifles and orders. So he did what he had gotten good at, which was stand where he could watch and be unremarkable, and he smoked one cigarette down to the filter and thought about Edith in a cell, and about the walnut desk with its drawers open, and about what was in those drawers.

Because he knew what was in those drawers. He had delivered most of it.

That was the job she'd given him three years ago, before the war, when carrying her correspondence was just part of driving: the county organizers, the prison letters, the pamphlet proofs. He had read them — he read everything, it was the disease he couldn't cure, he read bills of lading and love notes and now, God help him, army intercepts — and he had never once let his face show what he knew, and she had never once asked whether he read, and between those two silences a trust had grown that he would have defended with his body.

And underneath the official correspondence, in the bottom drawer, in a bundle he had glimpsed once, years ago, when the drawer stuck and she yanked it open carelessly — a bundle in her handwriting, addressed to him.

He had never mentioned it. She had never explained it. He had gone home that night and drunk half a bottle and told himself he'd imagined the address, and had spent three years knowing perfectly well he hadn't.

Now the army had it. Boxes of it. String-tied, catalogued, filed somewhere in a building full of men whose profession was reading other people's mail.

Freya dropped the cigarette stub and ground it under his heel and stood very still in the cold, doing the arithmetic he hated. To get to Edith's letters he would have to go into the evidence chain. The evidence chain ran through the requisitioned customs house, through its vaults and its boxes and its numbering system — through Box Forty-One, if Hobbes hadn't lied, where his own ledger sat with half the town's real names in his own handwriting.

One theft, then. Or two. Or however many it took. The building already had him halfway inside it, had his literacy conscripted and his name on a work roster; the distance between sorting the enemy's mail and stealing it back was shorter than any road he'd ever driven.

He turned away from the customs house and walked, fast, toward the records annex, where Corporal Hobbes started his shift at ten and where, Freya had learned, a man with cigarettes and a sympathetic ear could buy almost anything except courage — until today, maybe, if today he asked for it.

The ice on the river groaned, far off, like a door being tested.

Freya walked faster.
