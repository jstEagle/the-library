## 18. Letters Never Delivered

The evidence room was in the basement of the customs house, which meant it had been a wine cellar before the army, and before that a counting room, and before that, if you believed old Marta Vane, a place where smugglers hid brandy in false keels. Every layer of that building was theft. The army had only added its own.

Freya learned its shape over three nights of sorting mail. The intercepted correspondence came down on a wheeled cart at six, and Corporal Hobbes — promoted by attrition, not merit, to sergeant of the letter room — logged it in a ledger of his own, an ugly green thing with a broken clasp. Freya sorted. That was his job now: read, classify, stamp. *Personal — no intelligence value. Commercial — hold. Suspect — flag.* His literacy, which Edith Marlowe had once called a weapon and taught him to sharpen, was now a broom in someone else's hands, sweeping other people's lives into piles.

But he had learned the room. He had learned that Hobbes left at nine sharp because his wife locked the door against him. He had learned that the sentry on the customs house did one circuit of the ground floor and none of the cellar stairs after ten, because the stove up there kept him warm and the stairs went down into cold. He had learned that the evidence room itself — a separate cage of shelving inside the letter room, locked with a padlock older than the war — held everything the army took from people it didn't trust: Edith's papers, the Vanes' seized charts, three Bibles from German-born families whose owners were already inland behind wire, reading scripture by electric light.

And somewhere in that cage, he was nearly certain, were Edith's letters. All of them. Including the ones she'd received that he had never delivered — the ones he'd read, sealed again with a breath held between his teeth, and carried home instead to his own room, because some words you cannot hand across a threshold without becoming part of them.

He told himself it was for her. It was partly for her.

* * *

Thursday night it rained, which was good. Rain made sentries hunch and hurry, made the town pull its shutters early, made every sound in the street explainable. Freya wore his driver's coat, the one with the oilcloth collar, because drivers were everywhere and questioned by no one. He had a legitimate errand in his pocket — a requisition slip for Edith's file, half-forged, dated wrong on purpose so that any clerk who checked would find the error and feel clever catching it, and stop looking further. Hobbes had taught him that trick without knowing it: the best forgery contains one visible flaw.

He let himself into the customs house through the coal chute, which he had measured with his shoulders the way he measured everything, by what fits. The chute was built for sacks of coal, and a man folded correctly is not much bigger than a sack. He came down into the boiler room black as a pocket, stood until his eyes adjusted, and counted his own heartbeat the way Greta Weiss counted days — one, two, three — until fear became arithmetic and arithmetic became calm.

The letter room door was unlocked. Of course it was. Who would steal other people's grief?

Inside, he struck no match. He knew the cage by touch: the shelving, the padlock hanging open on its hasp because the key lived upstairs with the duty officer and nobody bothered to lock what nobody feared. The army's arrogance was a wide door and he walked through it. He found Edith's correspondence in the third box on the second shelf — her name stamped on the side in fresh stencil, MARLOWE, E., and beneath it, in pencil, a classification he refused to read twice: *seditious association.*

He lifted the box down. It was heavier than paper should be. Inside: her rally speeches, confiscated mid-argument. Her account books. A bundle of letters tied with the blue ribbon she used for nothing but correspondence, and under the ribbon, his own handwriting staring up at him like a face at a window.

Not hers. His. Letters he had written for her, dictated at her kitchen table while she paced — *Dear Mrs. Ashcombe, regarding the petition* — and sealed and never sent because the post had stopped running south. But beneath those, a smaller bundle, unbound, loose as fallen leaves. Letters addressed to Edith from women in three counties, women he had never met, women who wrote to her the way sailors write to lighthouses. And tucked among them, in an envelope gone soft at the corners from handling, a letter in a hand he knew better than his own.

His own.

He hadn't written it in four years, but he knew it the way you know a scar. He sat down on the cold floor of the evidence room, in the dark, holding the envelope, and for a long moment he could not make himself open it, because he remembered writing it, and remembering was worse than reading.

Her name was Livia Sorel. She had worked the ticket window at the steamboat office the summer the harbor was young to him, and she laughed at his seriousness the way rain laughs at dust. He was twenty-three and drove a delivery cart then, before Edith, before the ledger, before anything, and he had written her a letter one night in March — a whole letter, pages of it, saying plainly what he could not say aloud: that he loved her, that he was poor but not forever, that he would learn any trade she asked. He had written it and re-read it forty times and then, on the morning he meant to give it to her, he had learned that she was leaving — engaged to a cousin in Montreal, a man with a warehouse and a future — and Freya had done what he always did with things too heavy to carry.

He had filed it. In the back of the leather ledger, behind the debts and the promises, where he kept the accounts he could not close.

And when the army swept the municipal records — when they took his ledger off his own desk like a thief taking a Bible — he had thought, among all the other fears, a small and shameful one: *they will read that page.* He had told himself they wouldn't understand it. Names and dates. A woman's name, a boat's departure. Nothing.

But this wasn't the ledger copy. This was the original. Which meant —

Which meant he had given it to Edith. Years ago. One drunken, honest evening, or one sober desperate one — he couldn't remember which, and the not-remembering was its own indictment — he had handed Edith Marlowe the most private thing he owned and asked her to keep it, or maybe asked her to burn it, or maybe asked her nothing at all and simply left it in her keeping the way you leave a wound with a doctor. And Edith, who kept everything, who believed paper was testimony, who filed her life the way he filed the town's — Edith had kept it.

And now it lay in an army evidence box, classified *seditious*, and any clerk, any captain, any Rourke could open it and read a love letter written by the same hand that appeared in a confiscated ledger full of names the state wanted explained.

His secrets were no longer his alone. They hadn't been for years. He had simply been permitted to pretend otherwise, and the pretending had just ended, here on a cold floor, in the dark, with rain hammering the chute above him like applause for something terrible.

He opened the envelope anyway. He had come this far; cowardice could wait outside.

*I have watched you count change for strangers with more patience than I can count my own reasons,* he had written, twenty-three years old and foolish and true. *I am not asking you to stay. I am telling you that if you go, I will be the kind of man worth coming back to, and I will spend every year until then becoming him.*

He put it down gently, the way you put down something sleeping. He had become the man, more or less. She had not come back. The arithmetic balanced at zero, and zero is the cruelest number there is.

Then he heard boots on the stairs.

* * *

Not many boots. Two, unhurried, and the low murmur of men who own the building they walk through. Freya went flat behind the shelving cage, box in his lap, and made himself small the way he'd made himself small in the coal chute. Through the gap between two shelves he saw lamplight swing into the letter room, and then Captain Rourke — he knew the silhouette now, the way you know weather — and beside him a lieutenant with a clipboard.

"Marlowe's file stays segregated," Rourke was saying. "Trial material. Nothing leaves this room without my signature, not even for the prosecutor. Especially not for the prosecutor."

"Yes, sir."

"She writes to people. People write to her. I want to know every name in those letters cross-referenced against the registration lists by Monday." A pause, the sound of gloves being flexed. "The driver. Crane. He sorts downstairs?"

"He's not assigned tonight, sir."

"Pity. He reads well." Another pause, longer, and Freya felt the pause settle on him like a hand. "A literate man in a harbor full of illiterates. Do you know what I was before the war, Lieutenant? An auditor. You find the truth the same way everywhere — you don't look for what's hidden. You look for what's *counted.*"

The lamp swung away. Boots receded. The door closed.

Freya stayed flat for two hundred heartbeats. Then he moved, fast and silent, and did the thing he had come to do and the thing he had not known he'd come to do: he took Edith's letters out of the official bundle — the private ones, the women's voices, the ribbon — and slid them inside his coat. And from his own pocket he took the half-forged requisition slip and left it in the box, dated wrong on purpose, a flaw for some clever clerk to find and feel proud of, pointing at a paperwork error instead of a theft.

Last, he took Livia's letter — his letter — the original, soft-cornered, twenty-three years old. He held it over the little spirit stove the clerks used for sealing wax, and touched the corner to flame, and watched four years of a younger man's hope curl and brighten and go to ash in an ashtray that smelled of someone else's tobacco.

Some accounts you close yourself, before the state closes them for you.

He went up the coal chute into the rain and walked home the long way, past the burned bakery, past the dark shipyard where a candle stub might at this moment be burning in a loft above unfinished boats, past Edith's shuttered house where a warrant waited in a file with her name stenciled on it. At his own door he stopped, and pressed his hand flat against the letters inside his coat — dozens of voices, a chorus of women who had trusted Edith Marlowe and now, unknowing, trusted him.

In the morning, Hobbes would find the requisition slip with its deliberate error and raise an eyebrow and say nothing, because Hobbes was learning what it cost to look away, and the price was going up.

And somewhere in the customs house, in a segregated trial file, Captain Rourke had said his name — *Crane* — aloud, in a room full of counted things.

Freya unlocked his door, sat down at his table, and began, by candlelight, to copy every name from every letter before dawn could take the ink's warmth from his memory. If the letters were going to hang anyone, they would hang no one whose name existed only in one place.

By the time the gulls woke, he had thirty-one names, and a plan forming around Box Forty-One like ice forming around a hull — thin, and cracking, and bearing weight anyway.
