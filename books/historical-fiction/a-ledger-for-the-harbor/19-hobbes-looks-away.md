## 19. Hobbes Looks Away

Corporal Hobbes had counted the ledgers twice, and both times the number was the same, and neither time did the number tell him anything he wanted to know.

Four hundred and eleven volumes. That was what requisition meant when it came down to paper: four hundred and eleven books, each one a town. He sat in the records room of the old customs house with his sleeves buttoned at the wrist and a pencil behind his ear and the radiators ticking like something counting down, and he made lists of other people's lives in a hand so neat his sergeant called him the schoolmistress. Hobbes accepted the nickname the way he accepted most things — quietly, and without argument, because argument was a door you couldn't close again.

He was twenty-three years old and he had joined the army because his father had joined the army, and his grandfather before that, and none of them had ever been asked whether they wanted to spend their lives filing the world into boxes. The war was supposed to be over there. France, Belgium, someplace with mud in it and a reason. Instead Hobbes had drawn garrison duty in a harbor town that smelled of tar and herring, and his war consisted of inventory.

Box 41 sat on the third shelf of the vault, between a customs register from 1888 and a bundle of harbor pilots' charts. It was not an impressive box. Brown cardboard, string-tied, the string gone gray at the knots. Someone had written LEATHER GOODS & MISC. PERSONAL across the top in the round bureaucratic capitals that were taught, apparently, wherever forms were born. Inside it — Hobbes had checked once, early, out of boredom, before he understood what checking things cost — lay a single leather ledger with a cracked spine and a brass clasp gone green with salt air, and pages and pages of small, slanted handwriting that recorded debts and favors and names the way a parish register records births.

Hobbes hadn't read more than a page or two. He told himself that was discipline. Lately he suspected it was fear.

* * *

The deportation happened on a Tuesday, and Hobbes would remember afterward that the light had been ordinary, which seemed like an obscenity. If a thing like that was going to happen, the light should have at least had the decency to fail.

The man's name was Kessler. August Kessler, baker, sixty-one years of age, resident of the harbor for thirty-eight years. His papers were in order — Hobbes knew this because he had filed them himself — but his son had written letters that contained the word *Kaiser* in a context the intelligence captain didn't care for, and so Kessler senior went onto a list, and the list became an order, and the order became a train.

Hobbes was assigned to accompany the escort as far as the station. Not to participate. To observe and record. He stood on the platform with his notebook and watched two privates walk a stooped old man past the fish stalls, past the chandler's, past the bakery with its windows boarded since the first week of the war, and the whole street stopped to watch them go, and nobody said anything, and the silence was worse than shouting. A woman crossed herself. A boy picked up a stone and then put it down again, slowly, like someone setting down a thought he was ashamed of.

At the train there was a delay. There was always a delay; the army ran on delays the way the harbor ran on tides. So for eleven minutes Hobbes stood next to August Kessler on the platform while the old man held a carpet bag containing everything the state had decided he could carry, and Kessler looked up at him and said, in English better than Hobbes's own German, "You are the one who writes."

"I keep the records," Hobbes said.

"Yes." Kessler smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which was somehow unbearable. "I brought bread for the journey. Thirty-eight years I bake in your town, and now I bring bread for a journey. You will write that down?"

Hobbes did not write it down. He stood with his pencil over the blank line and found that his hand had opinions of its own, and the line stayed empty, and then the train came, and the bread went where the bread went, and the doors shut, and the platform was just a platform again with a smudge of flour on it that a porter swept away within the hour.

That night Hobbes sat in the records room after hours with the lamp turned low and looked at Box 41 and understood, with the clarity that comes only after something has broken in you, exactly what his neat handwriting was for. The lists weren't the war's bookkeeping. The lists *were* the war. Somewhere in France men died of bullets; here they died of categories, and the categories were written by clerks, and the clerks were boys like him who kept their sleeves buttoned and their pencils sharp and their eyes forward.

He thought about Kessler's smile. He thought about it for four days.

* * *

On the fifth day, Crane came in.

Freya Crane was a name Hobbes knew from the manifests — the driver, the one who ferried correspondence for the Marlowe woman before her file got thick. The army had conscripted his literacy the way it conscripted horses: he sorted intercepted mail three mornings a week in the back room, quick-fingered and quiet, and he never asked questions, and he never lingered. Hobbes had marked him early as either the most obedient man in the district or the least, and had not yet decided which, and the not-knowing itched at him the way an unbalanced column itches.

Crane came to return a sorting tray, which was the pretext, and stood at the counter, which was the truth, and said nothing for a moment too long.

"Something wrong with the tray?" Hobbes asked.

"No," Freya said. "It's fine. It's a good tray."

"Then we're agreed on the tray." Hobbes set it aside. His heart was doing something unprofessional. He made himself look at the counter instead of at Crane's face, because he had rehearsed nothing and if he looked at the face he might say all of it at once and be lost.

"You seem tired, Corporal," Crane said. Careful voice. A voice used to carrying things without spilling them.

"There was a deportation Tuesday," Hobbes said. "To the interior. A baker." He heard his own voice come out flat and official and hated it, tried again. "His name was Kessler. He offered me bread."

The silence stretched. Down the hall a typewriter clattered like distant rifle fire, which was the sort of thing Hobbes usually managed not to notice.

"That's not why you're telling me," Freya said quietly.

"No."

"You want something."

"I want—" Hobbes stopped. Started again, lower. "I want to know how a man sleeps at night in this town. Because I've been trying it, and the answer is badly, and I'm starting to think the fault isn't mine."

Freya looked at him then, fully, and Hobbes felt examined down to the carbon copies. Whatever Crane saw, he didn't put a name to it. He said, "You should be careful, Corporal. Talking like that in this building."

"I'm aware."

"People have been arrested for less than a conversation."

"I've filed the arrests," Hobbes said. "I know the going rate."

Another silence, but a different kind now, the kind with weight in it, the kind two men stand inside rather than across. Hobbes took a breath and did the thing he had not let himself plan, because planning it would have made it treason and unplanned it was merely — weakness, conscience, whatever the court-martial would call it.

"The vault inventory," he said, "is maintained by me. Box 41 is among the personal effects seized in the general sweep. Its contents are under review by intelligence. Review can take weeks. Review is currently taking weeks." He smoothed the counter with his palm, a clerk's gesture, ordering the world. "There will be an audit Thursday evening. After the audit, during the shift change, the key log must be reconciled. Reconciliation takes time. An hour, perhaps. During reconciliation, the key is signed out to no one. It exists nowhere on paper. A man could stand at that shelf for an hour and be, administratively speaking, invisible."

He heard himself finish and felt slightly sick and slightly lighter, both at once, the way a boat rides after cargo shifts.

Freya had gone very still. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you sort the mail," Hobbes said, "and you've never once read aloud. And because a man on a platform gave me bread and asked if I'd write it down, and I need to write *something* down, Crane, and it can't go in any ledger of mine. Do you understand? I'm not offering anything. I'm describing a gap in a schedule. Gaps occur. Clerks are imperfect. That's the whole of it."

"That's the whole of it," Freya repeated, not quite a question.

"If you repeat this conversation, I'll deny it and you'll be flogged for sedition and I'll be shot for treason, and we'll both have wasted a perfectly good afternoon." Hobbes straightened the tray on the counter so its edge ran parallel to the wood. "Thursday. Shift change is at nine. The sentry does his rounds at quarter past and half past the hour, never on the hour — he says the irregularity confuses spies, which tells you what he thinks of spies. The vault lock is new; the old key won't fit; the new key hangs in the sergeant's office until the audit closes it out."

"How do I get past the sergeant's office?"

"That," Hobbes said, "is your department. I carry letters' worth of secrets through this town every week in my head, and I sleep badly, and I'd like to sleep at all someday. One hour, Crane. At ten minutes past ten, I reconcile the log, and the key stops existing. At ten past eleven, I find it again. What happens in between is not on my paper and therefore, as far as I am able to arrange the world, did not happen."

"And if it goes wrong?"

Hobbes looked at him. "Then it goes wrong the way things have been going wrong here all winter. On a platform, in daylight, with everyone watching and no one saying anything." He picked up the returned tray and turned toward the shelves. "Good afternoon, Mr. Crane. Mind the step on your way out. It's been loose since September and nobody's fixed it. Nobody fixes anything in this building unless it's on a form."

* * *

Afterward he stayed late, though there was nothing left to file, and copied out the audit schedule in his neatest hand, and initialed it, and dated it, and sat looking at his own initials as if they belonged to a stranger whose motives he was required to certify.

He thought about his father, who had spent thirty years in uniform and told him once, drunk on Armistice Eve of another war entirely, that soldiers don't fight wars, they fight orders, and the difference kills you slowly. He thought about the boy with the stone on the platform, putting it down. He thought about Kessler's bread, and where bread went, and who ate it, and whether somewhere inland tonight an old man was sleeping badly in a camp barracks and calling it safety.

At ten o'clock he blew out the lamp and locked the records room and walked home through streets that smelled of frost and coal smoke, and at the corner by the chandler's he passed a tall figure in a dark coat who did not look at him and whom he did not look at, and the two of them occupied the same square of lamplight for perhaps three seconds, two men who had agreed on nothing and were therefore bound completely.

In his lodging room Hobbes lay awake listening to the harbor ice groan out in the dark — a long, splitting sound, like furniture being broken in another room — and he took out his pocket notebook, the private one, the one with no official standing in anyone's army, and beneath a grocery list and the score of a football match he wrote:

*Bread, for a journey. Given freely.*

And under that, because he was still a clerk and always would be, and because some part of him needed even treason to balance:

*One hour. Thursday. Debited against my account, whoever keeps it.*

He slept, that night, better than he had in four days, and hated himself for that too, a little, and got up on time anyway.
