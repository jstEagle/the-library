## 20. One Hour

Hobbes's key was warm from his pocket.

That was the detail Freya would remember afterward — not the lock, not the dark, not the smell of the customs house after a year of army occupation, but the small animal heat of brass that had lived against a corporal's thigh all day while he filed other men's lives into boxes. Hobbes had pressed it into Freya's palm behind the coal shed on Water Street without looking at him, the way you'd pass contraband to a stranger on a train, and said only: "One hour. The relief comes at two. If I'm asked, I was at the latrine, and if I'm pressed, I'll say a man with your build took my coat."

"And your coat is where?"

"Locker forty," Hobbes said. "I'm not brave, Crane. I'm tired. There's a difference, and it matters to no one but me."

Then he'd walked away, shoulders squared like a man going to his own execution, and Freya had stood in the coal shed's shadow counting sixty seconds because counting was what he did when the world went wrong.

Now he counted again, standing before the customs house steps at ten minutes past one in the morning, snow coming down fine as sifted flour, and told himself the arithmetic of it: four hundred paces from the shed, three minutes to the vault corridor, twenty minutes to find Box 41 among however many boxes there were, twenty minutes back, slack for error. One hour minus what he'd already spent being afraid.

Fear spent like money, he thought. You could budget it or let it bankrupt you.

He went up the steps.

* * *

The customs house had been built by men who believed in daylight — tall windows, pale marble, a rotunda meant to make commerce feel like civic religion. The army had filled those windows with sandbags and hung blackout cloth over what remained, so the rotunda was a cave now, and Freya moved through it by memory and by the thin grey bleed of snow-light around the window frames' edges. His boots were wool-wrapped, Greta Weiss's idea, communicated through Anton with a straight face: *the girl says soldiers hear boots before they see anything. She says you should wrap them.* He had wrapped them. He would think of that later, her voice arriving through her father's mouth, and it would steady him the way a hand on the shoulder steadies a man carrying something heavy down stairs.

The records corridor ran off the east side of the rotunda behind a door marked REQUISITIONED MATERIALS — BY AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, which was the army's way of saying *everything*. Hobbes's key turned the first lock. The second lock — the new one, the one that had beaten him in December — was a padlock hasped through an iron eye, military issue, and it was open.

Open.

Freya stood in front of it for a long moment, long enough for the cold to find its way through his wrapped boots, and felt the old dockworker's instinct rise in him: *a door that should be shut and isn't is either luck or bait.* But Hobbes had said the night clerk locked the outer door and left the hasp to the day shift — had said it twice, apologetically, the way he said everything — and discipline was just another word for trusting someone else's sloppiness with your neck. Freya lifted the hasp out of the eye, set it down soft as an egg, and went in.

The corridor smelled of paper and lamp oil and, underneath, faintly, of mildew — the smell of the harbor's whole history boxed and shelved. Racks ran both walls, tin boxes numbered in white paint, and somewhere a clock ticked with the patience of institutions. He found 41 between 40 and 42, exactly where arithmetic said it would be, and for one absurd second he almost laughed: a year of terror reduced to a number between two other numbers.

His hands were shaking. He made them stop by looking at them, the way you'd scold a dog.

The box came off the rack heavier than he expected. He carried it to the work table at the corridor's end, where a shaded lamp sat with its wick trimmed low — Hobbes had left oil in it, another small treason — and struck a match with fingers that wanted to fumble and didn't get to.

Box 41 held: three bundles of harbor manifests, tied with cord. A tin of surveyor's instruments. And his ledger.

It lay face-down on top of the manifests like a bird with a broken wing, its leather cover scuffed, the corner stamped in violet ink: RECEIVED — MILITARY GOVERNOR'S OFFICE — DATE. Someone had catalogued it. Someone had written a number inside the front cover, small and neat, in a hand nothing like his own.

He opened it to that page first, because a man checks his wounds before anything else, and read:

*Crane, F. — driver, Marlowe (E.). Ledger recovered in sweep of Marlowe premises, [date]. Cross-ref: Box 41. See attached summary.*

Attached summary.

He turned the pages fast now, no longer careful, and the lamp threw his shadow huge against the racks, a giant hunched over a small book. Page fourteen — the record of Anton's boat repairs, the payments logged fair and square, the note in his own shorthand, *A.W. — passage owed, promise kept when ice goes out* — was flagged with a slip of pink paper. Page nineteen, Edith's rally fund, names of women who'd given a shilling each, flagged. Page thirty-one, the Vane brothers, marked with a slip of a different color, blue, and beside the entry, in pencil, someone else's handwriting:

*Van(e) Bros — known runners. Who is "the reader"?*

Freya sat down on the work stool because his legs informed him they were finished standing.

*Who is "the reader."*

He read it three times. The words didn't change. Around him the clock ticked and the snow ticked against the high windows and the harbor's whole secret life lay open on a table under a lamp, copied out by strangers in colored slips and neat pencil, half-known, half-hunted, waiting for someone with time and authority to finish the sentence.

He forced himself to do the inventory, because panic was a luxury priced beyond his wages. He went page by page, quick, ruthless, making lists inside himself the way he made them on paper. The copied pages: fourteen, Anton. Nineteen, Edith's fund — though the fund itself was legal, barely, women's shillings being the least seditious currency in the empire. Thirty-one, the Vanes. Forty-four, the fish-market men who'd passed letters north. Fifty-two — his breath caught — fifty-two was the page where he'd recorded, in his tidy private shorthand, the route of Edith's correspondence through the Netherlands, the names of the two clerks in Rotterdam who received it.

Fifty-two had a blue flag too.

But — he counted again, slower, disbelieving his own mercy — the rest were clean. Sixty-odd pages untouched. No flags on the entries about the bakery debts, the doctor's unpaid account, the widow Halloran's coal, the ordinary ledger of a harbor town running on credit and kindness. No flag on page fifty-eight, where he'd written, because writing things down was how he prayed: *Greta W. counted 41 soldiers today. Asked if our name is a bad word. Told her names are tools; depends who holds them.*

They hadn't read everything. Or they hadn't understood everything. His shorthand was his own invention, half-dockside slang, half-private marks, and whatever clerk had been set to copying had worked fast and shallow, flagging the obvious — boats, money, foreign names — and missing the connective tissue. They knew pieces. They did not yet know the shape.

*Half-known,* he thought. *Which means half-safe, which means half-dead.*

And then the thought arrived, the one he'd been walking around the way you walk around a hole in the ice, and it arrived with terrible clarity:

If they had copied it, the original was evidence. If the original was evidence, then taking it was destroying evidence. If he took it and was caught, the copies already existed in some intelligence officer's file cabinet, and the theft would prove everything the copies implied. The ledger in his hands was no longer a shield for his people. It was an indictment with his name embossed on the cover, and every minute he stood here holding it he was signing it.

Put it back. That was the sane voice, the voice of the man who wrapped his boots and counted his seconds. Put it back, walk out, find another road to Anton's salvation that doesn't run through a firing squad.

But there was no other road. He had looked for one since December. The trains were watched, the roads were watched, the river was freezing, and the only key to Box 41 was cooling in his pocket with a tired corporal's warmth still on it.

Besides — and this was the thought that settled it, quiet and cold as the river under its ice — the copied pages existed whether he took the book or left it. Leaving it wouldn't un-copy them. Taking it wouldn't un-copy them. What taking it would do was keep the rest of the book out of their hands, the sixty clean pages, the names they hadn't yet learned to read, and buy time for the people whose names were already flagged — time being the only cargo left moving through this harbor.

He was a driver. He knew about cargo, and about routes, and about the fact that sometimes you carried a thing knowing it might break you, because the thing was worth more than you were.

He took the pink and blue flags out. He tore the catalog slip from the inside cover. Then he closed the ledger, wrapped it in the oilcloth he'd brought for the purpose — Anton's oilcloth, cut from sail canvas, smelling of tar and linseed — and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where its small weight sat like a second heartbeat.

In place of the ledger he arranged the manifests to fill the box's depth, squared the corners, and set the lid on. It would fool no one doing more than glance. He was gambling on glances. The army ran on glances; it was too large to do otherwise.

He relit nothing, touched nothing else, and paused once — only once — at the rack, because Box 38 sat at eye level and he knew from Hobbes's inventories what it held: Edith's correspondence, seized. Her letters. His letters. He put his hand flat against the tin lid, feeling the cold of it, the way you touch a coffin lid at a viewing, confirming the fact of it without opening it. Not tonight. Tonight there was one hour and one book and one road north. But he memorized the number the way he memorized everything, because forgetting was a habit he refused to learn.

Then he went out, drawing the hasp through the iron eye behind him, and the padlock hung open like a mouth deciding what to say.

* * *

Outside, the snow had thickened. Water Street was white and empty, the warehouses along the quay black shapes breathing smoke from army stoves, and somewhere down the harbor a sentry called a challenge and got an answer, voices flattened by snowfall. Freya walked the four hundred paces back at the pace of a man going home from a card game, which was the pace least likely to be stopped, and his heart beat against the oilcloth bundle the whole way, two rhythms arguing.

At the coal shed he stopped, took out the key, and weighed it in his palm. Returning it meant Hobbes touching the thing again, Hobbes's fingerprints joining his on a story that could hang them both. Keeping it meant Hobbes reporting a lost key, an investigation, questions. He thought about Hobbes's face in the lamplight of the records room — no, not the records room; the mail room, weeks ago, when Freya had shown him how to decipher a requisition form and Hobbes had laughed, actually laughed, at some bureaucratic absurdity, and for a moment they had been two clerks of the same crumbling empire, men who processed paper while the world burned.

He wrapped the key in a twist of paper, wrote on the paper in pencil — *locker 40, return it yourself, tell no one you missed it, say you found it in your other coat* — and slid it under the barracks mess hall door, where Hobbes would step on it at breakfast and understand, and be afraid, and be, Freya suspected, quietly glad. Some men needed permission to have a conscience. Freya had just given him one, forged in his own handwriting.

The last thing, before home, was fire.

There was a brazier outside the harbor office where the night watchman kept coals alive, and Freya crossed to it with the torn flags and the catalog slip in his glove. Pink paper, blue paper, violet-stamped slip: he fed them to the coals one at a time and watched the ink lift off in flames, the words *passage owed* and *known runners* and *who is the reader* curling into light and gone. The watchman's chair stood empty; the watchman was asleep somewhere, or warming himself elsewhere; the snow fell on Freya's shoulders and did not melt, which told him how cold he was, how long he'd been out, how nearly the hour was spent.

He stayed until the last scrap went to ash, then stirred the coals with a poker until the ash broke apart and mixed with the cinders beyond reading. Paper remembered everything, he'd always believed that — it was why he kept a ledger, why he trusted writing over the slippery kindness of memory. But tonight he taught paper to forget, and it felt less like destruction than like surgery: cutting away the parts of himself the enemy had already seen, so the rest could live.

The ledger rode home against his chest. In his rooms above the chandler's shop he did not light a lamp. He sat in the dark with the book open on his knees, reading by snowlight the sixty clean pages, the names of the baker and the widow and the doctor and the child's counted soldiers, his own handwriting holding the town together the way caulk holds a hull, and he understood at last what he was carrying.

Not a shield. Not anymore. A shield stops arrows; this thing invited them. It was a map of everyone he loved, written in his own confession of a hand, and he would carry it north tomorrow into the teeth of the machine that had already photographed half of it, because the other half was still free and the other half was worth the risk.

Somewhere across the sleeping town, in a shipyard loft, Anton Weiss was laying planks by candle stub, building the boat that would carry this book and his daughter and himself upriver before the ice broke. Somewhere in a cell, or a borrowed room, Edith Marlowe was composing in her head the speech they hadn't let her finish. Somewhere in the intelligence offices, a man with neat pencil handwriting was asking a question — *who is the reader?* — and every day he wasn't answered was a day bought with luck, and luck in this harbor had always been a short-term loan.

Freya closed the ledger and slept two hours, fully dressed.

In the morning, he would begin moving the flagged names — warn the market men, burn the Rotterdam route, get word to the Vanes that the price of their silence had gone up. And in the afternoon, because the machine required his literate hands and he required access to its files, he would report to the mail room as usual, sit down opposite whichever officer chose to observe him, sort the intercepted letters of his neighbors with steady fingers, and lie with his whole face.

He had stolen the book. Now he had to steal himself back.

The knock, when it came — three raps, unhurried, official — arrived an hour earlier than any morning errand had a right to, and the voice through the door was not Hobbes's, and not a friend's.

"Mr. Crane. Captain Rourke. Might I have a word about the mail?"
