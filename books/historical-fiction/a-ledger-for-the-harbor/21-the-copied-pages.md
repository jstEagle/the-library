## 21. The Copied Pages

The summons came on a Tuesday morning, folded inside a requisition slip like a knife inside bread.

*F. Crane to report to the Customs House, Room 3, eleven o'clock. Routine inquiry regarding postal services.*

Routine inquiry. Freya read it three times standing in the doorway of Edith's empty office, where the desk still bore the ring of her coffee cup and the calendar had stopped on the day they took her. He knew what routine meant now. Routine was what the army called everything it intended to make permanent — the checkpoints, the curfew whistles, the gray trucks idling outside the German bakeries. Routine was the word they used so you would walk into the room on your own legs instead of being carried in.

He arrived at ten minutes to eleven because arriving early looked eager and arriving late looked afraid. The Customs House smelled of cold stone and fresh ink, the way it always had, but the ink was different now — military ink, blacker, the kind that didn't fade in sunlight. A sentry at the stairs checked his papers without looking at his face, which Freya understood was its own kind of test. They were watching whether he offered more than was asked.

Room 3 had been the harbormaster's parlor once, when the harbor still answered to itself. Now there was a steel filing cabinet where the tide charts used to hang, and behind a small oak desk sat Captain Rourke.

Freya had seen him twice before, always at a distance: a lean man in his forties with a face like a closed ledger, sandy hair going gray at the temples, hands that never seemed to be doing nothing. Intelligence officers in novels were supposed to look like wolves or spiders. Rourke looked like a schoolmaster who had grown tired of children.

"Crane." Rourke did not stand. He indicated a wooden chair across from the desk with two fingers. "Sit. Thank you for coming promptly."

"You said eleven."

"I said eleven." Something that might have been approval crossed the schoolmaster's face. "You drive for Mrs. Marlowe."

It was not a question, but Freya treated it as one. "I did. Before her arrest."

"You're not employed by her anymore?"

"My wages stopped when she stopped paying them. I do odd work. Letters, mostly. People still need things carried."

"People always need things carried." Rourke opened a folder on his desk — a manila folder, thin, which Freya's eyes went to despite himself and then away from, deliberately, the way you look away from a card you're not supposed to see. "That's rather your reputation in this town, isn't it? The man who carries things. Discreet. Literate. Knows everybody from the fish market to the yacht club."

"I know who hires me."

"Which is nearly everybody." Rourke leaned back. On the desk between them lay an ashtray, a bell for summoning the clerk, and a cup of tea gone cold. No handcuffs. No second man guarding the door. Everything about the room said *this is a conversation*, and Freya distrusted it the way he distrusted calm water over a sandbar.

"Why am I here, Captain?"

Rourke smiled slightly, as if Freya had asked the wrong question politely. "Because you're useful. Because you can read four languages and half this garrison can't read one. And because—" he turned the folder around and slid a single sheet across the desk, "—I'd like your opinion on a document."

The sheet was a copy. Freya knew it was a copy before he touched it because the original paper in this town had all been seized weeks ago, catalogued, boxed. This was army stationery, crisp, the handwriting not handwriting at all but a clerk's careful transcription. His own transcription habits, ironically, were better disguised.

It was a letter. Intercepted correspondence, marked with a reference number and a date nine days past. Written in English by someone signing himself only *A friend of the yard*, addressed to no one, found — according to the stamp — in the dead-letter bin at the post office. It spoke of boats. Of a hull that could be finished if certain materials could be obtained. Of *a man who reads everything and forgets nothing, who can be trusted because he has already trusted us*.

Freya read it twice. His heart was performing its own arithmetic — fast, then faster — but his hands were steady, because his hands had learned years ago, holding letters over candles while he read other people's secrets, that steadiness was a skill like any other and could be practiced.

"This is the document," Rourke said. "What's your opinion?"

"It's vague."

"It is. Deliberately so, I think. Someone writing around a censor." Rourke watched him. "But notice the phrasing. *A man who reads everything.* In a town where the army employs exactly one civilian reader of intercepted mail."

"That would be me."

"Yes. That would be you." Rourke let it sit there. Outside the window, a truck changed gear on the quay road. Somewhere below, gulls argued over something dead. "Do you know this correspondent?"

"No."

"No?"

"I know maybe six hundred people in this town, Captain, and most of them have asked me to carry something at some point. A note unsigned, talking about boats — that describes half the shipyard and all the smugglers. I couldn't begin to guess."

"But you have an opinion on it."

Freya set the page down carefully, aligned its edge with the desk's edge, a small fussy motion that bought him three seconds. "My opinion is that whoever wrote it wants whoever reads it to believe there's a trustworthy man available. That's either a recruitment attempt or bait. If it's bait, it's meant to be found, which means the writer wanted it intercepted — which means the writer wanted this conversation to happen." He looked up. "You may be sitting in the trap, Captain, not me."

For the first time Rourke moved — a small shift, elbow off the desk. Not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "You think someone is trying to compromise my reader."

"I think someone wrote a letter designed to be read by intelligence, and you read it, and now you're interviewing me. So either I'm compromised or I'm suspected, and both of those serve whoever wrote it."

A silence. The tea sat cold. Then Rourke laughed — short, genuine, quickly put away like a coin. "You argue well, Crane."

"I'm told I'm useful."

"So you are. Which brings me to the second matter." He drew the letter back, squared it into the folder, and produced from the drawer beneath the desk a second folder — thicker, buff-colored, string-tied. He did not open it. He simply rested his hand flat upon it, the way a man rests his hand on a dog that might bite. "Routine inquiry. That's what the slip said, and I try not to lie in writing. But between men in a room, I'll be plainer. We are reviewing names."

"Names."

"Persons of interest. Not charges — interests. There's a difference, and the difference is that a charge requires evidence and an interest only requires attention." His hand stayed on the folder. "Some of these names come from intercepted mail. Some from informants. Some from documents recovered in the requisition sweep of municipal records."

The room got very slightly smaller. Freya kept his breathing even through force of habit and thought, absurdly, of the ledger — of its weight in his hands when Hobbes had pressed it on him in the corridor, wrapped in a customs manifest, four nights ago; of the oilcloth in the loft above the shipyard where it now slept beside candle stubs and wood shavings; of the pages already copied by other, earlier hands, the ones he had discovered missing, the ones whose absence had been screaming at him since.

They had copied his ledger. Of course they had. And somewhere in that buff folder might be his own handwriting, transcribed, itemized: *Anton Weiss — passage promised. Greta Weiss — school fees advanced. Edith Marlowe — favors owed and owing.* Three years of kindnesses written down like debts, looking now like conspiracy.

"Go on," he said.

"Your name appears." Rourke said it mildly, almost apologetically, the way a doctor says *there's a shadow*. "As a carrier of correspondence. As a literate man moving through town freely. None of which is a crime. But names collect, Crane. Like driftwood. One name washes against another and pretty soon you have a raft." He tilted his head. "Tell me about Anton Weiss."

There it was. Not hidden, not approached by indirection. Laid on the table like a card.

Freya had prepared for this question for four days without knowing it — had rehearsed answers in the dark of his room above the livery, lying awake, testing sentences against the ceiling. Now the rehearsal paid, and he heard his own voice come out level, almost bored.

"The shipwright. Everyone knows Anton. He built half the working boats on this river."

"Do you know him socially?"

"I've drunk his beer at launching days. I've hired him to patch a wheel. I drove his daughter to school once when her mother was ill." Each true. Each useless to deny. Denial of the checkable was the first mistake liars made, and Freya had spent a lifetime watching people lie badly in their letters. "Why do you ask about Anton?"

"Because Anton Weiss is an enemy alien who failed to report for internment." Rourke's voice stayed mild. "Because he is, as far as we can determine, hiding somewhere in this town. And because a letter talks about finishing a boat — and Anton Weiss has a boat half-finished in his loft, or had one, before our last inspection of the yard." He paused. "You visited the shipyard recently. Tuesday of last week. The sentry log notes it."

"I did."

Another pause. Rourke had expected denial, Freya judged, and the plainness wrong-footed him again. Good. Keep giving him truth; it was the only currency the man couldn't counterfeit.

"For what purpose?"

"To buy a boat."

"A boat."

"There's a skiff in the yard, lapstrake, green bottom. Anton built it two seasons ago and never sold it. I want it for spring fishing. I asked him to hold it. He said come back when things settle." Freya shrugged, a whole biography compressed into the gesture. "Things aren't settling, Captain. For anyone."

"And you'd buy a boat from a fugitive."

"I'd buy a boat from whoever owns it when the money changes hands. Anton doesn't own it anyway — the yard does, and the yard is requisitioned, so technically I'd be buying it from the Crown. You should probably add that to my file. Attempting to purchase government property." He let the dryness show, just a little. Fear smelled like fear; insolence smelled like confidence, even when it was only fear wearing better clothes.

Rourke studied him for a long moment. The gulls cried. The truck on the quay road reversed, backing beeps harsh in the cold air. Then the captain untied the string on the buff folder — slowly, theatrically, or perhaps just tiredly; with this man Freya genuinely could not tell performance from habit, and that frightened him more than anything else in the room — and withdrew a single typed page.

He held it out.

"Read this," Rourke said. "And tell me what you notice."

Freya took it. It was a transcription — clerk's hand, army stationery — and his stomach dropped through the floor of the world before he read the first word, because he recognized the format. He recognized it the way you recognize your own coat on a stranger: the columned layout, the abbreviations, the little system of dashes and debt-marks he had invented himself over seven years and never shown another living soul.

It was a page of his ledger.

Copied in full. Every entry. *Weiss, A. — passage, promised, conditions unstated. Marlowe, E. — carriage, indefinite. Vane, S. & J. — goods conveyed, consideration pending.* Names he had written down to remember kindnesses, rendered now in the army's black ink, looking exactly like what Rourke wanted them to look like: a network. An index of conspirators, compiled by a man who knew everyone, signed by no one, found in Box Forty-One of a vault that had been robbed.

His face did nothing. That was the miracle of it, or the practice. Seven years of reading love letters and blackmail and deathbed confessions over candle flames had taught his face to be furniture. Inside, he was running the calculations at speed: how much did they understand? Did they know the handwriting was his? Was this a test of recognition — *do you claim this, Crane?* — or a test of composure?

He read it top to bottom, unhurried, then turned it over as though checking for a second page, then laid it down.

"What am I noticing, Captain?"

"That's the question I'm asking you."

"All right." He tapped the page once. "This is a ledger. Bookkeeping. Debts and favors — the way harbor towns have always done business, except most people keep theirs in their heads and lose them. Whoever wrote this writes a cleaner hand than most clerks I've seen." He looked up. "You want to know if it's mine."

"Do you say it is?"

"No." Steady. God, steady. "I've seen this style before, actually. Old Mr. Calloway the customs agent used columns like that — I sorted his files when I worked the mail room, years back. Half the shipping clerks on the quay learned bookkeeping off Calloway. You could find twenty men in this town whose ledgers look like that." All plausible. All unverifiable. Calloway was dead two years and his files were in an army warehouse, and Freya hated himself with a clean bright hatred even as the words came out smooth as oiled rope.

"Calloway," Rourke repeated. He wrote the name down. Whether it was a real lead or a hook to watch Freya grab, there was no telling. "Interesting."

"Is it in your inventory? The original?" Freya asked, and the question was a gamble and he knew it — but a refusal to ask would be its own answer, and curiosity was the one emotion a records-obsessed occupier would expect from a man like him. "Because if it's municipal property, it'll turn up eventually. Ledgers always turn up. That's the trouble with writing things down."

Something flickered behind Rourke's eyes. Satisfaction, or its imitation. "Yes. It was recovered in the sweep." A beat. "Though not, apparently, in its box. The vault inventory shows it logged, and the physical search shows it absent. Paper doesn't walk, Crane."

"Rats," Freya said. "Customs House is full of them. They ate half the tariff schedules before the war; my father complained about it for a decade. Paper with glue sizing is like cake to them." He spread his hands. "Or a clerk borrowed it. Or it was misfiled. Vaults are run by bored corporals, Captain. I sort your mail; I know what boredom does to a filing system."

"I imagine you do." Rourke retrieved the page, squared it back into the folder, retied the string. The interview, Freya sensed, was entering its final movement, and he had no idea what the verdict was. That was the design, surely. Leave the mouse unable to tell whether the trap sprang or not. "Last thing. Purely administrative."

"Of course."

"Your movements will be logged for the next while. Nothing formal — no pass requirements, no restrictions. Just that the sentries have your description and the gate logs will note your comings and goings. Consider it... housekeeping."

"Housekeeping," Freya echoed.

"You're not detained, Crane. You're not charged. As far as I'm concerned you're a literate civilian doing honest work in an occupied town, and I'd like to keep it that way." Rourke stood, finally, and extended his hand, and his grip was dry and exact. "One piece of advice, free of charge. From a man who reads everything." The echo of the letter, deliberate, a blade shown and sheathed. "Whatever you're carrying these days — carry it lightly. Heavy loads leave tracks in the snow."

"I don't follow."

"No," Rourke agreed pleasantly. "I don't suppose you do. Eleven-thirty. You're free to go. Oh —" as Freya reached the door, "— the skiff. Green bottom, lapstrake. If the sale goes through, I may take up fishing myself. I'll want to know who beat me to it."

Freya held the door handle a moment longer than necessary, which was the only tremble he allowed himself. "I'll send word through the proper channels, Captain."

"Do that. I do so enjoy reading the proper channels."

* * *

Outside, the cold hit him like a slap and he welcomed it. He walked, not too fast, past the gray truck, past the sentry who did not look at him, down the quay steps to the waterline where the wind could take the smell of his own sweat off him. Only there, gripping a mooring ring rusted soft as bread, did he let the shaking start, hands first, then shoulders, breath sawing.

He counted it out the way Greta counted days — one for the letter, one for the name, one for the page, one for the sentry logs, one for the snow — until the arithmetic steadied him.

They had his ledger's contents. They did not have the ledger. They suspected him of everything and could prove nothing, and Rourke had let him walk out of the room, which meant either that Rourke believed him or that Rourke was reeling line and wanted him swimming so the pull would show.

Either way, the clock had changed shape. It was no longer only the ice, no longer only the Vanes' arrest and the mention of *the reader* in their seized books. Now it was a captain with a buff folder and a polite smile, logging every step Freya took past every gate.

And tonight — moonset, the ketch, the water — was the last night there would be time to care which of them was right.

He pushed off the mooring ring, straightened his coat, and started walking toward the shipyard, taking the long way, past the burned bakery, letting the town see him doing nothing at all.
