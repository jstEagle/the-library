## 22. Anton's Last Boat

The candle stub gave him forty minutes, maybe fifty if the draft was kind. Anton Weiss had learned to measure time the way a drowning man measures air — not in hours but in what could be done inside them.

He struck the match against his boot heel and cupped the flame until it steadied. The loft above the sail loft smelled of cedar shavings and cold tar and, beneath it all now, of fear. Fear had a smell. He had not known that before this winter. It was something like wet iron and old sweat and the particular sourness of bread eaten too fast in the dark.

Below him, through three floors of a town that had stopped saying his name, Greta slept. Or lay awake counting, which she did more often than sleeping. He knew because he could hear her sometimes through the floor — not words, she had given up words six weeks ago, but the small rhythmic tap of her finger against the boards. One for the gulls. One for the soldiers at the gate. One for the days since school closed. His daughter had become an abacus, and every night the numbers grew, and every night he lay in the dark wishing he could carry some of them for her.

But tonight there were planks.

* * *

The ketch was nineteen feet on the waterline, which was eleven feet less than any boat he had ever built for money, and it was worth more than all of them together. He had begun her in October, when the requisition officers were still measuring warehouses with their thumbs and laughing about how quickly it would all be sorted. He had begun her the way a man begins a prayer — without certainty that anyone is listening, but with both hands anyway.

She had no name yet. Names invited attention. She was *the loft boat*, *the little one*, *she*. In his head, though — in the place where a shipwright keeps the true drawings, the ones no client ever sees — she had a name already. He would not say it even to himself. Saying it would make her real, and real things could be taken.

He set the candle in its tin saucer against the starboard frame and picked up where he had left off the night before: the second garboard strake, port side, spiled from a pattern he'd cut from the back of a flour sack. The wood was good. That was the one mercy the army's occupation had left him — they had requisitioned the finished boats, the lumber piles, the customs ledgers, but nobody had thought to inventory a shipwright's offcuts. The scraps under the sawdust had become, plank by plank, the most important vessel on the river.

Anton fitted the strake dry first, as he always did. Dry-fit, then again, then a third time, because glue and clamps forgave nothing and he had no wood to waste and no light to waste and no nights to waste either. The plank went against the frames with a whisper. He held it there with one knee and looked along the line of her, stern to stem, the way a priest looks down a nave.

*Good,* he thought, in German, because his thoughts still came in German no matter what the registration forms said. *Gut. Sie ist gut.*

They had made the word *German* into a bad word here, in the town his hands had helped build. He had laid the keel of the customs launch those officers now used to patrol the river. He had hung the doors of St. Anne's. He had rebuilt the wharf piling after the ice took it in '09, standing chest-deep in November water because he was the only man fool enough and able enough, and old Marta Kessler had brought him brandy on the dock after, and everyone had laughed. Everyone had laughed then.

Now his name lived on a list in a box in a vault, and men with forms came to the gate, and his daughter did not speak.

He drove the first copper nail.

The sound was very small. That was the discipline of it — the whole boat built at a volume that would not wake a sleeping child two floors down. He had wrapped his mallet head in leather. He had learned to push nails home with hand pressure alone where he could, to use the drawknife in long quiet strokes rather than short noisy ones. Thirty years of making noise with wood, and now he was unlearning it, plank by plank, like a man teaching himself to write with his left hand.

It occurred to him, driving the second nail, that this was the truest work of his life precisely because it was the quietest. Every boat he had ever built had been built to be seen — launched before crowds, christened with wine, admired by men who would never know what a fair curve cost. This one would be seen by no one. If God or luck or whatever governed harbors was kind, she would slip downriver on a black tide and be forgotten by everyone except the three people aboard her. A boat built entirely out of refusal. Each plank a word in an argument he was having with an empire: *You have listed me. You have measured me. You have decided what I am with your stamps and your categories. Now watch my hands.*

His hands were the one thing they had not been able to classify.

* * *

The candle burned low. Forty minutes gone, perhaps. He paused to trim the wick with his thumbnail and, in the enlarged flame, checked the pattern for the third strake. The spile was close. Not perfect — he was working without his good battens, without his lofts drawn full-scale on the floor, working instead from memory and string and a stick marked in pencil. But a small boat forgives. That was another thing he had relearned: big vessels punish error cumulatively; a nineteen-foot ketch lets you argue with her and win.

In the old days — three years ago, five years ago, the days that now felt like someone else's photographs — he had told Crane about lofting. They'd been sitting on the caprail of the *Meridian*, waiting on Edith Marlowe's driver to finish some errand, and Crane had asked why shipwrights drew boats full-size on the floor before building them half-size drawings.

"Because paper lies politely," Anton had said. "A drawing will tell you a curve is fair when it is not. Lay it out life-size, walk along it, sight down it with your eye level to the floor — the wood cannot lie to you at that scale." And Crane had nodded in that way of his, the way that meant he was filing the sentence away somewhere, in that ledger he carried, that little book of names and debts. Anton had never been allowed to read it. He'd teased him about it once — *what do you write about me in there, boy?* — and Crane had gone carefully blank and said, *the terms of a promise,* and hadn't explained, and Anton had understood enough not to ask twice.

The promise. Safe passage, if ever things turned sour. Said lightly, on a warm evening, over the smell of fresh caulking, the way men promise things they believe they will never have to keep.

Things had turned sour.

And the promise had come back across the harbor to him like a message in a bottle — carried by the same young man, in the same quiet voice, except the voice was quieter now and the eyes older: *There is a boat leaving before the ice breaks. I'm getting you on it.* And Anton, who had spent thirty years being the man who fixed other people's hulls, had said the only thing left in him: *Then I had better have a hull worth putting you in.*

Because that was the other truth, the one he had not said aloud even to Crane: the smugglers' boats were fine for cargo and coin, but a man does not put his daughter on a stranger's hull in winter water unless he has no choice. So he had chosen to have a choice. Nineteen feet of choice, built by candlelight, one stolen hour at a time.

* * *

He worked. The third strake went on port, then its partner to starboard, and the hull began to show what she would be — narrow in the entry, full in the midsection where the load would sit, a transom raked just enough to take a following sea without broaching. He had designed her for the river first and the open water second, because the river was the gauntlet: nine miles of patrol lights, picket boats, a navy that had moved upriver as the cold deepened. After the river, the bay. After the bay — he would not think past the bay. A shipwright builds one plank at a time. A fugitive survives one mile at a time. Same discipline, different wood.

Somewhere in the middle of the fourth strake his mind went where it always went when his hands found their rhythm — to Lübeck, to his father's yard, to the smell of hot pitch and the sound of his father's voice singing the same three bars of the same song all day because he could never remember the rest. Anton had been eight, holding nails the way Greta now counted soldiers. His father had told him: *Ein Schiff ist ein Gebet mit Planken bekleidet.* A ship is a prayer dressed in planks. He had thought it was just something fathers said. He was fifty-one years old, crouched in a hay-loft-turned-shipyard above his confiscated life, and he finally understood it. You do not build a prayer because you are certain of the answer. You build it because your hands need somewhere to put the fear, and because work is the only argument against despair that has never once lost.

Greta's tapping started around midnight. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

Not restless. Deliberate. Their code, invented in the first week of hiding, when speaking aloud felt too dangerous even at a whisper: one tap for *I'm awake*, two for *I'm frightened*, three for *come*. Then a pause. Then, slow and distinct:

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Tap.

*I'm awake. I'm frightened. Come.*

He set down the mallet. Some prayers you answer with wood; some you answer now.

* * *

He came down the ladder rungs he'd built himself, quiet as a man descending into his own grave, and crossed the loft's lower room where the sails of other men's boats hung in their long white shrouds. Greta sat up in the nest of sacking behind the stacked canvas, and even in the near-darkness — one shaded lamp turned down to a coal — he could see her eyes tracking him, bright and unblinking, the eyes of a child doing arithmetic on the world.

"You should sleep," he whispered, though he knew the rule: he talked, she didn't; that was the shape things had taken and he did not press against it. Pressing made it worse. Dr. Aldous had used a word once — before the doctor himself had been registered, and catalogued, and put on a train — *selective mutism*, trauma wearing silence like armor. Anton hadn't needed the Latin for it. His daughter had watched Herr Brandt dragged out of his shop, had watched the bakery burn, had done the sums children should never be given, and had concluded that words were unsafe currency. So she had withdrawn them all from circulation.

He sat beside her. She leaned into him, forehead against his ribs, and her finger tapped against his arm — not code now, just rhythm, the way a person taps when the inside is too loud.

"I worked on the boat tonight," he murmured into her hair. "Two strakes. She's beginning to look like herself. When we get to the water, you'll see — she'll sit in it like she was born there, because she was, in a way. Born right over our heads."

Tap-tap against his arm.

"Yes," he agreed, to the question she hadn't asked, because he had gotten fluent in her silence the way other men got fluent in French. "Soon."

That was a lie of size, and they both knew it, and he felt it go through her like a shudder. *Soon* had been October's word. Then November's. Now it was nearly January and the ice groaned at night out on the river like something huge turning over in its sleep, and Edith Marlowe — before they took her, before the warrant — had told Crane what every riverman already feared: once the ice broke, the navy owned the water until next winter. Weeks, not months. Fewer weeks now.

"Listen," he said, and tilted her chin up so she could see his face, because his face was the last honest document she had left. "I will tell you the truth, since truth is all I can give you that they haven't taxed. We leave when the boat is ready and the night is right and Crane comes for us. Not before. Not after. Your job between now and then is one thing only: eat everything I give you and grow strong, because there will be a rope in your hands on that night, and I need those hands to be certain. Can you do that?"

Her eyes searched his face the way she searched everything now — for the catch, for the fine print, for the moment the world would reveal it had been lying. Then, slowly, she nodded. Once.

"And Greta." He almost stopped. Almost kept the safe thing unsaid. But he had promised himself, up in the loft among the planks, that he would not let the state be the only one defining who his family was. "Our name. You asked me once whether our name is a bad word now."

Her whole body went still.

"It is not," he said. "Weiss means white. It means snow and sailcloth and clean pine and the foam at the leading edge of a wave. Men with lists don't get to change what a word means. Only the people who live inside it get to do that. And in this family, *Weiss* means: we build, and we keep our promises, and we do not become small because someone tells us to. Say it back to me."

She stared. Six weeks of silence, and he was asking — not for words, never for words, he would not do that to her — but for something. Her mouth moved without sound: *Weiss.*

"Good girl," he whispered. "Best shipwright's daughter on the river."

For a moment — one breath, maybe two — the corner of her mouth lifted. Not quite a smile. The sketch of one. The first drawing of a smile, spiled on paper, waiting for fairer weather to build it full-size.

Then her finger tapped his arm, quick and urgent, and pointed upward, toward the ladder, toward the street beyond the shuttered windows.

Boots. More than one pair. Slow, unhurried, coming along the yard fence line — the walk of men doing a sweep, not men passing by.

Anton killed the lamp with his palm and stood in the dark with his heart going like a fouled engine, listening, while above them the unfinished ketch waited in her shroud of canvas with two garboard strakes showing and a prayer still under construction.

The boots stopped at the foot of the outside stair.
