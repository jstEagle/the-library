## 23. Greta Speaks Again

Greta had stopped counting soldiers on the eleventh of November, because on that day there had been too many to count and her arithmetic had failed her for the first time in her life.

She remembered the number anyway. It was thirty-one. She remembered it the way she remembered everything now — not because she wanted to but because her mind was a ledger too, apparently, and it kept its own accounts whether she consented or not. Thirty-one soldiers. Four lorries. One officer with a voice like a door closing. And Papa's hand over her mouth, which she had never once felt before and hoped never to feel again, though she had felt it twice since.

That was the day they searched the shipyard.

* * *

She had been in the loft, of course. She was always in the loft now. School had closed in October — *closed*, the headmistress said, though Greta knew what closed meant when the teacher who stayed behind was the one whose brother drove a lorry for the army — and since then her days had a single shape: up before light, down the ladder only when Papa whistled the low note, bread and dripping, lessons from the half-burned arithmetic book she'd rescued from the schoolhouse skip, and then the boat.

The boat did not have a name yet. Papa said you didn't name a hull until it could float, because names were promises and promises needed water under them. Greta thought this was probably true about people too, though she didn't say so. She hadn't said much of anything since the fourteenth of September, when Herr Brandt was dragged out of his chandlery by men who didn't knock first, and she had watched through the window while his wife stood in the doorway holding a teapot that never stopped pouring, pouring tea onto the step, into the gutter, an entire pot of tea going to waste because her hands had forgotten how to stop.

Greta had opened her mouth that day. She was sure of it. She had been standing at the glass with her breath fogging it and her mouth open, and nothing had come out, and nothing had come out ever since.

Papa took her to Doctor Ellery, who looked into her ears and throat with little cold instruments and pronounced her physically sound. *Physically sound*. Greta had liked that phrase. It meant the doctor agreed the problem was elsewhere, somewhere he couldn't reach with his instruments, somewhere below the ribs where words were made before they became sounds.

"It will come back," Papa told her every night, candle stub burning down between them, his hands still smelling of oakum and pitch no matter how he scrubbed. "Words are like tides, mein Schatz. They go out. They come back."

But she had begun to suspect her tide had gone out for good, and she had started building a life on the mudflats of herself, the way crabs do — sideways, silent, watchful.

* * *

The thing about not speaking, Greta discovered, was that it made you invisible in a way that being small never had. Before, adults had looked over her head; now they looked straight through her. Soldiers on the street glanced at the silent girl in the patched coat and saw nothing worth a second look. A woman selling herring counted her change without meeting her eyes. Even Papa sometimes forgot she was there, murmuring to himself over the frames and planks, and she would sit in the shadow of the unfinished ketch with her slate and chalk and take attendance of the world: three gulls, one patrol boat, two hours until dark, zero words.

Zero was her most constant number. She wrote it at the bottom of every page. Zero words today. Zero yesterday. The zeros stacked up like coins.

Which is why what happened on the morning after the search began as an accounting problem.

* * *

Papa had gone out before dawn. He went out most mornings now, walking the long way to the customs house to sign his name in the enemy register, because the rules said every man of German birth must present himself weekly, and Papa obeyed the rules the way a tree obeys wind — visibly, minimally, without giving up an inch of root. He left Greta in the loft with instructions: bolt the lower hatch, keep off the north window, if anyone comes, be furniture.

*Be furniture.* That was Papa's word for it. She had decided she was a very good chair.

So when boots came into the shipyard just after eight — six pairs, maybe seven, she counted by sound because counting was all she had — Greta did not panic. She moved the oilcloth over the ketch's stern where Papa's newest work showed, banked the candle stub until the loft smelled of nothing but cold tar, and sat down on the crate by the wall with her slate on her knees, becoming, in her own estimation, an extremely convincing chair.

They searched for forty minutes. She timed them.

They went through the wood store. They went through the tool racks, and one of them — a young one, his voice cracking on the vowels — read the maker's stamps aloud like a boy doing sums: *Weiss. Weiss. Weiss.* As if the name itself were contraband. Another laughed and said something about the Kaiser's carpenter, and the first one said quieter, almost apologetic, that the boats were fine work actually, his uncle had a fishing smack built in this yard, and the older soldier told him to shut up and check the loft ladder.

Greta heard him climbing.

There is a particular quality to the sound of someone else's boots on a ladder that leads to you. It is not fear exactly, or not only fear. It is a kind of terrible clarity, as if the world had been blurred your whole life and only now came into focus: each rung, each creak, each scuff of hobnail distinct as struck matches. Greta sat on her crate and did the math she could not stop doing: one ladder, twelve rungs, one hatch above it, and herself — one girl, ninety centimeters tall sitting down, armed with a slate, half a stick of chalk, and silence.

He came up into the loft the way boys come everywhere, too fast, all elbows, and stood blinking in the dimness with a lantern that swung shadows across the rafters.

It was the same one. The young one. The uncle-with-the-smack one.

He looked at her. She looked at him.

"Fritz," someone shouted from below. "Anything?"

"Loft's empty," he called back. Then, to her, lower: "Empty except for you."

Greta said nothing. Chairs don't talk.

He walked the length of the loft, lantern raised, passing within an arm's reach of the oilcloth. Under it lay four months of Papa's nights: the steamed-bent frames, the fair body plan drawn in charcoal on the floorboards, the keel timber smuggled in plank by plank inside sacks of coal dust. If Fritz lifted that cloth, the war arrived in the shipyard for good. Greta understood this with the whole of her body, the way you understand a falling thing is falling.

Fritz stopped. Turned back. Looked at the oilcloth.

And then he looked at her again — really looked, the way nobody had looked at her since September — and something passed across his face that Greta recognized, because she'd seen it on her father's face and in her own mirror: the expression of a person deciding, right now, what side of themselves to be.

He lifted the lantern higher, sweeping the light theatrically across bare rafters, and shouted down, "Nothing here but rats!"

Boots receded. The yard gate clanged. Silence poured back in like water finding its level.

Greta sat on her crate for a long time afterward, listening to her heart do its loud private business. Then she got up, checked the oilcloth, checked the charcoal plan, checked everything twice, and added a new entry to her slate: *One soldier, kind, name unknown, owes us.*

Everyone owed somebody something. That was the harbor's oldest law. Her father said so. Freya Crane said so — well, Freya didn't say things so much as write them, but Greta had seen his ledger once, before, and understood immediately that she and the letter-carrier were colleagues.

*Owes us.* She underlined it.

* * *

Freya came that evening, as he always did on the nights the moon rose late, coming along the reed path with his collar up and his breath showing. He knocked the way he always knocked — two, pause, one — and Papa let him in, and the three of them, the whole secret nation of them, gathered around the candle by the ketch.

Freya talked to Papa in the low fast way he had, hands moving, and Greta caught fragments the way you catch fish with your hands: *ice breaking early... warm front... Vanes want an answer... days, not weeks.*

Then Freya said the thing that broke her open.

"I can't find another boat," he said. "If we miss the ice, it's the camp for you and — " He stopped. He never finished sentences about Greta. He would start them — *if we miss the ice, Greta goes to* — and then his mouth would close like a drawer, and everyone would understand the ending without hearing it. "I need to know the ketch can launch the night I say. Can she?"

Papa ran his thumb along a seam, thinking. "Two more planks on the starboard quarter. Caulking. She needs three nights of good work."

"We may have two."

"Then I'll work faster."

"And if they come back to the yard?" Freya asked, and there it was — the question underneath all their questions, the one they fed and kept and never killed. "They searched once. Rourke doesn't search once."

"They won't find her," Papa said. "The loft looks like a loft. There's nothing — "

"There were soldiers here." 

Both men turned.

Greta was standing by the ladder with her slate pressed to her chest, and her mouth — her long-closed, rusted-shut, tide-gone-out mouth — had opened, and sound had come out of it, and the sound was not about her at all.

"Yesterday morning," she said. Her voice came out strange, low and rusty, like a gate unused all season. "Six of them. Maybe seven. They went through the tools. One climbed to the loft." She swallowed; the next part cost her. "He stood this close to the boat. He said there were rats and left."

Papa made a sound Greta had never heard him make, half prayer and half wound. He crossed the loft in two strides and knelt in front of her, and for a moment the shipwright's hands — hands that could fair a curve by eye, that had built eleven boats and repaired a hundred — hovered at her shoulders, afraid to land, as if she were wet paint.

"Greta." His voice cracked clean through. "You spoke."

She nodded. Then, because she was still herself, still the keeper of accounts: "Forty-one days. I counted."

"Why today?" Freya asked quietly. "Why tell us today?"

Greta considered the question honestly, the way she considered all questions, and found the true answer waiting where she'd least expected it.

"Because it wasn't my name I said first," she said. "When I opened my mouth again. It was yours."

She hadn't planned that part. But it was true — in the instant before *there were soldiers here* came out, some word had risen first, some name, and she had swallowed it, and the name had been *Freya*. Not Papa, whom she'd been calling silently for forty-one days. Not Mama, gone three years now into the ground outside Hamburg. Freya Crane, letter-carrier, keeper of ledgers, the man who measured the world by what fit in two hands and had somehow made room in them for a shipwright and his daughter.

Some words, she thought, are like boats. You don't get to choose the water they float on. You only get to choose whether to launch them.

"You should know," she went on, steadier now, the gate swinging easier with each push, "that the kind one — Fritz — he'll come back alone. Tomorrow, maybe. He wants to see me. I could tell. He looked at me like I was a person." She paused. "People don't do that much anymore."

Papa pulled her in then, finally, arms landing, and Greta let her face go into the wool of his coat where it smelled of pitch and coal dust and safety, and she felt him shaking, this man who had carried her past the burned bakery without a word, this man who built boats against orders, shaken now by four words from his daughter.

Over his shoulder, she watched Freya Crane. He had taken out his ledger — the stolen one, wrapped in oilcloth, the one that could hang half the town — and he was writing in it by candlelight, fast, and she saw him enter something and underline it, and she knew without looking that his entry matched hers.

Everyone owed everybody something. That was the harbor's law.

But some debts, she was beginning to understand, were paid forward instead of back. A boy-soldier who saw a girl and chose mercy. A girl who spent her first words on a warning. A letter-carrier who would spend, soon, something larger than words.

Freya closed the ledger. "Tomorrow," he said, "I need to move. If the ice cracks early — " and then he stopped, because outside, faint but unmistakable, rolling upriver through the frozen dark, came a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

The ice. Talking.

And beneath it, farther off, thin as wire: a whistle. Two short notes and a long.

Papa went white. "That's the yard signal," he whispered. "Someone's at the gate. Now."
