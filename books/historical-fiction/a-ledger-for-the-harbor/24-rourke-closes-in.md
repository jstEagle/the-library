## 24. Rourke Closes In

Captain Rourke had a theory about ledgers.

He'd formed it in the customs house in Cork before the war, when he was a junior man with ink-stained cuffs and a superior who taught him that every smuggling ring, every seditious cell, every conspiracy worth the name eventually wrote itself down. People could lie with their mouths all day, the old man said. But give them a page and they turn honest. They want to remember. They want to be owed. A ledger is a confession that hasn't been read yet.

Rourke had come to the harbor town believing that. Four months of occupation had tested the belief and found it sound. The town ran on paper — manifests, tariffs, debt notes, letters — and underneath the official paper there was always another layer, unofficial, unnumbered, kept by somebody who thought himself careful. Find that somebody, and you found the town's true government.

He thought he might have found it this morning, in a water-stained notebook pulled from the pocket of Silas Vane.

* * *

The arrest itself had been almost disappointing in its ordinariness. Rourke hadn't ordered it; Lieutenant Pryce had run the operation out of the provost's office, acting on a naval intelligence tip about fuel diversion — someone had been siphoning from the navy's own supply chain, and the trail, after two weeks of dull arithmetic, ended at a fish-smoking shed on the mudflats where the Vane brothers stored more than herring.

Rourke went down to the cells at seven because he didn't trust anyone else to do the reading.

The brothers were held separately, which was standard and also cruel, and Rourke made no apology for either. Tomas Vane sat on the bunk with his hands open on his knees, a big man gone soft around the middle, watching the door like a man watching weather. Silas, the younger one, stood when Rourke entered, which was either defiance or habit — dock men stood for officers the way they stood for gulls, without meaning anything by it.

"Sit down, Mr. Vane."

Silas sat.

"You know why you're here."

"Smuggling." Silas said it flatly, the way a man names a disease he's decided not to fight. "We heard."

"Fuel diversion. Selling navy petrol through your cousin at Millbrook." Rourke laid the charge sheet on the table between them, then, deliberately, left his hand on it. "That's the army's problem. It's not mine."

Silas's eyes came up. Careful eyes. Eyes that had spent thirty years measuring what an officer wanted to hear before the officer knew himself.

"I'm intelligence," Rourke said. "My concern is people. Names. Who knows whom. Who carries messages." He let the pause sit. "Your boat has carried passengers this winter. I have statements."

Silas said nothing, but something moved behind his face — not fear exactly, recalculation. A man shifting cargo in the dark.

"The charge for moving contraband is two years, maybe three, if the tribunal is feeling patriotic. The charge for aiding enemy aliens to escape is different. That one can go badly." Rourke opened the notebook now, the water-stained one, and turned it so Silas could see the cover without being able to reach it. "We found this in your coat. You're a better seaman than a bookkeeper, Mr. Vane."

"We keep accounts," Silas said. "Everybody keeps accounts."

"Everyone does." Rourke flipped pages slowly, letting Silas watch him read. Most of it was what you'd expect: sums owed for tobacco, for tar, for a barrel of nails; half-legible entries about runs up the coast with dates coded as weather. But twice, three times now that Rourke counted again — four times — the same notation appeared. Not a name. An abbreviation, or perhaps a title. In Tomas's hand and in Silas's both, which meant it was shared vocabulary, agreed upon, used deliberately.

*"Paid — per the reader."*

*"Reader's cut, 2/6."*

*"Ask reader re: north pax."*

North pax. North passengers. Rourke had read enough intercepted correspondence these past months to know what north meant in this harbor's dialect of silence. North meant away. North meant the German shipwright who hadn't reported for his internment train in November, whose loft the search parties had torn apart twice and found only candle stubs and shavings, and whom Rourke privately believed was still within twenty miles of the river because men did not abandon unfinished boats.

And reader. He set the word down inside himself and looked at it.

There was a man in this town who read things. Who had, apparently, for years — long enough to become infrastructure. The suffragist Marlowe's driver, some of the older files suggested, a courier who'd carried her letters everywhere before the requisition. Freya Crane. Rourke had interviewed him once, casually, over copied pages from a recovered ledger — a ledger, Rourke noted, that had vanished from evidence custody along with its box number, a failure of clerical discipline he had not yet finished punishing. Crane had lied to him with a steady voice and sweat through his coat, and Rourke had let him walk because steady lies were cheap and he'd wanted to see where the man would go next.

He'd gone, apparently, into business with smugglers.

"Who is the reader?" Rourke asked.

Silas looked at the wall. It was a practiced look, a dockworker's whole philosophy compressed into a single direction of gaze. The sea takes what it takes and tells no one; neither will I.

"You understand I can make this very expensive for you," Rourke said. "Both of you. Your cousin at Millbrook too, and your cousin's wife, who signs her husband's invoices when he's drunk, which is forgery of a naval document. I can spend a week pulling that thread and hang expenses on every knot." He closed the notebook. "Or you can tell me a name, and I lose interest in everything except the name."

Nothing. The wall continued to be a wall.

Rourke hadn't expected otherwise, quite. The Vanes were professionals, and professionals understood arithmetic: a name given under pressure would be denied by everyone, verified by no one, and the giving would be remembered forever by people with long memories and access to boats. Silence cost them two years apiece and bought them standing. He respected it the way he respected good fortifications — by planning to go around rather than through.

But he'd learned long ago that prisoners talk to each other anyway, through walls, through meals, through the grapevine of guards who could be bribed with cigarettes, and that the thing to do was control the channel. So he stood, tucked the notebook inside his tunic, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Vane — I don't need you to tell me. I only needed to see whether you'd flinch at the phrase." He watched Silas's shoulders, and got what he'd come for: the smallest tightening, a man hearing a guess land near the truth. "You flinched. Good evening."

In the corridor he stopped at the duty sergeant's desk. "Tomas Vane gets his supper late tonight. Half an hour late. And the tray goes in through the main door, not the hatch."

The sergeant frowned. "Sir?"

"So he can be seen carrying it past the other cells. Men notice trays, Sergeant. Men notice everything when they've got nothing to do but notice." Rourke put a coin on the desk, not a bribe, just theater — a captain who paid attention to detail. "I want to know who asks about him. Anyone. Even a guard."

* * *

Back in his office above the customs house, Rourke spread the campaign across his desk the way other men spread maps.

The room was cold; the stove drew poorly and he'd stopped complaining about it, because complaints marked a man as uncomfortable and discomfort was a kind of information. He worked in his coat. On the blotter he arranged his materials: the Vane notebook. The inventory of the requisitioned records, with its maddening gap where Box 41 should have been logged out and wasn't. The file on Edith Marlowe, arrested eleven days ago mid-speech, held without charge while the legal office decided whether a woman shouting about votes was sedition or merely noise. And beneath those, a thin folder he kept apart from the others because it was personal, though he'd never have admitted that: the interview notes on Freya Crane.

He read back through them. Crane, driver and courier, literate, unremarkable, employed since before the war by the Marlowe household. Interviewed once following the recovery of a handwritten ledger containing names of residents of enemy birth alongside sums and favors — a document whose existence was itself suggestive, and whose disappearance from custody was a scandal Rourke had buried in paperwork because scandals about one's own vaults traveled badly upward. At interview, Crane had explanations for every name. Charity, he'd said. Debts. A town runs on favors. His voice had been level throughout. Only his coat had told the truth.

Steady voice, sweating coat. And now: *the reader*, appearing in a smuggler's accounts, attached to payments and to *north pax*.

It was not proof. Rourke distrusted proof, which arrived conveniently and usually forged; he preferred pattern. And the pattern was assembling itself with a neatness that made him suspicious of his own eagerness. Consider: a courier who read letters. A ledger-keeper whose ledger walked out of a locked vault. A smuggler network that suddenly, this winter, acquired a passenger service pointed north. A records clerk — Hobbes, the corporal, amiable, mediocre, the sort of man who lost keys the way other men lost gloves — stationed precisely between the vault and the world.

Four threads. Any one of them snapped under weight. Braided together, they'd hold a man.

But Rourke had learned patience in Cork too, and something else besides: that the moment you move against a man like this is the moment the network does what networks do — scatters, burns, forgets. Move too early and you catch couriers and lose the web. He didn't want couriers. He wanted the web entire, and ideally he wanted it caught in motion, in flagrante, boats at the waterline, papers in oilcloth, guilt so dense even a tribunal couldn't misfile it.

Which meant timing. Which meant he needed to know how close the network was to moving.

He took out a fresh sheet and wrote, in his small clerk's hand, a list of unknowns:

*Identity of reader — probable: F. Crane. Confirm.*
*Location of A. Weiss and daughter.*
*Status of Box 41 contents — copied? destroyed? removed?*
*Hobbes — compromised? Watch.*
*Next run north — when. Ice report says river opens within days.*

He underlined the last line twice. The ice was the master clock here, and it kept its own counsel. The warm front sitting over the valley had been eating the river all week; the naval patrol launches at the mouth were fueled and crewed and waiting for open water. Once the patrols came upriver, no smuggler's boat would survive ten minutes of the channel. Whatever the Vanes' passenger service intended, it intended it soon — days at most, hours possibly — or never.

Four days, Rourke wrote at the bottom of the list, and then crossed it out, because a guess written down became a promise to oneself and promises to oneself were how careers died. Instead he wrote: *Act on first hard fact.*

Then he began manufacturing facts.

* * *

By noon he had set three machines running.

The first was simple: he sent a clerk down to the harbormaster with a request — genuine, official, and deliberately loose-lipped — for the registry of all vessels surveyed for seaworthiness this winter, including private yards. If Anton Weiss had finished a boat in secret, someone had supplied him with canvas, paint, fastenings, and a berth, and suppliers talked even when shipwrights didn't. Within a day he'd know whether a hull existed. A hull was a hard fact.

The second machine was Corporal Hobbes himself. Rourke didn't summon him — summons warned. Instead he arranged to encounter him, socially, at the canteen, where Hobbes took his supper at six-fifteen with the punctuality of a man whose life had narrowed to mealtimes. Rourke bought him a beer and talked about nothing: the cold, the mail backlog, the incompetence of the quartermaster's office. He watched Hobbes drink. He watched him not ask about the Vane arrests, which was in itself remarkable — the whole barracks was talking about the Vanes, and a man with nothing hidden would have asked something, even just gossip. Hobbes asked nothing, laughed at the right moments, and left at seven o'clock sharp, walking slightly faster than usual.

Men walking slightly faster than usual. Rourke filed it.

The third machine was the cruelest, and he set it in motion knowing it was cruel: he authorized the transfer of Edith Marlowe from the magistrate's holding rooms to the army detention block, effective Friday, with the paperwork routed through the ordinary channels — visible channels. If the network had any reach left, if the reader was real and attentive, the movement of Edith Marlowe would function as a stone dropped in a pond. Either the ripples showed him faces, or the pond was dead and he could act against the Vanes without fear of scattering anything worth catching.

He disliked using a woman as bait. He noted the dislike, examined it, and found it shallow — sentiment, the occupational disease of intelligence work — and set it aside. She had chosen her war. She'd stood on a platform and shouted while her country armed itself; she was not a bystander swept up in events. Still, he gave the order for her transfer to be conducted respectfully, with a covered cart rather than a lorry, because respect cost nothing and occasionally bought cooperation later.

At four o'clock the first result came back, early and wrong-footing him.

Not the vessel registry — that would take until tomorrow. Not Hobbes. It was the duty sergeant from the cells, upstairs, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man reporting an anomaly he doesn't fully believe.

"Begging pardon, Captain. You asked about visitors, inquiries, anyone asking after the Vane brothers."

"I recall."

"There's been one inquiry. This morning, before you went down. A woman came to the front desk asking could she bring the brothers their Sunday clothes and some tobacco, family friend, she said. Desk sergeant turned her away, took her name like regulations say."

"And?"

"She gave a name, sir. Mrs. Aldous, off Harrow Lane." The sergeant shifted. "Only — my wife knows Harrow Lane, and there's no Mrs. Aldous on it she's ever heard of. Might be nothing."

Might be nothing. Rourke thanked him, waited until the door closed, and sat for a while with his pen idle.

A false name, volunteered smoothly, at the front desk, within hours of the arrests — which meant the network had learned of the arrests almost immediately, through channels he hadn't mapped, and had responded not by running but by provisioning. Clothes and tobacco for jailed friends. That was discipline, that was loyalty, and most of all that was organization. Somewhere a decision had been made, quickly, calmly: we cannot free them, so we will care for them, and no one will know us.

He almost admired it. Then he did admire it, briefly and professionally, and got back to work.

He spent the last hour of the day with the Vane notebook again, reading it a third time, hunting not for names but for rhythm. Ledgers had rhythms. Entries clustered before runs and thinned after; payments for provisions — bread, lamp oil, fresh water — preceded departure by two or three days, because smugglers provisioned late to keep the food good. He went through the winter's pages with a pencil, marking purchases, and the pencil found what he'd hoped it wouldn't and feared it would: the last entry, dated five days ago, was bread, cheese, lamp oil, fresh water in quantity — quantities, he calculated, that fed more than two men.

Provisioned five days ago. Waiting on weather. Waiting on ice.

He looked out the window. Downriver the light was going copper behind the cranes, and the river lay under its gray plate of ice, groaning now in the warm snap, the sound carrying up through the town like something turning over in its sleep. Two days ago the naval launch commander had told him open water at the mouth within the week. Once the launches came upriver, the game was over; whatever moved, moved before then or not at all.

Rourke took out a clean sheet and wrote the order he'd been drafting in his head all afternoon: surveillance on the shipyard, doubled; a watch on the Marlowe house — empty now, sealed, but watched; a discreet eye on Corporal Hobbes's movements off-duty; and a note to the launch commander that he wanted to be informed personally, at any hour, the moment the channel was judged navigable.

At the bottom, after consideration, he added one line more — an instruction to pull every piece of intercepted civilian correspondence from the past year that mentioned books, accounts, reading, or letters, and bring it to him unsorted. Somewhere in that paper flood, he suspected, the reader had left fingerprints of a literary kind. A man who read everything eventually read something he shouldn't have, and quoted it, and quoting was a signature.

He signed the order, blotted it, and sat a moment longer in the cooling office while the stove ticked and failed and ticked.

Four days. Fewer, probably. The ice was talking, and Rourke had lived beside frozen rivers long enough to know that when the ice began to talk, it was negotiating with spring, and spring always won.

Somewhere below him in the dark, he thought — in the loft or the yard or the reeds — a shipwright was hammering planks by candlelight, and somewhere a man with a steady voice was counting the same nights he was, and one of them would finish first.

Rourke blew out the lamp and went down to make sure it wouldn't be the other one.
