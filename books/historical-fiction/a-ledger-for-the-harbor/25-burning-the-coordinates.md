## 25. Burning the Coordinates

The stove in the back room of the chandlery had a crack down its left side that let out a thin whistle when the fire drew hard. Freya had noticed it years ago, buying tar and rope for Edith's errands, and had never thought of it as anything but an annoyance. Now it sounded like a warning he was choosing to ignore, which felt appropriate.

He laid the papers out on the floorboards first, all of them, before he burned anything. He wanted to see the whole shape of what he was about to amputate. That was a habit from the ledger itself — measure everything by what fits in two hands, and know exactly what your hands are holding before you let go.

There wasn't much, in the end. Four days since Rourke arrested the Vanes on charges of smuggling — charges that were true, though not the whole truth — and four nights of Freya going through every hiding place he'd ever used: the loose brick behind the stove at Mrs. Calloway's boarding house, the hollow under the third stair, the oilskin packet sewn into the lining of his spare coat, the space beneath a floorboard in Edith's empty office where he'd once hidden her rally funds. He had always believed his memory was the best vault in town. He'd been wrong about a great many things lately.

What he assembled on the chandlery floor:

Three notes in Silas Vane's hand, block letters, no signature, arranging pickup times in the reeds. One note in Josiah Vane's hand, which was smaller and slanted the other way, confirming payment "in kind" — the phrase they'd agreed on for fuel, because neither of them had wanted to say *navy diesel* out loud even on paper.

A scrap of chart paper with the coordinates of the navy fuel cache written in Freya's own neat script, folded twice. This was the wound. This was the thing he had copied out of intercepted correspondence with hands that shook so badly he'd had to brace his elbow on the table to keep the figures straight. He had given those numbers to the Vanes, and the Vanes had taken them, and whether the brothers had sold them onward or simply kept them as leverage or scrawled them in their own books — that was the question Rourke would be answering right now, this hour, with the Vanes' ledgers open under a lamp in the customs house.

And then there was the ledger itself.

His recovered ledger sat on his knee like something rescued from a drowning. Box Forty-One. One hour in the vault. Pages already copied by intelligence officers — he knew that now, knew which pages, because Rourke's interview had shown him the shape of what they held. The names of dockworkers of German birth, recorded in defiance of the registration lists, with their real trades and their real families. The debts and favors of half the harbor. And, entered in his tidy hand on a Tuesday in October because he recorded everything, everything, because keeping was what he did:

*Vane, S. & J. — passage north, 2 pax + 1, price: cache coordinates, paid.*

It was one line. Eleven words. It connected him to the smuggling run, to the coordinates, to the theft of the ledger itself, to treason on three separate counts. A firing squad needed less than that. Rourke needed less than that.

Freya opened the ledger to the page.

* * *

He had started keeping records at fourteen, working the ferry skiff for old man Brandt, because men who paid in fish instead of coin still owed you something countable, and Freya had learned early that a debt written down is a debt that exists. His father had kept no records at all — a stevedore, illiterate, who trusted everyone and died owing three months' rent — and Freya had decided somewhere in the grief of that winter that he would never be caught unproven again. Every name. Every favor. Every promise made to him and by him, entered fair and dated.

It had seemed like integrity. It had seemed like the opposite of the army's forms, which turned his neighbors into categories; his ledger gave them back their names, their actual lives, the true weight of them. He had told himself that right up until the sweep took it, right up until he saw his own handwriting listed in the requisition inventory like a weapon awaiting inventory classification.

But here was the thing he understood only now, kneeling on the chandlery floor with a box of matches and a cracked stove: the ledger had never been only theirs. It had been his too. The Vane line wasn't a betrayal of the dockworkers. It was a betrayal of himself — proof that even his private book of truths contained the evidence that could end him. You cannot carry everything. He had built a life on the belief that you could. That if you wrote it all down faithfully enough, honestly enough, the record itself would protect you, because truth was ballast and ballast keeps a boat upright.

The river outside said otherwise. The river said ice breaks early when the weather turns warm, and patrol boats move within hours, and Anton Weiss and a twelve-year-old girl were loading a ketch tonight at moonset, and Freya Crane had perhaps four days before Rourke finished reading the Vane ledgers and came looking for *the reader*.

He struck the first match against the box and held it to the corner of the page.

The paper caught reluctantly — cheap stock, damp from the vault — and he had to cup the flame with both hands like a boy lighting a cigarette he shouldn't have. Then it went up all at once, the way paper does, the way four years of entries go up when you feed them to a fire. He watched the ink darken and lift. He watched the Vane line vanish. He watched a debt old man Brandt had owed him at fourteen vanish too, and the cost of Greta Weiss's boots, and the promise he'd made to Marta Kellner about her husband's wages, and forty other small honest weights that had nothing to do with anything except that they lived on the same pages as the things that could kill him.

You cannot burn one line. That was the lesson. Paper doesn't take surgery. You can tear a page out, but tearing leaves a stub, and a stub is a confession — *something was here, ask me what.* So it was all of it or none, and none was death, and so: all of it.

He fed the ledger in sections. Cover last. The leather curled and blackened and smelled like a shoe left too near a stove, ordinary and obscene. The brass clasp fell through the grate onto the ash pan and lay there glinting, the one part of the book that refused to be destroyed, and he stared at it for a long moment before picking it up and putting it in his pocket. He didn't know why. Maybe because it was the only piece that had ever been just metal, just hardware, guilty of nothing.

The whistle in the stove crack rose to a thin keen as the fire drew, and he let it sing.

* * *

The rest went faster, and that was its own kind of grief — how quickly a network burns. Silas Vane's three notes: gone in under a minute. Josiah's careful slanted hand: gone. He saved the chart scrap for last, because he wanted to look at the coordinates one final time, and he hated himself for wanting that, and he looked anyway.

Forty-seven degrees and some minutes north. Numbers that had cost him more than any words he'd ever written, including the love letter. Especially the love letter — no, he corrected himself, kneeling there, feeding the chart paper to the flames edge-first. Not especially. That was cowardice talking, ranking his sins so the freshest one could hide behind the oldest. He had betrayed a duty of confidence to the army to save a shipwright and his daughter, and he would do it again tomorrow, and he had also broken something in himself that morning in the records room when he copied those figures, a clean pane he didn't have a name for, and both things were true and neither excused the other and the fire didn't care.

The paper flared blue-green where the ink pooled and then went to ash with the rest.

When it was done he swept the ashes cold, methodically, crushing every fleck of char that still held a legible curve, and carried them out the back door in the ash pan and scattered them along the waterline where the tide could finish the work. A gull screamed at him from a piling, aggrieved, and he thought of Greta counting gulls in the loft, her arithmetic of terror, and had to stand still for a moment with his hands on the rail until his breathing steadied.

Then he went back inside and faced the question he'd been postponing: what was left?

Not much. That was the point. That was the whole discipline of it. He laid out the survivors on the floorboards the way he'd laid out the condemned, and counted.

His identity papers — registration card, work permit for the army mail detail, the letter authorizing him to handle intercepted correspondence. All of them forged or finessed to one degree or another, all of them necessary to walk past a sentry tonight, all of them staying.

Edith's remaining letters, the ones he'd retrieved from the evidence room — hers, not his to burn, and anyway they proved charity, not sedition. They stayed, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden somewhere new that he invented on the spot and memorized twice.

And the blank pages. A dozen sheets of good rag paper he'd kept from the ledger's back section, untouched, unnumbered, carrying no history at all. They stayed too. He couldn't have said why, exactly, except that a man who has just burned everything he ever recorded needs to believe recording is still possible — that the habit might survive the archive.

He packed the blanks into his coat. The brass clasp clicked against them in the pocket, small and cold, like a pulse.

* * *

Mrs. Calloway found him at the door of the boarding house at dusk, and she looked at him the way landladies look at men who smell faintly of woodsmoke at the wrong hour.

"You've been burning something," she said.

"Rubbish."

"Mr. Crane." She lowered her voice and glanced up the street, where a patrol was passing, two soldiers swinging their arms in the exaggerated rhythm of the bored. "I've kept rooms for thirty-one years. I know rubbish burning from paper burning, and I know paper burning from *important* paper burning, and I also know better than to ask." She pressed a parcel into his hands — bread, cheese, a flask that sloshed with something stronger than tea. "For your errand tonight. Whatever it is."

"It's delivering a letter," he said, and heard how the old answer sounded in his mouth now, worn smooth as a river stone.

"Of course it is." She squeezed his arm once, hard, and went back inside, and he stood on the step understanding that he had just been added to someone else's ledger — an entry he'd never see, kept in a place no soldier would ever search.

* * *

Full dark found him at the reed line, an hour before moonset, doing the last thing there was to do: nothing. Waiting. The ketch wouldn't put out until the moon dropped behind the ridge and gave them ninety minutes of true dark, and Anton would be checking his lines twice and Greta would be counting — gulls, waves, heartbeats, whatever arithmetic held her together — and Freya would stand here in the cold with empty pockets where the evidence used to be.

Empty pockets. He kept testing the idea, pressing his thumb into it like a bruise, looking for the fracture. Fourteen years of keeping, and now he carried nothing but blanks. It should have felt lighter. Everyone said secrets weighed on you; the whole vocabulary of concealment was weight, burden, load. But standing in the reeds with the ice groaning out on the river — a long tearing sound, like furniture dragged across a floor above you — he found the lightness was worse. The ledger had been a self. However dangerous, however damning, it had been *him*, complete, accountable, every debt acknowledged. He had burned the version of himself that kept everything, and what remained was a man holding a flask of contraband brandy and a head full of testimony, waiting to see if the night would let him spend it.

A sound behind him — soft, deliberate, a boot placed flat rather than rolled heel-to-toe — and his whole body went rigid, hand closing on nothing, absolutely nothing, no knife, no paper, not even the clasp, he'd left the clasp hidden under the stove brick, stupid, stupid—

"Hobbes," said Corporal Hobbes, stepping out of the dark with his collar up and his breath smoking. "Don't shout. For God's sake don't shout."

Freya turned slowly. "You're supposed to be on shift."

"I was. I'm not anymore." Hobbes's face was gray in the starlight, and there was something wrong with his left eye, swollen at the lid. "Rourke read the Vane ledgers today. All afternoon. There's a word in them — *the reader* — and he stopped writing notes about an hour ago, which is when I knew, because Captain Rourke always writes notes." He swallowed. "He'll have it by tomorrow. Maybe tonight, if the brothers talk, and Silas will talk, Silas talks when you look at him hard."

"Then why are you here?"

Hobbes looked out at the river, at the black ice and the far shore lights of nowhere in particular. "Because I lost a key once," he said, "for exactly one hour, and I've been trying to figure out ever since whether I'm a thief or something else, and I think—" His voice snagged. "I think the boat goes tonight. Your boat. I checked the patrol schedule."

Freya's blood went very quiet. "How—"

"It doesn't matter how. What matters is the picket at the second bend got new orders at six o'clock. They're stopping everything after moonset, Mr. Crane. Everything." Hobbes finally looked at him, and behind the swelling his eye was steady. "Moonset is in forty minutes. And whatever you're not carrying — you'd better go not-carry it downriver fast."

Out on the ice, something vast shifted and cracked, and the sound ran the length of the river like a sentence being passed.
