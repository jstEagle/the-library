## 26. Visiting Edith

The visiting room at the county gaol smelled of boiled cabbage and floor wax, and Edith Marlowe had decided within her first week that she would not let either smell defeat her. She sat now at a scarred table with her hands folded — she kept them folded deliberately, because she had noticed that folded hands made the matron uneasy, as though a woman who could sit still might be capable of anything — and watched the door.

She had known Freya Crane would come before he knew it himself. She had read the boy — no, the man; she corrected herself even in private thought, because a movement that called its drivers boys would never win its own argument — since the first winter he'd carried for her. He was a man who measured everything: distances, debts, hours, the weight of a letter in his palm. Such men always came eventually to the thing they couldn't measure. And then they came to her, because she was, whatever else she'd become in the eyes of the government, still the person who could tell you what a thing weighed when no scale would serve.

The bolt turned. The matron opened the door and Freya stepped through carrying a leather document case under one arm, wearing his most official expression, which had never once fooled anyone.

"Miss Marlowe," he said formally. "Counsel sends these papers regarding your case."

"Thank you," said Edith gravely. "Do sit down, Mr. Crane."

The matron lingered at the door, a heavy woman named Mrs. Prewitt who had, over three weeks, progressed from open hostility to something Edith could only describe as reluctant curiosity. Mrs. Prewitt had begun, on the fourth day, to ask questions. Not many. One or two, delivered while pretending to check the bars of the window. *Is it true they want women to have the vote in England already? Is it true you spoke to mill girls?* Edith had answered each one plainly, without rhetoric, and had watched something shift behind the woman's face like ice moving on a river.

"You may leave the papers," Mrs. Prewitt said now. "I'll be outside the door. Ten minutes."

"Twenty," said Edith. "Legal consultation is allotted twenty minutes by regulation. I've read the regulations. It's remarkable what one can read in here." She smiled at the woman, and Mrs. Prewitt, after a moment's visible struggle with herself, grunted and pulled the door shut.

They listened to her boots recede down the corridor.

Freya set the document case on the table but didn't open it. His hands were restless — that was new, Edith thought. Three years of driving and delivering had given him hands as steady as a surgeon's, and now the fingers kept finding each other and letting go, finding each other and letting go.

"How are they treating you?" he asked.

"They're treating me as a nuisance, which is the correct treatment. A nuisance gets fed, gets her letters, gets left mostly alone. It's the dangerous ones they break." She leaned back. "I'm a nuisance, Freya. I intend to remain one. Now. Tell me about Anton Weiss, and don't arrange the story first. Give it to me raw."

Something moved through his face — relief, she thought, and beneath the relief a kind of exhaustion so deep it had gone past tiredness into geography, into landscape. He told her. The requisition sweep, the ledger taken from his rooms, Box Forty-One, Hobbes, the first failed attempt at the vault, the Vane brothers and their price, the coordinates he'd copied out of intercepted mail. He told her about Edith's own arrest warrant sitting in a file somewhere, and how he'd learned of it, and how he'd broken into the evidence room and found among her seized correspondence a letter he'd written seven years ago and never delivered — to a woman named Clara, who had left the harbor before the ink was dry, and whom Edith had never heard him mention.

He did not cry. But his voice went flat and careful on that part, the way a voice goes when a man is walking across thin ice and has decided not to look down.

Edith let the silence sit after he finished. She had learned long ago that silence was not the absence of speech but a kind of speech itself, and that most people could not bear more than eight seconds of it. She counted. At eleven seconds, Freya looked up.

"You're going to say I should leave," he said. "Get on the boat with them. Go north."

"I'm going to say nothing of the kind."

That surprised him. She saw it land — the small recalibration of a man who had rehearsed an argument all the way across town and arrived to find the argument unnecessary.

"Then what—"

"I'm going to say you must save Anton. You must get him and Greta onto that ketch and downriver before the ice breaks, and you must do it cleanly, without leaving threads. And then—" she held up one finger before he could speak, an old habit from platforms, "—and *then* you will come back here. To this town. To this trial. Because my trial needs a witness, and the only witness alive who can read what the prosecution will call evidence is a man who reads everything."

"The ledger's burned. The pages I tore out—"

"Burned, yes. But you kept the rest. You told me yourself — wrapped in oilcloth, hidden where only you can find it. Those pages prove what my 'seditious association' actually was. Food for the Kessler family when their shop was boycotted. Money for the widow Brandt. Letters carried free for every dockworker too proud to ask. They call it sedition, Freya. Your pages call it charity. Which of us do you think a jury will believe, if someone finally puts the truth in front of them in ink?"

"And if they hang me for it?"

It came out harder than he'd meant it to — she could see that, see the flicker of apology cross him. Edith didn't flinch. She had been stared down by editors, aldermen, husbands of suffragists, and once by a bishop, and she had learned that flinching taught people they could make you flinch.

"Then they'll hang me knowing I was right," she said. "Which is more than they'll get if everyone scatters. Freya, listen to me. This is the part nobody wants to hear, so I'll say it plainly and you'll hate it, and afterward we'll both pretend I said it gently." She unfolded her hands — deliberately, again, letting him see them open, empty, steady. "A movement does not die when its leaders are arrested. It dies when its members conclude that being arrested means losing. Every time one of us runs, they learn that fear works. Every time one of us stands in the dock and answers their questions in a clear voice, they learn something else. I have spent twenty years teaching women that a courtroom is just a platform with worse acoustics. Do you imagine I'll crawl out of this one through a smuggler's boat?"

"It isn't the same. They're talking about internment camps for—"

"For enemy aliens, yes. For German-born dockworkers whose only crime was building this harbor with their hands. I know. I also know what they're talking about for me. 'Seditious association' is elastic, Freya. It stretches to fit whatever they need it to fit. Today it's speeches. Tomorrow it's letters. Next month it could be a rope, if the war goes badly enough and they need an example with a familiar face." She paused, and softened — not her argument, never her argument, but its temperature. "I am not asking you to be brave. I am asking you to be useful. There's a difference, and the difference matters. Bravery is what you feel. Usefulness is what you do. Save the shipwright. Then testify. That's all. That's the whole of what I ask."

* * *

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside the narrow barred window, gulls were working the harbor wind, and Edith followed their cries with her eyes and thought, not for the first time, that the birds had the right of it — they went where the water allowed and asked no government's permission.

"There's something else," Freya said at last. "Rourke."

"Captain Rourke. Intelligence. Tell me."

"He interviewed me. After the vault. Casual, friendly, offered me cigarettes. He had the copied pages from my ledger on his desk, turned toward me, and he asked about names. I lied to him with a steady voice. I think he believed me. I think." Freya's jaw worked. "Edith, the Vanes are arrested. Smuggling charges, unrelated — Rourke was clever enough to keep it clean. But their books mention 'the reader.' Someone who reads things. Someone who trades in what documents say. When they connect that word to me—"

"They won't connect it in time, if you move tonight."

"Tonight? The ice—"

"Cracked yesterday. You didn't know?" She watched him go still. "Mrs. Prewitt hears everything in this building. The navy moved two patrol boats upriver this morning. The window in your plan closed while you were walking here, Freya. It may already be shut."

He stood up so fast the chair skidded. For a heartbeat he looked, to her great surprise, very young — a boy at a door with news too large to hold, the way he must have stood in some kitchen years ago when war was announced. Then he mastered himself, the way he always did, folding the panic away into some interior pocket, and Edith felt the full weight of what she was doing settle onto her shoulders. She was sending him into the worst night of his life. She was sending him alone.

"No," she said quietly. "Sit down. Not yet. You'll go when the visiting hour ends and not one minute before, because a man who bolts from this room looks like exactly what he is, and Mrs. Prewitt notices everything. Sit."

He sat. His knee bounced under the table.

"Now," said Edith, "we have perhaps fifteen minutes, and I intend to use them. First: the oilcloth bundle. If anything happens to you between here and the river, where is it?"

He hesitated.

"Freya. Where."

"The false bottom of the old ice chest in the boathouse off Cutter Lane. The one with the broken hinge. Nobody uses that boathouse since the ferry changed routes."

"Good. Second: if you reach the crossing, and Anton and Greta are safe, you come back. You don't think about it, you don't weigh it, you don't stand on neutral sand having a conversation with yourself about whether the world needs another exile. You come back, because I have built twenty years of work on the principle that someone stays to tell the truth, and you, Freya Crane, are the best reader I have ever met. Third—"

"Stop." His voice cracked on it, not loudly, but it cracked. "Stop talking like a general sending troops. You're my friend. You're—" He stopped himself, and Edith understood that the sentence he wasn't finishing had Clara in it somewhere, and grief, and seven years of undelivered mail.

"I am your friend," she agreed. "And friends tell each other true things. Here is a true thing." She reached across the table and took his hand — hers dry and cool, his cold as a rail in January. "I chose this. Understand that. I chose it the morning I stood on that platform and watched the crowd thin because patriotism had swallowed reform, and I kept speaking anyway. I chose it when the warrant was drawn. Nobody forced me onto this platform, and nobody forces me off it. What they can do is decide what it costs. That's all arrest is, Freya — the price tag made visible. And I would rather pay it visibly than live invisibly."

"That's a fine speech. You should give it from a platform instead of a cell."

"Give it yourself, then. That's rather the point of the whole movement." She squeezed his hand and let go. "You refuse to accept this gracefully. I can see it sitting in you like a stone. Good. Keep the stone. Graceful men negotiate with themselves until they've talked their way out of doing anything at all. But hear the strategy underneath the sacrifice, because it is genuinely strategy, and you above all people should appreciate a sound ledger. My public trial, with the public press in attendance, is worth more to every woman in this country than my private freedom. If I flee, they win the argument. If I stand, they have to hold the argument in front of witnesses. And arguments held in front of witnesses have a way of being lost by the side with the weaker case. They arrested me because they thought I'd frighten easily. Let's cost them the mistake."

* * *

The boots came back down the corridor. Mrs. Prewitt's knock, twice, brisk.

Freya rose. He gathered the document case — empty except for one sheet of actual legal boilerplate, so the fiction would hold — and stood there a moment with his jaw tight, and Edith watched him fight the last battle, the one against her, the one he was going to lose.

"If the trial goes wrong," he said. "If they convict you anyway. If the sentence is—"

"Then you visit me again, and we complain about the food, and I give you more instructions, and you carry them out, because that is what you do. You carry things." She almost smiled. "You know what I thought the first day you drove for me? I thought: here is a young man who believes the world is a list of obligations, and that if he keeps the list honestly the world will keep it honestly back. And I thought: the world will break that belief in him inside a year." She shook her head slowly. "It hasn't. It's tried hard. It's taken your letters, your records, your friends, your peace. And you still keep the list. Don't ever lose that, Freya. It's rarer than courage, and worth more."

"Edith—"

"Go save the shipwright. That's the instruction. Everything else is commentary."

He nodded — once, sharp, the nod of a man accepting terms he hates — and knocked on the door, and Mrs. Prewitt opened it, and he was halfway through when Edith spoke once more, softly enough that only he could hear it.

"Tell Greta Weiss that school exists where she's going, and that I expect a report on her arithmetic."

Something eased in his face — the smallest loosening, grief and purpose settling into the same frame. "I'll tell her."

The door shut. The bolt slid home. Edith Marlowe returned to her chair, folded her hands, and began, methodically, to compose in her head the statement she would deliver from the dock — the one the newspapers would print, the one the women in the mill towns would read aloud to each other — while outside her narrow window the gulls wheeled over a harbor where, somewhere in the dark between the warehouses and the water, a man was running toward a boat that had one night left to sail.
