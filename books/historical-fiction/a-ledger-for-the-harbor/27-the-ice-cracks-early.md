## 27. The Ice Cracks Early

The sound woke me at half past three — a crack like a rifle shot under the floorboards of the world, then a long groaning sigh that went on and on, the river clearing its throat after weeks of silence.

I was out of bed before I understood why. Bare feet on cold boards, window up, breath smoking. The town lay gray-blue under a sky that had lost its edge of frost, and down at the harbor the ice — which had lain solid and trustworthy as a warehouse floor since December — had begun to move.

Not break. Move. That was worse.

A lead of black water had opened off the coal wharf, wide as a cart track and growing, and along its edges the ice was lifting and tilting like floor planks pried by a crowbar. As I watched, another crack ran the length of the channel, sharp and sudden, and the whole sheet shrugged an inch downstream and settled again, groaning.

Three weeks early. Maybe four. The old men who read ice the way clerks read ledgers had said mid-March if we were lucky, April if the cold held. It was the last week of February, and the river was already talking.

And when the ice broke, the patrol boats would come up from the naval station with their guns bolted to their decks, and every inch of water from the bridge to the bar would belong to them. No ketch, no reed channel, no moonless night would be enough. The Vanes knew the river better than the charts, but they were sitting in cells now, and even if they weren't, no smuggler alive could outrun a steam launch in open water.

We had tonight. Perhaps tomorrow night. After that, nothing.

* * *

I dressed without lighting a lamp and went down through streets still dark and slick, past the bakery — rebuilt now, or half-rebuilt, new brick pale against the scorched stone of the neighboring wall, though nobody had taken the space inside. A town learns what it will tolerate by what it lets burn. The bakery had been allowed to burn because the man who owned it had been born in Danzig, and once it burned, nobody said much about him again.

At the shipyard loft I did not knock. There was a rhythm to knocking now, in this town; you knocked when you wanted witnesses. I climbed the ladder and found Anton already awake, sitting on a crate with Greta asleep against his shoulder, her breath slow and even, his eyes fixed on nothing.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question.

"The whole harbor heard it."

"Then you know what it means." He shifted Greta carefully, laid her back among the blankets in the corner where she'd slept for five weeks, and stood. In candlelight he looked older than he had in autumn, but his hands were steady when he picked up the plane and set it down again — the gesture of a man who needed his hands doing something, anything. "I have the rudder iron to fit yet. The mast step wants shimming. Two days' work if I worked in daylight."

"You won't get two days."

"I know." He looked at the boat — the small ketch, half-finished, tucked into the shadowed end of the loft like a secret kept in wood. Twenty-two feet of refusal. He'd built her plank by plank over five weeks of stolen hours, and every board had been milled and fitted so quietly you'd think the lumber itself had conspired. "How long?"

"One night. Maybe two. When the channel's clear enough for us, it's clear enough for gunboats, and they'll come upriver within the hour. They've been waiting all winter for exactly this."

Anton nodded slowly. He didn't argue. That frightened me more than arguing would have. A man who argues believes there is time to win the argument.

"And Edith?" he asked.

"Her trial is set for the twelfth. She wouldn't leave if I carried her out on my shoulders, and she'd never forgive me if I tried. She wants the courtroom. She says a movement that scatters dies quietly, and she intends to die loudly instead — in public, with a transcript." I heard my own voice go bitter. "It's a strategy, she tells me. Sacrifice with footnotes."

"She is braver than either of us."

"She's braver than anyone. That doesn't make it easier to watch." I pulled the ledger from inside my coat — wrapped now in oilcloth, always, since the vault, since Rourke's casual questions and the copied pages I could never unlearn. Half of it was gone already, torn out and burned page by page in the stove at Mrs. Quill's, every name connected to the Vanes fed to the fire. What remained was thinner, lighter, and heavier for it. "Everything else is ready. Food for six days, water, the chart with the reed channel marked in pencil. I need only know the hour."

Anton was quiet a moment. Then: "Tonight, then. If tonight can be done."

"If tonight can't be done, there is no other night."

He took that the way he took everything — with the particular stillness of a man who has already counted the cost and decided the count doesn't matter. But Greta stirred in her blankets, and both of us went silent until she settled, and I understood that whatever courage Anton had, half of it lived in that sleeping arithmetic of hers: days counted, soldiers tallied, gulls numbered against the coming of spring.

* * *

By seven I was at the customs house steps, and by seven-ten I understood how badly the night had gone.

They were unloading crates. Not filing them — loading them onto an army lorry backed up to the vault entrance, and Corporal Hobbes stood at the top of the steps holding a clipboard he wasn't reading, his face wearing the particular gray look of a man watching his own usefulness end.

I walked past slowly, the way a man walks past something he has no interest in. Hobbes spoke without looking up. "Crane. A word about the road survey."

There was no road survey. I turned and mounted the steps beside him, and he kept his voice at the pitch of bureaucratic tedium, pitched for any passing sergeant to hear. "Warm front came through overnight. Ice is going out fast. Naval command wants everything movable moved inland by noon. The requisition inventory — boxes thirty through fifty — goes on the morning train to the depot at Halden."

My stomach dropped through the steps, through the earth, into the black water under the ice.

"All of them?" I made it sound like a clerk's complaint. Paperwork delays, nothing more.

"All of them. Captain Rourke signed the order himself at five this morning. He's been awake all night, apparently. Something about the smuggling case." Hobbes finally looked at me, one second too long. "Box forty-one travels with the rest."

The train left at noon. The train arrived at Halden by evening. And whatever chance existed of stealing a box from a guarded customs vault in a town where I knew every corridor and every bored sentry's habits evaporated somewhere between here and a fortified depot forty miles inland, behind wire, among ten thousand boxes I couldn't name.

"Thank you," I said. "I'll note it for the survey."

"Note it quickly," Hobbes said, and went back to not reading his clipboard.

I walked away at a normal pace, which cost me more than running would have. At the corner I stopped and did what I always did when the world collapsed: I measured. What can be carried in two hands. The ketch was loaded except for the last stores. The tide turned just before midnight; the reed channel had six inches of water over the bar at low slack, nine at flood. The sentries changed watch at eleven. The navy's launches were moored at the station dock, and they would not come upriver until the channel was navigable — which meant sometime today, perhaps by afternoon, certainly by tomorrow.

But the order for Box 41 was signed, sealed, and scheduled.

One night. I had known it since the first crack at three o'clock, but knowing it in the abstract and hearing it in Hobbes's flat voice were different animals. Now the clock wasn't ticking. It was falling.

* * *

Edith received me in the visiting room of the county jail at ten o'clock, under the pretext of legal errands, across a table scarred by fifty years of other people's desperate fingers. The matron sat by the door and pretended to mend a sock.

"They're moving the records today," I said quietly. "All of them. Including what's left of mine."

"Then you've stopped needing it." Her hands were folded on the table, and though her face was thinner than in autumn and there were new lines the jail had written around her eyes, her voice hadn't changed at all. Edith Marlowe's voice was the same voice that had held eight hundred people silent in a rainstorm the week the war began. "Good. A record can hang you. Memory can't be subpoenaed."

"The ledger isn't for hanging me. The surviving pages are the proof — the relief payments, the passage money, all of it marked plain. Without them, your 'seditious association' is just their word against yours."

"My dear Freya." She leaned forward slightly. "Their word against mine is the whole architecture of this war. Do you imagine a ledger changes it? They'll call your handwriting forgery. They'll call you a liar and a traitor and possibly a German spy, because that is what they do, and it costs them nothing." Her eyes held mine. "Save the Weisses. Tonight, while the water is open and the patrols are still warming their engines. That is the only thing in this town worth the risk, and it is worth all of it."

"And you?"

"I am worth exactly what the newspapers decide I'm worth, and they'll decide more if I stand in the dock with my chin up than if I vanish in the night and confirm every charge." She smiled — the fierce one, the one that had frightened aldermen. "Besides, if you steal me out of jail, Rourke stops chasing smugglers and starts hunting you in earnest, and you cannot outrun a steam launch any more than the Vanes could."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to comfort. It was meant to organize you." She reached across and touched my wrist, briefly, the way she touched everyone — as if to say, *I see you, get on with it.* "Go. You've an hour of daylight and a boat to check."

I stood. At the door I paused, because some things must be said aloud even when they're useless. "If the verdict goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," she said. "It will go however it goes, and either way I will have said my piece in a room where they were obliged to write it down." A pause, and her voice softened by one degree, which from Edith was a flood. "Freya. Keep the promise. That's all any of this was ever for."

* * *

I spent the afternoon being useful in ways designed to be invisible. I drove a military courier to the telegraph office and memorized the schedule board on the wall: *Patrol Launch 4 — fueling — ETA upriver 1600, weather permitting.* Weather permitting. The launches would wait for the channel to clear properly, and the channel wouldn't clear properly until the ice finished going out, and the ice was going out in great groaning sheets all day long, and by four o'clock the middle of the river might be open water.

Which gave me the arithmetic I'd been dreading: the launch would pass the shipyard at roughly the same hour the tide made the reed channel passable. We would go out with the navy coming in, close enough to hear their engine, hidden by the reeds and the dark and the simple fact that no one looks for a rowboat-shaped ketch when they own the whole river.

Or we would be caught in the open between two lights.

I checked the boat twice — stores lashed, ballast true, the mast stepped and the rigging run, Anton's work immaculate even by candle stub. I walked the reed channel at low water and paced off the bar with a stick, and the stick told me nine inches at flood, which was enough, which was barely enough, which was exactly the kind of margin that drowned people who deserved better.

At dusk I bought cigarettes I didn't smoke and stood outside the customs house until Hobbes came off shift. He crossed the street to avoid speaking to me — openly, deliberately, a performance for the sentry on the steps — and then, twenty yards on, without turning his head, he said, "Vault's inventoried and sealed at nine. After that, the manifest travels with the crate and nobody opens it till Halden." And then louder, to no one: "Road survey's postponed indefinitely."

Sealed at nine. Which meant that whatever happened to Box 41 after nine o'clock happened to a box whose contents were officially accounted for. If the box were empty, no one would know until Halden. Possibly no one would ever know.

Hobbes disappeared around the corner, and I stood there in the blue dark feeling the shape of the thing he'd handed me: not a key, not an hour, but a fact. The manifest was sealed. The train left at noon. Between nine and noon, Box 41 was a lie waiting to be told.

I didn't need the ledger anymore. I needed the pages in it — the ones proving what Edith's money had actually paid for — and those pages were paper, and paper fits in two hands, and two hands are the measure of everything I have ever believed.

* * *

The river cracked all evening. From the shipyard loft I listened to it while Anton fitted the rudder iron by the light of two candles and Greta sat against my side with her tally book, drawing her careful rows of marks — days, gulls, soldiers — and adding a new column without explaining it. I looked. The column was headed, in her small precise hand, *nights*, and it had four marks in it, and she was drawing the fifth.

"Is that nights till what?" I asked.

"Till the boat," she said simply, and went on counting.

Anton drove the last bolt at half past ten, tested the rudder with both hands until it swung sweet and true, and blew out the candles. For a moment the three of us sat in the dark together, listening to the ice talk — long cracks now, closer together, the river shedding winter in pieces — and Anton said, in German, softly, something I only half understood and chose to understand completely: a prayer, or a christening, or a goodbye to the yard where he'd built boats for nineteen years.

"Midnight," I said. "Tide makes the bar at twelve-forty. We carry her down the slip on rollers, quiet as we can, load the last stores, and take the reed channel at flood. By two o'clock we're below the point, and after the point—" I stopped, because after the point there were no promises, only water.

"After the point," Anton agreed, "there is only water. Good. Water is honest."

Greta put her tally book away and took hold of my sleeve, and I realized she was trembling — not with fear, she'd stopped showing fear weeks ago, but with the effort of staying absolutely quiet, which is its own kind of terror. I put my hand over hers.

"One condition," Anton said, in the dark. "If the launch comes. If they find us on the water. You swim for the far bank and you don't turn back. Swear it."

"I've sworn things to you before."

"You swore passage and protection in peacetime, when it cost nothing. This one costs. Swear it anyway."

I swore it. We shook hands in the dark, and his grip was a shipwright's grip, and it closed on mine the way a hull closes on water — tight, shaped, made to hold.

Below us, the ice split end to end with a sound like the world's largest door opening, and through the gap came the smell of open water, cold and endless and possible.

One night. The clock had fallen all day, and now there was no clock at all — only the tide, the dark, and twenty-two feet of unfinished prayer waiting on rollers above the slip.

I went down the ladder first.
