## 28. Loading at Moonset

Anton Weiss had built forty-one hulls in his life, and he could tell you the weight of every one of them by the sound the water made against the planking. This ketch — the last one, the small one, the one no ledger in the customs house knew existed because she had never been entered anywhere at all — drew eighteen inches light and sat in the black water of the shipyard slip like a held breath.

He listened to her now, crouched at the head of the ladder in the loft's trapdoor, while above him the February sky did its slow arithmetic toward moonset. The river talked under the ice shelf along the pilings. A dog three streets over gave one bark and thought better of it. Otherwise nothing, which was not the same as silence. Nothing was what the town sounded like when it was pretending to be asleep with its eyes open.

Behind him, Greta waited on the loft floor with a basket on her hip and her boots in her hand, as instructed. She had put her boots back on twice already without being told to, and taken them off again each time he looked at her, and he understood that this was not disobedience but practice. She was rehearsing obedience so that it would be ready when obedience mattered. She was twelve years old and she had learned to drill herself the way soldiers drilled, and there was a grief in that so large he could only walk around it, never through.

"Again," he whispered.

She counted on her fingers. "Food low and aft. Tools amidships, wrapped so they don't speak. Water forward of the mast. The papers—" she stopped.

"The papers go where?"

"With me," she said. "Under my coat. Because if they take you, they'll search you first, and nobody searches a girl for paper."

He had told her that once, three nights ago, in the dark, thinking she was asleep. He had said it to himself, really, working out the logic aloud the way he worked out scarf joints aloud, and she had heard it and filed it away the way she filed everything now: gulls, sentries, days since school closed, reasons her father might be taken from her and what she would do about it.

"That's right," he said. "And after the papers?"

"After the papers I go down the ladder first, because I'm lighter and faster, and I hold the boat steady while you come down with the last load. And if anyone comes—"

"Anyone comes?"

"I don't scream. I get flat in the bilge under the tarp and I stay there until your hand finds my shoulder. Two squeezes means lie still. Three means run."

"Run where?"

"Anywhere. Away." She said it without flinching, and that was the worst part, that his daughter had a word like *anywhere* in her mouth and it meant what it said.

* * *

They came down the ladder in the last hour of dark, and Anton made himself move the way the work demanded, which was slower than fear wanted.

Loading a boat is a science even in daylight with time to spare. Loading a boat at moonset with an army sleeping four hundred yards upriver is a different discipline entirely, and Anton Weiss had spent twenty years learning the first so that he could invent the second in a single night. Every object had to be decided before it was lifted. Every lift had to be decided before it was begun. A dropped wrench in the shipyard would carry across the ice like a church bell; a creaking plank would bring the patrol from the coal yard inside of four minutes, he'd timed them, or rather he'd lain awake counting while they passed, night after night, building the number the way he built anything, plank by plank.

The food went first. Salt pork in a canvas sack, hard bread, a crock of rendered fat, dried apples Greta had strung herself in the autumn when stringing apples was just something a family did and not provisioning for flight. He lowered each item down the ladder on a rope rather than carrying it, because the rope made less noise than his feet on the stairs, and Greta received each load into the boat and stowed it low and aft as drilled. The ballast question had kept him awake two full nights: too little weight forward and she'd yaw in any chop coming downriver; too much and she'd sit heavy enough to show a deeper line than a fishing boat ought to draw to a bored picket with a lantern. In the end he split the difference and trusted the hull, because he'd built the hull and the hull was the only honest thing left in his life.

The tools came next, and here his hands betrayed him, once, briefly.

The auger was his father's. Beech stock, worn to the shape of a grip that was now three grips old — his grandfather's, his father's, his own. It went into the tool roll with the saws, the adze, the planes wrapped in sacking so their iron wouldn't ring against each other, and he cinched the roll and set it amidships and stood looking at the boat for longer than the schedule allowed, because the tools were not cargo. The tools were the argument. They were proof that Anton Weiss was a shipwright and not whatever word the registration forms used. A man who carried his auger across open water in February was planning to build things on the other side of it, and the army could confiscate a house and a shop and a name, but they had never yet found a way to confiscate what a man intended.

"Papa." Greta, from below, barely air. "The light."

He froze. Downriver, past the point, a white beam swung once across the water and was gone. The picket boat making its round, early or late or exactly on time — he didn't know which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed nail. They stood in the shadow of the loft, the three of them and the half-loaded ketch, and let ninety seconds drain away doing nothing, because doing nothing was sometimes the whole craft.

The light did not come back.

* * *

The ledger came down the ladder last.

Freya had brought it to him that afternoon, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, and had handed it over with both hands the way you hand over a child or a verdict. *It's yours,* Freya had said. *Every name in it is yours or yours to give away, and you should decide which.* And Anton had wanted to burn it then and there — had wanted it with a hunger that frightened him — because every page was a rope around all their necks now that Rourke's men had copied half of it. But Freya had said the other thing too, the thing that had settled the matter: *the pages that prove Edith's innocence are in there, and if I hang for it, fine, but her trial needs them more than we need safety.*

So the ledger lived now in the oilcloth, and the oilcloth lived in a space Anton had cut into the ketch herself, between the cabin sole and the hull planking, a cavity six inches deep behind a board that fit so true you'd need a chisel and an hour to find the seam. He'd built boats for smugglers for fifteen years. He'd never once asked what they smuggled. Now he understood that he'd been apprenticing his whole life for this night, and that the Vanes' coin had been paid to him long ago in skills.

But the ledger did not go in the cavity tonight.

Tonight it went under Greta's coat, against her chest, because that was the plan and the plan was the plan, and a man who started amending plans at moonset deserved whatever the river did to him.

Greta took it without being told. She tucked it flat and buttoned her coat over it and stood straighter under the added weight, and Anton looked at his daughter standing there with a nation's worth of danger pressed to her ribs and thought: *she is the strongest thing I have ever built.*

* * *

The water jugs were the mistake.

There were six of them, earthenware, filled at the yard pump through the day and stored in the dark corner where the frost couldn't reach. Five went down the rope and into the boat without incident. On the sixth, the rope slipped.

Not far. Eight inches. The jug swung against the piling with a knock no louder than a knuckle on a door.

In daylight it would have been nothing. At moonset on the last night, it was a sentence read aloud, and both of them stood motionless while Anton's heart performed its own kind of loading — freight after freight of dread stacked low and aft — and the river said nothing, and the town said nothing, and somewhere a clock that he could not hear struck a quarter hour that felt like a year.

Then Greta moved. She took the jug off the rope, held it to her chest, and climbed down into the boat with it, silent as snowfall, and stowed it forward of the mast with the others, packing straw between them so they couldn't talk to each other in the swell.

He watched her do it and understood that the plan had quietly changed itself, the way good timber changes a design: she would ride in the boat now, not wait at the bottom of the ladder. Fine. Better. She was steadier in a boat than most grown men he'd shipped with, and the fewer times anyone crossed that ladder, the better for everyone.

He finished the loading alone.

Lines coiled and hung clear. Anchor bent on, flaked so it would run. The small sail bagged and lashed under the foredeck, because they would row out and step the mast beyond the point, and a bare pole at the slip might pass for maintenance but a bent-on mainsail would pass for escape. Oars shipped. Bilge dry — he checked it twice with his palm, flat against the boards, reading the water the way he'd read it since he was younger than Greta. A bailer. A lantern with the glass blacked except one thumb-width, for the moment they'd need it and no sooner.

Last of all he went back up the ladder, alone, into the loft, and stood in the room where he had lived since autumn.

It looked like nothing. That had been the discipline of it, months of it: candle stubs carried out and sunk in the river, shavings burned a handful at a time in the forge where they'd vanish among a shipwright's ordinary leavings, the cot stripped, the stove cold for a week so the chimney would show no heat to any glass trained on it. He had built the ketch in this loft the way a spider builds in a place no one looks, and now the loft held only the smell of oakum and pine, and the ghost-geometry of a hull that had grown in its middle the way children grow, invisibly and then all at once.

He allowed himself thirty seconds. He counted them off on the beechwood auger handle in his mind, because his father had taught him that a man who can't measure time can't measure anything.

Then he went down.

* * *

The last hour was the worst, because there was nothing left to do.

They sat in the boat, the three of them — though it was two of them and one girl-shaped bundle of competence — under the loft's shadow at the head of the slip, waiting for the signal Freya was to bring. Anton held the bow line. Greta sat amidships with the ledger under her coat and her hands flat on her knees, and every few minutes her lips moved, and he realized she was counting — sentries, gulls, minutes, he didn't know — keeping her tally even now, especially now, because the tally was how she held the world together, and he loved her so much it interfered with his hearing.

The ice spoke. Out in the channel the sheet boomed once, a long cracking report that rolled up the valley, and both of them went rigid, and then it did it again, and Anton felt the sound travel through the hull into his spine and translate itself: *soon.* The warm front had come two days early and the river was giving notice, the way rivers do, the way everything honest gives notice and everything else — forms, warrants, categories — simply arrives.

"Papa," Greta breathed. "If the ice breaks while we're still tied up—"

"It won't. Freya will come."

"What if he doesn't?"

"He'll come."

"But what if—"

"Greta." He turned in the thwart to face her. In the dark he could find her face by memory better than by sight. "Three years ago I shook a man's hand on a promise, and the promise was that when things went sour, there would be a way out, and the way out would keep. Men break promises every day. But that one — I watched him write it down. Your friend Freya keeps a book of what people owe each other, and he has never once lost track of a debt. Not one. So he will come, and he will come on time, because to him we are an entry in that book, and he would wade the river in March before he'd leave an entry unsettled."

Greta considered this with the gravity she gave all arithmetic.

"So we're a debt," she said.

"We're the best kind," Anton said. "The kind somebody intends to pay."

She nodded, satisfied, and went back to her counting, and Anton faced downriver and watched the black water and thought about the handshake — the dockside, the sun, the easy laughter of a peacetime morning three years gone, two men agreeing over a fence rail that of course nothing bad would happen, and if it did, well then, of course there'd be a boat. He had made the promise the way you admire a hull from the outside, seeing the shape and none of the water it would have to hold. And now the water was here, all of it, the whole gray river of it, and the promise held, and holding hurt, and that was what promises were for.

Downriver, at the edge of hearing, a whistle. Two notes. Soft, wrong for a patrol, patient.

Then footsteps on the yard gravel, quick and light, and a figure detached itself from the darkness by the gate — a tall figure walking with the particular care of a man who has memorized which stones shift — and Anton stood up in the boat with his heart going like a caulking mallet and saw the outline of Freya Crane against the last of the stars, moving fast, too fast, and behind him, far up the road toward the coal yard, a lantern came alight.

Freya reached the head of the slip, breathing hard.

"They moved the checkpoint," he said. "The sentry's already been down here once tonight — Hobbes bought us maybe ten minutes, no more. We go now, or we don't go."
