## 29. Sentry at the Slip

Private Albert Hobbes had learned one truth about sentry duty in a requisitioned harbor town: nothing happened, and the nothing happened slowly. The moon was going down over the customs house roof, dragging its light off the water in long gray strips, and Private Wilcox — nineteen years old, three weeks at his post, and terrified of both the dark and his own rifle — stood at the head of the shipyard slip with his bayonet fixed and his breath coming out in little white flags of surrender.

Hobbes had watched him all week. That was the thing about clerks that nobody understood, not Rourke with his copied pages and his careful files, not the officers who signed forms without reading them. A clerk watched. A clerk noticed that Wilcox took his tea at eleven and his second tea at one, that he checked his watch by striking a match because he'd lost the little crown wheel, that he flinched at gulls the way other men flinched at gunfire. A clerk noticed that Wilcox's boots were half a size too small, because Wilcox had told the quartermaster so twice and been refused twice, and a man in boots that pinch is a man thinking about his feet instead of his post.

Hobbes knew all of this the way he knew the requisition inventory of an entire town: by heart, by rote, by the terrible patience of a man whose whole war consisted of watching paper.

And tonight the paper said Box 41's key had been returned to its hook at 21:40, logged in Corporal Hobbes's own hand, which was true, and that no key had been drawn since, which was also true, because the key Hobbes had drawn was still warm in his coat pocket, and the hook held a duplicate he'd filed under a requisition number belonging to a warehouse that had burned down in October.

He had been telling himself for six days that he wasn't going to do it.

He had been lying to himself for six days, which was fitting, since lying on official documents had turned out to be the one skill the army had actually taught him.

* * *

It had started, properly started, three days ago, at the deportation.

They didn't call it deportation. The form called it *removal of enemy subjects, inland*, and the form was very thorough about the railway car numbers and the ration allocation per head and the escort strength, two corporals and eight privates, which struck Hobbes as a great deal of army for four old men, a lame woman, and a boy of sixteen who had been born in this town to a father born in Bremen.

Hobbes had been assigned to check the manifest against the register. Names out, names ticked. It should have taken twenty minutes. It took forty, because his hands wouldn't work right, because the fourth name on the list was Weiss — no, not Weiss, Weiss was gone, hidden somewhere in the town like a coin dropped in deep water — but Brandt, Otto Brandt, the chandler, the man who sold Hobbes pipe tobacco every Saturday and asked after his mother in the infirmary with what Hobbes now understood to be genuine feeling.

Brandt had looked at him across the clipboard. Not pleading. Worse than pleading. He'd looked at him the way a customer looks at a clerk when the clerk says *the form doesn't allow for it* — that flat, patient look that says: I know you are only the pen, but you are still the pen.

"Anything in my shop," Brandt had said quietly, in English, perfect English, thirty years of English, "you want before they seal it, you take."

"I can't do that, sir."

"No," Brandt agreed. "I know." And he'd picked up his single permitted bag, and he'd walked down the quay between the soldiers with his back straight as a mast step, and the whole way he hadn't looked at anyone, because looking was begging and he would not beg, and Hobbes had stood there ticking his name off a list and understanding, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what he was the pen for.

That night he'd sat in the records room until two in the morning with a cigarette he couldn't light because his hands were shaking, reading Box 41's inventory card over and over. One item, handwritten ledger, leather-bound, approximately 200 pages, seized under Requisition Order 88-C. Owner unknown. Subject: Crane, F., driver, suspected courier.

He had traded cigarettes with Freya Crane for months. Glimpses of inventories, a look at a fuel manifest once, small things, things Hobbes had told himself were harmless — a bored man selling boredom to a charming man, that was all. He had liked Crane. He had liked the way Crane listened as if listening were a skill rather than a wait. He had thought, in the vague way of men who have never been asked to cost anything, that their arrangement was a friendship with tobacco in it.

Then Rourke's intelligence section had come through in November with gloves and trolleys, and boxes had gone from the vault to the map room, and Hobbes had seen the copying lists — *names extracted, cross-referenced, distribution: Capt. Rourke* — and understood that the ledger was not sitting in Box 41 anymore so much as bleeding through it, page by page into Rourke's files, into warrants not yet written, into a future already drafted for a dozen people in the town who trusted a driver with soft hands and a good memory.

And then last week, the Vanes. Arrested at their moorings, smuggling charges, clean enough. But their boat ledgers mentioned a reader, Rourke's report said. Mentioned *the reader*. Four days, the timetable in Hobbes's head whispered, maybe less. After that, Crane wouldn't need a key to Box 41; he'd need a chaplain.

So Hobbes had done the arithmetic he was built for. Columns. Debits. On one side: his career, his mother's care home paid up through spring, his clean record, his small safe life inside the machine. On the other side: Otto Brandt's straight back, and a twelve-year-old girl named Greta whom he had never met but whose existence he knew from a school census he'd filed himself, and a shipwright's unfinished ketch that appeared on no manifest at all because it existed only in candle stubs and wood shavings and a father's refusal.

The columns did not balance. They never balanced. You just chose which side to be short on.

He drew the key at 21:38, logged it, and pocketed it.

* * *

Now he moved along the fence line in the dark, past the tar shed, toward the slip where the sentry's match-flame winked as Wilcox checked his watch for the ninth time that hour.

Hobbes had planned it like a filing system. That was the joke of it — treason conducted by a man whose only method was paperwork. The night patrol route altered at midnight; the sergeant of the guard did his rounds at ten past and again at fifty past; the gap between them was twenty-two minutes, verified across five nights, entered in his head under a heading he refused to write down anywhere. The launch schedule showed the picket boat upriver until two. The floodlamp on the customs house had a blown fuse reported Tuesday and not yet repaired, because repairs required Form 12-B in triplicate and nobody, in the entire occupying army, could be bothered.

The empire ran on forms, and the forms ran on men too tired or too stupid to chase them, and tonight Hobbes was going to spend every ounce of that great slow negligence at once.

He heard the boat before he saw it. That was wrong — everything was wrong, the plan said they'd load at the far cradle, out of sightline — but there it was: the creak of a hull taking weight, the knock of a fender, and then Wilcox's voice cracking upward through the dark like a boy's voice does when it's frightened and pretending not to be.

"Halt! Who goes there?"

Hobbes broke into a run, which no clerk ever does, and which therefore alarmed Wilcox more than the boat did.

* * *

Freya Crane stood in the shallows at the foot of the slip with water to his knees and his heart going like a hammer in a crate, and he watched a boy soldier level a rifle at him with hands that shook so badly the bayonet described small anxious circles in the air.

"On the ground! Hands — put your hands—" Wilcox's voice climbed an octave and cracked. "Hands where I can see them!"

Freya raised them slowly. Behind him, in the black throat of the slipway, the ketch lay low in the water, Anton motionless at the tiller, Greta a smaller darkness curled among the cargo. Nobody breathed. Somewhere up the yard a halyard slapped a mast, counting seconds.

"I'm armed," Wilcox announced, to the water generally. "I'll fire. I'm allowed to fire."

"You're allowed," Freya said gently, "and you don't want to. Look at me. I'm not moving. Nothing here is moving."

"That's a boat. There shouldn't be a boat. There's no permit for—"

"There's no need for a permit," said a new voice, dry and unhurried, and Corporal Hobbes came down the slip out of the dark with his cap squared and his collar buttoned and the bearing of a man arriving to solve a problem rather than cause one. "Because I inspected it. This afternoon. Stand down, Private."

Wilcox wavered. "Corporal, sir, I didn't — nobody told me—"

"Nobody tells you anything, Wilcox, that's the entire structure of the army." Hobbes reached them and planted himself between the rifle and the water, and Freya saw — only Freya saw, in the last gray sliver of moonlight — the sweat shining on the clerk's temples despite the cold, and the way his eyes flicked once, fast, to the ketch, counting heads, and away. "Inspection of hulls and cargoes at the civilian slips falls under Section Nine of the requisition order. Records division handles documentation. That's me. I inspected this vessel at fourteen hundred hours today. Hull sound, cargo listed, papers in order."

"There's no papers," Wilcox said, with the doomed courage of the literal-minded. "Sir. There's no papers on it now."

"The papers are with me, Private, because papers left in open boats get wet, and wet paper makes more work for me, and I have made a personal decision that my war will contain no more work than it strictly must." He said it wearily, perfectly, the exact voice of every NCO Wilcox had ever feared disappointing, and Freya, standing hip-deep in the river with his hands still up, marveled at the craftsmanship of it. Hobbes was lying the way he filed: completely, systematically, with cross-references.

"But I have to log any vessel—"

"You have to log vessels that move during patrol hours. This vessel moves under inspection authority, Section Nine, paragraph three, which permits repositioning of impounded craft for survey purposes. I am repositioning it. To the survey anchorage." Hobbes paused. "Upstream. By the old ferry steps. Where I will personally verify the survey at first light and file the movement report myself, so that if there is any error anywhere in this transaction, Private, it will be mine, and you will be able to say, truthfully and in writing, that you halted an unidentified party, challenged it correctly, and deferred to the ranking present authority as your training instructs. Is there a single word in that account that isn't to your credit?"

Wilcox considered. Freya could feel the boy wanting it, the way you could feel ice wanting to crack — the desperate hunger to have done the right thing, to have a version of tonight he could survive. "No, Corporal."

"Then lower that rifle before you shoot a records clerk and give me a month of forms."

The rifle came down. The bayonet stopped circling.

* * *

What happened next took ninety seconds and cost Hobbes everything, though none of them knew it yet except him, and he had known it since Tuesday.

He walked Freya up the slip, out of the water, past the boy, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and he spoke low and fast without turning his head, a clerk dictating a final entry.

"Picket boat's upriver until two. Sergeant's round at ten past twelve — you've got maybe fifteen minutes to clear the point at the refinery, after that you're visible from the customs house lamp and that fuse has *not* blown, I checked, I lied about the fuse, I'm lying about all of it, every word." His breath fogged. "The key to Box 41 is in your coat pocket, left side, and it was never drawn and never returned and if anyone asks I will deny it with such thoroughness that even I will believe me."

"Hobbes—"

"Don't. Don't thank me, don't explain, I don't want to know where and I don't want to know who, ignorance is the only thing they can't court-martial." A muscle worked in his jaw. "My mother's in the cottage hospital, third ward. If this goes wrong she gets letters. Someone writes her letters and doesn't tell her why they stop."

"They won't go wrong."

"Everything goes wrong. That's what records are for." And then, louder, in the parade-ground voice again: "Move along there! Survey party, keep it moving, Private Wilcox has this post well in hand!"

It was, Freya thought later, the strangest kindness he'd ever witnessed: a man spending himself in the currency of bureaucracy, buying an hour with counterfeit paragraphs, walking them past the checkpoint under the flag of his own ruin. He wanted to say something true — about Brandt, about the letters, about what it meant that the occupier's own clerk had chosen — but Hobbes's face was closed as a ledger balanced to the penny, and there was no time, and some debts you honor by never naming them.

"Third ward," Freya said instead. "Letters. Understood."

Hobbes nodded once, sharply, like a stamp coming down on a document, and turned back to the dark.

* * *

He stayed at the slip until the oar-sounds faded upriver. Then he stood beside Wilcox in the cold and did the thing he did best, which was make the record agree with the world.

"Note the time, Private. Twenty-three fifty-one. Unidentified person challenged at the civilian slip, failed to answer the countersign, withdrew upstream on foot upon my approach. No shots fired. No incident requiring report beyond the log entry." He watched Wilcox write it, watched the boy's hand steady as the words gave him a story he could live inside. "You did well tonight."

"Did I, Corporal? Only I thought—"

"You did exactly as trained. Go on, get out of the wind." And when the boy had gone gratefully toward the brazier at the gate, Hobbes took out the log book and, in a hand as neat as Sunday, wrote the lie that would end him, dated it, signed it, and blotted it with a care that would have moved a stranger.

Somewhere upriver, a boat he had officially never seen carried a shipwright, a girl, and a burned man's secrets toward the point at the refinery, and the ice talked to itself in the dark, groaning like a giant turning over.

Fifteen minutes, Hobbes thought, lighting a cigarette at last, his hands almost steady. God help them. God help us all, and file it under miscellaneous.

Behind him, at the top of the customs house steps, a lantern swung — early, wrong, a full hour ahead of any round — and Captain Rourke's voice floated down the frozen air, mild as milk:

"Corporal Hobbes. A word, if you're free. About boats."
