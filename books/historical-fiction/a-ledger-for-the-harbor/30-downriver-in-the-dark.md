## 30. Downriver in the Dark

Greta had learned to count the way other children learned to pray.

She counted the soldiers at the gate: four, then two, then none after moonset. She counted the crates going aboard the ketch: eleven, and the twelfth was the one with the oilcloth bundle inside it, which was not a crate but which she counted anyway because counting kept her hands from shaking. She counted her own heartbeats between the boom of the church clock striking three — she got to fourteen before the last stroke faded over the water, and she decided that was a good number, though she could not have said why.

Fourteen was also how many days since Papa had slept more than an hour at a time. She counted that too.

"Take the tiller," Papa said, and his voice was the quiet voice he used when the loud voice would cost too much. "You remember what I showed you?"

"The river is a road," Greta said. "The road has edges. Stay off the edges."

"And if I say duck—"

"I duck."

He kissed the top of her head, once, fast, like sealing a letter, and went forward to cast off the lines. Mr. Crane crouched beside her at the helm and did not say anything at all, which she had come to understand was how men said *I am afraid too* when they were not allowed to say it.

The ketch slid out of the slip with no sound but the water talking against the planks. Above them, on the bluff, the town was dark except for the customs house, where a lamp burned all night now, every night, like an eye that had learned not to blink. Greta counted the windows of the customs house because she knew them by heart: nine lit, two dark, same as yesterday. She watched them shrink behind them and felt something in her chest that was too big for a word.

*We are leaving,* she thought. *We are actually, actually leaving.*

Then the first patrol light came around the bend ahead of them, and arithmetic stopped being a comfort.

* * *

There were three lights between the shipyard and the open river. Papa had told her this at the loft, drawing the channel in wood shavings on the floor with a bent nail: *Navy picket at the coal wharf, navy launch mid-channel below the bend, and a lamp on the point that they move, so nobody knows where it will be.* He had said it the way he explained scarf joints and plank curves, calm and exact, because that was how he made fear into a thing with edges you could work against.

But the light at the coal wharf was wrong tonight. It should have been steady. It was sweeping.

Greta put the tiller over two hands' width and brought the ketch toward the near bank, into the shadow the warehouse threw across the water. The beam swung past their bow, close enough that she saw dust motes turning in it, close enough that she held her breath until her chest hurt, and then it went on and found nothing but gulls sleeping on the pilings.

"Papa," she whispered, too low for anyone but the wind.

Ahead, the launch sat mid-channel with its engine idling — she could hear it, a slow chug like a heart that wasn't in any hurry. Two men stood in it. One was smoking; the ember of his cigarette moved when he breathed, a small red star floating on black water.

Mr. Crane leaned close. His breath smelled of the cold. "How's your line?" he asked Papa, meaning the jib sheet, meaning: *can we sail if we must?*

Papa didn't answer right away, and Greta knew why before he said it. She had watched him rig the ketch by candle stub for weeks. She knew every rope on the boat better than she knew the streets of the town they were stealing away from.

"It's fouled," Papa said quietly. "Jib sheet's jammed at the block. I have to clear it or we can't come about, and if we can't come about—"

"—then the launch owns us." Mr. Crane looked at the cigarette-star in the dark. "How long?"

"Two minutes if nobody shoots at me. Longer if somebody does."

Nobody said the third option, which was: *if somebody shoots at him while he's working, there is no number of minutes.*

Papa squeezed Greta's shoulder. Then he crawled forward along the deck, low, the way she'd seen cats go under fences, and began to work at the jammed block with his knife.

Greta steered.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about her, Greta thought later, gripping the varnished wood of the tiller with both hands: they all thought the silence was emptiness. The doctor had said so, gently, to Papa, when she'd gone quiet after the bakery burned and Herr Brandt was dragged from his shop with his apron still on. *Give her time,* the doctor said, *the words will come back.* As if words were birds that had flown off and might return in spring.

But Greta hadn't been empty. She'd been full. Full of numbers.

She had counted everything since school closed. Soldiers: forty-one at the peak, down to thirty-one now that some had gone to the front, which meant the ones left were either too young or too old or too mean to send. Gulls on the customs house roof: never fewer than six, always exactly six at dawn, which she had decided was a law of the universe. Days since Papa's name became a bad word: ninety-three. And now, new counts, counts that mattered more than any others: feet of channel, degrees of wind, seconds of sweep between the patrol light's passes.

She had spent her whole life learning that the world ran on things you could count, and everyone else had been busy learning that it ran on lists instead. Lists of names. Lists of enemies. But a list was just counting done badly, done by people who wanted the numbers to say what they already believed.

The river did not care what anyone believed. The river only cared about depth and current and the angle of your hull, and those were numbers, and numbers were hers.

The wind came off the western bank in soft, unsteady puffs. She read the water the way Papa had taught her without knowing he was teaching — the dark ripples meant gusts, the glassy patches meant lulls, and the little V-shaped wakes of fish rising meant nothing at all except that the world was still doing its ordinary business underneath all the human noise. She eased the tiller to catch each puff, letting the ketch drift downstream at walking speed, quiet as a leaf.

Behind her, Papa's knife clicked against the jammed block. Click. Click. Click. She matched her breathing to it.

The launch's engine chugged. The cigarette star drifted along its rail. A voice carried over the water — young, bored, complaining about coffee. Greta counted the words she could make out: seven. Seven bored words meant a bored sentry, and a bored sentry meant eyes aimed at his own misery instead of at the shadows.

Then the light on the point — the moving one, the one nobody could predict — flared up directly ahead of them.

* * *

It was not pointed at them. It was pointed at the far bank, sweeping the reeds, maybe looking for smugglers, maybe looking for nothing. But its edge touched the water in a long white finger, and the finger lay across the channel exactly where the ketch would have to pass.

Greta did the sums faster than she had ever done them in school.

If they waited, the light would swing back toward the point eventually — but the launch sat mid-channel below them, and drifting down onto a lit launch was death. If they turned back, there was the coal wharf light, and morning coming behind it, and ninety-three days undone. If they crossed now, they crossed through the edge of the beam, visible for perhaps ten seconds, maybe less if the helmsman of the light-boat was watching the reeds instead of the channel.

Ten seconds. She could hold her breath for twenty-two. She had timed herself in the loft, in the dark, lying under the unfinished hull, because counting was the only prayer she knew.

"Papa," she breathed. "Now. Cross now, before it comes back this way."

His head came up from the block. In the faint starlight she saw him look at the light, look at the launch, look at her — and she saw the exact moment when he decided to trust her, and it went through her chest like warm water.

"The sheet?" he whispered.

"Clear it while we cross. I'll hold us straight."

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Papa that the soldiers would never understand, Greta thought: they thought he was small because his name was on a list. But he heard a twelve-year-old girl read the water and tell him *now*, and he obeyed, because competence was the only rank he recognized.

She put the tiller over. The ketch came up into the wind, sails easing, and began to slide across the channel on the quietest tack she had ever sailed, straight through the white edge of the searching light.

The light caught them.

For one full second — she counted it, one, Mississippi-one, the way she counted everything — the deck was bright as noon, the sail a white wing, Papa crouched at the jammed block with his knife raised, Mr. Crane flat against the cockpit boards, her own hands ghost-pale on the tiller.

One second. Then the beam swept on toward the reeds, and they were dark again, and nobody shouted.

On the launch, the cigarette star paused. Stopped moving.

Greta's whole body turned to ice water. She knew what a paused cigarette meant. It meant someone had seen something and was deciding whether it was worth the trouble of being sure.

"Sheet's clear," Papa whispered, sliding aft. "Tell me when."

The launch engine changed note. Not alarm — curiosity. It began to turn, slow, its bow swinging toward the near bank, toward the shadow where a leaf-shaped boat had just been.

"Greta," Mr. Crane said, very quietly, "what do you see?"

And here is the moment she would keep forever, the moment she took out and counted again on every hard night afterward: she looked at the launch turning toward them, at the light-boat sweeping the wrong shore, at the black run of open river below, and she did not freeze. Her father's daughter, the shipwright's child, raised among measurements — she read the situation like a hull plan.

"They're turning toward where we were," she said. Her voice came out level, and she was almost proud of that. "So we shouldn't be where we were."

She put the tiller hard over and bore away downriver, dead before the wind, sails filling with a soft double thump, the ketch leaping forward like something let out of a stall. Behind them the launch completed its turn and played its searchlight over empty shadowed water, over the place a boat had been ten seconds and one decision ago.

"Hold course," Papa murmured, hauling the sheet taut, and the ketch steadied and ran, and the wind of their own speed made the only sound in the world.

* * *

They were a mile downriver when the picket started shooting.

Not at them — at the dark, mostly. Nervous fire from the coal wharf, three shots, then five, the muzzle flashes stitching little orange flowers across the water. Somewhere a whistle shrieked. The light on the point began swinging in fast, panicked arcs, and the launch's engine rose to an angry churn, but it was churning upstream, back toward the commotion, because the army had taught its men to run toward the loudest thing rather than the truest one.

A bullet cracked past overhead — she heard it the way you hear a snapped twig, a small dry sound with a large meaning — and Mr. Crane pulled her down behind the cockpit coaming without a word. Papa flattened himself against the mast. The shots went on behind them, getting smaller, getting confused, shooting at a buoy, probably, or at their own reflections.

Greta stayed down and counted. Nine shots total. Four seconds of whistle. One boat, theirs, moving at — she estimated, feeling the spray and the heel of the hull — six knots, maybe seven with this wind. The launch would take fifteen minutes to sort itself out and come downstream, and by then—

By then they would be at the narrows, where the channel threaded between the shoals, where no navy launch dared follow at night, where only a man who had built boats on this river for twenty years and a girl who had memorized his charts could thread the needle in the dark.

"Can you hold her?" Papa called forward, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before, or rather something missing from it: fear. He sounded like he sounded in the loft, laying planks. Like a man doing the work in front of him.

"Yes," Greta said.

And she did. Through the narrows, where the current grabbed the keel and the shoals breathed white foam on both sides, she steered by the sound of the water — deep water made a low rushing note, shallow water hissed — with Papa calling depths from memory and Mr. Crane bailing and the stars wheeling overhead and the gunfire fading behind them into something that might have been thunder, might have been a door slamming in another country.

Halfway through the narrows, her hands stopped shaking. She noticed it with surprise, the way you notice snow stopping. Somewhere in the last hour the shaking had become unnecessary. There was no room in her body for it anymore; she was too full of work. Tiller. Wind. Water-sound. Numbers.

The terror didn't leave, exactly. It transfigured. It turned into exactly the thing it had always secretly been: attention. Pure, total, holy attention. She was the most awake she had ever been in her life, and she understood suddenly why Papa loved building boats — because this was building, too. Building a safe passage out of darkness and arithmetic, plank by plank, second by second.

They came out the bottom of the narrows into wide black water, and the river opened its arms, and the lights of the town were gone entirely, and the only sounds were the wake and the wind and Papa beginning, very softly, to laugh.

* * *

He laughed the way other men cried. Greta let him. She held the tiller and let her father laugh in the dark like a man set free from prison, because in every way that mattered, he was.

Mr. Crane climbed up beside her. He was soaked with spray and sweat and he looked ten years older than he had at moonset and ten years lighter. He didn't touch the tiller. He just sat close, shoulder almost touching hers, and watched her steer.

"You counted the crossing," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd been watching her lips move.

"One second in the light," she said. "Nine shots. Fifteen minutes until the launch sorts itself. We needed eight."

"Where did you learn to do that?"

"From you," she said. "All of you. You count everything. You just pretend you don't. Your ledger is counting. Edith's speeches are counting — she counts who's listening. Papa counts planks." She adjusted the tiller a hand's width for a puff of wind off the point. "The soldiers don't count anything. They just write names. Names are easy. Numbers are true."

Mr. Crane was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had a crack in it that he didn't try to hide.

"When this is over," he said, "I'm going to teach you navigation properly. Charts. Sextant. All of it. You'll be better at it than any harbor pilot on this coast."

"Promise?"

"Promises are what I'm for," he said.

Ahead of them, far off, the sky above the harbor glowed faintly orange — searchlights, or fires, or the customs house burning its midnight oil brighter than ever, hunting for a shipwright and his daughter who were no longer in any list. Greta looked back once, only once, at the orange smudge where her whole life had happened, and then she faced forward, downriver, toward the open water and whatever waited there.

She counted the stars until dawn. She lost track somewhere past four hundred, and for the first time in ninety-four days, she found she didn't mind.

Because behind them, in a shipyard loft that would soon be full of soldiers and candle stubs and terrible understanding, a man in a good coat was about to find an empty workshop — and Greta, who could not know it yet, had left him the only clue she'd ever want him to have: three sets of footprints in the wood shavings, and not a single word.
