## 31. Rourke at the Empty Loft

The ketch was gone before Captain Rourke knew it had ever existed.

He learned it the way he learned most things in this town — secondhand, and too late. A telephone call at six-forty in the morning from the naval picket station upriver: a small craft sighted below the bend at Millstone Point, running dark, refusing the signal lamp, one shot fired wide. By seven the report had softened into uncertainty. By half past seven Rourke was standing in his office with the message flimsy in his hand, reading it for the third time, feeling something cold assemble itself along his spine.

A small craft running dark on a river full of patrol boats did not happen by accident. It happened by design, by knowledge, by someone who understood exactly where the lights were and when they swung.

Rourke put on his coat, took his cap off the hook, and told Sergeant Doyle to bring the truck around to the shipyard.

* * *

The Weiss shipyard sat on the last working slip before the reeds began, a long timber shed with a loft above and a gantry crane rusting at its elbow. The army had sealed it in November when Anton Weiss failed to report for internment transport. Sealed meant a padlock and a paper seal across the door, and a sentry who walked past twice a night and saw nothing, because there was nothing to see, because the world was orderly and men did what the forms said they should do.

Rourke stood at the gate now and looked at the padlock hanging open.

Not broken. Open. The hasp was shut but the lock's shackle sat loose in it, turned to the position of a man locking a door behind himself in a hurry — or a man who had a key, or a file, or simply the patience of someone who had done this sort of thing before. The paper seal had been peeled back and pressed down again, neatly, so that the tear ran along the existing fold rather than through the stamp.

Doyle came up beside him. "Sentry says it was intact last night, sir. Twenty-two hundred hours."

"Then whoever opened it did it after twenty-two hundred." Rourke stepped through the gate. "And before the ice went out of order."

The yard smelled of tar and cold iron and river rot. Frost still clung to the north side of the shed where the sun hadn't reached it. Rourke crossed the cobbles slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and made himself look at everything the way he had been trained to look — not at things, but at absences. At what should be here and wasn't.

The boat cradle nearest the shed was empty. Not just empty — swept. Sawdust raked into a pile against the wall. Wood shavings gathered rather than scattered. Someone had cleaned up after months of work, and cleaning up takes time, and time taken means the departure was planned, not panicked.

"Doyle. When did Weiss's requisitioned materials get inventoried?"

"October, sir. Hull frames, planking stock, fastenings — all logged as remaining on site. Standard practice for a closed yard."

"And now?"

Doyle looked. The lumber racks along the shed wall were stripped bare except for warped boards nobody would want and a stack of rotting pilings. The fastening bins stood open and vacant. The bronze — a shipwright's bronze, screws and fittings worth more than most men earned in a year — was gone.

"He built it," Rourke said quietly. "Inside the sealed yard. He built an entire boat under our noses."

"Couldn't have, sir. Not a seaworthy hull. Takes months—"

"Yes," Rourke said. "It does."

* * *

Inside the shed the light came down gray through gaps in the roof boards. Rourke struck a match and lit the lantern hanging by the door, and the interior assembled itself out of the dark in pieces: workbench, vise, the black mouth of a cold stove, coils of rope hung on pegs like sleeping snakes.

On the bench, arranged with what Rourke could only describe as tidiness, were three candle stubs.

He picked one up. It was burned down to less than a finger's length, the wax pooled and hardened around the wick in the shape of its own exhaustion. Beside them lay a tin cup holding candle drippings, salvaged, melted down — wax is wax, and a man building a boat by night cannot waste light. There was a stub of pencil worn to the size of a thumbnail. There was nothing else. No papers, no plans, no tools beyond a broken chisel and a plane with a cracked sole that even a desperate man would have left behind.

But the floor told the story anyway. Rourke crouched and ran his gloved hand over the planking of the shed floor and found the drifts of shavings that had escaped the broom — pale curls of cedar and oak, fresh-cut, the ends still sharp-edged. Frost had not touched them. They had not been lying here through the winter. They were days old. Perhaps hours.

Above him, the loft.

The ladder was a fixed affair, treads worn smooth by years of boots. Rourke climbed it slowly, lantern held out ahead of him, and came up into a low room that ran the length of the shed beneath the rafters. A straw pallet, folded. A blanket, folded tighter. Two crates pushed together to make a table. On the table: a tin plate, wiped clean; a spoon; the remains of a loaf gone hard as brick; a jar of pickles with two left in it.

Two plates.

Rourke stood in the middle of the loft and let that arithmetic settle. Two plates. The girl had been here too. Twelve years old, according to the registration form he had signed off on himself four months ago, her name entered in his own careful hand — Greta Weiss, born in this harbor, spoke English better than German, school records attached. He remembered thinking at the time that the file was thin. He remembered deciding that thin files were efficient.

There was a window at the gable end, a square of gray light looking out over the river. Below it, on the sill, sat a row of objects that stopped Rourke where he stood.

Gull feathers. Seven of them, laid out in a line. A brass button. A scrap of blue cloth. And a strip of paper folded into a small tight square, which he opened with two fingers and found blank on both sides.

It took him a moment — longer than he would ever admit — to understand what he was looking at. Not a code. Not a signal. A count. A child's census of the world: days marked, soldiers counted, gulls tallied. He had seen such collections before, in the effects of smugglers and deserters, always in the possessions of people who had lost control of time and were trying to take it back.

Except this one had been maintained inside a sealed military zone, directly beneath the nose of an army that had declared this family enemies of the state, and no one — not the sentry, not the provost patrols, not the intelligence section, not Captain Rourke himself — had known.

His fury, when it came, arrived not hot but glacial, the way it always did. Heat made men sloppy. Cold made them thorough.

* * *

He spent two hours in that loft and did not touch anything after the first ten minutes. He walked its perimeter. He measured it with his eyes. He reconstructed the life that had been lived here the way an engineer reconstructs a bridge failure — load by load, stress by stress — until the whole structure of his own failure stood revealed in the middle of the room.

They had been fed. That was the first thing. Someone had brought food up that ladder — bread, pickles, candles, matches — across weeks, past the padlock, past the sentry, past the seal. You could not carry a week's provisions through a checkpoint without being seen. So the supplies must have come in small amounts, carried openly, hidden in plain delivery baskets or tool bags, passed by someone with legitimate access to the yard.

Who had access? The yard was Weiss's own property, requisitioned but not reassigned; the army used the outer slip for barge moorage in November and abandoned it when the ice came. The neighboring premises: the rope walk, disused. Harbormaster's annex, closed. The Vanes' boathouse — and there his jaw tightened — the Vane brothers' boathouse, currently occupied by two men sitting in cells awaiting trial on smuggling charges that had seemed, until this morning, satisfyingly straightforward.

Second: the boat itself had been built here, in the loft or the shed below, from requisitioned stock, by a man whose hands Rourke had personally catalogued as those of a master shipwright. The inventory said the materials never left. The materials had never needed to leave. The army had locked a shipwright in a warehouse full of boat parts and called it containment.

Third: the launch. Last night. Ice breaking early, patrols moved upriver within hours — Rourke had ordered that movement himself, had felt the satisfaction of a net drawing closed — and the net had instead become the starting gun. Whoever ran that boat last night knew the new patrol schedule. Knew it before the patrol boats had finished taking on fuel.

Which meant the information had come from inside. From his own side.

Rourke descended the ladder, set the lantern on the workbench, and stood looking at the candle stubs again. Three of them. Burned at different rates. A man working nights, rationing light, saving the stubs because waste was a sin when you were building your daughter's escape out of stolen hours.

He thought: I have read this man's file forty times. I know his height, his trade, his debts, his politics — moderate, cooperative, no record. I know his daughter's birthday. I have interviewed every German-born man on the harbor register, and Weiss sat across from me in March with his hands flat on the table and answered every question I asked him, and I wrote in my notes: *cooperative, no flight risk.*

No flight risk. Rourke almost laughed. The man had built an ark inside the prison and sailed it through the flood, and the prison had helped him, and the flood had carried him.

So who else?

* * *

Back at headquarters by noon, Rourke spread the case across his desk like a surgeon laying out instruments. The Vane interrogation transcripts. The harbor registry. The requisition inventories. The patrol schedules, old and new. And the item that had bothered him since the first hour of the morning: the list of civilian employees cleared to handle intercepted mail.

Because that was the thread he could not stop pulling. The new patrol schedule had leaked, and leaks of operational material pointed one direction only — toward the sorting room, where every captured letter in the district passed through civilian hands, where a literate man with steady nerves could read the entire war a page at a time.

He had approved those clearances himself. He remembered the reasoning: use local literacy, save trained clerks for the front, keep the civilians supervised and grateful. Grateful men don't spy. That had been the theory.

He pulled the employment roster and ran his finger down the names. Clerks, a retired teacher, a customs translator, and—

Freya Crane. Driver and courier. Employed since September on the recommendation of— Rourke checked — a character reference from the Marlowe woman's legal counsel, countersigned by the provost office. Cleared for the mail room in October. Cleared, also, for night filing duty in December — a request Rourke had approved without reading closely, because the man had volunteered, and volunteering looked like loyalty.

Volunteering looked like access.

Rourke sat back. Outside his window the harbor lay gray and locked, the ice groaning in long slow notes as the warm front worked at it. Somewhere downriver, past the picket lines and the signal stations and all the machinery of his command, a twelve-year-old girl was steering a stolen ketch through the dark with her father's hands over hers, and the schedule she was evading had been written by Rourke and delivered to the enemy by a man Rourke had cleared, promoted, trusted—

Had he trusted Crane? No. That was the humiliating truth of it. He had *used* him, the way you use any competent instrument, and he had never once wondered what the instrument was reading. Because Crane was quiet, and punctual, and asked no questions, and in Rourke's experience the dangerous ones were the ones who argued.

The dangerous ones were the ones who listened.

He opened the folder on Edith Marlowe — the sedition file, thin and getting thinner, the woman sitting in custody while public opinion curdled against the arrest — and looked at the connection line he had drawn weeks ago between Marlowe and Crane and never followed. Driver. Confidant. Carrier of letters. The suffragist's courier now sorting the army's mail, and the shipwright's boat launched on a night only someone with the patrol schedule could have survived.

Three threads. One pair of hands.

Rourke did not feel triumph. Triumph required surprise, and what he felt instead was the colder thing: the recognition of a pattern he should have seen in October, in November, in December, at any of the dozen moments when the pieces had lain on his desk in plain view waiting for someone to arrange them. He had been managing a town. The man across the desk had been running a railroad.

* * *

By three o'clock the warrants were typed.

Rourke dictated them himself, standing at the window while the clerk's keys rattled. Warrant one: Freya Crane, driver, residence on Cordage Street, charged under the Defence regulations with conveying enemy persons from justice, with theft of military property, and with communication of operational information to unauthorized parties. Warrant two: search of Crane's rooms, his effects, his correspondence, and any vehicle or storage to which he held claim.

"Anything else, sir?" the clerk asked.

"The Marlowe file stays open," Rourke said. "Add a cross-reference. If Crane runs, I want the press asking why her driver fled the same week."

"And if he doesn't run?"

Rourke considered that. Considered the empty loft with its folded blanket and its saved candle wax, and the swept floor, and the neat pile of shavings — a man who cleans up after himself is a man who intends to come back to his life, or a man who intends to leave nothing behind but questions. He considered the report from the courthouse runner: Crane had been seen that very morning in the visitors' gallery corridor, carrying a lawyer's document case, calm as Sunday.

"He won't run," Rourke said. "Men like Crane don't run. They stay and testify. It's worse than running — it's braver, and bravery is harder to charge." He straightened the warrants into a square. "Take six men. Cordage Street first. If he's not home, watch the Marlowe woman's counsel, the harbor gates, and the mail room. Quietly. No sirens, no spectacle. I want him taken like a ledger entry — accurately."

The clerk hesitated at the door. "You knew him, didn't you, sir? By sight, at least."

"I shook his hand in October," Rourke said. "He thanked me for the work. Steady voice. Clean cuffs." He turned back to the window, where the ice on the harbor was talking to itself in the afternoon light, breaking in long white seams. "That's the lesson, if you want one. The reader is never the loud one."

The clerk left. Rourke stood alone with the harbor going soft and rotten at its edges, and somewhere under all that breaking white, a boat with a hull named for a seditionist was carrying his failure north, and the man who had arranged it all was walking through the town with clean cuffs and a document case, about to discover that the arithmetic had finally caught up with him.

Rourke hoped — and the hope surprised him, and he filed the surprise away to examine later, the way he filed everything — that Crane would make it difficult.

Difficult men left records. Records could be read.

He reached for his hat, and went down to make the arrest himself.
