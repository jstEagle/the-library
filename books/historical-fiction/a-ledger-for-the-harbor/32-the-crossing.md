## 32. The Crossing

The ketch cleared the last of the patrol lights an hour before dawn, and Anton Weiss did not allow himself to believe it until the lights were gone.

He had spent three years learning not to believe in things until they were finished. A hull was not a boat until it floated. A seam was not sealed until it had taken water and held. A promise was not kept until the person you made it to was standing somewhere safe, breathing, unremarkable. So he stood at the tiller with his hands aching from the cold and watched the harbor's glow sink behind them, and he said nothing to Greta, who slept in the cockpit under two blankets and the tarpaulin, because saying it aloud would make it a claim, and claims invited correction.

Freya Crane slept too, wedged against the mast step with his coat over his knees like a schoolboy on a long train ride. Even now, even out here where there was nothing left to read or carry or steal, he held something in one hand — the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, tucked under his arm the way another man might hold a child. He had not let go of it once since they'd loaded at moonset. Not when the picket boat's searchlight had swept the water astern of them and found only fog. Not when the fouled jib sheet had needed cutting and re-tying while bullets from a nervous sentry cracked flat across the river and slapped into the hull like thrown stones. Not when Greta had steered them through the gap between the channel buoys with her jaw set and her eyes enormous, twelve years old and doing a man's work without being asked.

Anton looked at him sleeping and felt something he could not name cleanly. Gratitude was part of it. Pity was part of it, though pity for Freya Crane sat wrong in the mouth — the man had done more in six weeks than most men do in a life. But there was something else, something older, the way you look at a tool that has been used hard for your sake and will never be quite the same again.

The wind held from the southwest, which was luck, and luck was not a thing Anton trusted either. He trimmed the mainsail by feel, checked their heading against a coastline he knew from thirty years of working these waters — before the war had put lines on maps and names on lists — and let the ketch run.

Open water came at first light. Not truly open — this coast offered no true open water within a week's sail — but wide enough that no shore was visible, wide enough that the swell came in long and regular from the northeast and lifted the bow and set it down again with a patience the river had never taught him. Greta woke when the motion changed. She always woke when the motion changed; she had her mother's ears for it.

"Papa." Just that. Her voice was still careful, still rationed, the way a person spends money after a famine. She had been speaking again for two weeks now, but every word still sounded to Anton like a coin counted out twice.

"We're past them," he said. "Past all of them."

She climbed up beside him and looked back the way they'd come. There was nothing to see. That seemed to matter to her more than anything ahead. "There's no smoke," she said.

"No."

"Yesterday there was smoke everywhere."

"Yesterday we were there."

She thought about that. Greta thought about everything; she had inherited his slowness to speak and none of his talent for hiding what the thinking cost. "Will I count today?" she asked. She had kept her tally through everything — soldiers, gulls, days since school closed — scratched into a plank offcut with a nail. The plank was in the bilge bag now, wrapped around the bread. He had told her she didn't need it anymore and she had looked at him the way she looked at anyone who suggested she stop counting, so he had said nothing further.

"You can count anything you like," he said. "Count waves if you want. Count how many kinds of bird we see."

"I'll count miles," she decided. "But I don't know how."

"Then count hours. One hour is about five miles when the wind holds."

"One hour is about five," she repeated, satisfied, and settled into the arithmetic of freedom as if it were any other sum.

Anton watched the horizon. His hands were shaking. They had been shaking since the slipway, since the young sentry's lantern had swung toward them and Hobbes had stepped out of the dark with his clipboard and his bored voice and lied like a man reading a laundry list — *already inspected this one* — and walked them past the checkpoint himself. Anton had shaken hands with Corporal Hobbes at the waterline. It had lasted half a second. Neither of them had said anything worth remembering. But Anton had built boats for thirty years and knew what a joint under load felt like, and that handshake had been a joint taking the whole weight of the boat, and both men had known it would shear eventually, and both had set it anyway.

He thought: *that man will pay for this.* He thought: *and he knew it when he shook my hand.* He thought: *write it down somewhere. When there is somewhere safe to write things down.*

The ledger rode under Freya's arm, asleep.

* * *

By noon the weather turned soft and gray, and Freya woke, and they ate cold sausage and hard bread and drank tea made on a spirit stove so small it was nearly a toy — one of the tools from the loft, packed among the chisels. Greta had insisted on packing the chisels. *If there is a yard there, Papa needs tools,* she had said, in the flat reasonable voice she used for facts, and so the tools had come, wrapped in sacking, riding in the bilge like ballast that meant something.

Freya ate slowly and watched the water. He had been quiet since the checkpoint — quieter than usual, which for Freya Crane was saying a great deal, since his ordinary silence was already a kind of architecture, load-bearing, built deliberately. This silence was different. Anton recognized it because he had seen it in men leaving a country. It was the silence of a man carrying more than he can set down anywhere.

"You should sleep more," Anton said. "You've not slept properly since the vault."

"I slept."

"Like a cat sleeps. With one ear working."

That almost got a smile. Almost. "I keep thinking about Edith," Freya said, to the water rather than to Anton. "In that cell. Reading whatever they let her read, which will be nothing, which will be the walls." He turned the teacup in his hands. "She told me to go. She was very clear about it. She said my testimony was worth more than my company."

"And?"

"And she's usually right about things." He said it like a man reporting a wound. "It's infuriating."

Greta, from the bow, where she was counting birds with great seriousness — "four, five, six, seven, the seventh one is different" — did not appear to be listening. Children never appeared to be listening. That was their camouflage.

"There will be lawyers," Anton said. "Public trials make noise. Noise protects people, sometimes. Your Edith knows that better than anyone — isn't that the whole trade she's in? Making noise?"

"Yes." Freya drank his tea. "Yes, it is." But he kept looking south, back along their wake, as if the wake were a road and the road led somewhere he'd left unfinished.

Anton had known Freya Crane for four years, and in four years he had learned perhaps six true things about him, each one surrendered like a tooth. He knew that Freya read every letter he carried. He knew that Freya loved a woman who had left — had learned it sideways, from a name spoken once and never again, from the way Freya handled certain envelopes. He knew that Freya measured the world in what fit in two hands. And he knew — had known since the night three years ago behind the shipyard, over beer that tasted of sawdust — that the man kept promises the way other men kept grudges: totally, permanently, at cost.

So he waited. Waiting was most of craft. You did not force a plank; you let the wood tell you when it was ready to take the bend.

It took until the light went long and gold, until the coast rose up ahead of them — low dunes, then pines, then the thin gray thread of smoke that meant a village — it took until then for Freya to say it.

"I'm not staying."

Greta stopped counting.

"I'll see you ashore," Freya said. "I'll see you to your cousin's door. And then I'm going back."

Anton had prepared himself for many versions of this crossing. He had prepared for capture, for weather, for the engine of his own fear failing him at the tiller. He had not prepared for this, and yet some part of him, the part that reads wood grain, had known it was coming since the checkpoint, since Freya had looked back at the harbor lights one time longer than caution required.

"The Vanes' cousin will hide you," Anton said. "A week, a month. Papers can be got. There are routes north—"

"I know there are routes. I've spent six weeks building one." Freya laughed, briefly, without much humor in it. "That's rather the problem. I know exactly how they work. Which means I know exactly how they fail."

"They'll hang you," Anton said. Flatly. Because it was true and because Greta was at the bow and children should hear true things in flat voices or they learn to hear lies in every voice.

"They might. They'll certainly try." Freya looked at him steadily. "Rourke has warrants. Half my network is burned. If I disappear, they say *the reader ran*, and every name in that ledger becomes a confession. If I'm standing in a courtroom when Edith's trial comes — and it will come, Anton, they've invested too much in her to let it be quiet — someone has to be there who can read the evidence. Who knows which pages are real. Who carried the letters." He paused. "Who wrote them down."

"You wrote us all down," Anton said. "Every debt. Every favor. Every name."

"Every real name," Freya said. "Not the ones the army's lists use. That's the point of it. That's always been the point of it." He touched the oilcloth bundle, lightly, the way you touch a thing you are about to give away. "The ledger proves what the 'sedition' actually was. Charity. Letters to wives. Bread for strikers. Passage for a shipwright and his daughter. Let Rourke read his own copies aloud in court. Let the whole town hear what the empire calls treachery."

The ketch rose and fell. Greta had come aft without either of them noticing — she moved quietly now, she had learned that in the bad weeks — and stood at the edge of the cockpit looking at Freya with her mother's grave eyes.

"You're going back to the bad place," she said.

"Greta—" Anton began.

"It's all right, Papa." She had not raised her voice since she'd started speaking again; she never raised it. "I just wanted to say it out loud so it's true." Then, to Freya: "You gave me the pencil. In the loft. When I couldn't talk. You said writing counts too."

Freya's face did something complicated and brief. "It does."

"So you have to write down what happens," Greta said. "To Miss Marlowe. Because if nobody writes it down, it's like it happened to nobody." She considered. "And it didn't happen to nobody. It happened to her."

Neither man spoke for a moment. The sail filled, eased, filled again.

"That," Anton said finally, "is the smartest thing anyone has said on this boat, including me."

"She gets it from her mother," Freya said softly, and Greta smiled — the first full smile Anton had seen on her since the bakery burned — and went back to her birds.

* * *

They made the anchorage off the fishing village as the light died. It was nothing — a half-moon bay, a stone jetty, a dozen hulls hauled up above the tideline, nets drying on frames like the rigging of drowned ships. A boy of maybe fifteen met them at the jetty with a lantern hooded against the wind, and when Anton said the cousin's name — Marta Vane's cousin, the smugglers' web extending further than any chart — the boy simply nodded and said, "Room for three," and Anton corrected him: "Two."

The house was low and warm and smelled of fish smoke and tar, and the cousin was a broad woman with her father's jaw and none of his cunning, and she fed them herring and potatoes without asking a single question, which was its own dialect of hospitality. Greta fell asleep at the table with her head on her arms, mid-sentence, having reached seventeen birds and lost interest in favor of bread.

Later, by the stove, Anton and Freya sat with coffee that was mostly chicory, and there was nothing left to plan, and planning was the only language they shared fluently, so for a while neither spoke.

"Three years ago," Anton said at last, "behind the yard. I said, *if it ever goes sour.* Do you remember what you answered?"

"I remember."

"What did I say?"

"You said, *you shouldn't promise that so easily.*" Freya looked into his cup. "And I said promises are cheap in peacetime, which is exactly when you're supposed to make them, because wartime is when you find out what they weigh."

"I've thought about that a lot," Anton said. "These last weeks. Lying in that loft listening to the gulls and the boots on the cobbles." He turned his cup the way Freya had turned his tea. "I build things. That's the whole of me. Boats, joints, seams. And I used to think that was small work. Honest work, but small. Men like your Edith move nations. Men like me move planks." He looked up. "But sitting in that loft with Greta, with nothing, stripped of everything — the shop, the name, the country — I understood that what they took wasn't mine. My tools aren't mine. My yard isn't mine. What's mine is what I can carry in two hands, and what I can carry is a craft and a daughter and a promise somebody kept." He set the cup down. "You kept it. Quietly. Without ceremony. The way a good seam holds — nobody sees it, and the whole boat depends on it."

Freya was silent for a long moment. "My father was a customs clerk," he said finally. "Did you know that? Twenty-six years of lists. Names in, names out. He believed the list was the truth. Write a man down correctly and he exists; miss him and he doesn't." He shook his head slowly. "I spent my whole life trying to prove him wrong. Turns out he was right about one thing. Writing matters. He was just wrong about whose hand holds the pen."

Outside, the wind shifted and the pine tops hissed. Somewhere down the beach a dog barked twice and stopped, and the whole village seemed to listen, and then the night closed over the sound.

At dawn they stood on the jetty. There was no ceremony; ceremony belonged to peacetime, and anyway neither man was built for it. The cousin's boy brought a borrowed dinghy for Freya — the Vanes' route ran back the way it came, quiet, unhurried, a man traveling alone draws fewer eyes than a fugitive — and Greta stood between the two of them holding the bilge bag with the bread and the plank with the tally scratched into it.

"Take the plank," Anton said suddenly.

Greta looked at him. "It's mine."

"I know it's yours. Give it to him anyway. For the trial." He nodded at Freya. "Show them what a twelve-year-old counted while the empire did its work. Soldiers, gulls, days. Let them add it up in court."

Greta thought about this with complete seriousness, weighing ownership against purpose, and then held the plank out. "Bring it back," she said. "When it's done. It's mine and I want it back."

"I'll bring it back," Freya said. He took it in both hands — of course he did; he measured everything by what fits in two hands — and then he embraced Anton, briefly, hard, the way men embrace when they have agreed not to say the actual words.

"Go well," Anton said. "Build something."

"Keep well," Freya said. "Float."

And then he was in the dinghy, and the boy was rowing him out toward the gray line where sea became sky, and Greta waved, small, controlled, and Freya did not wave back — he was facing them, watching, memorizing, doing what he always did, writing it down somewhere no requisition order would ever reach.

Anton stood on the jetty with his daughter's hand in his and watched until the dinghy was gone.

"Well," he said. "We have a yard to start."

Greta looked up at him, then north, toward whatever was waiting — a cousin, a village, a life with no lists in it. "Papa," she said, "what will we call the first boat?"

Anton looked at the water where the dinghy had been, and the answer was already there, had been there for weeks, waiting like wood ready to take the bend.

"Edith," he said. "We'll call her *Edith Marlowe*. And when she floats, everyone who asks why will get the whole story."
