## 33. What Freya Kept

The fishing village had no name on the chart the Vanes used — just a smudge of ink where a river met a colder sea, and a stone jetty that some mason had built crooked and nobody since had cared to fix. Anton's cousin waited at the end of it with a lantern turned low and a cart that smelled of herring. It was the kind of arrival Freya had imagined for three years without ever believing in it: the promise made in peacetime, kept at last in wartime, standing on a foreign shore with salt spray freezing in his collar.

Anton shook the cousin's hand. Greta looked back once at the boat — at the hull her father had built by candle stub, the ketch that had carried them downriver between patrol lights while she steered with hands too small for the wheel and steady enough for it anyway — and then she did not look back again.

"Freya." Anton said it quietly, the way men say things they mean to say only once. "Come with us. The cousin has room. There is work in the yards here — good work, German work, they'll not ask questions of a man who can read a plan."

"I know," Freya said.

"You could be a shipwright. You learned enough from watching me."

"I learned enough from watching you," Freya agreed, "to know I'd never be one."

Greta came and stood beside her father. She had been speaking again for two weeks now, but sparingly, the way other people spent money, and when she spoke it was usually someone else's name. Now she said his.

"You'll write?"

He crouched so they were eye to eye. Twelve years old and she had counted soldiers and gulls and days like a clerk of the occupation; she had warned him of the search with his own name as her first word after weeks of silence. There were people he would grieve all his life and people he would fail all his life and Greta Weiss was neither, which was its own strange comfort — she was something he had helped carry, whole.

"Not letters," he said. "Letters get read."

She considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. "Then how will we know you're alive?"

"You won't. You'll assume it. That's what assuming is for."

"That's stupid," Greta said, and hugged him hard enough to crack a rib, or so he chose to believe.

* * *

The cousin's cart went up the hill toward the village lights. Anton stood at the jetty's end a moment longer, and Freya saw the question still working in him, the offer not withdrawn but banked, interest accruing. Three winters ago, in the loft above the shipyard, over a bottle of plum brandy Anton kept for occasions that never came, Freya had promised passage and protection if ever things turned sour. He had made the promise lightly then, the way you promise anything in peacetime — meaning it entirely, understanding it not at all. Now it was kept, and he understood it at last: a promise is ballast. You do not feel its weight until the water rises.

"The trial," Anton said. Not a question either.

"They've set it for the spring assizes. Edith Marlowe, sedition, correspondence seized under warrant. Rourke built the file himself. Half of it is forged and half of it is true, and the true half is worse." Freya watched the lantern climb away up the hill. "The letters they took — hers, mine, the ones I never delivered — they'll be read aloud in court as evidence of conspiracy. Read by men who don't know what they're reading. A letter asking after a widow's coal allowance becomes treason if the magistrate wants it to. And no one will be there who knows it isn't."

"There are lawyers."

"There are lawyers who can read. There is no one who can testify." He heard his own voice go flat and certain, the voice he used for numbers. "I sorted the army's mail for four months. I know their filing system better than Hobbes does. I know which intercepts are real and which are Rourke's embroidery. I can stand in that courtroom and read the evidence out loud myself — the actual evidence, page by page — and let everyone hear what charity looks like when the state transcribes it. If I'm there. If I come back."

"And if you're there," Anton said, "they arrest you at the door. Or on the dock. Or before the boat clears the harbor mouth. Rourke has your name now, Freya. He wrote it himself. Warrants don't expire because the man crosses a border."

"No," Freya said. "They don't."

They stood together in the wind. Behind them the ketch knocked gently against the jetty tires, Anton's last boat, the one he'd finished by candle stub in a loft that smelled of shavings and fear, each plank a refusal to be made small. Ahead of them the water ran black toward the open sea and, beyond it, toward everything.

"You measured everything," Anton said at last. "All those years. What fits in two hands. What a man owes. What a man carries." He turned, and in the dark his face was mostly voice. "So tell me. Weigh it. Your freedom against her trial. Which fits in two hands?"

Freya had been weighing it since the crossing — since the moment off the point where the patrol light swept past and Greta steered them through, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he could simply keep going. North. Away. A new name in a new country; he had copied enough false papers in the army's own files to build himself three. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth in the hold. The evidence was in his head. He could carry it all anywhere.

But testimony is not cargo. It only weighs something in the place it was gathered.

"She needs a witness who can read," Freya said. "Not a letter. A witness. And every witness worth the word is already arrested, fled, or frightened down to a whisper. Except me. I'm the only one left holding the other copy — the one in my skull, the one Rourke can't seize, because he doesn't know it exists." He almost smiled. "Four months sorting the enemy's mail. He taught me the whole system himself. It would be ungrateful not to use it."

Anton put out his hand, and they clasped forearms the way they had that first day in the yard, shipwright's grip, honest and heavy. Then, instead of letting go, Anton pulled him in briefly, once, and released.

"When it's done," Anton said, "the cousin's address doesn't change."

"When it's done," Freya said, "you'll get a blank piece of paper in the post, and you'll understand it."

"A blank paper."

"The only kind I trust anymore."

Greta came running back down the jetty before the cart could take her — her father called after her, half-angry, half-something else — and pressed something into Freya's hand and ran again. A wooden bird, whittled small enough to fit in a closed fist. A gull, maybe, or a tern; the wings were suggestion more than anatomy. He knew the knife-strokes. He had watched them for three years across the shipyard.

What can be carried. All his life he had measured the world that way — letters, ledgers, debts, the exact weight of a favor — and here at last was the answer, small enough for a pocket, worthless to any customs officer, and heavier than the ketch itself.

The tide was turning. He got into the boat.

* * *

He told the Vanes' cousin — the one who ran the northbound runs, a woman named Sorrel with hands like rope — that he needed putting ashore south of the point, on the mudflats below the old lime kilns, where nothing official had bothered to look since before the war.

Sorrel looked at him for a long time. Smugglers are not sentimental, but they are observant, and she had watched him load the ketch at moonset and she had watched him watch the shore ever since.

"You're going back," she said. Not a question either. It seemed nobody asked questions anymore; everybody just said the terrible thing plainly and waited.

"I'm going back."

"They'll hang you."

"Firing squad, probably. Quicker." He said it lightly and saw it land wrong, saw her decide he was either very brave or already dead, and understood that from the outside the two look identical.

"Why?"

And here was the thing: he had an answer ready, a good one, the trial answer, the Edith answer, and it was even true. But there was another truth underneath it, older, and it came out of him before he could stop it.

"Because I've spent my whole life writing down what other people owe," he said. "Names in a book. Debts and favors, columned and ruled. And when the army took my ledger I thought that was the worst thing — my handwriting hanging half the town. But it wasn't. The worst thing was realizing I'd let a book keep the names instead of me. That I could burn every page—" and he had, he'd burned pages in chapter after terrible chapter of this winter, amputating himself to survive— "and still owe them. Still carry them. A ledger you can steal. This other thing—" he touched his temple— "this one they have to take me apart to get."

Sorrel said nothing to that. She put the tiller over.

The mudflats came up gray under a sky going the color of dishwater. She grounded the boat easy, stern-first, the way smugglers ground boats, and nodded at the shingle beyond the reeds.

"Two miles to the harbor road. Patrols at the bridge after six." She paused. "There's a boy in the village owes the Vanes. He can walk you as far as the kilns. After that you're a rumor."

"Sorrel." He stopped with one leg over the side. "If a blank paper comes north, addressed to Anton Weiss — see it travels."

"If a blank paper comes north," she said, "it'll travel faster than anything I've ever carried. Now go, before I talk sense into you."

* * *

The two miles took him most of the night, because he made them take it. There was no sense in arriving at the harbor gates at dawn with mud to the knees and salt in his hair; a man walking into arrest should arrive like a man with errands. So he waited in the kiln shadow until the sky decided what it was doing, and he inventoried himself, because inventory was the oldest habit he had.

What he carried:

One wooden bird.
One name, his own, on a warrant he had not yet seen but could recite anyway: *Freya Crane, alias the reader, charges to follow.*
One memory, complete, of Box Forty-One and every document that had passed through his hands in four months of reading the enemy's mail — dates, initials, routing stamps, the particular lie of Rourke's forged cover sheets, the coal allowance, the widow Hearn, the sewing circle minutes that Edith had dictated to him herself in a kitchen smelling of bread.
One promise, kept, and one debt, outstanding, to a woman sitting in a cell who had told him through wire mesh to save Anton and let her case play out publicly, and whom he intended to disappoint in exactly one respect: he would save Anton *and* play her case out, loudly, from the witness box, whether she wanted grace or not.

That was the whole manifest. He turned it over in his mind the way he'd once turned coins, testing weight, checking for clipped edges, and found — for the first time since he'd begun keeping books at fourteen years old — that the account balanced. Everything fit in two hands. Everything fit in no hands at all. The state could requisition warehouses, customs houses, municipal records, every scrap of paper in the harbor; it could not requisition what he was about to say out loud in a courtroom with the public gallery full. It could shoot him afterward. It could not unsay him first.

He thought about that word — *afterward* — and found it didn't frighten him the way the vault had frightened him, the way the drunk sentry and the fingerprint in the dust had frightened him. Fear of dying was a luxury of men with something left to lose. He had spent his losses carefully, all winter, page by page, and what remained was the part they couldn't confiscate.

The sky committed to gray. Somewhere inland a church bell tried six and got lost halfway.

He climbed from the kiln shadow to the shingle, and then to the road, and walked toward the harbor the way water walks downhill — without drama, without decision, because it is the shape of the thing. At the bridge the sentry was young, younger than Hobbes, with a rifle held like a borrowed coat, and he looked at Freya coming up the road alone, empty-handed, unhurried, and something in the man's training stuttered. Fugitives run. Men carrying contraband walk unevenly. This one walked like a clerk late for opening.

"Name?" the sentry said.

"Freya Crane."

The boy blinked. He knew the name — of course he knew it, it was on a warrant in the guardroom behind him, Rourke's neat capitals — and for a long moment the rifle wavered between suspicion and disbelief, because no one had ever answered that question honestly before.

"You're — there's a — you're wanted."

"Yes," Freya said. "I imagine I am. Shall we go and find whoever wants me? I'm expected at the assizes in the spring, and I'd rather not miss the reading of the evidence."

The sentry stared at him the way Greta had once stared at arithmetic that refused to behave. Then he blew his whistle.

It went up the harbor street, shrill and thin over the water where the ice was beginning to groan again, louder now, daily, the river talking in its sleep — and doors opened along the quay one after another, dockmen and clerks and women with pails, faces appearing at windows he had delivered letters to for seven years. None of them moved to help him. None of them needed to. They simply watched, every one of them, and he understood that they would go on watching, all the way to the courthouse and past it, and that a town which watches is not the same as a town that agrees.

Two military policemen came at a run. Hands on his arms, firm, almost gentle — arresting a man who has walked to you is an awkward business — and as they turned him toward the guardhouse Freya looked back once down the length of the harbor: the cranes, the customs house flying the army flag, the shipyard loft with its candle stubs and shavings, the black line of the river under its breaking ice.

Somewhere north of all of it, a hull named *Edith Marlowe* was taking shape in a stranger's yard.

"Walk, then," said the older policeman, and Freya walked.

In the guardhouse, behind a desk stacked with forms, a familiar figure looked up from the inventory book — Corporal Hobbes, thinner than winter had made him, eyes going wide, then narrow, then doing something complicated that in another life might have been called hope. His hand drifted, casually, terribly, toward the bell-pull, and stopped.

"Well," Hobbes said, loud enough for the room. "Caught him at the bridge, did you?" And then, quieter, under the scratch of his own pen as he wrote the entry no one would later be able to find: *"You mad bastard. Do you know what Rourke will do?"*

"Yes," Freya said. "That's rather why I've come."
