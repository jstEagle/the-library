## 34. The Trial of Edith Marlowe

They held the trial in the customs house, which was the first cruelty of it — the same hall where the requisition forms had been stamped, where Box 41 had sat in the vault below my feet while I lay awake counting sentries. Now they had cleared the counters away and set up a bench, and hung a portrait of the King above the magistrate's head as if to remind everyone whose paper this was.

The town came anyway. That was the second thing nobody had planned for.

They filled the benches and stood along the walls and spilled out onto the steps in the cold, dockworkers with sawdust still on their sleeves, shopkeepers who had signed nothing and said less for two years, women with Edith's old leaflets folded into their purses like contraband they'd finally stopped being ashamed of. The army had requisitioned our warehouses and our records and our neighbors, but they had not requisitioned this room's memory, and memory turned out to be the one thing they couldn't seize by form.

I sat near the front with the ledger pages against my chest, inside my coat, wrapped in oilcloth out of habit. Eleven sheets. All that remained after I'd torn out everything that could hang the Vanes, everything that could hang me. What was left was the other half of the book — the half I had never thought of as evidence because I had never thought of it as anything but debts. Names owed. Names owing. Favors entered in my small crabbed hand across three years of peacetime: *Mrs. Aldous, coal, repaid in mending. A. Weiss, passage north, promised. E. Marlowe, printing costs, 40 shillings, no repayment sought.*

Forty shillings. That was what they were trying her for, in the end, dressed up in words like *seditious association* and *correspondence with persons of enemy sympathy.* Forty shillings she had given me for ink and paper, and never once asked when I'd pay it back, because she hadn't considered it a loan at all. She had considered it the price of the work.

The prosecution did not see it that way. Captain Rourke — promoted now, or perhaps just wearing a better coat — laid out his case like a man laying out instruments. He read from seized correspondence in a flat, careful voice, letting each sentence sit in the air until it curdled. He read the word *solidarity* until it sounded like a code. He read a letter from a German-born woman in the next county thanking Edith for a parcel of food and clothing, and he read the woman's maiden name twice, slowly, so the room would understand that charity and treason were the same thing if you squinted hard enough and wanted to.

Edith sat straight through all of it. She wore grey — her good dress, the one she'd worn to speak on the platform the day the crowd thinned and thinned and she kept talking anyway. Her hands were folded on the rail in front of her, and she did not look at Rourke, and she did not look at the magistrate, and she did not look defeated. She looked like a woman waiting out bad weather because she knew the harbor better than the forecast did.

Once, only once, her composure slipped. It was when Rourke read aloud a line from one of her letters — a letter I knew, because I had carried it, because I had read every letter she ever sealed before sealing it, that was my trade and my sin — and the line said *we do not stop being neighbors because the government finds it inconvenient.* Rourke read it as an accusation. But something crossed Edith's face when she heard her own words spoken back to her in that flat prosecutorial voice, and it wasn't fear. It was closer to grief, the particular grief of hearing a song you wrote sung badly.

Then the magistrate called witnesses for the defense, and there were very few, because most people who could have testified had learned what testimony costs in a town under occupation. And then the magistrate looked over his spectacles and said, "Is there anyone else who wishes to speak to the character of the accused?"

And I stood up.

My legs did not feel like mine. The oilcloth packet weighed nothing and everything. I walked to the stand and gave my name — Freya Crane, driver, courier, keeper of accounts — and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was sweating through my coat the way I had sweated across from Rourke in that little interview room, lying with a steady voice about names in a copied ledger. This was different. This time I meant every word, and meaning it made it harder, not easier.

"You were employed by the accused," the prosecutor said. Not Rourke; a solicitor brought up from the city, dry as a file. "As a driver."

"As a driver of letters," I said. "She paid me to carry correspondence between her and the families of men detained under the emergency orders. Wives. Mothers. Some of them wives of men born in Germany who had lived here twenty, thirty years, who had built half the boats in this harbor."

"And you read these letters." He said it like a trap. Maybe it was one.

"Every one," I said. "It was my habit before sealing them. I told myself it was to protect her — to know what I was carrying. It was also because I couldn't help it. Words are what I have." A murmur went along the benches. I let it die. "I will tell you what was in them, since that seems to be the question. Money for rent. Medicine for children. A request that someone check on an old man's stove. A photograph enclosed so a husband in an internment camp could see how tall his daughter had grown. In two years of carrying Edith Marlowe's letters, I never carried one word against the Crown. I carried bread. I carried names. I carried the fact that people here remembered each other."

The solicitor flipped a page. "You are aware the accused associated with persons of enemy birth."

"I am aware she associated with persons," I said. "Anton Weiss built the ketches that carry your navy's mail. His hands are in every hull in this harbor. When we needed him, he was a shipwright. When we didn't, he was a category on a form. Edith Marlowe is the only person in authority in this town who never once confused those two things."

I reached inside my coat. For one absurd moment the whole room seemed to hold its breath — the magistrate leaning forward, the soldiers by the door shifting their rifles, Rourke's face gone very still and very interested, because he suspected, I think he had always suspected, exactly what I might be carrying and exactly what it could cost me.

I set the oilcloth packet on the rail and unwrapped it. Eleven pages. I handed them up.

"These are pages from a private ledger I kept," I said. "Entries in my own hand, dating back three years. They record money Edith Marlowe spent — out of her own purse, unreimbursed — on food parcels, on legal fees for detained men, on coal for widows, on postage. Page four records forty shillings for printing leaflets about voting rights. Not sedition. Suffrage. She has been fighting for the vote longer than this war has existed, and the war is the only crime she has committed against anyone."

The magistrate read. He read for a long time. The room made no sound at all — not the courtroom sound of respect, but the held-breath sound of a whole town watching its own history being read back to it in one man's handwriting. I recognized some of the faces on the benches. Mrs. Aldous was there, the coal, the mending. A man from the fish market whose mother's name was in my book under *medicine, winter '15.* They were reading their own names. That was the thing I understood as I stood there: the ledger had never been my secret. It had been theirs all along, kept by me, and now I was giving it back.

"The prosecution will wish to examine these documents," the solicitor said at last, reaching.

"With pleasure," I said. "Examine them closely. You'll find no codes, no ciphers, no arrangements. You'll find arithmetic. Charity keeps poor books, sir. If she were running a conspiracy, I promise you the entries would be tidier."

A laugh went up from the benches before anyone could stop it — short, startled, real. The magistrate rapped for order, but the laugh had already done its work. Laughter in a courtroom is a dangerous thing for a prosecution. It means the room has stopped being afraid, and fear was the entire architecture of their case.

Rourke stood. He had been sitting very still through all of it, and now he rose with the unhurried certainty of a man who keeps one more card even after the hand looks lost.

"The court should know," he said, "that this witness is himself under investigation. That the witness's own records were recovered and partially duplicated by intelligence. That the witness removed documents from military custody — an offense carrying the death penalty — and has destroyed portions of them." He let that settle. "The court should ask itself whether a self-confessed thief of official papers is a credible character witness."

"I'll answer that," I said, before the magistrate could rule. My heart was going like a fouled engine. "Everything he says is true. I stole the ledger back from your vault because it had my friends' names in it and I knew what your machine does with names. I burned the pages that would endanger smugglers who helped me move a shipwright and his child out of this country before they were interned — yes, Captain, I'll say it plainly, since saying it plainly seems to be the only currency left in this room. And I kept these eleven pages because they prove that the woman sitting at that rail spent two years feeding people your paperwork declared didn't exist. So ask yourself, sir — ask the court — whether a man who broke the law to tell the truth about a good woman is worth less than a captain who broke nothing and tells it slantwise."

Silence again. Different silence. The kind that comes after a wave breaks.

The magistrate looked at Edith. "Do you wish to address the charge?"

She stood. She had been waiting for this the way a river waits for ice-out. She did not go to the stand; she stayed at the rail, and she spoke to the room, not the bench — I watched her make that choice, watched her turn her shoulders a few degrees toward the crowded benches, and I understood she had decided that whatever happened here, the trial was going to become the rally the war had taken from her.

"They say I committed seditious association," she said. "Let me tell you what I associated with. Hunger. Rent arrears. Children who stopped writing to their fathers because they didn't know what to say. A girl named Greta Weiss, twelve years old, who asked me whether her family's name had become a bad word." Her voice did not rise; it dropped instead, into the register that had held crowds on platforms when the wind tried to steal her words. "I associated with the truth, which is that this town ran on the labor of men we now call enemies, and that feeding their families is not treason, it is arithmetic — and unlike the Crown's arithmetic, mine never rounded anyone down to zero."

She looked at the magistrate then, finally, directly.

"Convict me," she said. "I'll wear it. But write in your verdict what I actually did, in plain language, so that when this war ends — and it will end — the people of this harbor can read it and know which side of themselves they were on."

* * *

The magistrate deliberated for less than an hour, which surprised everyone; I think he had made up his mind somewhere around the laugh, or possibly around page four, forty shillings, suffrage.

He found her guilty. Of course he found her guilty — power concedes nothing it isn't forced to concede, and the forms had been filed, and a magistrate who acquitted her would have been telling London the whole apparatus was theatre. But he sentenced her to time served and a fine, no prison, and he did it quickly, and he did not meet her eyes, and the fine was small enough that everyone in the room did the arithmetic at once and understood it for what it was: a door left open by a man who wanted it noted that he had been forced to close it.

The gallery erupted. Not cheering exactly — the soldiers' presence kept it below that — but a low roar, boots stamping, hands clapping, Mrs. Aldous weeping openly into her mended gloves. Soldiers moved toward the aisles and then, remarkably, stopped, because the noise contained nothing they could name as an enemy. It was just a town, being glad.

Edith stood in the middle of it and looked, for the first time, tired. Then she found my face in the crowd, and mouthed one word — *foolish* — which from her was the highest praise she had.

But I barely heard any of it. Because while the room roared and Edith stood unbowed and the magistrate gathered his papers, a constable worked his way along the wall toward me with a folded paper in his hand, and his eyes were apologetic, and I already knew.

"Freya Crane?" he said. "You're to come with me. There's a warrant."

Rourke, across the room, did not smile. He simply checked his watch, patient as tide, and I understood that this too had been scheduled — that the trial had never been the point, only the stage, and that my testimony had been permitted precisely so that I would stand up in open court and convict myself.

The pages of the ledger lay on the magistrate's bench, eleven sheets of arithmetic, my handwriting, my whole philosophy of what can be carried. As the constable took my arm I thought: I chose this. Walking out past the benches, past Edith's stricken face, past the town that had learned my name in the worst possible way — I thought of blank paper, and what I would write on it, and how long the docks would take to teach my hands the rest.

They took me down the steps into the cold, and the river ice groaned somewhere below us, breaking early, the way it always does when you've finally run out of time.
