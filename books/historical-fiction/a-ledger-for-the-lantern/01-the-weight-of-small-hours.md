## 1. The Weight of Small Hours

The knock came at the hour when even the mice in Vellbruck had given up on ambition, and Coral Ashgrove was already awake to answer it.

This was not virtue. It was habit — the particular insomnia of someone raised in a tower full of ticking things. You cannot sleep beside forty clocks any more than you can sleep beside forty whispering relatives. They mean well, but they never stop talking.

Coral pulled a coat over their nightshirt, took the lantern from its hook by the door — the one Halloway had lit for them fifteen years ago, when they'd arrived as a bundle and a note and nothing else — and opened it to find young Pieter Voss standing in the snow with his hat crushed in his fists like a bird he'd caught by accident.

"It's the master clock," Pieter said. "Master Halloway sent for you himself. He said—" Pieter swallowed. "He said to tell you it's *wrong*."

Coral considered this. In Vellbruck, where every train, every tide table, every debt entered in every shopkeeper's ledger moved to the rhythm of the observatory clock, "the clock is wrong" was roughly equivalent to announcing that the mountain had begun to walk. "Wrong how?"

"Just wrong." Pieter's eyes were wide and unhappy. "It struck eleven at eleven, but the strike was... off. My father says he counted thirteen. Or nine. He'd been drinking, mind. But Mrs. Alderby heard it too, and she hasn't touched spirits since the Kaiser's coronation."

"Thirteen or nine," Coral repeated. "That's quite a range."

"He's my father," Pieter said, as though this explained everything. It did, actually. Old Voss had once sworn before the whole tavern that a cow could count to six if you paid her enough.

Coral fetched their tools, wrote a note for Marta — *gone up to the tower, don't burn the porridge out of spite* — and followed Pieter into the dark.

* * *

The observatory sat above the town like an old man listening for his own heartbeat, and inside, the stairs wound upward past the great telescope room and through a door most townsfolk never saw, into the clock chamber itself.

Coral stopped on the threshold and did what they always did first: listened.

A clock tells you what ails it, if you let it speak. A healthy escapement ticks with a sound like a well-bred person clearing their throat — precise, unhurried, faintly smug. This one ticked like someone trying to remember a name mid-introduction. Tick — pause — tick-tick. There was a drag in the beat, a hesitation on the left side of the swing, and beneath it all a thin metallic whine that made Coral's back teeth ache in sympathy.

"You came quickly."

Halloway stood at the base of the great movement, lantern in hand, and Coral performed their other lifelong habit: they read him like a mechanism.

Master Elias Halloway was seventy-one years of springs and wheels, and Coral had spent those same years learning his tolerances. His griefs ran slow and deep, like the weight-driven trains of the clock itself. His anger was quick and cold and rare, a snapped pallet-stone. And his evasions — his evasions were the one thing about him that didn't run true. When Halloway lied, he didn't stammer or flush. He simply wound himself tighter. His sentences got shorter. His hands went still. A man's hands, Halloway had taught Coral, were the most honest escapement they owned; watch what they do when the mouth is busy.

His hands were perfectly still now. Both of them. Wrapped around the lantern like a man holding onto a rail in a storm.

"What happened?" Coral asked, setting down the toolbox.

"The strike miscounted last night. I checked the count wheel; nothing amiss. I advanced the minute hand, reset the strike, and it behaved itself until three o'clock, when it refused to strike at all." He paused. "Then at half past, it struck seven."

"At half past three it struck *seven*?"

"I have kept this clock for forty years," Halloway said quietly. "I have seen it through two floods, one avalanche, and a visit from the Bishop of Grunstadt, who sneezed on the dial. It has never once told me a lie. Tonight it is telling me several, and I would very much like to know why."

There it was again — that tightness. Coral filed it away, the way they filed everything, in the great mental cabinet of drawers labeled *later*, and climbed the ladder into the movement's iron ribs.

Up close, the master clock was a cathedral of brass. The going train filled the left wall, weights descending on cables thick as a wrist; the strike train occupied the right; between them, mounted on a frame of blackened steel, hung the escapement — the heart, the governor, the small fierce mechanism that converted falling weight into measured time. Coral put their ear against the frame and let the vibration tell them stories.

"There," they said, after a while. "The left pallet. Feel it."

Halloway climbed up beside them, which took longer than it used to, and pressed one knotted finger to the pallet stone. The tick came. His frown deepened. "Oil starved?"

"No — I oiled her Tuesday, and look, the film's still good." Coral tilted the lantern. "Feel how she hesitates *before* the lock, not after? That's not friction. That's the tooth not trusting the pallet. Something's changed the geometry. Worn pivot? No, there'd be wobble..." They reached in with a feeler gauge, delicate as a surgeon, and stopped.

"Coral?"

"Someone's been here." Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "Since Tuesday. The pallet arbor has been adjusted. Badly. Quarter-turn off true, and re-pinned with the wrong gauge wire — see, this pin's soft iron, we use hardened steel. Anyone who works this bench knows that."

They both looked at each other. Then, together, at the room.

Nothing was disturbed. The floor was clean. The tool board hung in its careful order. But the window latch — Coral crossed to it — was unhooked, and the sill bore a smear of snowmelt already drying to a ghost.

"Perhaps I forgot the latch after oiling," Halloway said slowly. But his hands had gone still again, and Coral watched them not moving and felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't fear exactly. More like the moment a clock stops: the sudden awareness of silence where there should be a beat.

"Master," Coral said. "Who else comes up here?"

"You come up here. Marta brings the tea as far as the landing." He descended the ladder with the care of a man buying time. "And the town council sends a deputation twice a year to admire the escapement and eat my biscuits."

"And?"

Halloway set the lantern down. For a long moment he studied the great dial face from below, pale in the lamplight, its hands frozen in their wrongness.

"And no one else," he said. "Let us fix the clock."

* * *

It took them until the grey edge of dawn. Coral trued the pallet arbor, replaced the soft pin with proper steel from the bench stock, adjusted the drop until the tick fell back into its well-bred throat-clearing purr, and then sat on the ladder rung eating the bread and cheese Halloway produced from some drawer, watching the pendulum swing with the satisfied gravity of a large animal resuming a nap.

"Better?" Halloway asked.

"She'll hold. Though whoever tampered with her knew just enough to be dangerous. Which bothers me more than the tampering." Coral brushed crumbs from their coat. "You know what else bothers me?"

"I suspect you'll tell me regardless."

"When Pieter woke me, he said you sent for me yourself. But Marta said nothing, and Marta hears everything in this house before I do. So either you sent word down the hill at four in the morning without waking the cook, which is impossible—"

"Difficult."

"—or you were already awake, sitting down here, waiting for the clock to do something strange enough that you'd have an excuse to summon me." Coral held his gaze. "You've been odd for weeks, Master. Every time the name-ledger comes up — the council wanting the census copied, the archivist writing from the capital asking after records — you go quiet as a stopped clock. And now someone's been at the escapement, and you won't say who, and your hands haven't moved right all night."

Halloway looked at them for a long time. Outside, the sky over the mountains had gone the color of pewter, and the first bell of the town below rang the quarter hour — obediently late or early by whatever the master clock decreed, and now, thankfully, decreeing nothing at all.

"The ledger is a long book," he said at last.

"It's the only book this town agrees on. Every foundling, every widow, every worker who ever belonged to the observatory. You've shown it to me once, when I was ten. I remember the smell of it."

"Do you remember your page?"

The question dropped between them like a weight released too fast. Coral laughed, because the alternative was not laughing. "Of course not. Foundlings don't get pages, do they? I'm in the intake margin. A date and a line."

"Yes," Halloway said. "A date and a line."

And his hands — his honest, terrible hands — folded themselves together and went utterly, perfectly still.

Coral had repaired a thousand mechanisms. They knew the exact sound a thing makes when a hidden fault finally shows itself: not a crash, not a snap, just a small change in the rhythm, a hesitation where none had been. They heard it now, in the silence between the old man's words, and understood that whatever was wrong with the master clock, it was the smallest thing wrong in this tower tonight.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of boots on the observatory steps — many boots, early, unhurried, and entirely wrong for the hour.

Halloway lifted his head. "Now what," he murmured, "have they come for?"

The knock, when it came, was military: three strikes, evenly spaced, timed by someone who had never needed a clock to know what time it was.
