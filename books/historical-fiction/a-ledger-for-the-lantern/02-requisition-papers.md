## 2. Requisition Papers

The knock came at half past seven, which was itself an offense. Nothing in Vellbruck knocked at half past seven. The baker knocked at four, the milk boy at five, and the observatory's own bell announced the hour so thoroughly that a human fist on a door was an impertinence, like someone humming over a choir.

Coral had been oiling the strike train's third wheel for the better part of an hour, sleeves rolled to the elbow, fingers smelling of whale oil and brass polish, when Master Halloway looked up from his desk with an expression Coral had learned to read the way one reads a dial: something was winding down behind his eyes.

"Stay here," Halloway said.

"I live here," Coral pointed out.

"Then stay *here*," he said, meaning the workshop, and went to answer the door himself, moving with the careful dignity of a man who has decided that whatever is on the other side of it will be met standing up.

Coral stayed exactly where they were for eleven seconds — they counted; counting was what they were for — and then drifted to the top of the stairwell, where the acoustics of the old observatory carried every word up through the stairwell's throat as faithfully as any speaking tube.

There were three of them. Coral could hear it: two sets of army boots with the heavy flat tread of men who walked on floors they didn't own, and one lighter pair, leather-soled, official. The official voice did the talking. It had the smooth, rehearsed warmth of a man reading terms aloud to a room he assumed had already lost the argument.

"—grateful for Vellbruck's cooperation. The mobilization exercises will commence in six weeks. The town's position between the valley lines makes it ideal for staging. You'll find the schedule enclosed."

"Vellbruck," said Master Halloway, in the voice he used when a customer suggested a cuckoo clock might be repaired "with a good shake," "is not a garrison."

"The town will host a full army mobilization, sir. Troops, supply trains, field hospitals. It's an honor."

"It's a mountain town with four hundred souls and a clock that keeps every train and tide table within forty miles. What could you possibly want from us besides trouble?"

"All civic records are to be surrendered for logistical audit," the officer said, and Coral heard paper being smoothed against wood — the particular dry whisper of an official document being flattened so its seals faced upward. "Population rolls, tax ledgers, parish registers, work rosters." A pause, precisely measured, like a beat inserted by a man who knew how to make silences do his arguing for him. "And the observatory's name-ledger."

The silence that followed was longer than any the officer had engineered. Coral found themselves gripping the banister.

Halloway's voice came back quieter. "That ledger is not a civic record."

"It records persons resident under the observatory's authority. Foundlings, widows, pensioned workers. That makes it civic by any definition the War Office recognizes."

"It records *people*," Halloway said. "It is the only proof some of them exist. Widow Anselm cannot read or write. Old Piet has no birth certificate — there wasn't a registrar in the upper valley until 1898. That book *is* their certificate. You take it, and you take them."

"With respect, sir, we take nothing. We audit. Everything will be returned."

Everything will be returned. Coral turned the phrase over the way one turns a suspect gear, looking for the tooth that doesn't mesh. In Coral's experience — and their experience of promises came mostly from watching Halloway make them to frightened customers — a man who says *everything will be returned* is a man who has already decided what won't be.

"Master Halloway," the officer said, gentler now, the tone of a man offering a ladder out of a hole he had dug, "I am Captain Voss. I have orders countersigned at the district level. You may write all the letters you like. Meanwhile, the requisition stands. I'm told you have an apprentice. A capable one, I'm told further. The master clock must be kept running during the exercise. Trains on military schedules. We'd take it kindly if your household cooperated."

There it was. Coral felt it land even from the stairs — the pivot from threat to flattery, done so smoothly most people wouldn't notice the hand in their pocket. Halloway noticed. Halloway always noticed. But noticing and stopping were different trades.

"You'll want my apprentice working under your supervision, then," Halloway said flatly.

"For everyone's convenience."

"My apprentice works alone in that tower, or the clock stops. Those are the terms. I've kept that mechanism for thirty-one years and I'll not have a corporal with clean boots telling me when to wind it."

A murmur between the soldiers. Then Voss again, pleasant as a receipt: "Your terms are noted. The records, however, come with us today. The clerk will inventory them at the door if you wish to witness."

Coral heard the front door open wider — more boots outside, then, a whole detail of them — and made the decision that would define the rest of that year: they came down the stairs openly, wiping their hands on their apron, wearing the expression they practiced for difficult customers, which was attentive, harmless, and entirely unbothered.

Three soldiers in the hall, gray-green and mud-cuffed. A clerk with a ledger-case of his own, small and smug, like a purse beside a coffin. And Captain Voss, who was younger than his voice, with a neat mustache and eyes that moved around the hall cataloguing exits, windows, and persons, in that order.

"You're the apprentice," Voss said. Not a question.

"Coral Ashgrove. Clockmaker's apprentice." They put a slight emphasis on *clockmaker* that Halloway would have been proud of.

"Ashgrove." Voss said it the way a man tests a coin with his teeth. "You'll be working on the tower clock, then. Good. It matters to us that it runs."

"It matters to everyone that it runs," Coral said. "That's rather the point of it."

Something flickered at the corner of the captain's mouth — not quite approval, but a relative of it — and then he turned back to business, because business was armor and he wore it well.

* * *

They took the records in the parlor, where the light was best for inventory, and Coral stood in the doorway with Marta — summoned up from the kitchen by the sheer gravitational wrongness of strange boots in the house — and watched twenty years of the town's memory get wrapped in brown paper and string.

Marta had come up holding a bread knife and had thought better of it, converting the weapon into hospitality with the speed of a woman who had fed hungry men through two bad winters and one worse spring. She pressed rolls on the soldiers with such ferocious kindness that two of them accepted purely out of self-preservation. The third, a very young one with chapped hands, looked like he might cry into his.

But the clerk was immune to rolls. He worked down a list: parish register, tax roll, work rosters, school attendance book. Each item wrapped, labeled, stacked. And then he said, in the tone of a man reading off his own shopping list:

"The name-ledger of the Observatory of Vellbruck. Large volume, green binding, brass corners."

Halloway went still. It was a horologist's stillness, Coral thought later — the stillness of a man watching a mainspring reach the end of its coil and knowing exactly how much energy was stored in it.

"That book stays," Halloway said.

"Sir—"

"That book stays, or you may arrest me, and you may explain to the War Office why the town's only record of its dependent poor was seized by force from a seventy-year-old clockmaker. Write that in your inventory. I'll wait while you find the words."

The clerk looked at Captain Voss. Voss looked at the ledger, which sat on its lectern by the window where it had sat for as long as Coral could remember, fat and green-cornered, smelling of tallow and dust and the particular sweetness of old iron-gall ink.

"Wrap it carefully," Voss said quietly. "It will be returned."

"Captain—" the clerk began.

"*Carefully,* Aldermann."

Two soldiers carried it out between them, and this was the moment — Coral would replay it for nights afterward, turning it over like a wheel with a flaw they couldn't locate — this was the moment Coral saw it.

The binding had been mended.

Not long ago, either. Along the spine, where the green leather met the boards, ran a strip of new calf skin, pale as uncooked dough, stitched with waxed thread in a herringbone pattern. The dye hadn't taken evenly; there were darker patches where the old leather showed through like roots under snow. And the stitching — Coral's eye went along it the way other people's eyes went along faces — the stitching was too tight at the head and loose at the tail. Amateur work. Recent work. Work done by lamplight by someone whose hands shook.

Coral knew every mended book in that house. Knew the atlas Halloway had rebound in 1904, the hymnal Marta's mother had left her, the account books going back to the observatory's founding. The ledger had gone to the bookbinder in Brunnstadt twice — Coral remembered both trips, remembered the smell of the cart — and neither time had returned with pale new calfskin on the spine.

Somebody had opened that binding. Somebody had taken something out — pages, perhaps several — and closed it up again and hoped the brass corners and the familiar fatness of the volume would keep anyone from looking too hard.

*Coral*, said the thought, arriving with the cold specificity of a struck hour, *that was mended from inside this house.*

Their gaze went, before they could stop it, to Master Halloway.

He was watching the ledger go out the door with his hands folded behind his back, and his face was composed, dignified, grief-stricken in the ordinary way any man's face is when his life's records are carted off by strangers. It was a good performance. It was almost perfect.

Almost. Because Halloway, who could feel a miscounted tooth through three gears of steel, had not once glanced at the mended spine. A man who notices everything does not fail to notice the one thing he mended himself — unless he is working very, very hard not to look at it.

The door closed. The boots faded down the hill toward the town. Somewhere below, a train whistle blew the quarter-hour, and overhead the great clock ticked on, indifferent, keeping time for a world that had just begun keeping secrets.

"Well," said Marta, into the silence, gathering up abandoned rolls. "They seemed hungry."

"Marta." Coral's voice came out steadier than they felt. "When was the ledger last out of its lectern?"

She paused, cloth in hand, and gave them the look she reserved for questions that mattered more than they appeared to. "Why?"

"The spine. Someone re-bound it. Recently. Badly."

Marta looked at the empty lectern, and Coral watched something cross her face — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a woman filing a fact away in a drawer she intended to open later, privately, with the door shut.

"Halloway had it out all one week in the winter," she said slowly. "Told me not to disturb the study. Said he was checking entries against the pension lists."

"And was he?"

Marta set down the cloth. "Coral Ashgrove," she said, "in thirty years I have learned that the men who check things twice at midnight are never checking what they say they're checking. Now help me clear this parlor before I start thinking about what I just said."

But Coral was already thinking about it. All through the morning's work, all through the oiling and the winding and the careful false cheer of lunch, one image turned in their mind like a flywheel: pale new calfskin, herringbone stitch, tight at the head, loose at the tail — and underneath it, somewhere in the fat green body of that book, a gap where something had been cut away.

Someone in this house had removed a page. And Coral had a terrible, creeping suspicion — rising in them like water in a gauge — whose page it was.
