## 3. What the Binding Remembers

The empty binding slot sat in Halloway's private papers like a tooth knocked from a jaw.

Coral had not meant to find it. That was the thing they would tell themselves later, turning it over in the small hours the way one turns a stubborn screw — that they had gone into the back room of Halloway's quarters for entirely honest reasons. The army had taken the ledger two days ago, and with it every scrap of official paper in the observatory, and Marta needed proof of her late husband's pension years before the clerks would release his wages to her, and Halloway kept duplicates of everything because a horologist who trusts only one copy of anything is a horologist who deserves what happens next.

So Coral had gone looking for the duplicate register. And found, instead, a slim leather binding — same oxblood as the ledger, same brass corners, same stitched spine — sitting flat and hollow at the bottom of a locked drawer that had been unlocked.

That last part snagged in them like a burr. The drawer was never unlocked. Coral had been picking locks since they were seven, purely for the pleasure of understanding them, and had never once picked this one, because some doors you leave shut out of respect. It stood open now by perhaps an inch, and Halloway was downstairs arguing on the telephone with a requisition clerk who kept putting him on hold, and the argument was going badly enough that he would be a while yet.

Coral looked at the open drawer for a long moment.

Then they looked away from it, which took even longer.

In the end they told themselves the same thing Halloway had always told them about a mechanism: you cannot diagnose a fault by refusing to open the case. They lifted the binding out with both hands, the way one lifts something sleeping.

It weighed almost nothing. A ledger's worth of covers and spine and stitching, and inside — nothing. Not blank pages. *No* pages. The signatures of the thread still held their knots where pages had been sewn in, dozens of stations along the spine, each one gripping a ghost. Coral tilted it toward the window light and saw, caught in the sewing, tiny tufts of paper fiber where the sheets had been torn free rather than cut — torn by someone in a hurry but trying not to be, which is its own kind of signature.

And along the spine, in the valley of the binding where glue collects when a book is mended rather than made, there was residue. Old adhesive, yellowed, laid down thickly in places and thinly in others, following the shape of whatever had been pressed against it and then removed.

Coral knew that glue. Every clockmaker knows glue; it is the coward's solder and the sinner's friend, and half the repair work in any town consists of undoing it. This was hide glue, the old recipe, the kind Halloway mixed himself in a double boiler every autumn and kept in a jar labeled, in his cramped hand, *for emergencies and other lies.*

More than that: Coral knew this particular application of it. Fifteen years ago — they had been eight, maybe nine, small enough to sit on the workbench with their heels drumming the drawer front while Halloway worked — there had been a week when the master ledger was brought down from the tower for rebinding. Coral remembered it oddly well, the way one remembers things witnessed from a workbench: the smell of the glue, the stack of loose quires waiting their turn, and Halloway working late three nights running with the door closed, which he never did, telling Coral through it that the fumes were bad for growing lungs.

They had thought nothing of it at eight. At twenty-three, sitting on Halloway's own floor with a hollow book in their lap, they thought of very little else.

* * *

When Halloway came up the stairs an hour later, still muttering about clerks and their "logistical audits," he found Coral at his table with the empty binding between them and a lamp turned up bright, and he stopped in the doorway the way a man stops when he hears a tick go wrong in a room that had been ticking correctly all his life.

"That drawer," he said.

"Was open," Coral agreed. "I know."

"I see." He came in slowly, hung his coat on its peg with more care than the coat deserved, and sat down across from them. He did not ask them to put it back. That, more than anything, told Coral he had been waiting for this the way one waits for a fuse to burn down.

"I wasn't going to look," Coral said. "I want that on the record. I went in for Marta's pension papers. But the drawer was open, and Master — you never leave that drawer open. You check it twice at night. I've heard you check it twice at night."

"Old habit."

"It isn't, though, is it. Because habits are for things you're afraid of losing, and you weren't afraid of losing an empty book." Coral turned the binding so the spine faced him, the glue catching the lamplight. "This was repaired. Autumn, fifteen years back, when the ledger was down for rebinding. Hide glue, your own mix. And these aren't cut marks in the sewing — they're tears. Someone pulled the pages out after they were glued in, which means someone glued them in first, which means—" They stopped, because their voice had developed a wobble they despised, and started again, quieter. "You took pages out of the ledger, Master. You rebound it around the hole. And then you hid the binding itself in a locked drawer for fifteen years."

Halloway looked at the binding for a long time. His hands, which could true a balance wheel to within a hair's breath, lay flat on the table as if he'd set them down somewhere safe.

"Yes," he said.

"Why?"

"You were small," he said. "You used to sit on that very bench with your boots knocking the wood. You liked the smell of the glue. You said it smelled like—" His mouth did something complicated. "Like Sundays."

"Master Halloway." Coral rarely used the whole name. When they did, it landed like a weight on a scale. "Why did you take pages out of the ledger?"

He rose, went to the shelf, and took down the tea things, which was what he did instead of answering questions — Coral had read that mechanism years ago. But tonight his hands shook slightly, and the lid of the tin chattered against the caddy, and he set it all down again without making anything.

"Because there was someone," he said finally, "who could not afford to be in that book."

"The ledger protects people. That's what it *is*. Widows. Foundlings. Workers. You've told me a hundred times — it's the town's promise that these people count—"

"And it does. And it did. And for nearly everyone in it, being written there was armor." He turned and looked at Coral then, and his eyes were tired in a way Coral had never seen, not through any illness or hard winter or the death of his own brother. "But there are times, child, when armor painted with your name tells a hunter exactly where to shoot. There was a page in that ledger that, if certain parties had ever seen it, would have done harm. Real harm. To someone I—" He stopped. Started over. "To someone whose safety mattered more to me than the completeness of a record."

"To whom?"

"No."

"Halloway—"

"No." The word came out with edges on it. He softened it immediately, the way he always did, reaching across to touch Coral's wrist where it rested beside the binding — a horologist's touch, brief and precise, feeling for a pulse that wasn't a clock's. "I know how this looks. I know what I've taught you: that a record is sacred, that a gap in the mechanism means someone has lied about the mechanism. And you have learned your lessons too well, which is the fate of every teacher who teaches honestly. But I am asking you — I am *asking* you — to let me keep this one thing. One gear sealed in its case. For their sake. For mine."

Coral pulled their hand back. Gently. Everything gently, like handling a movement they didn't want to stop.

"The army has the ledger now," they said. "Soldiers carried it out of my workshop in a crate marked *miscellaneous stationery*. If there's a page in there dangerous enough that you gutted a sacred book over it — don't you think I ought to know whether it concerns me? Whether it concerns—" They couldn't finish it. The word *me* seemed suddenly enormous.

Something crossed Halloway's face then, quick as a shadow over the tower dial, and Coral, who had spent twenty-three years learning to read this man the way they read escapements — by the sound of the lock and the fall — caught it, or thought they caught it.

And then he said, "It doesn't concern you," in a voice that was perfectly level and perfectly calm, and Coral understood, with a coldness that started behind the sternum, that they were hearing their first lie from him.

Not a kindness-lie. Not *no, the fumes won't hurt you* or *yes, that gear is nearly new* or any of the small merciful falsehoods that oil a life. A real one. Deliberate. Chosen, right now, sitting across from them, knowing exactly what he was doing — because Halloway never did anything without knowing, that was the whole of him.

"Right," Coral said.

"Coral—"

"No, it's — it's fine. You've protected whoever it is for fifteen years. That's not nothing. That's fifteen winters of mixing glue and locking drawers and lying with a straight face, and whatever it cost you, you paid it without complaining once." They stood, and lifted the empty binding, and set it back in the drawer, and pushed the drawer until it clicked home, and left it *unlocked*, deliberately, because they wanted him to know they knew it was a door now and not just a drawer. "Keep your sealed case, Master. I won't pick it."

"Thank you," he said quietly, and it sounded like it cost him.

"But I'll tell you what a clockmaker knows that a liar forgets." Coral paused at the doorway, hand on the jamb, not turning around. "You can seal a gear in its case, but the rest of the train still has to turn. Whatever you removed left a gap, and gaps have shapes. And I have spent my whole life learning to machine a part to fill a gap by studying the shape of the space alone." Now they turned. "The soldiers have the ledger. The soldiers audit things. And if they find that gap before I understand it, they won't be gentle about opening the case the way I was."

Halloway said nothing. In the silence, downstairs, the hall clock struck the hour — six strokes, even and unhurried, indifferent as ever — and Coral watched their teacher watch the door of the drawer as if he could hear the pages rustling inside it.

They went down to the workshop, where the soldiers' inventory chalk still ghosted the walls where tools had hung, and sat at their bench among the dismantled remains of the master clock, and did not sleep, and did not pick the lock, and thought about the shape of a gap.

It was Marta who changed everything, two days later — bursting into the workshop with her shawl still on and her breath short, holding three loose ledger pages wrapped in butcher's paper like contraband, saying words Coral would remember for the rest of their life:

"Lass. The clerk's been selling the pages off as scrap. And one of these has your intake date on it — and the name field's been *cut out*. Cut clean, with a blade. Not torn. Not by Halloway's hand at all."
