## 4. The Town That Keeps Time

Marta Voss had fed the observatory for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned that a kitchen was simply a clock made of bread. Everything turned on schedule: the dough rose when it pleased, the kettle sang at six, and Coral came down the tower stairs smelling of oil and graphite precisely when the second pot went on. She did not need the master clock to know the hour. She had better instruments — stomachs, mostly.

But this morning the schedule had broken. Soldiers stood at the foot of Lantern Hill with surveyor's poles and coils of wire, unspooling a fence line across the cobbles as if they were hemming the town into a garment two sizes too small.

"They're fencing the lower quarter first," said Pieter Kroll, the fishmonger, arriving at her door with two trout he had absolutely not been asked to bring. "Sergeant told me the market square is now 'Zone B.' I asked what Zone A was, and he said the observatory."

"And us?"

"We're apparently inside our own zone." He handed over the fish with the gravity of a man surrendering state secrets. "Marta, they took the ledger yesterday. Old Fenwick from the tannery says once the archive goes to the capital, none of us can prove we've paid our water rights since the flood year. He's terrified. He's got sixty years of tannery fees in that book."

"Sixty years of tannery fees," Marta repeated, "and not one of them paid on time."

"That's why he needs the book."

She wrapped the trout, set them to soak, and pulled on her shawl. There were rounds to make. There were always rounds — but today the rounds had changed shape. For years she had carried bread and broth up and down Vellbruck's lanes because people needed feeding. Now she carried questions instead, and found people far hungrier for those.

* * *

Coral, meanwhile, was learning what it meant to work under supervision.

Two soldiers had been posted in the workshop overnight — "to secure the equipment," Lieutenant Brenner had said, in the tone of a man reading words off a card he didn't write. They sat on stools by the door like a pair of unmatched weights, one asleep, one picking his teeth with a splint of pinewood and watching Coral file a pallet stone with the fascination of someone watching weather.

"You're holding your breath," the tooth-picker observed. "When you file. You hold your breath."

"I'm concentrating."

"My sister does that. She's an embroiderer. Says the breath shakes the hand." He considered. "She'd like you."

Coral did not answer, but privately filed the observation away, as they filed everything: the soldiers were bored, not hostile; bored men could be talked to; talked-to men could be trusted slightly more than strangers. Brenner, by contrast, had come twice that morning, watched the escapement work without speaking, and left each time with the air of someone rehearsing a question. Coral read mechanisms for a living. People were only clocks with worse manners.

The trouble was the ledger-shaped hole in everything. Halloway had gone quiet again — that particular silence of his, all hinges and no swing — and Coral had spent a sleepless night turning over the glue residue and paper fibers in their mind like a watchmaker turning a jammed barrel. Someone had cut pages out. Halloway knew. And the pages had come from fifteen years ago, which was precisely the age at which a foundling stops being a baby and starts being a person who asks questions.

Marta would know things. Marta always knew things. But asking Marta felt like prying open someone else's movement case, and so Coral filed, and breathed, and waited for the town to do what the town always did.

* * *

Which was, of course, trade.

By noon Marta had conducted three transactions of the old economy. At Widow Sarelt's house above the dye-works, she traded a heel of rye bread for the information that a clerk with ink-stained cuffs had been seen at the requisition office carrying loose papers bundled in string — *loose* papers, not bound ones, which meant the ledger might already be coming apart like a cheap hymnal.

At the lamplighter Toma's cottage, she traded a jar of plum preserve for the news that the fencing crew had orders to enclose the observatory hill entirely by week's end, and that Toma himself had been told his lantern route through the upper lanes would be "suspended during the mobilization."

"Suspended," Marta said. "They're suspending light."

"I'm still lighting them," Toma said placidly. "I just won't be doing it officially. There's a difference between a lantern and permission for a lantern. One of them keeps working."

And at the house of Grete Anselm, retired observatory keeper-of-records, Marta traded nothing at all, because Grete refused payment and pulled her inside by the wrist instead.

Grete was eighty-one and had kept the ledger's hand for forty years before her eyes failed, and she received Marta the way a fortress receives a friendly courier: quickly, bolted behind her, tea poured before coats were off.

"They came here too," Grete said. "Three days ago. Before the soldiers even arrived — a man in civilian clothes, very polite, very dry. Asked whether I remembered the intake records from the winter of '98. The factory-collapse winter."

Marta's hands went still around her cup. "The Ashgrove winter."

"I didn't say that name."

"You didn't have to."

Grete's blind eyes fixed on a point slightly to the left of Marta's face, which somehow made her gaze feel more accurate. "He wanted to know if any pages were missing. I told him my memory was the only record I kept, and memories don't audit well." A thin smile. "Then I asked him why the army cared about foundlings from fifteen years ago, and he stopped being polite. Not unkind. Just finished. Like a door closing on well-oiled hinges."

"What did you tell him after that?"

"Nothing. I'm very good at nothing. Forty years of bookkeeping teaches you which entries matter and which silences do." She reached across the table and gripped Marta's hand with surprising strength. "Listen to me. Whatever page they're hunting — whoever it concerns — there are people in this town who remember every name that book ever held. Widow Sarelt remembers because she signed as witness for half the intakes. Toma remembers because he lit the lantern for every arrival. I remember because I wrote them. A ledger is just paper, Marta. Paper burns. But we are the binding."

* * *

That evening, Coral found the phrase waiting for them.

Marta came back up the hill after dark, past the new fence stakes glinting in the lamplight — Toma's unofficial lamplight, burning defiantly along routes no order had sanctioned — and found Coral at the kitchen table with the escapement parts laid out on cloth like organs awaiting transplant.

"You look like a surgeon," she said, "or a murderer. Possibly both."

"I feel like both. How was the town?"

"Frightened. Trading. Grete Anselm fed me tea and prophecy." Marta untied her boots, which was how Coral knew she intended to stay a while. "She said something I want you to hear. She said a ledger is paper, but the people are the binding."

Coral looked down at the delicate stack of steel in front of them. "We're going to need more than proverbs if the army ships that archive to the capital."

"Then we'll need names. All of them. From memory, testimony, whatever people will swear to." Marta studied their face — that careful, closed face, the face of a child who had learned early that wanting things loudly got them taken away. "You know whose name starts the list."

"Don't," Coral said quietly.

"Somebody cut pages out of that binding, love. Fifteen years ago. Right around—"

"*Don't.*" It came out sharper than Coral meant, sharp enough to ring against the copper pans. They softened immediately, the way a spring settles after overwind. "I'm sorry. I just — if I start wondering tonight, I won't stop, and I have an escapement to finish under two soldiers' noses by Friday. I can't be two broken clocks at once."

Marta crossed the room and put her flour-dusted hand on top of Coral's oiled one. "Then sleep. But hear me: whatever's on that missing page, it doesn't change what walked up my stairs at six years old, or what sits at my table now. Paper's paper. You're the entry that matters."

Coral nodded and did not trust themselves to speak.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the knock came — soft, apologetic, the knock of a person who hoped no one was home.

Lieutenant Brenner stood on the observatory step with his cap in his hands and the awkward posture of a man attending a funeral he hadn't liked the deceased at.

"Lieutenant," Coral said. "The workshop is closed until morning."

"I know. I'm not here about the workshop." He glanced over his shoulder at the empty lane, then back. "Tomorrow the fencing detail moves up the hill. After that, this building is inside the perimeter, and movement in and out gets logged. Logged means recorded. Recorded means—" he stopped, visibly editing himself.

"Means it ends up in the same archive as the ledger," Coral finished.

Brenner's jaw worked. "I've served eleven years," he said finally. "Eleven years, and I've never once been asked to seize a book of names. Ammunition, yes. Grain, fine. But names—" He shook his head. "There's a widow on Fisher Lane. My landlady. She showed me the spot in her copy of the water-rights roll where her husband's name used to be, from the last time there was an audit. Someone inked him out. She keeps the page like a wound."

Coral said nothing, but their heart was filing this away at speed.

"I'm not telling you anything," Brenner said, suddenly formal, the officer reassembling himself piece by piece. "I'm a soldier. I follow orders. Remember that tomorrow, and the day after, whatever happens." He put his cap back on, hesitated, and added, almost too quietly to catch: "Your master clock. When it's running again — how many strokes can it strike? Exactly twelve? Or could it strike... other numbers?"

Coral felt the question go through them like current through a coil.

"Any number," they said carefully. "If the mechanism is built for it."

Something flickered across Brenner's face — relief, or fear, or both wearing the same uniform. "Good night, Ashgrove." And he was gone down the dark lane, past the unofficial lamps, leaving Coral standing in the doorway with a new gear turning silently in their mind: a secondary train, a hidden sequence, strikes enough for every name in a book the army thought it owned.

From the kitchen doorway, Marta's voice, dry as good flour: "Well. Either that boy is your enemy, or he's the strangest ally Vellbruck's ever produced."

"Knowing this town," Coral said, closing the door, "he'll probably turn out to be both."
