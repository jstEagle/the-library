## 5. Escapement

The soldiers arrived at the observatory at six o'clock sharp, which Coral found almost touching. Whatever else could be said of the army — and by the third morning of occupation, Vellbruck had said most of it — they respected a schedule.

There were two of them posted in the clock room: a broad private named Ostrick who smelled of pipe tobacco and had already fallen asleep twice against the doorframe, and Lieutenant Brenner, who was twenty-four at most and stood with his hands clasped behind his back like a man holding himself together with his own knuckles.

"Proceed," Brenner said.

Coral wiped their palms on their apron and opened the master clock's case.

The great clock filled the tower room the way a heart fills a chest. Its pendulum hung nine feet of polished iron; its weight drum sat fat and patient above; and at its center, where a heart keeps its valves, the escapement ticked out the seconds that every train, every tide table, every ledger line in Vellbruck depended on. Halloway liked to say that an escapement was the only honest thing ever built by human hands, because its whole job was to say *no* — no, not yet, not yet, not yet — until the exact moment when yes was true.

The escapement was lying now. It had begun to say *no* a half-beat late, and the whole town had felt it the way you feel a skipped heartbeat.

Coral climbed the step-ladder with a lamp and looked closely at the anchor pallets. There it was: wear on the left pallet face, a bright crescent where brass had been slowly arguing with steel for fifteen years and losing. The oil had gone to varnish. The drop was uneven. A simple diagnosis, honestly earned.

"You've found it?" Brenner asked. He had drifted closer without seeming to move, the way cats do.

"Worn pallets. Uneven drop. It wants re-oiling, re-pitching, maybe a new set of stones if I can't dress these." Coral kept their voice flat and professional. "Three days' work."

"You have two."

"Then I'll work quickly," Coral said, "and the clock will keep time for your inspection, and afterward it will keep time badly, because good work takes three days."

A muscle moved near Brenner's jaw. He looked, Coral thought, like a man who had been handed a rulebook and discovered halfway through that he agreed with more of it than he'd expected to.

"Two days," Brenner said. "Do what you can."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Coral dismantled the escapement piece by piece under Ostrick's dozing supervision, laying each part on a cloth in order of removal — pallets, arbor, crutch, suspension spring — the way Halloway had taught them, saying *a mechanism taken apart carelessly is a confession written in a language you'll forget by evening.*

And while their hands did honest work, their mind did dishonest work.

Because here was the thing Coral had realized on the second sleepless night after the requisition, staring at the ceiling while Marta snored through the wall: the army wanted the ledger. They had seized it for a "logistical audit," which was a phrase that meant nothing and took everything. Pages could be lost in audits. Names could be misplaced. Fifteen years ago, someone had already cut pages out of that binding — Coral had seen the glue, the fibers, the wound in the paper — and if pages could be removed once by one careful hand, they could be removed again by many careless ones.

But a chime, now. A chime was different.

A chime happened in public, in air, in ears. You could seize a book, but you couldn't un-ring a bell. And the master clock's strike train — the great geared sequence that counted out the hours on the tower bell — was, when you looked at it properly, nothing but a machine for counting and remembering. It remembered twelve, and then it struck twelve. That was all remembering was, really: a count held in brass until the moment came to speak it aloud.

What if it could hold a different count?

Coral began sketching, in the margins of their parts list, in pencil so faint it might have been shadow: a secondary train. Small. Driven off the going barrel with a slipping clutch so it would never burden the main movement. A pin wheel instead of a conventional count wheel — pins settable, movable, each pin a single stroke of a small hammer on a small bell. Ninety-nine positions. Enough.

Enough for what? Coral didn't let themselves finish the thought. Not yet. Not with a lieutenant ten feet away.

"What are you drawing?"

Coral's pencil stopped. Brenner had come around the ladder without a sound. Ostrick, usefully, was asleep.

"Parts list," Coral said. "Annotations."

Brenner leaned in. His eyes were pale gray and entirely too awake for a man assigned to watch brass. They moved across the margin sketch — the pin wheel, the clutch, the little hammer — and Coral watched him read it the way Coral read people: quickly, mechanically, noting which teeth were worn.

"That's not an annotation," Brenner said quietly.

"It's an improvement."

"To the strike train."

"To my own education," Coral said. "An apprentice fills every margin eventually. Master Halloway says idle margins breed revolutionaries." Which was either a joke or a warning, depending on how the lieutenant took it, and Coral watched him take it as neither, which was somehow worse.

Brenner straightened. For a long moment he looked at the great clock itself, at the slow swing of the pendulum, and something crossed his young face that didn't belong to an occupying officer — something more like homesickness.

"When I was a boy," he said, "there was a church clock in our town that struck thirteen once. Just once. Some fault in the count wheel." He smiled, briefly, at some private memory. "Half the village swore it was a ghost. My father said clocks never lie, they only tell the truth badly. I've thought about that sentence for years."

"Clocks don't lie," Coral agreed. "People make them lie. It's called regulation."

Something flickered behind those gray eyes — surprise, maybe, or recognition. Brenner glanced toward the sleeping Ostrick, and when he spoke again his voice was lower.

"Why does the army want this clock running, Lieutenant?" Coral asked, before he could decide whether to say whatever he was deciding. "Trains run on stationmasters' watches. Tides don't consult anybody. Your mobilization has bugles. What does Vellbruck need a tower bell for?"

For a heartbeat Coral thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "Punctuality is morale," in the tone of a man reciting, and Coral heard the seam in it, the place where the memorized words ended and the truth began.

"And past that?"

"Past that—" He stopped himself. His hands came back behind his back. "Repair your escapement, apprentice. Two days."

He walked to the door and stood there a moment, looking at the sleeping Ostrick with something like envy, and then he was gone down the spiral stair, boots ringing on stone.

Coral looked down at the margin sketch. The pin wheel stared up at them, ninety-nine blank positions, waiting to be told what to remember.

* * *

That evening Marta brought supper up to the tower room — barley soup, brown bread, and a wedge of cheese she claimed was for the clock, since everything else in Vellbruck had been requisitioned and she refused to let the army have her humor too.

Ostrick had been relieved at dusk, replaced by a younger soldier whose name nobody had bothered to learn and whose attention was fixed entirely on a letter from home, so Marta and Coral could talk in the low shorthand they'd developed over seventeen years.

"Well?" she said. "How goes the surgery?"

"Pallets dressed. New stones ordered from the drawer stock. Drop evened." Coral tore the bread. "Two days, they gave me."

"Halloway says they'd give a man two days to raise a cathedral if it suited the timetable." Marta settled onto the crate she always claimed was bad for her back and had occupied daily for a decade. "He's been strange today. Stranger."

"How so?"

"He went into the archive room — what's left of it, poor stripped thing — and just stood there. An hour, maybe. Then he came out and started polishing silver that hasn't been polished since the Kaiser's birthday parade, and when I asked him why, he said, 'Because if they take everything else, let them at least take it clean.'" She shook her head. "Your master polishes when he's grieving. I've fed him thirty years, Coral. I know his weather."

Coral chewed slowly. The lie between them still sat there, unhealed — *I removed pages, I did it to protect someone, ask me no more* — and Coral had been carrying it like a splinter worked deep under the skin. But Marta's report rearranged something. Grief wasn't the weather of guilt. Guilt was colder, tidier. Grief was silver polish at midnight.

Maybe Halloway hadn't lied about protecting someone. Maybe he'd only lied about being able to bear talking about it.

"Marta," Coral said. "When I was brought to the observatory. Do you remember anything? Anything at all?"

Marta's hands went still on her apron. Outside, far below, the army's new fences caught the last light, wire turning briefly to gold before it went back to being wire.

"I remember the lantern," she said softly. "Halloway lit the tower lantern, the way he did for every foundling arrival, so whoever left the child would know the door was warm. Only nobody came back for you. Nobody ever came back." She reached over and gripped Coral's wrist, flour-dusted fingers strong as a clamp spring. "So we came to you instead. That's how I've always reckoned it. Don't you go letting some cut-out page tell you different."

Coral turned their hand over and held hers.

After she left, Coral worked by lamplight until midnight, dressing the last pallet stone, and then — checking first that the young sentry was asleep over his letter — took out the margin sketch and made it real in pencil, proper and precise, dimensioned like a promise.

Ninety-nine pins. One small bell. A count that could be set, changed, spoken.

At the top of the sheet, in letters too small to read across a room, Coral wrote the only annotation that mattered:

*A clock cannot be audited after it has spoken.*

Then they blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the escapement tick — *no, not yet, not yet* — and somewhere below, faint through the floor, came the sound of Halloway's footsteps, pacing, pacing, like a man rehearsing a confession he hadn't yet found the courage to perform.
