## 6. Pages in a Soldier's Pocket

Marta Voss had been feeding soldiers for three weeks, and she had learned their appetites the way she'd once learned her late husband's moods: through the stomach. The ones who missed home ate everything and asked for seconds with their hats in their hands. The ones who liked being soldiers ate quickly and watched the door. And the requisition clerks — those were a category unto themselves. They picked at their food like men doing arithmetic on every bite, and they never once said thank you.

The clerk named Dorn was the worst of them, and also, as it happened, the best.

She'd marked him early. A narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a way of sitting slightly sideways to authority, as though he expected it to turn on him. He was billeted at the inn across from the bakery, and every evening he came to Marta's kitchen for whatever was left of the stew, because the army's own cooks — she had tasted their output, once, out of morbid curiosity — could not make soup that didn't taste like regret.

"You salt this," he'd said the first night, suspicious.

"With my own hand," Marta said. "I know. Scandalous."

He'd come back the second night. By the fifth he was telling her things. Not secrets, exactly. Clerks don't have secrets; they have *inventory*. But inventory, Marta knew, was its own kind of confession.

It was on a Thursday, over the last of the barley soup, that he mentioned the paper.

"Terrible waste in these audits," Dorn said, tearing his bread into pieces with the concentration of a man defusing something. "You'd not believe what gets condemned as surplus. Boxes of it. Records nobody wants."

"Nobody wants records?" Marta kept her voice idle, her eyes on the pot she wasn't really stirring. "I thought you lot came here for nothing else."

"Old records." He shrugged. "Ledgers past their usefulness. The archive boys flag them for disposal, and disposal means—" he made a small gesture, palm down, like smoothing a wrinkle from a tablecloth "—the fire, usually. Though some of it's good stock, mind. Rag paper. Fine rag paper." He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face she'd seen before on the faces of men selling things they shouldn't. "There's a fellow down by the freight yard sells it off as scrap. Bookbinder's stock. You'd be surprised who buys it."

"Would I," said Marta.

* * *

She told herself she was going to the freight yard to price paper. She was a practical woman; the observatory went through wrapping paper the way the master clock went through oil, and if a soldier wanted to sell good rag stock cheap, then patriotism could wait until after market day. That was the story she gave herself, walking down through the town with her shawl pulled tight against the morning wind, past the fences the army was putting up along the lower districts — white posts and wire going in neat as teeth, sectioning off streets where people had lived since before anyone alive could remember.

Frau Ellmann stood at her gate watching the surveyors. She was eighty-one and had been born in the house behind her, which the new maps now labeled Sector Four, Temporary Use.

"They measured my parlor," Frau Ellmann said as Marta passed. "My *parlor*, Marta. With little wheels on sticks."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the floor was uneven. It isn't. Let them compensate for it." Her chin lifted. "I'm a widow of the observatory rolls. My name is in that book of theirs. Whatever else happens, they wrote me down themselves, so they can't pretend I was never here."

Marta squeezed her hand and walked on, and did not say what was sitting cold in her chest, which was that the book had already gone, carried out under a corporal's arm like a stolen ham, and that nobody — not one soul in this town — knew anymore exactly who was written in it or who had been left out.

* * *

The freight yard smelled of coal and horse and the particular sourness of men who work outdoors but resent it. The scrap seller had set up at the end of a siding, behind a trestle table loaded with the army's idea of rubbish: broken crates, coils of rusted wire, canvas with more holes than cloth. And at the near corner of the table, tied in bundles with string, paper.

Marta drifted toward it the way she'd learned to drift toward anything in a market — slowly, hands visible, interest mild. The seller was a private with a red nose and the eyes of a man perpetually calculating how much trouble he was in versus how much money he might still make.

"Rag stock," he said, seeing her look. "Good weight. Condemned archive material. Five pfennig the bundle, ten for three."

"Condemned from where?"

"From the audit." He said it grandly, as though he personally had audited something. "Official disposal. Perfectly legal." Which, Marta thought, was exactly what a man said when it wasn't.

She untied the first bundle and her heart stopped before her hands did.

She knew that paper. She had dusted around that paper for twenty years, in the observatory's record room, in the long cabinet beneath the window where Master Halloway kept the name-ledger. She knew the faint watermark of the Vellbruck mill that had closed when she was a girl. She knew the smell of it, old glue and stone air. These were pages of the ledger. Loose. Sold for five pfennig the bundle to be cut into patterns and wrapped around cheese.

"Something wrong?" the private asked.

"Paper's foxed," Marta said, which was true, and let her frown cover everything else. "Half of these have got writing on the back."

"Scrap is scrap."

"Mm." She turned a page over, careful as handling an egg. Columns of names. Intake dates. A child's entry, marked *foundling, female, aged approx. 2* — and the name field blank, blank, blank, as if whoever wrote it had stopped mid-thought forever. Another page: debts recorded against the observatory's charity fund in a hand she recognized as Halloway's younger self, all sharp angles and certainty. Another: a list of workers pensioned after the boiler accident of '98, each name followed by a small sum and the word *paid*, except the last line, where the word was *owed*, and someone had drawn a line through the whole entry.

Her hands were steady. Marta noted, distantly, that her hands were always steadiest when her heart was loudest.

"How much for these three?" she said.

"Those are mixed lots—"

"*How much.*"

He looked at her face and made a rapid internal calculation, and to his credit arrived at the correct answer. "Two marks."

Marta paid him two marks, folded the three pages into oilcloth, and put them inside her bodice, against her heart, where they felt less like paper than like birds.

* * *

She climbed the observatory hill slower than she needed to, giving herself time to think, and finding that thinking refused to cooperate. Because there were only two possibilities, and both of them hurt. Either the ledger had been gutted *now*, during this requisition — in which case the army was destroying the town's memory piece by piece while everyone watched the clock tower instead — or it had been gutted long ago, and Halloway had known. Had sat across from Coral at supper for years, knowing pages were missing, saying nothing.

She found Coral in the workshop, sleeves rolled, bent over the escapement with a jeweler's glass screwed into one eye and Lieutenant Brenner standing at a polite distance, watching the way other men watch a card game they haven't been allowed to join. Marta knocked on the doorframe anyway, though the door was open. Old habits. Some doors you knock on even open, to give people warning.

"Coral. A word, when your audience permits."

Brenner glanced between them, inclined his head with surprising grace, and withdrew to inspect the corridor, which contained nothing whatsoever worth inspecting. Marta waited until his boots faded.

"Give me your hands," she said.

"I'm holding a screwdriver."

"Then hold this instead." She pressed the oilcloth bundle into Coral's palm and watched their face as they unwrapped it — watched the craft-trained fingers go gentle, go slow, go still.

"That's ledger paper," Coral said quietly. "That's *our* paper. Where—"

"The freight yard. A private is selling the archive off as scrap, page by page, and Dorn the clerk knows about it and eats my soup and says nothing, and I bought back three pages for two marks, and Coral—" Marta heard her own voice fray and steadied it the way she steadied gravy, with patience and heat applied gradually. "Look at the dates on them. Look at the edges."

Coral looked. Coral was better at reading objects than people — had been raised among gears, spoke fluent mechanism — and Marta watched them do what they always did, which was turn a thing over until it confessed. They held the pages to the light. They examined the cut edges with a loupe borrowed from the bench.

"These cuts are old," they said at last. Their voice had gone strange and flat. "The fibers are oxidized. Glue residue along the gutter margin — this matches binding repair, but the adhesive has yellowed. Fifteen years, maybe more. This wasn't done last month when the soldiers took the ledger. This was done—" they stopped.

"Say it," Marta said gently.

"This was done before I understood what a ledger was." Coral laid the pages flat on the bench, aligning their edges with the unconscious precision of their trade. One bore the date of Coral's own intake — Marta saw them touch it, just once, with one fingertip, like touching a stove to check if it was hot. "The name field is blank. But the intake date is mine. Marta, this is my page. This is my page and someone tore it out before I could read."

"And Halloway told us he removed pages once," Marta said. "To protect someone. He wouldn't say whom."

"He let me believe—" Coral's jaw worked. "He let me believe the gap in the binding was *his*. His lie, his knife, his choice to make on my behalf. And all the while the army had already been through this book years ago, cutting out whatever it didn't want remembered, and he—" They stopped again, and Marta watched something move behind their eyes, some large machinery shifting gear. "He was hiding the wound, not making it. He stitched the binding closed over a hole someone else had cut, and then he couldn't bear to tell me the hole was there."

"Or he was ashamed," Marta said. "Ashamed that the town's own keeper let the army's scissors in without a fight, back when fighting cost more than it does now. Men like Halloway would rather be thought a betrayer than a coward."

"He's neither." Coral said it fast, and then slower, testing the words like a repaired tooth. "He's a man who keeps time. And time, Marta — time doesn't erase anything. It only moves things out of sight." They gathered the pages up with both hands, careful, careful. "These aren't the only loose ones. If one private is selling scrap, there's a whole archive being condemned somewhere upstairs in this mobilization, and every page in it is somebody's name."

"So we buy them back," Marta said. "Bit by bit. Soup for information, marks for pages. I've fed worse men for less."

"No." Coral was looking at the escapement on the bench, at the half-built second train hidden in plain sight among the parts, and Marta saw the moment the plan became load-bearing, the moment it stopped being an idea and started being a machine. "We buy them back, yes. All of them. Every page. And then we put the names back where no audit can reach them."

"In the binding?"

"Somewhere better," said Coral, and smiled, and it was not entirely a comfortable smile. "But first — bring me Dorn. Bring me the clerk who eats your soup and knows where the rest of the condemned archive sits. Because there's a page missing from these three, Marta, and I think I know which one, and I want to know who's been carrying it in a soldier's pocket."

Marta looked at the young clockmaker — at the foundling with the blank name field, holding three scraps of paper like they weighed more than the tower — and went to fetch the soup pot.

Some negotiations, she had found, went better with bread.
