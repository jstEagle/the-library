## 7. Halloway's Confession

The mended pages sat between us on the workbench like a confession waiting to be spoken.

Marta had brought them up at half past six, still warm from being carried against her apron, and now they lay in the lamplight where I'd smoothed them flat: three leaves of the name-ledger, torn cleanly at the gutter, the army clerk's pencil marks still visible along one margin — *scrap, 4 dozen, assorted* — as though ninety years of Vellbruck's belonging could be sold by the pound.

I had read them four times. The third page bore my intake date. The fourteenth of October. A cold Tuesday, Halloway always said, the first frost coming early that year. And where a name should have been, there was nothing. Not a smudge, not a water stain. The field was blank in the way paper is blank when someone has deliberately scraped it — you can feel the ghost of the ink if you know how to listen with your fingertips, and I did. I'd been taught by the man who was now refusing to look at me across his own workbench.

"Master," I said. "The binding glue matched fibers from fifteen years ago. You told me that yourself, when I asked what repairs you'd made to the ledger."

"I said no such thing." He was polishing a pallet stone he had already polished twice.

"You said, *the binding wanted attention.* That is a clockmaker's answer to a question nobody asked."

That earned me a flicker of his eyes — the old irritation, which I found, strangely, comforting. Irritation meant he was still speaking to me. It was the silences I had learned to dread.

"Marta bought these back from a soldier for two hens and a promise of plum jam," I went on. "The army cut them out during this audit, yes. But Master — the page with my date has been scraped. Not torn out. Scraped clean and left in place, and then the whole gathering was removed later and the binding repaired over the wound. Two different hands. Two different times. Yours, and theirs."

The polishing cloth stopped moving.

He set down the pallet stone with the exaggerated care of a man handling something explosive, and for a long moment the only sound in the workshop was the master clock overhead, ticking through the tower wall like a patient heart. Then he pulled off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and he looked suddenly every year of his age, and several he hadn't earned honestly.

"Lock the door," he said.

* * *

He didn't tell it standing at the bench like a craftsman explaining a mechanism. He told it sitting down, on the old stool by the window, with his hands folded in his lap like a schoolboy called before the magistrate, and that inversion — Halloway small, Halloway summoned — frightened me more than anything he might have said.

"The year you came," he began, "Vellbruck belonged to the Ashcombe Consolidated. Textiles, timber, the freight line. Old Ashcombe liked to call himself the town's father, which meant the rest of us were children to be disciplined. There was a mill on the river side — Corvane Mill. You've seen the foundations; they flood every spring."

"The one behind the tannery ruins."

"The same. In September of your birth-year, the second-floor drive shaft went. Bad steel, purchased cheap, inspected by a man who signed the inspection without climbing the stairs. Forty-one people were on that floor when it let go." His voice did something I had never heard it do — it thinned, and cracked at the seam, and continued anyway. "Nineteen died. Eleven of them women who worked the spinning frames. Two of them were a young couple married eight months. He fed the boiler. She ran the third spindle from the window."

My hands had gone still in my lap. I am not a person given to stillness; my fingers are always finding some small work, a screw, a thread, the edge of a cuff. But they stopped, because somewhere beneath my ribs a very old and wordless thing had lifted its head.

"The owners paid for funerals," Halloway said. "Quick ones. They paid the newspaper to print *boiler accident, regrettable, safety standards exceeded all requirements* — a sentence I have hated for fifteen years, because I wrote the inspection schedule myself and knew the shaft had been flagged twice. And then they paid for silence, and silence was cheap, because grief makes people tired."

"And the ledger—"

"The ledger was mine to keep. Foundlings, widows, workers, debts of kindness — everything that made Vellbruck a town instead of a workforce. When the company's auditors came through — this was their habit, every few years, an 'audit' exactly like the one sitting in our square right now — they wanted the ledger too. Names are leverage, Coral. A widow who can prove her husband died in their mill can prove she is owed a pension. A child whose parents are named in a disaster can grow up asking questions. Ashcombe's men went through my ledger page by page with a list, and they struck out every name connected to Corvane."

"But mine wasn't struck out. Mine was scraped."

"Because yours wasn't there yet." He looked at me then, fully, and his eyes were wet and furious and gentle all at once, which is a combination I have only ever seen in him. "You arrived the following month. Left at my door in a fish crate lined with a blanket, with a note pinned to your wrappings. I have that note, Coral. I have kept it in the strongbox under the floor for fifteen years. It named your parents. Both of them. It named Corvane Mill. It asked — whoever found you — to see that you were *loved and unclaimed*, those words, in a woman's hand. *Loved and unclaimed.*"

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below, Marta was banking the kitchen fire, and I could hear her moving through the house with the deliberate loudness of a woman making certain we remembered she was near.

"Do you understand what I saw?" Halloway said. "A baby with a name that would tie them to nineteen graves and a company that buried its dead like evidence. If I entered you in the ledger under your parents' names, the next audit would find you. Strike you. Or worse — flag you, file you, make you a claim against Ashcombe before you could walk. Companies do not fear orphans, Coral. They fear orphans with documents. So I did the only protection I had. I scraped the entry blank. I left you nameless in the record but named in my heart, and I raised you myself, and every year when the auditors came I stood in front of that book like a wall stands in front of a door, and I lied to them, and I lied to you, and I have hated every day of both."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Why not tell me? Even without the note — why let me spend fifteen years thinking I was nobody's?"

"Because knowing is a weight," he said simply, "and I judged you weren't strong enough to carry it. Which was arrogance. I see that now. I decided what you could bear the same way the company decided what the town could bear." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "We become what we guard against, given time."

I wanted to be angry. I reached for anger the way I reach for a tool — it should have been there, in its usual place. But what I found instead was the memory of a hundred mornings: him teaching me to true a wheel, humming off-key; the lantern he lit in the tower window whenever a foundling arrived, so the town would know to bring clothes, milk, a cradle; the way he'd stood in the square last week arguing with soldiers until his voice gave out, over a book that contained, among other things, the proof of what he'd done for me.

"You hid me inside the one place they trusted you to keep," I said slowly. "The ledger itself. You made me a blank page."

"A page with no name can't be erased," he said. "It took me years to understand that was what I'd done. I thought I'd hidden you. Actually, I made you invisible to them and unavoidable to me." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way he brushed filings from a plate. "And then I got old, and cowardly, and I let the silence calcify until it seemed kinder than the truth. That part has no defense. Only an apology, which I owe you, and which you may collect with interest whenever you like."

"Plum jam," I said. "Marta says interest is payable in plum jam."

He laughed — a wet, broken laugh, but a real one — and something in the room that had been bent since the soldiers came finally straightened.

* * *

We worked late into the night, but not at the bench.

Halloway cleared the tea things off the long table and spread out what we had: Marta's three recovered pages, his own memory, a stack of fresh paper, and the stub of a pencil he sharpened with the seriousness other men reserve for surgery. The plan assembled itself the way a movement does — one wheel at a time, each part tested against the last.

"The archive ships in nine days," he said. "Whatever the army takes to the provincial depot, we will never see again. So everything missing must be rebuilt here, in nine days, from the only source they cannot requisition." He tapped his own temple. "People."

"Ninety-nine entries were struck or removed. I counted the stitch holes in the binding."

"Ninety-nine. Widows, foundlings, pensioners, the Corvane dead themselves — the company even struck *them*, did you know? As if dying inconveniently were a breach of contract." He wrote *99* at the top of the sheet and underlined it twice. "Marta knows half the widows personally; she'll gather testimony under cover of trading jam and bread, which no soldier thinks to censor. Old Pieter Kess kept the mill pay-rolls in his head until the day they carried him off — but his daughter Anke has his notebooks, if she'll give them up, and she will, if you ask her about her father rather than about the mill. The midwife Frau Ostrow baptized half the foundlings; her memory is better than her eyesight, thank heaven. And the church register—"

"—was copied from the ledger," I finished. "Father Ilm said once the ledger was more accurate than his own books and he resented it."

"He flattered us. But copies survive, Coral. That's the whole secret. Erasure only works on originals. A name written in nine places in nine hands is a name with nine lives." He looked up at me, and the lamplight caught the wet tracks still drying on his face, and he said, quietly, "Your parents' names go first. Whatever else we fail to recover, that page gets rebuilt tonight."

So I sat, and he dictated, and in my own hand — the hand he had trained, letter by letter, at this same table — I wrote into a fresh page ruled to match the ledger:

*Ashgrove entry, 14th of October.* And beneath it, for the first time in fifteen years, spoken aloud in that room: my mother's name, my father's name, the mill, the collapse, the note that said *loved and unclaimed.*

Unclaimed. I set down the pencil.

"No," I said. "That's wrong now."

Halloway looked up.

"It said *loved and unclaimed,*" I said. "But I'm claiming them. Tonight. In ink. And tomorrow—" I nodded toward the window, toward the dark square where the army's fence posts stood like bad teeth, "—we start collecting the other ninety-eight. Before they can ship a single page out of this town."

From the kitchen below came the sound of Marta setting the big kettle on the stove, early and deliberately, the way she did when the household was about to stop sleeping.

She already knew.
