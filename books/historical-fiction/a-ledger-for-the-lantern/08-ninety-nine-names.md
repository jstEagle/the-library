## 8. Ninety-Nine Names

The first name came in at nine minutes past ten that evening, carried up the observatory hill by Marta herself in a cloud of flour and triumph.

"Old Piet Voss," she announced, setting down a tray of seed buns as though it were a crown on a cushion. "Signed his testimony with an X, because his eyes have gone soft, but he made me witness the X and he pressed his thumb beside it like a wax seal. Ninety years old and he remembers to the *day* when Widow Kessel's roof was mended by the observatory fund — because he was on the ladder and dropped a shingle on the foreman's hat."

Coral took the paper. The thumbprint was enormous, blue-black from Piet's homemade ink, and slightly crooked, as if even his thumb had opinions about where things belonged.

"Name, date, what the ledger owed him," Coral said, reading aloud. "One winter of coal, 1899, paid in full." They looked up. "That's one."

"Eighty-eight to go," said Halloway from the corner, not looking up from the intake register he was reconstructing from memory, his pen scratching like a beetle in matchwood.

"Ninety-eight," Coral corrected. "We have eleven already. The baker's widow brought three this afternoon, and Tam Linnet remembered four foundlings off by heart, including one he claims bit him."

"He deserved it," Marta said. "Tam Linnet has always been biteable."

* * *

By the second night, the kitchen of the observatory had become something no army manual had a category for: a counting-house of the uncounted.

They came in ones and twos, past the sentry at the fence line who had learned — through Marta's agency and her seed buns' diplomacy — that the path to the cook's door was "for deliveries only," which was technically true, since nearly everyone arrived carrying something. A jar of plums. A bundle of candle-ends. A memory wrapped in a handkerchief.

Marta kept the kettle going and the talk flowing, because she understood what Halloway did not, that testimony is a shy animal and will not come if you stare at it. She fed people first. She asked after their knees, their grandchildren, their grievances against the price of salt. And somewhere between the second cup and the third grievance, the memories came out into the light, blinking, and were written down.

Coral sat at the workbench with the ledger's binding open before them like a patient on the table, transcribing. They had developed a system: each name entered twice, once in ink on paper, once in pencil on the master list hidden inside a disassembled fusee barrel, because Coral trusted no container the army could inventory. The names went down in their best hand — the hand Halloway had drilled into them for fifteen years, the hand that had labeled every drawer in the workshop — and each entry got its small proof. Birthmark above the left knee. Debt of two crowns forgiven at Christmas 1904. The scar from the shingle.

It struck Coral, somewhere past midnight of the second night, that they were doing clockwork in reverse. A mechanism takes many small parts and makes them one motion. This was taking one motion — ninety-nine lives that had moved together through the town's days — and proving every small part of it, gear by gear, tooth by tooth.

"You're smiling," Halloway observed without looking up.

"I'm working."

"You're smiling *and* working. I've seen you do both before, but rarely at once, and never this late. It worries me."

"It shouldn't." Coral dipped the pen. "I found out today that Frau Adler remembers being paid in buttons. Actual buttons, from the uniform factory, during the hard winter. Seventy-two buttons for a month's washing. She kept them all and sewed them onto her son's coat, and when the audit clerk asked how she supported herself she opened the coat and said, 'With distinction.'"

Halloway laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in weeks — and then pressed his lips shut as if laughing were also contraband.

"They can take the ledger," Coral said quietly. "They can't take the coat."

* * *

The soldiers accelerated.

Coral noticed it the way one notices a clock running fast — not all at once, but in accumulated seconds. On Monday, two crates of tools went down the hill. By Wednesday, it was six crates, and the lathe was crated whole, and Lieutenant Brenner stood in the doorway with a clipboard ticking off items while Corporal Weiss wrapped Coral's burnishing wheel in straw like a thing being prepared for burial rather than transport.

"They want the workshop clear by Friday week," Brenner said. His voice was flat, official, and his eyes were doing something else entirely, tracking the kitchen door, the comings and goings, the smell of too much bread baking for too few mouths.

"So I've been told," Coral said, fitting a pallet arbor into its jewel, hands steady, heart conducting its own unscheduled count.

"The requisition order specifies records and materiel." Brenner turned a page on his clipboard. "It does not specify... visitors."

"No," Coral agreed. "I'd noticed that omission myself."

Brenner looked at them then, directly, for one long second, and Coral read him the way they read mechanisms — the mainspring of ambition wound tight, the escapement of conscience catching, releasing, catching. He wrote something on the clipboard that Coral was fairly sure was not true, and left.

The moment the gate clanged, Halloway appeared in the doorway. "He knows."

"He suspects. There's a difference. Suspecting is what springs do before they're set."

"And when they're set?"

Coral looked at the fusee barrel on the shelf, fat with its secret list. "Then we'd better be finished."

* * *

On the fourth night, the testimonies stopped coming uphill, so Coral and Marta went downhill to them.

Vellbruck under occupation was a town holding its breath, and yet the breath kept escaping in useful ways. In the back room of the chandler's shop, seven retired observatory workers sat around a lamp and argued — warmly, viciously, with forty years of practice — over exactly who had carried the lantern on the night the twins were left at the gate in 1887. Each man insisted it was himself. None would yield. It was finally settled by Frau Adler, present as witness, who pointed out that all three claimants had been too drunk to walk straight that night, and that it had in fact been her late husband, whose boots they were all still describing accurately because they had spent the walk home staring at them.

"That's testimony," Marta said, wiping her eyes. "Four witnesses, one pair of boots, unanimous on the important part."

The important part. Coral wrote it down and thought: *that is what the ledger always was.* Not a record of debts and dates, though it held those. It was the town agreeing, page after page, on the important part — that these people belonged here, that someone had lit a lantern for them, that their boots had been seen.

At the widow Kessel's house, a girl of perhaps ten named Liesel waited on the step with a folded paper she thrust at Coral and immediately regretted. "Grandmother says I'm to give you this and not to tell anyone and also that you're to stay for supper, which is contradictory, but she's my grandmother and I've learned not to argue."

Inside was a lock of hair, gray, tied with thread, and a note in a shaking hand: *For the foundling pages, so they know we kept tokens too. Every child had a token in the box under my bed. Ask me before Friday.*

Coral stood very still on the step.

There had been a box. There had been tokens. Which meant there had been a ritual, a keeping, a whole architecture of belonging that the ledger had merely indexed — and none of it required paper at all.

"Marta," Coral said that night, back at the bench, holding the lock of hair, "how many tokens do you think are still in Vellbruck?"

"How many grandmothers?" Marta countered. "Dozens. How many boxes under beds?" She smiled slowly. "More than the army has crates."

* * *

They were caught on the sixth night.

Coral knew it before the door opened — the particular quality of silence that means someone is standing in it, the way a pendulum registers a fingerprint on the case. They slid the fusee barrel under a rag with the ease of long practice and turned around.

Lieutenant Brenner stood in the workshop, alone, cap in his hands. Behind him, through the window, the sentry walked his post, looking anywhere but here, which meant he had been paid or persuaded or simply possessed of excellent peripheral vision.

"Corporal Weiss reported irregular foot traffic," Brenner said. "Deliveries after dark. I was ordered to investigate." He paused. "I investigated."

"And?"

"And I found a clockmaker repairing a clock, a list of spare parts, and a great deal of tea." He set his cap down on the bench, which was either surrender or the beginning of a speech; with officers it was often both.

Coral did not move toward the rag. "You could report more than that."

"Yes." Brenner picked up a stray gear train component — part of the secondary chime mechanism, its purpose disguised as a decorative repeat-strike assembly, badly, because Coral had disguised it in a hurry — and turned it over in his fingers. "This isn't decorative."

"It strikes any sequence you ask it to," Coral said. There seemed little point in lying now, and some obscure satisfaction in not doing so. "Any sequence. As long or as short as required."

Brenner was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the sentry's boots crunched through their round. Somewhere below, the town's few remaining lamps made a constellation of the streets.

"When I was a boy in Lorrach," Brenner said finally, "my mother worked the mill. When the mill changed owners, the new owners burned the wage books. All of them. Eleven years of her labor became a rumor." He set the component down with exaggerated care, aligning it precisely where it had lain. "She talked about those books until she died. Not the wages. The *books*. The proof."

"Lieutenant—"

"I have a copy of the final missing intake page," he said. "March 1898. It was removed during the previous audit — before your time, before the old man's, by a clerk named Vogel who sold such things to officers who wanted leverage on the observatory's funding disputes. I bought it four years ago because I intended to use it against Director Kranz. I am given to understand," and here his voice went dry as old glue, "that Director Kranz authored the current requisition, and that the archive ships Monday."

Coral's hands had gone cold around nothing.

"Where is it?"

Brenner reached inside his tunic and produced an envelope, thin, soft-cornered, carried long enough to be worn smooth as a river stone. He held it out — then, at the last moment, drew it back an inch.

"One condition. Not for me." He nodded at the window, at the dark hillside, at the whole breathing town. "Whatever this mechanism does — whatever you intend it to strike — it had better be loud enough that no one in that inspection hall can pretend afterward they didn't hear it."

Coral took the envelope. Inside, behind glassine gone brittle, was a single yellowed page. An intake date: March 1898. A note in a stranger's cramped hand, the words Coral had spent a lifetime not knowing. And at the bottom, in the column for names, the blank space that had shaped an entire childhood — and, beside it now, in Brenner's own careful pencil, a single word added:

*Coral.*

Not the name they'd been given. The name they'd chosen, written into the record by the last person Coral would ever have predicted.

"Why that name?" Coral managed.

"Because it's the one the whole town used when I asked around this week," Brenner said, retrieving his cap. "I did my investigating thoroughly." At the door he paused, backlit by the lamp, suddenly young. "Monday noon, clockmaker. Make it loud."

The door closed. Coral stood holding the last page of themselves while upstairs, faint through the floorboards, they heard Halloway's pen stop scratching — and start again, faster, as if he too had heard the future change weight.

Eighty-nine names confirmed. Ten to go. Four days.

And a clock that had never yet struck anything but hours, waiting to learn a new language.
