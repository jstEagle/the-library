## 9. The Strike

I finished the last wheel at half past four in the morning, and my hands were shaking — not from fear, though there was plenty of that folded neatly underneath everything else like a spare mainspring, but from the sheer animal fact of having been awake for thirty-one hours.

The workshop was half a skeleton by then. The army's movers had come through two days prior with crates and straw packing and the cheerful efficiency of men who did not know what they were carrying. The lathe was gone. Halloway's tool cabinet, forty years of gravers and broaches in their felt-lined drawers, had been inventoried and nailed shut. What remained was the great clock itself, bolted to the tower wall like a heart that refused to be transplanted, and my modification: the secondary chime train I'd spent nine nights building from scrap brass, a broken music-box comb, and one of Marta's knitting needles, which she had donated with the solemnity of a woman lending her own bone.

It sat now behind the strike train, small and ugly and true, a mechanism no inspector would think to look at because it looked like nothing — a repair patch, an afterthought, a bit of apprentice cleverness. Which is exactly what it was, except for what it said.

Each name, one strike. Ninety-nine strikes at noon instead of twelve. The sequence locked into the pinned drum I'd cut myself, working by lantern light while soldiers slept off their supper two floors below. Every pin placed by hand. Every pin a person.

* * *

Marta arrived at five with coffee strong enough to strip varnish and a face arranged into its most dangerous calm.

"You look terrible," she said, setting the pot on the one crate they hadn't taken.

"I feel wonderful."

"Liar." She looked up at the clock, at the little brass train crouched behind the great wheels like a secret kept in plain sight, and her voice dropped. "Does it work?"

"It worked at three o'clock this morning. Quietly. With the dampers on." I wrapped my hands around the cup. "At noon it won't be quiet."

"Good," she said. Then, because Marta could never let tenderness pass without disguising it: "You've got grease on your chin. You've had grease on your chin since Tuesday. I've decided to stop mentioning it as a kindness."

* * *

The officers came at eleven.

They came up the tower stair in a clatter of boots — Colonel Vasch at the front, gray and neat, the kind of man who ironed his collar even for a mountain town; two majors behind him; and Lieutenant Brenner last, carrying the inspection ledger under his arm, his face doing something careful and unreadable that I had learned, over these weeks, to read anyway.

Colonel Vasch inspected the way he did everything: quickly, loudly, and with the assumption of ownership. He rapped the clock case with a knuckle. He asked me questions about the escapement and listened to perhaps a quarter of each answer. He remarked that Vellbruck would be proud to have its timekeeping certified fit for military coordination, as if the clock had been built in 1913 rather than 1781, and as if we should be grateful.

"The mechanism is sound?" he demanded.

"Sounder than it has been in twenty years," I said. Which was true, and also the whole point.

Halloway stood by the case, hands folded, wearing his good coat that smelled of camphor. He had not slept either. Neither of us had spoken much since dawn. There was nothing left to plan; there was only the waiting, which is the hardest gear in any machine because you cannot oil it.

Brenner caught my eye over the colonel's shoulder. One glance at the case. One almost imperceptible nod. And then he looked away and became a bored junior officer again so thoroughly that I nearly applauded.

* * *

Noon came the way noon always comes in a clocktower — not all at once but gathering, the minute hand climbing like something wading upstream.

The officers stood in a semicircle. Below us, through the open louvres, I could see the square: townspeople gathered in the cold, more than the inspection required, more than the army liked. Marta had told everyone. Of course she had. Half of Vellbruck stood down there pretending to admire the architecture, and among them, I knew, were the widows and foundlings and retired workers whose names waited in brass pins above my head.

The minute hand touched the hour.

The gravity arm released.

The first blow fell — deep, bronze, enormous, rolling out over the rooftops and down into the valley where the requisitioned trains waited on sidings like patient animals.

One.

The colonel checked his watch. Two. Three.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Seven.

His head came up slowly, the way a man's head comes up when the ground has begun to disagree with him.

Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

Twelve — and did not stop. Thirteen struck full and round, fourteen, fifteen, and the sound went on and on, each blow distinct, unhurried, deliberate as a clerk reading a roll, and below us in the square I saw the crowd go still and then begin — I heard it even through stone — to count along. Old Frau Imke counting on her fingers in the doorway of the bakery. Dockhands taking off their caps. Children who didn't understand yet, counting anyway, because counting is contagious.

Twenty. Thirty. Forty.

"What," said Colonel Vasch, very quietly, "is happening."

"Sir," I said, "the clock is striking."

"I can hear that."

"Then I'm not sure what else to tell you." My voice only shook a little. "Every chime is a name. Ninety-nine names from the observatory ledger — the record your audit seized. The town is counting them. They'll have them all memorized by nightfall."

Fifty-one. Fifty-two. The sound filled the tower the way water fills a lock, rising, lifting everything in it. Halloway had closed his eyes. His lips were moving slightly, and I realized he was counting too, and that he knew every name, because he had lit a lantern for every single one of them.

Sixty. Seventy. A major said something about sabotage and Vasch raised one finger without looking at him, and the finger stayed raised, because the colonel was doing arithmetic of his own — I could watch him do it. If this was treason, then the whole town standing in the square counting along was treason. If this was a malfunction, then the malfunction knew ninety-nine names. To punish it, he would have to name it. To name it, he would have to admit there had been names to erase. The ledger's blank page sat in some army crate like a wound, and every stroke of the bell pressed on it.

Ninety. Ninety-one.

"Ninety-eight," said Halloway aloud, suddenly, to the room — and stepped forward, and in the pause before the final blow he said, clear and cracked and unashamed:

"Coral Ashgrove. Taken in at this observatory, the fourth of March, 1898. Keeper's own."

Ninety-nine.

The bell fell silent, and the silence afterward was the largest thing in the mountains.

Down in the square, nobody moved for a long moment. Then old Frau Imke began, softly, to sing — some old foundling-lantern song I hadn't known the town still remembered — and the sound came up through the stonework and into the tower and into the clock, and I swear on Halloway's tools that the pendulum seemed to swing easier for it.

Colonel Vasch looked at the clock. At me. At Halloway. At Brenner, who wore the expression of a man watching weather he had personally forecast.

"Log the mechanism as repaired," the colonel said at last, to his majors, in a voice sanded flat. "Note an anomaly in the strike train. Recommend... further study." He put his hat on. At the door he paused. "You understand, horologist, that anomalies get dismantled."

"With respect, sir," I said, "you'd have to dismantle the whole town to reach it."

He left. The boots went down the stairs. Brenner went last, and at the landing he turned, just briefly, and tapped two fingers against his breast pocket — where, I understood, a certain removed page had traveled for many weeks, waiting.

Below, the singing grew.

Halloway put his hand on the clock case the way you touch the shoulder of someone who has done a hard thing well.

"Well," he said. "Now we put the pages back."
