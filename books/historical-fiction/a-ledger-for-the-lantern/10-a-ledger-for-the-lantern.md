## 10. A Ledger for the Lantern

The army took three days to leave, which was two days longer than it took them to arrive and considerably less dignified.

Coral watched from the observatory steps as the last of the lorries ground down the mountain road, their canvas backs flapping like sulking birds. A private had dropped a crate of ration tins on the turn and hadn't bothered to pick them up; Marta was already down there with a handcart, conducting what she called "the town's reparations" and what everyone else correctly understood as Marta getting free peaches.

"They left in such a hurry," she said on her return, setting the tins on the kitchen table with a thump that rattled the teacups, "that Lieutenant Brenner forgot his gloves. Again."

"He didn't forget anything," Coral said. "He's left something here every week since he arrived. I think he's trying to pay us back without anyone noticing."

"Then he's a poor accountant and a decent man, which is the best combination an officer can manage." Marta peeled off her apron. "The archive wagon's at the square. They're unloading it now. Halloway sent word — he wants you there when they hand it over."

* * *

The requisition clerk who had sold pages as scrap stood at the tailboard of the wagon with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and realizing attendance was mandatory. He handed the ledgers across one at a time, checking each against his manifest, and when he reached the great name-ledger of Vellbruck observatory — its binding scarred, its spine re-sewn twice, once by Halloway and once by whoever had come before him — he held it a moment too long.

"There's a page missing," he said. "Numbered forty-one."

"There are four pages missing," said Halloway, who had come down from the tower for the occasion and stood in his good coat with his hands folded like a bishop at a christening. "Numbers forty-one through forty-four, removed during the audit of 1898 by your predecessor's predecessor's office. The damage is documented in the binding itself. You may note that in your report."

The clerk noted it. His pen scratched. Somewhere behind him, a soldier sneezed, and half the gathered townspeople said "bless you" in unison, which was not kindness so much as habit — Vellbruck blessed everything, including the weather, the trains, and once, memorably, a runaway goat.

Coral took the ledger into their arms.

It weighed more than Coral remembered. Or perhaps Coral had simply grown lighter, these past weeks, in some way that had nothing to do with scales. The binding smelled of glue and old iron gall ink and, faintly, of the lantern oil Halloway had spilled on it one winter night fifteen years ago, carrying it up the stairs two steps at a time because somewhere below a baby had been crying.

They carried it home.

* * *

The repair of the ledger took place on the workshop bench, under the lamp, beside the master clock's dismantled escapement — though the escapement was no longer dismantled. It ran now. It had run since noon on inspection day, and it had struck ninety-nine times while a colonel's face performed a series of maneuvers usually reserved for cavalry drills, and no one had been arrested, because there were ninety-nine names and every one of them had witnesses.

Coral laid out the recovered pages in order: three bought back from the scrap-seller, and the fourth returned by Brenner, who had delivered it wrapped in brown paper with no note at all, only his gloves tucked inside as if he still couldn't stop leaving things behind. Pages forty-one through forty-four. Intake records. Foundlings. Widows entered onto pension rolls. Workers whose hands had built the very gears Coral now oiled.

The cut edges were ragged. Whoever had taken them in '98 had been hurried or careless or ashamed — Coral liked to think ashamed. The stubs remaining in the binding were thin but present, and paper remembers the way bone remembers a break: there is always a seam.

"You'll want wheat starch paste," Halloway said. He sat across the bench, ostensibly supervising, actually just watching, which was what he did now instead of evading. "Not the army glue. Never the army glue. That stuff holds forever, which sounds like praise until you need to change your mind."

"When have you ever needed to change your mind about a binding?"

"Once." He didn't elaborate. He didn't have to. Coral sewed.

The needle went through old holes first — the original sewing stations, punched by some keeper decades dead — and then through new ones where the pages had been added. Linen thread, waxed, doubled. Coral worked the way Halloway had taught them: slowly enough to see, quickly enough not to second-guess. Each page settled home with a small sound, like a word being spoken quietly.

Page forty-one bore a date Coral knew better than their own heartbeat, and a name field gone soft with handling, and beneath it, in a hand Coral had never seen before but somehow recognized — the handwriting slanted the same direction theirs did — a line of parentage:

*Of the Ashgrove family, late of the Carding Mill. Lost in the collapse, March the ninth.*

Ashgrove. Not a foundling name assigned by a clerk with a book of respectable options. Their own name, given by people who had loved them long enough to say it aloud.

Coral put down the needle for a moment and pressed both palms flat on the bench until the wood stopped seeming to move.

Halloway said nothing. He slid the tea closer instead, and that was its own sentence, and Coral drank it.

* * *

It took two evenings to finish. On the third morning, Coral turned to the keeper's line at the front of the ledger — the page where, generation after generation, the keeper of the observatory's names signed on, the way a clockmaker signs a movement.

There was Halloway's signature, firm and slightly theatrical, dated thirty-one years back.

Above it, faded almost to rumor, the name of the keeper before him. And above that, others, marching up the page like teeth in a gear train, each one turning the next.

Coral dipped the pen. Their hand hesitated — not from doubt, but from the particular weight of a first signature, which every apprentice knows and no apprentice is warned about.

"What do I write?" they asked. "Just my name?"

Halloway considered. "The line says *keeper*. It doesn't ask what kind." He tapped the margin. "Though I'd add the date. Future auditors appreciate thoroughness. It confuses them wonderfully."

So Coral wrote:

*Coral Ashgrove, keeper of the names. Entered this day by choice, not by chance.*

And underneath, because it seemed right, because the whole town would expect nothing less and Marta would be insufferable about it otherwise: *Apprenticed to E. Halloway, who never once lost me, though he tried to lose a few pages.*

"I will pretend I did not read that," Halloway said.

"You read it."

"I did," he admitted. "It's accurate. Accuracy is the soul of record-keeping."

* * *

That evening, the lantern came down from its hook above the workshop door — the brass lantern, dented on one side, that Halloway had lit on the night a bundle arrived on his step, and had relit on eleven other nights for eleven other arrivals, and had let go dark for fifteen years because there seemed no one left to light it for.

He set it in Coral's hands.

"I'm too old to climb," he said, which was true and also not the reason. "Besides, it isn't mine anymore. It belongs to whoever keeps the door."

Coral trimmed the wick, filled the reservoir, and struck the match. The flame caught, wavered, steadied. They carried it up the tower stairs — ninety-nine steps, someone had counted, and Coral suspected they knew exactly who — and hung it in the window that faced the town.

Below, Vellbruck was lighting its own windows. Word traveled fast in a town that kept time together: the ledger was back, the pages were sewn in, the clock had struck ninety-nine and meant it. Old Widow Ferencz was standing in the square pointing up at the lantern, telling anyone within range — and several people well outside it — that she'd been entered in that book herself in 1881 and had the receipt to prove it. The Kessler twins were arguing about whether the chime sequence could be played as a waltz. (It could. Coral had already worked out the arrangement. Some secrets keep.)

Marta appeared at the tower door with a tray: bread, cheese, the confiscated peaches stewed with more sugar than any army deserved to lose.

"Well," she said, looking out at the town, at the lantern, at the clock face glowing pale above them all. "Ninety-nine names, one clock, one lantern, one keeper." She counted on her fingers. "I make that a complete inventory. Nothing missing."

"Nothing missing," Coral agreed.

But as they ate, listening to the clock strike the hour — twelve honest strokes, plain and public, with the ninety-nine waiting patient in the hidden train like names held in the mouth — Coral noticed something they had missed before, something small and mechanical and impossible.

In the ledger's binding, sewn shut into the spine itself, deeper than any audit would ever cut, there was a pocket. A keeper's trick, older than Halloway. And inside it, folded to the size of a stamp, a slip of paper in handwriting that slanted the same direction theirs did, that began:

*If you are reading this, then someone kept the light burning—*

The clock ticked. The lantern burned. Coral unfolded the note with hands that, for the first time in their life, shook.
