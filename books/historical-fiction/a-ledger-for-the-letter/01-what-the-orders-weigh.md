## 1. What the Orders Weigh

The cold did not come to Ward C all at once; it came the way grief comes, in layers, each one finding a new place to settle. It found the gaps under the door where the wind pushed snow across the duckboards like spilled salt. It found the seams of Tamsin Amberdale's gloves, which had been mended so many times they were more thread than leather. It found the men.

By four o'clock that morning there had been twenty-two of them in Ward C, carried up from the advanced dressing station with mud still on their boots and the particular silence of morphine in their eyes. By seven, when Sister Brightwater's lamp made its slow circuit and stopped at every cot a moment longer than it needed to, there were fifteen. By nine there were six. And by the time the orderlies came through with the stretchers folded flat against their chests like trays at a funeral tea, there were none.

Tamsin stood in the doorway afterward and let the cold have her face. The stoves had gone out twice in the night—the coal ration was a joke told by men who sat in warm offices—and the canvas walls breathed with wind, and the whole ward smelled of carbolic and blood gone cold and the sweetish ghost of chloroform from the theatre next door. Fifteen men. Not wounded-to-die, either, not the ones you marked with the little chalk cross on the forehead because there was nothing left to do but keep them comfortable. These were men who might have lived. Two of them had been walking wounded when they arrived, joking about the food, asking after letters.

She counted the empty cots again, though she already knew the number, though she had known it since before dawn, because counting was the only prayer she had left that still worked.

"Tamsin." Brightwater's voice behind her, low, pitched for her alone. "Reports."

"I know."

"The morning report goes out on the ten o'clock lorry. Matron wants it signed before then."

"I know what time it goes out, Sister."

Brightwater looked at her for a long moment. In the grey light from the doorway her face was all angles and shadow, fifty years old or sixty—no one knew, no one asked—and her eyes held the particular steadiness of a woman who had been at Ypres and had come out the other side of it with something intact that she did not discuss. "Then sign it," she said quietly, "and do whatever else it is you do," and walked on down the line of cots without waiting for an answer.

That was the closest Brightwater had ever come, in eleven months, to saying *I know*.

* * *

The clerical tent was warmer than the wards, which was its own kind of obscenity. A brazier glowed in the corner and the adjutant's clerk, Corporal Fenwick, sat at his desk with his greatcoat over his knees and his pen moving in the quick dry strokes of a man who believed in forms the way some men believed in God. The morning report lay open in front of him, a sheet of flimsy paper ruled into columns: *Rank. Name. Number. Regiment. Nature of wound. Disposition.*

*Disposition.* Died of wounds. Evacuated. Returned to duty. Transferred.

There was no column for what had actually happened in Ward C between midnight and dawn, which was that the coal ran out and the wind found them and death moved down the rows like a matron on rounds, unhurried, thorough, checking names off a list of his own.

"Amberdale." Fenwick didn't look up. "You've got the final count for C?"

"Fifteen dead since last night's return."

He wrote it. She watched the nib travel. Fifteen became a stroke of ink, a single figure in a column, weightless as weather.

"And the disposition—died of wounds, all?"

"Died of wounds," she said, and it was true, in the way that everything in this war was true if you narrowed your eyes enough. They had wounds. They had died of them. What the form did not ask was whether they might have survived their wounds in a clearing station that could be heated, whether the stretcher-bearers had spent forty minutes lost in the dark because the guide-lamps were requisitioned for a staff officers' billet, whether Private Ellis, nineteen years old, had spent his last hour calling for his mother in a voice that carried the length of the ward while Tamsin sat holding his hand because there was nothing else in the entire supply chain of the British Army left to give him.

Fenwick blotted the page, squared it, added it to the folder. Somewhere behind him a pigeon shifted in its cage, waiting for the reports it would carry. Fifteen names would go up the line today, reduced to arithmetic, and somewhere in a bureau in Rouen or Boulogne or London a man with clean cuffs would run his pencil down the column and enter the total into the total of totals, and the war's great bookkeeping would balance, and no one would ever know that Private Thomas Ellis of the 2nd West Kents had liked cricket and was afraid of the dark and called for his mother in a voice like a boy half his age.

Except one person would know. Because one person intended to write it down.

* * *

The ledger had begun three weeks ago with a name that wasn't even a name.

It had been a bad convoy, forty men straight from a raid that had gone wrong in every way a raid could go wrong, and among them a sergeant whose face was gone—not disfigured, *gone*, taken whole by a shell that had left the rest of him curiously untouched—and who had no identification disc, no paybook, nothing but a tattooed anchor on his forearm and a wedding ring soldered shut around a finger too swollen to pull it from. The dressing station sent him up with the label *Unknown, British Other Ranks*, and he died on the table in Ward B at two in the morning with Tamsin's hands inside his chest doing the work his heart had abandoned.

They buried him the next afternoon in the little cemetery behind the elms, under a wooden cross that said only: *A Soldier of the Great War, Known unto God.*

Known unto God. Tamsin had stood at the graveside with her hands folded and thought: *known unto me*. I know he was married. I know he was a stoker or a dockhand, from the burn scarring on his forearms, old burns, earned ones. I know he was afraid, because everyone is afraid, and I know he said *Annie* once, just before the ether took him, the way men say the name they will say last.

And then the burial party moved on, and the morning report recorded one unknown, and the world rolled forward over the grave like a wheel over a coin, flattening it into the road.

That night she had taken a blank exercise book from the schoolroom stores—a child's book, blue cloth boards, *Composition* stamped on the cover in faded gold—and she had written, in her small even hand, the first entry:

*Sgt. (name unknown), Royal Garrison Artillery by buttons, married, ring present. Called for Annie. Died Ward B, 0210, 14th inst. Buried rear cemetery, row 3, grave 7. Known unto God. Also unto me.*

It had felt, writing it, less like defiance than like setting a bone. Something had been knocked crooked in her and this was how it went back straight.

Three weeks later the book held thirty-one names. Some were men the reports had simply swallowed—died en route, died unrecorded, their paperwork lost between dressing station and clearing station the way everything got lost here, gloves, rifles, men. One was a Canadian private listed as *deserted* who had in fact been killed by his own side's barrage while carrying water to a forward post, a fact Tamsin knew because she had dressed the wound he came back with, and the fact of desertion had been entered by an officer who had never liked him and never troubled to correct it. Desertion followed a family home like a dog with a grudge. She had seen what it did to mothers. She would not let it stand.

Two entries were different, and she did not let herself think about why they were different except late at night when thinking happened anyway. Two entries concerned deaths that the reports did not merely omit but contradicted—one man recorded *evacuated, base hospital* who had never left the ward alive, another recorded *returned to duty* who had died of gas the following week. Clerical error, perhaps. Perhaps. But errors ran in odd directions, always toward tidiness, always toward fewer questions asked, and there were convoys that passed through here with orders attached to them that no nurse was meant to read.

The ledger did not accuse anyone. That was its discipline and its protection both. It simply recorded: *this man existed, and he ended here, and here is what was true about how.* If someday someone wanted to know—if a mother wrote, as mothers did, letters that went into the void and came back stamped *No Trace*—then somewhere, in a child's composition book, there would be an answer.

She kept it in the bottom of ration crate 114, under the tins of Maconochie that nobody ate unless starving, in the store tent where the quartermaster's own chaos served as a better lock than any lock. A crate among four hundred crates. A book among the army's million books. It should have been enough. It had been enough for three weeks.

But fifteen men had died last night, and the morning report had weighed nothing in Fenwick's hand, and standing there watching him blot the ink Tamsin had understood, with the cold clarity that sometimes came after a bad night like frost forming on glass, that a hidden book was not enough. Hidden things stayed hidden. Hidden things died with the people who hid them. If she was hit tomorrow—if the clearing station was overrun, as clearing stations were, in the ordinary course of this extraordinary war—thirty-one names would rot in a ration crate until someone burned it for kindling.

It had to go somewhere. It had to reach someone who could make it *weigh*.

* * *

At dusk she made her entries.

She waited until the relief shift had settled, until the new fires were lit and the moans of the evening convoys had sorted themselves into the familiar geography of sound, and then she sat on an ammunition box in the lee of the store tent with the composition book on her knees and her pencil stub moving. Fifteen names. She wrote them all out entire—rank, number, regiment—because the ledger's rule was completeness; a partial record was just another form. Then, beneath each, the particulars that no column would hold:

*Pte. T. Ellis, 2nd W. Kent. Age 19. Walking wounded on admission. Died 0540 of shock and exposure, coal exhausted. Asked for his mother. Held my hand.*

Her pencil paused on that last line. In three weeks of entries she had never written herself into the record. But it seemed suddenly important—important beyond explaining—that someone had held his hand. That the official account of Private Thomas Ellis, if it ever grew one, should contain the fact that he had not died alone in the dark, whatever else it contained.

She wrote the other fourteen. Sergeant Doyle, who had recited Shakespeare badly and often, misquoting with tremendous confidence. Lance-Corporal Nwosu of the Labour Corps, whose name the clerk had spelled three different ways on three different forms and whom Tamsin spelled correctly because he had shown her how, laughing, tracing the letters on the blanket with one finger: *the w is silent, Sister, like the thanks.* Gunner Price, who had talked about his allotment the whole way from the dressing station, broad beans, he'd said, you can't kill broad beans, and had died with his boots muddy from a garden he would never see.

When she finished, her fingers were stiff and her breath hung white and the light was nearly gone. She wrapped the book in the oilcloth square she used for dressings, tied it with tape, and lifted the lid of crate 114. The Maconochies gleamed dully in the lamplight, fat and anonymous. She laid the bundle flat beneath the top layer, drew the tins back over it, and pressed the lid down until the hasps clicked.

Four hundred crates. A book among books. For now.

"For now," she said aloud, to the dark, to the tins, to no one, and the word sounded exactly like what it was: a promise with a clock inside it.

* * *

She was crossing the duckboards back toward the ward tents when the headlights found her.

A staff car, which was itself an event—staff cars did not come to a casualty clearing station in the last week of February unless they brought inspectors, chaplains-general, or bad news—and it ground up the frozen track with its lamps swinging and stopped in a slush of ice before the adjutant's tent. A driver climbed out and stamped his feet. A second man followed, and even at forty yards, in the dying light, Tamsin could see that he was wrong for this place: too clean, too upright, a man wearing his greatcoat like a suit rather than a skin. He carried a leather portfolio under his arm and a clipboard against his chest, and he stood for a moment surveying the clearing station—the wards, the marquees, the smoke, the cemetery's pale crosses showing above the snow at the edge of the trees—as though it were not a place where men were currently dying but a premises awaiting valuation.

Fenwick came out to meet him, saluting, obsequious. Their voices carried on the cold air in fragments.

"—from the Census of Personnel, War Office directive—" 

"—complete enumeration, all ranks, all units, reconciled against—" 

"—beginning tomorrow morning, first thing—"

Reconciled. The word went through Tamsin like the point of a needle, finding precisely the place where the fear lived. Reconciliation was what clerks did to numbers that disagreed. Numbers disagreed here constantly—convoys miscounted, transfers unlogged, men who appeared on no manifest and vanished from every roll—and a man whose profession was making numbers agree would find, within a day, within hours, that Ward C and the little cemetery behind the elms did not agree with anything at all.

The stranger turned, checking something on his clipboard, and for a moment the lamplight caught his face: youngish, narrow, conscientious, the face of a man who had never yet been lied to successfully and did not know it.

Tamsin pulled her collar up and walked on through the cold, past the wards, past the crosses, her boots ringing on the frozen boards, and inside her chest the ledger lay in its crate like a heart she had buried and could still feel beating.
