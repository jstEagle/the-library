## 2. The Man Who Counts Doors

The mud of the Somme did not, in Edmund Voss's considered opinion, constitute a proper working environment.

He had made this observation to the transport sergeant who met him at the rail siding, and the sergeant had laughed—a wet, unlovely sound—and said, "Wait till you see where they've put you, sir." Edmund had not appreciated being called *sir* in that particular tone, which suggested the word was a joke at his expense. He was not, strictly speaking, a sir. He was a Second-Class Census Officer of the Directorate of Manpower Statistics, attached by a clerical error he intended to have corrected to the Adjutant-General's office in Rouen, and he had arrived at Casualty Clearing Station No. 37 with two leather cases of blank ledgers, one fountain pen with an irreplaceable nib, and a conviction that this entire posting was beneath both his talents and his dignity.

He was thirty-one years old and he had counted eleven thousand four hundred and six doors since the war began. He knew this because he wrote it down. Every door, every window, every bed space, every cot, every bunk, every stretcher rack in every billet, hospital, depot, and transit camp from Étaples to the front edge of the world. The Army wanted numbers. Numbers were how the war ate: so many men in, so many men out, so many men—here the forms used the phrase *no longer effective*—and it was Edmund's job to make the arithmetic behave. He was good at it. He was very good at it. In three years he had never once failed to reconcile a return, and he had been promised, in writing, that upon completion of the current audit cycle he would be elevated to First Class and transferred to a desk in London that had windows.

Which was why the clearing station annoyed him. Clearing stations were chaos wearing a uniform. Men came in on stretchers and went out on lorries and some fraction of them went into the ground behind the compound, and the paperwork moved like syrup in January. His last posting—a horse remount depot, thank God for its dullness—had taken him four days. He had budgeted six here, seven at most, and then Amiens, and then the boat, and then the desk with the windows, and then perhaps a girl named Margaret Hallowes who worked in the Ministry of Pensions and who laughed at his jokes even when they were not funny, which he suspected meant she was kind rather than that he was amusing, but a man could hope.

The station sat in a fold of ground that had once, presumably, been a farm. Now it was tents and huts and duckboards and a smell that Edmund catalogued against his will—carbolic, mud, diesel, and underneath it something sweeter and worse that he refused to name. Wounded men lay in rows under canvas, and nurses moved among them with a briskness that struck him at first as coldness until he watched longer and understood it was the opposite: a tenderness so large it had to be organized or it would drown them.

A sister met him at the reception marquee—a tall woman, grey-eyed, with the particular upright exhaustion of someone who had been awake too long for reasons that were not her own fault. Her badge read *SISTER AMBERDALE*.

"Mr. Voss? Census?"

"Second-Class Officer Voss," he said, and heard how priggish it sounded even as he said it, and pressed on anyway. "I'll need accommodation for the duration, access to all ward registers, admission and discharge books, the mortuary register if you keep one, and—" he consulted his checklist, "—an accurate statement of burials conducted from this station in the past ninety days."

Something happened in her face. It was very small. Later he would turn it over in his mind like a coin, trying to read the stamp on it. At the time it looked like nothing at all—just a flicker, a tightening, gone before it finished happening.

"You'll find the registers in order," she said. "We're busy. I can't spare anyone to walk you about, but everything's marked."

"I don't require an escort," Edmund said. "I require accuracy."

"Then we should get along," said Sister Amberdale, and left him standing with his cases in the marquee, and Edmund had the distinct impression—he was rarely wrong about impressions, that was half of what made him a good counter—that she had just measured him to the ounce and filed him somewhere.

* * *

He began, as he always began, with the buildings.

This was the part of his work no one understood and everyone mocked. Before touching a single form, Edmund walked every structure and counted. Doors, bunks, bed spaces, latrine seats, water points, stove positions. A building's capacity was a fact; a register's claims were only words; and when the facts and the words disagreed, the facts won. He had caught three quartermasters falsifying ration returns this way, and one of them had gone to prison, and Edmund had thought about that sometimes at night—not with guilt exactly, but with the seriousness of a man who has learned what his small sharp instrument is capable of cutting.

So he counted. Forty-seven tents and huts. Three ward marquees of sixty cots each, one resuscitation tent of twenty, one officers' ward of twelve. An operating hut with two tables—he noted the blood on the floorboards around them without comment, because comment would have required stopping. A chapel in a requisitioned barn. A mortuary tent, canvas over a wooden frame, set apart near the gate as regulations required.

And beyond the wire, on the low rise the men had named—according to a chalkboard sign at the path's foot—the Down, a cemetery. Rows of crosses, new wood, most without names yet, just regimental tags wired to the uprights pending the Graves Registration parties. Edmund stood at the fence and counted the crosses twice, because counting twice was discipline. One hundred and eighty-eight. Then he took out his notebook and wrote the date and the number, and felt the small clean satisfaction of a figure entered true.

It was on the third day, cross-referencing, that the trouble began.

The admission book told him the story in its own cramped hand: men brought in, men operated on, men evacuated to base, men died. The death column was kept meticulously—Sister Amberdale's writing, he recognized it now, narrow and upright and unhurried, each entry dated, hour noted, cause abbreviated in the approved manner. For the past ninety days the death column totalled one hundred and fifty-two.

The burial returns—the official form, signed by the chaplain and countersigned by the adjutant—agreed. One hundred and fifty-two.

But the crosses numbered one hundred and eighty-eight.

Edmund sat in the records hut with the lamp turned low and did the subtraction four times, though there was no need. Thirty-six. Thirty-six more graves than recorded deaths. And before anyone might suggest the obvious—that older graves predated his ninety-day window—he had already checked the cemetery register itself, such as it was: a slate board at the path entrance where the burial party chalked dates. The chalked dates clustered. They clustered in the recent weeks, thickening toward the present like a pulse quickening, and several of the freshest rows bore chalk marks from days whose death columns he held open in front of him, columns showing two deaths, three deaths, on nights when the chalk below the hill said nine.

He put down his pen.

For a long moment he simply listened. Outside, a lorry changed gear. Somewhere a man moaned and was quieted. The lamp hissed. Edmund Voss, who had reconciled eleven thousand doors and never once let the arithmetic misbehave on his watch, sat perfectly still and looked at the discrepancy the way a man looks at a hairline crack in a dam.

There were explanations. There were always explanations; he had learned that much in three years of army arithmetic. Clerical lag—the dead buried before the register caught up, entries batched and backfilled. Misrouting—men who died here but were formally written up at the receiving hospital, their deaths booked elsewhere, their bodies interred locally by expedience. Battle pressure—when the convoys came through at forty an hour, even the best clerk dropped stitches. All plausible. All, he suspected, partially true.

But thirty-six was not a dropped stitch. Thirty-six was a pattern. And patterns, Edmund knew—the quartermaster with the phantom horses had taught him this—patterns meant either incompetence or intent, and Sister Amberdale's registers were the tidiest he had seen in three years of inspecting the untidy. No one that precise lost thirty-six men by accident.

He found himself thinking about her face at the reception marquee, that flicker, that coin-turn. *You'll find the registers in order.* Yes. That was precisely the trouble. They were in order. Too far in order, like a room straightened hurriedly before company arrives, cushions plumped over the stain.

Edmund closed the books, squared their edges exactly with the edge of the table, and wrote in his own notebook, in his smallest hand:

*Discrepancy: burials vs. deaths, CCS 37 — 36 excess, 90 days. Cause unknown. Query.*

Then he sat with the word *query* and found it did not sit comfortably. Because there was another possibility he had not written down, and it had arrived unbidden while he stood at the cemetery fence, looking at the little crosses with their wired-on tags, and it was this: that somewhere in France, right now, there were mothers and wives receiving telegrams that said *missing*, and that the difference between *dead* and *missing* was not a difference in what had happened to a man but a difference in what got written down afterward. He had processed thousands of those forms. He had never once thought about what the word did to the person who opened the envelope. Forms were forms. He had believed this the way he believed in the metric system.

He blew out the lamp, because his oil ration was his own affair, and lay on his camp cot listening to the wind work the tent guy-lines, and told himself that tomorrow he would ask Sister Amberdale one question—one, direct, professional—and she would give him some dull administrative answer, batching, backlog, the fog of war, and he would note it, reconcile the return with a footnote, initial the file, and be in Amiens by Friday.

He almost believed it. He was, after all, a man who trusted numbers, and by his count the odds were entirely in his favor.

But sleep did not come easily, and when it finally did, it came with the smell of carbolic and that other sweetness underneath, and with the image of a slate board and chalk dust settling over fresh-turned earth, thirty-six small silences that no form in his leather case had a column wide enough to hold.

In the morning, he would ask his question.
