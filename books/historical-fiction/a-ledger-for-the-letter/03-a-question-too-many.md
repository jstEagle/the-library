## 3. A Question Too Many

The interview room at No. 2 Casualty Clearing Station had been, in some former life, a linen store. Edmund Voss knew this because the shelves were still bolted to the walls, empty now except for a single candle in a jam jar and a clipboard he had brought himself. The room smelled of carbolic and cold canvas, and through the wall came the muffled rhythm of the wards—someone coughing, someone groaning, someone, mercifully, laughing low and quick as if laughter had to be smuggled in like contraband.

He set his papers out with the care of a man laying a table for a dinner he did not expect to enjoy: the station returns, the burial register extracts he'd copied from the chaplain's office that morning, his own tally sheets with their neat columns of figures, each digit drawn with the same pressure so no line looked more important than another. A census was only ever as honest as its most tedious number.

The candle guttered when the door opened, and Sister Tamsin Amberdale filled the frame the way winter fills a doorway—not large, exactly, but total.

"You wanted me, Mr. Voss." Not a question. She closed the door behind her without asking whether she should, which told him something already about who would control this conversation.

"Thank you for coming. Please sit."

She sat on the crate opposite him, spine straight, hands folded on her knees—red-chapped hands, scrubbed raw at the knuckles, a nurse's hands. He had interviewed forty-one officers, orderlies, and medical staff over three days, and every one of them had slumped or fidgeted or asked how long it would take. She did none of these things. She simply waited, the way stone waits for rain.

"I'll be brief," he said, though they both knew he wouldn't. "I'm conducting a personnel census under authority of the Adjutant-General's office. Station strength, admissions, dispositions of deceased. Routine."

"Nothing here is routine," she said mildly. "But go on."

He uncapped his pen. "Your station return for October lists two hundred and six deaths since September the first."

"That sounds right."

"The burial register—the one kept by Chaplain Whitcombe—records two hundred and twenty-one interments in the extended plot behind the ward tents."

The candle flame leaned, righted itself. Sister Amberdale's expression did not change at all, and that was the first strange thing. He had watched men hear worse numbers than this and flinch, laugh, weep, shrug. She did what a ledger does: she held still.

"Fifteen graves unaccounted for in your paperwork," Edmund continued. "Fifteen men buried in consecrated ground whose deaths appear nowhere in the official returns. No names, no units, no next of kin notified. I've been counting doors for eleven years, Sister. Doors don't lie. People do—but usually badly, and usually in ways I can see."

"And what is it you think you see?"

"A clerical error." He said it flatly, watching her. "Or fifteen of them. Except that errors cluster around negligence, and nothing in this station is negligent. Your dressings are changed on schedule. Your morphia book balances to the dram. You run the tidiest clearing station I have audited in France—and yet fifteen dead men have fallen between the pages."

Outside, somewhere near the theatre tent, a generator thumped its slow mechanical heart. Sister Amberdale turned her hands over and studied her palms as if reading something there.

"You've come all this way from London," she said, "to count our dead twice."

"I've come to make sure they're counted once, properly. That's the whole of my work. Every man who serves ought to exist on paper. Paper is how his mother finds out. Paper is his pension, his grave marker, his name cut into stone instead of lost in mud." He heard the heat creep into his own voice and reined it in; enthusiasm was a weakness in an interviewer. "So you understand why the discrepancy troubles me."

"It troubles me too." She said it simply, without performance. "More than you know."

"Then help me reconcile it. Wounded men transferred out who died en route—"

"Logged at the receiving station. I can show you the transfer chits."

"—men buried before identification, recorded later under unknowns—"

"The unknowns are numbered and cross-referenced. Whitcombe keeps a map of the plot down to the pace. Ask him."

"—or men whose deaths were entered late, after the monthly return closed—"

"Late entries carry a marginal note and my initials. Check the margins."

She answered each hypothesis like a fencer returning a thrust, not parrying but redirecting, offering him documents he could chase for days. And every answer was true—he could see that, and that was what unsettled him. Liars build walls. She was building corridors, endless ones, each ending in a filing cabinet he would have to walk to himself.

"Sister," he said, setting down his pen, "I'm going to ask the question plainly, because I think you're a plain woman. Where are the fifteen?"

For the first time she looked at him directly, and her eyes were grey and very tired and very awake, the eyes of someone who had decided long ago exactly what she would and would not spend herself on.

"Some men die between the dressing station and here," she said. "Some die in the ambulance on roads the shells have chewed up. Some are brought to us so broken that the only honest thing left to write is *unknown*. The war is untidy, Mr. Voss. Paper wants the world to be tidy. One of those things has to give, and it isn't going to be the war."

It was a good answer. It was even, he suspected, mostly a true answer. It explained perhaps five of the fifteen. It did not explain why, when she said *paper*, her mouth tightened the way people's mouths tighten around a wound they've stopped complaining about.

He tried another road in. "Your brother served with the Norfolks, I believe. From your file."

Something moved behind her face—quick, deep, like a fish turning under ice. "My file is about my service, not my family."

"Of course. Forgive me." He hadn't meant to say it at all; it had been loaded in him like a round in a chamber, and grief, he was learning, was a weapon everyone reached for. "I only meant—that loss makes us attentive to records. To getting names right."

"Yes," she said quietly. "It does."

And then, as if the mention of her brother had loosened something she hadn't meant to loosen, she reached into the pocket of her apron—for a handkerchief, he assumed, for composure—and a corner of paper came with her hand and caught on the pocket's edge.

It was a small thing. A folded booklet, dark cover gone soft with handling, one corner showing above the apron line for perhaps two seconds. Two seconds was an eternity to a man who had spent eleven years noticing whether a house had seven windows or eight. The cover was ruled—faint blue lines visible even in the bad light, the ruling of an account book, a ledger. And on the exposed page beneath, written small and upright in ink gone brownish-black:

*...Pte. R. Calloway, 7th Btn., d. 14 Oct., ward C...*

Then her fingers found it, and it was gone, tucked away with a smoothness that told him this was not the first time she had caught it falling.

The room went very quiet. Even the generator seemed to hold its breath.

*Pte. R. Calloway.* Edmund's memory, which was the finest tool he owned, opened its drawer without being asked: the burial register extract he had copied that morning, entry number one hundred ninety-eight, *Calloway, R., Pte., interred 15 Oct., Row 4, Grave 11* —a name that appeared in the cold earth and in the chaplain's careful hand and nowhere else in the entire apparatus of the British Army.

A name in her pocket. Written by hand. In a ruled book.

She saw him see it. He watched the knowledge land in her like a stone dropped into a well—no splash, just the long silence of falling water. Her chin lifted a fraction. Not defiance, exactly. Readiness. A woman bracing for the knock on the door she had always known would come.

"Mr. Voss," she said, "you were saying?"

*I was saying nothing. I was going to say nothing.*

His mouth was open. The words were assembled—*What is that book? Show me, please*—three sentences that would end whatever this was, that would send a report up the chain, that would bring a provost officer and a search and probably a court-martial, and would reconcile his columns beautifully. Fifteen graves, fifteen explanations, case closed, commendation likely, promotion certain. Major Voss by spring. His mother's face at the news. The tidy, climbing life he had built out of other people's numbers.

And underneath the assembly of those words, another voice, one he did not recognize as his own, said: *she wrote his name down when nobody else would.*

"Calloway," he heard himself say instead. His voice sounded distant, borrowed. "Row four, grave eleven. He isn't in your returns either, is he."

"No," said Sister Amberdale. "He isn't."

"Why?"

"Why do you think?" She rose from the crate, unhurried, gathering herself the way weather gathers. "You've seen the front of this hospital, Mr. Voss. Men arrive with their identity discs shot off, their tunics burned, their mates dead beside them and nobody left alive who knows their surname. Sometimes the army's answer to a man it cannot identify is to stop asking questions about him. Fifteen answers, if you like. Or—" and here she paused at the door, her hand on the latch, and looked back at him with something that was almost kindness, almost pity, and entirely unreadable— "or one reason. But you're a census man. You deal in counts, not reasons. I expect that's easier."

The door closed behind her. The candle steadied.

Edmund Voss sat alone in the linen store for a long time, listening to the wards breathe through the wall.

He should write it down now. That was the discipline—record observations within the hour, while detail was fresh. *Interviewed Sister T. Amberdale, re: discrepancy of 15 deceased. Subject evasive. Observed subject conceal handwritten register, contents consistent with omitted death entries. Recommend further inquiry.* Twenty-six words. He had written ten thousand such sentences in his career, dry as sand, each one a small brick in the enormous wall of *what actually happened*.

His pen hovered over the page.

Here was the trouble, and he examined it the way he examined everything, methodically, hating himself for the thoroughness: the trouble was that she had lied to him, and he could not find the lie. Everything she'd said was defensible, documentable, probably true. The only lie was the shape of the whole—a corridor built to lead him nowhere—which was not lying, precisely. Which was what any competent person did with an army full of forms. Which was, he realized with a slow cold turn in his stomach, what *he* did, every day, when he reduced a man to a figure and called the figure truth.

*Paper is how his mother finds out,* he had told her, with all his earnest heat. He had believed it when he said it. He believed it still. And one of those fifteen mothers—or wives, or sisters—was going to learn from a stranger's gravestone what the War Office had never troubled to post. Unless somebody had written the name down. Somebody with chapped hands and a ruled book and the nerve to keep it inside her apron three feet from a census officer.

He thought of his cousin Walter then—Walter, who had enlisted at seventeen with a forged signature and who had been *missing* now for eight months, missing the way a coat goes missing, a word that meant nothing and swallowed everything—and he pressed the thought down hard before it could become relevant. It was not relevant. Nothing outside this room was relevant. He was a census taker. He counted.

Edmund wrote: *Interviewed Sister T. Amberdale, re: discrepancy of 15 deceased. Subject cooperative. Discrepancy attributable to transit deaths and unidentified remains; documentation available via Chaplain and receiving stations. No further action recommended.*

He read it back. It was the first false sentence of his life, and it lay on the page looking exactly like all the true ones, which frightened him more than anything that had happened in France so far.

No further action recommended. He signed it, blotted it, squared it with the edge of the clipboard—and understood, sitting in the cold with the candle burning down, that he had just made a bargain with a woman who hadn't offered one. That he would come back tomorrow, and the day after, and ask his careful questions, and she would build her beautiful corridors, and he would walk down them, and both of them would know.

He hated himself for it. He hated how easily it had been done—one glimpse of a ruled cover, one name in faded ink, and eleven years of exactness had folded like wet cardboard. He blew out the candle, gathered his papers, and stepped into the grey afternoon, where Sister Amberdale was crossing the yard with a basin of bandages, upright as a mast, not looking at him at all.

Behind the ward tents, row four, grave eleven stood in the thin grass with a wooden cross and no name plate yet. Tomorrow, he decided, he would copy the entire burial register again. All of it. Every name.

Just in case somebody needed counting properly.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep in the requisitioned barn where the census staff were billeted, Edmund took out his private notebook—the one with his family dates, his measurements of childhood houses, the little archive of a small life—and on a fresh page, in his smallest hand, he wrote a single word from memory:

*Calloway.*

Below it, after a long minute, he added: *R.*

He did not know why he was doing it. He told himself it was professional habit, a note toward future verification. But as he closed the book and lay listening to the wind worry the barn roof, he admitted the plainer truth: he had started a list of his own. And somewhere across the yard, behind a locked box in a ration crate or sewn into a mattress or hidden wherever careful women hide dangerous paper, Sister Amberdale had a list too—and tomorrow he was going to find out how long it was, or die of the wanting to know.
