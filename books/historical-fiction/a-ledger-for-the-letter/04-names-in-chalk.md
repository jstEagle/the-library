## 4. Names in Chalk

The frost came through the canvas walls like a rumor no one could silence, and in the hour before dawn Tamsin Amberdale stood at the blackboard in Ward C and wrote names.

She wrote them the way she did everything now—with hands that did not shake because she had forbidden them to. Chalk worn down to a stub, its white dust settling on her cuffs like ash. Eleven names this week that the morning reports would not carry. Eleven men who had breathed, bled, spoken—some of them her name, or their mothers', or nothing at all—and who would vanish from the record as cleanly as breath vanishes from cold air.

She had learned to write small. The board was not large, and there were going to be more names than board.

Behind her, the ward slept the way wounded men sleep: lightly, in fragments, one ear always listening for the sound that meant it was their turn. Sister Brightwater's kettle murmured on the stove. Outside, somewhere past the wire and the frozen mud, the guns kept their long argument going, patient as weather.

Tamsin set down the chalk stub and looked at what she'd written.

*Pvt. E. Calloway, 9th Norfolks. Died 3rd inst., gas, 0400.*

*Cpl. J. Merrick, RFA. Died 2nd inst., wounds, never reached dressing station.*

*L/Cpl. A. Doyle. Died 1st inst., tetanus. Buried behind Ward B under the birch tree, marked with a ration tin.*

Names in chalk. That was all any of them were now—that was all most of them had ever been allowed to be, out here where men arrived as stretcher numbers and left as grave registrations, if they left as anything at all. But chalk could be wiped away with a sleeve. Paper could be lost, misfiled, burned, declared erroneous by a captain three miles and two ranks removed from the truth. Tamsin had watched the machinery do it too many times to believe in paper anymore.

So she kept the other thing. The ledger. Bound in oilcloth, hidden in the false bottom of a ration crate, filled in pencil by lamplight when the sisters thought she slept. Every man the reports omitted. Date, unit, wound, burial if there was one, last words if there were those.

She kept it for the reason she kept everything, which was a reason four years old and buried in a churchyard in Sussex under a stone that said *Missing*.

* * *

His name had been William, but no one but their mother had ever called him that. He was Will—Will Amberdale, who could whistle through two fingers loud enough to call cattle across three fields, who once carried a lamb half a mile inside his coat during the lambing storm of 1910 and caught such a scolding from Father about the mud that he laughed the whole time he was being lectured. He was nineteen months younger than Tamsin and had followed her everywhere since he could walk, into the orchard, into trouble, into the village school where the mistress despaired of them both.

When the war came, he followed her again. That was how she always told it, afterward, in the privacy of her own head: *I went first, and he followed.* She had trained as a nurse because it was the one useful thing a farmer's daughter with steady hands could offer. And six months later Will stood in the kitchen in his new uniform, cap too large, grinning that grin, and said, "Well, someone has to keep an eye on you."

"You'll be at the front," she said. "I'll be at a base hospital."

"Same war, Tam." He turned the cap in his hands, suddenly serious in the way he only ever let himself be serious for a moment at a time. "Same mud, near enough. If you get hit, I want to know somebody's holding your hand."

"And if I don't?"

He put the cap back on, crooked, the way he wore everything. "Then somebody's holding mine."

The last time she saw him alive was in August, at a casualty clearing station she would later come to work in herself without knowing, until the smell of the place hit her on her first day and she had to grip a bed frame until her knuckles went white. But that was later. In August it was only Will, dusty and cheerful and thinner than he'd been at Christmas, sitting on an ammunition crate outside the theatre tent while she changed a dressing nearby because it was the closest she could come to being off duty with him.

He talked about home. The orchard. Whether the Cox's Pippin would fruit this year or whether the late frost had got it. He asked after Mother's hands—the rheumatism was bad that summer—and whether Father had finally mended the gate or was still propping it shut with a brick. Ordinary things. He was very good at ordinary things; it was a kind of courage she hadn't recognized then, and recognized every day since.

"I've been thinking," he said, "about what happens after. When it's over."

"When it's over," she repeated, because neither of them could say *if* out loud, though they both heard it standing underneath the words like water under ice.

"There'll be lists," Will said. "In the papers. Names of the fallen and all that. And I keep thinking—" he laughed, embarrassed, picked at a thread on his cuff— "what if they spell it wrong? You know how clerks are. Amberdale, Amberdale. It's not so difficult. But some poor devil gets put down as Ambrose, or Hambleden, and then his mum's reading the list and she reads right past her own son because some clerk couldn't be bothered with a dictionary."

"You think too much," Tamsin said, which is what sisters say when they cannot bear what their brothers have seen.

"Somebody has to," he said. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it?"

Two days later his battalion went up. Four days after that, the dressing station where she worked received a flood of casualties from the same sector, and among the stretchers coming through the flap of the receiving tent she saw boots she knew—hobnailed, left boot patched at the toe because he refused new ones, swore the old ones fit his feet like opinions fit a vicar.

She was beside the stretcher before the bearers had set it down.

There was a great deal she did not remember afterward, and a great deal she would never stop remembering, and the two categories fought over the same territory for years. She remembered that he was conscious. She remembered that his first word was her name, said not with surprise but with a kind of rueful satisfaction, as though she'd turned up late for an appointment. She remembered that the surgeon shook his head before he even reached them, that small precise movement that nurses learn to read at forty paces, the movement that says *do not spend the morphine here, spend it where it can buy an hour.*

She remembered holding his hand, because she had promised, in the way you promise things without saying them, your whole life.

And she remembered this: at the very end, when the rattle had started and the light had gone strange and far away in his eyes, Will Amberdale gripped her wrist with the last strength he owned and said, clear as Sunday:

"Don't let them lose me, Tam."

Not *don't let me die*. He was past pretending about that, and so was she. Not *tell Mother*, though she bent close and said she would and he pressed her hand once to say he'd heard. What he spent his final sentence on—his whole hoarded capital of last words, spent in front of a sister instead of saved for a mother—was the thing he had joked about on the ammunition crate, the clerk's dictionary, the misspelled list, the name read past and lost.

*Don't let them lose me.*

"I won't," she said.

The guns went on. Somewhere a transport rattled past. A chaplain two tents away was singing something low and Latin, the sound threading through the canvas like smoke.

She closed his eyes. She straightened his collar. She took the disc from around his neck and noted his number in her little pocket book, in her neat schoolroom hand, because that was procedure, and procedure was the spine that held her upright, and then she sat with him until the bearers came, because there was no procedure for anything else.

* * *

The letter she wrote her mother took eleven drafts. The official telegram arrived first—she had dreaded that, had written to the adjutant begging to be the one to break it, and been refused, rules being rules—and so her mother learned of Will's death from a printed form delivered by a boy on a bicycle, and learned the details from her daughter three days later, and learned one thing from neither of them, because Tamsin did not know it yet herself.

*Missing*, said the battalion's eventual report. Later amended, after inquiry, to *missing presumed dead*. The paperwork had gone astray in the chaos of the advance—the field ambulance's returns were incomplete, a regimental orderly had been killed carrying the day's casualty list, and the correction that should have flowed from the clearing station's admission book back up the chain simply never flowed. Will Amberdale had died in a clean bed with his sister's hand in his and a chaplain within earshot, and the Army of which he had been, in its own phrase, *a soldier of the Empire*, recorded that he might possibly still be walking about somewhere in France, or perhaps in Germany, a prisoner, or perhaps—this was the cruelty the forms permitted—deserted.

Deserted. She had seen the word in a follow-up inquiry years later, in a file she paid a London searcher five shillings to pull. One clerk's query mark, hanging off the word like a loose tooth. Her mother had gone to her grave—not yet, thank God, but the phrase lived in Tamsin now like shrapnel—indefinitely unsure, officially unmothered, unable to wear black properly because mourning required certainty and the ledger-keepers of the world had declined to provide it.

Her father had aged ten years in ten weeks. The orchard went untended. The gate stayed broken, propped with the brick, because there are griefs that make repair seem obscene.

And Tamsin, who had promised, requested a transfer forward. To the clearing stations. Where the dying actually happened, and where the books were kept—or weren't.

It did not take her long, once she was there, to see how names went missing. They went missing the way water goes missing through a cracked pipe: not all at once, not maliciously, mostly. An admission book filled in hastily at three in the morning by a doctor elbow-deep in someone else's abdomen. A page smudged with rain. A man brought in unconscious with no disc and no papers, dead before he could speak his own name, buried under a number that turned out to belong to another man entirely. A sergeant-major who decided that reporting a death by self-inflicted wound would "serve no purpose." A colonel who preferred his returns tidy. A war, above all—a war so enormous, so industrious in its production of the dead, that the machinery built to record them choked on the volume and coughed, and whatever fell out in the coughing was gone.

Gone, unless someone stood at the crack in the pipe with a cup.

* * *

"Tamsin."

Sister Brightwater's voice, quiet, from the doorway of the ward. Tamsin blinked and the ward assembled itself around her again: the stove, the sleeping men, the blackboard with its eleven names in her careful hand. Dawn had come up grey behind the canvas. She had been standing at the board, chalk stub motionless in her fingers, for how long? Long enough that her feet ached in her boots.

"The 6:40 report," Brightwater said. "You'll want to see it before the MO does."

Tamsin crossed the ward and took the flimsy pink sheet from her. Ran her eye down it. Calloway—no. Merrick—no. Doyle—no. Three of her eleven, already erased by omission, and the day's writing not even finished.

"Same as ever," she said, and was pleased at how level her voice came out.

Brightwater looked at her a moment longer than necessary. The older woman had eyes the color of dishwater and missed nothing that passed through this station, and for months now Tamsin had suspected—had felt, the way you feel weather changing—that the suspicion ran both ways. "You're spending a lot of time at that board," Brightwater said.

"Somebody has to count."

"Mm." Brightwater took the report back, folded it. At the door she paused, half-turned, steam rising off the kettle behind her. "The census man comes today," she said. "From Records. Counting personnel, counting beds, counting God knows what. Voss, his card says. A Mr. Edmund Voss." Her gaze moved, briefly, deliberately, to the blackboard. To the eleven names. "Mind how you answer his questions, Nurse Amberdale. Men who count for a living notice what doesn't add up."

She went out. The kettle sang. Down the ward, a sleeping man called out for his mother in a voice like a boy's, and subsided.

Tamsin stood alone with the chalk dust and the names.

*Don't let them lose me.*

Four years, and she could still hear the exact grain of his voice, could still feel the grip on her wrist—weak, then weaker, then the terrible gentleness of almost nothing. She had failed him. Not in the way the word usually meant; he had died well-tended, mourned, loved. But somewhere between her hand and her mother's heart, between the admission book and the parish roll, between the truth and the record, William Amberdale had slipped through the crack in the pipe, and no power on earth seemed able to fetch him back except a woman with a stub of chalk and a hidden book.

She picked up the ledger's oilcloth corner of thought in her mind, weighed it. Tonight, after lights-out, there would be three more entries. Perhaps five. There were always more.

She wiped the board clean—carefully, name by name, transferring each one into memory first, the way you carry a flame from one candle to another—and hung the chalk stub on its string.

Outside, down the rutted road toward the officers' mess, a staff car she had never seen before was grinding to a halt, and a thin man in spectacles was stepping out with a leather case and an expression like an unpaid bill.

Tamsin watched him through the canvas gap, and her hand, quite on its own authority, drifted to her pocket where the pencil lived.
