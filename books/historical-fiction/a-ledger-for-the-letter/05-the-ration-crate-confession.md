## 5. The Ration Crate Confession

Edmund Voss had counted doors for eleven years. He had counted them in Birmingham, in Cardiff, in the drowned grey terraces of Glasgow where every fourth window held a candle for a son at the front. He had learned that a door told you everything: its paint, its hinges, whether it opened eagerly or was barred from within. Doors were honest in a way people were not.

So when Sister Tamsin Amberdale closed hers behind her, he noted it. The latch caught on the first try—she'd done it a thousand times—but she did not look at him while she did it, and her hand stayed on the iron handle one heartbeat longer than necessary.

"You wanted to discuss the discrepancy," she said.

"I wanted to discuss several discrepancies." He stood with his ledger-case under his arm like a shield, his boots leaving wet prints on the floorboards of what appeared to be a supply room: shelves of lint and ether tins, a rack of splints, the smell of carbolic fighting a losing war against the smell of mud. Somewhere beyond the wall, a man was groaning in a register Edmund had never heard before this posting—a low, animal patience that made his skin prickle. "Your morning reports list forty-one admissions for the week ending the fourteenth. My count of occupied beds, plus discharges, plus—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—departures, comes to fifty-three."

"Departures," she repeated.

"Deaths, Sister. The word is deaths. Twelve men appear in no report I have been shown. No transfer papers. No burial returns. And yet there are twelve fresh mounds behind the chapel that your own gardener—the orderly, Halloway—was filling when I walked past yesterday afternoon."

The lamp between them guttered. Outside, the wind moved through the canvas annexes with a sound like someone turning enormous pages.

Sister Amberdale did not flinch. That was the first thing he noticed, and he would think about it later, in the dark, with shame: he had expected flinching. He had built his whole career on the small betrayals faces committed without permission. Hers committed nothing.

"War is untidy, Mr. Voss," she said. "Paperwork is tidier than war. One of them must give way."

"That is precisely my point. It is my function to determine which."

"And have you determined it?"

He opened his mouth to deliver the sentence he had rehearsed on the walk over—the sentence about irregularities requiring escalation to the Deputy Director of Medical Services, about his duty being administrative rather than personal—and found that his mouth refused it. Not out of nobility. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, even now, even then. He said nothing because she was looking at him the way a surgeon looks at an area she has already decided to cut, and because somewhere under his ribs, in a place he kept locked like a strongbox, twelve mounds of frozen earth were doing arithmetic of their own.

"The ration crate," he said instead.

Her stillness changed quality. He felt it the way one feels a change in pressure before weather.

"On Tuesday," he continued, quietly now, "you sent Halloway to the stores for condensed milk. The requisition was for two tins. He returned having carried the crate itself—it's still sitting by the west wall of Ward C, I checked. A heavy crate, for two tins of milk. And yesterday, when the shelling started and everyone ran for the dugouts, you went the wrong way. Toward Ward C. You came back with milk tins in your hands and dirt on your knees, and you were gone perhaps ninety seconds longer than the journey required."

"You timed me."

"I time everything. It is what I am for." He set his case down on the splint rack. "I am not an idiot, Sister Amberdale. I know how that sounds coming from a census clerk, but I have spent eleven years finding what people hide in the spaces between their numbers. A crate that heavy, a detour that long, a report twelve names short. I can add."

The wind pressed at the walls. In the distance, the guns kept their slow, gluttonous rhythm—three shells, a pause, three more—as though the war were chewing.

Tamsin Amberdale crossed to the window. For a moment he saw her in profile, lit by the grey blade of winter light: a young woman made old at the edges, her cap slightly crooked in a way no matron would tolerate, her hands—those quick, competent hands he'd watched thread a cannula into a collapsing vein while shells came down—hanging open at her sides as if they had briefly forgotten their trade.

"Do you know what happens to nurses who keep unauthorized records?" she asked the glass.

"They are court-martialed."

"They are *disappeared*, Mr. Voss. Quietly. Posted to a base hospital in Rouen until the war forgets them, or worse. And the men in those graves stay unrecorded either way. Reporting me changes nothing except adding one more name to somebody's punishment ledger." She turned. "You strike me as a man who respects ledgers. So tell me—which entry serves anyone?"

* * *

Edmund had prepared for defiance, for tears, for the trembling appeal that so many made when cornered—the performance of innocence. He had not prepared for this: negotiation. She spoke of her own destruction the way he spoke of quarterly returns.

"I don't want to report you," he heard himself say, and was startled by the truth of it. "I want—I would like to see one page."

"No."

"One page. Then I will close it and decide what kind of man I am, and you will never see me hesitate again."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the guns paused—that strange, breathless lull that fell over the line sometimes, as if the whole front were listening.

"Why one page?" she said finally. "Why not the whole thing? If you mean to destroy me, one page will do it."

"Because a whole book asks me to believe a person," Edmund said. "A page asks me to believe a fact. I'm better with facts." His voice did something unfamiliar on the last words; he covered it by adjusting his spectacles. "My whole life I have written down other people's truths in columns and called it work. Show me one column of yours. Let me check your arithmetic."

Something moved across her face—not softness, exactly. Recognition, perhaps, of a fellow creature who trusted paper more than people.

She went to the west wall, knelt, and worked loose a board beneath the lowest shelf. From the cavity she drew a ration crate, military stenciling, the wood scarred and dark—and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath two tins of condensed milk placed there for exactly the occasion he had described, a book.

It was not grand. That struck him most. He had imagined something dramatic—a leather journal, maybe, with a lock. What she handed him was a stationer's ledger, the sort sold for sixpence in any town shop, its cover already warped by damp. She unwrapped it with the care of a woman handling morphine.

"Page forty-one," she said. "You may read page forty-one. Nothing else."

He opened it where she held it, her thumb pinning the pages like a specimen.

The handwriting was small, upright, mercilessly neat. A ruled table. Columns headed: NAME. RANK. REGIMENT. DATE OF DEATH. CAUSE. LAST KNOWN WORDS, WHERE RECORDED. BURIED. NEXT OF KIN NOTIFIED — YES/NO/UNKNOWN.

And below, in each row, the thing that made his chest tighten: entries marked with a red asterisk and a notation in the margin—*not on morning report*. *Not on burial return.* *Not on casualty telegraph list.*

He read the names. Private A. Cobbett. Lance-Corporal J. Ferrier. Sergeant W. Dunn—

His finger stopped.

The world did something it had never done in eleven years of careful counting. It lost its place.

**DUNN, WILLIAM H., PTE., 2/7th HAMPSHIRE RGT.** Date of death: 3 October. Cause: gas, then wounds. Last known words: *Tell my mum the dog'll be all right.* Buried: east of the chapel, third row. Next of kin: aunt, Mrs. E. Voss, 14 Cavendish Row, Clapham. NOTIFIED: UNKNOWN.

"Cousin," Edmund said. The word came out flat, wrong, like a coin dropped on stone. "Willie Dunn is my cousin. My aunt Edith raised us both the same summer my father died. I taught him to ride a bicycle on Clapham Common. He wrote me letters—cheerful, appalling letters, full of spelling mistakes. His last one arrived in September. I answered it. I answered it *promptly*."

His voice had risen. He brought it down.

"He's listed here as dead. October the third. There is no telegram at my aunt's house. I correspond with her monthly—she would have told me. There has been no notification. Eleven weeks, and nobody has told a woman in Clapham that her boy is buried under French mud with an asterisk beside his name."

"Notified: unknown," Tamsin said softly. "That's what I write when I can't find out. I've written it two hundred times. The system loses them, Mr. Voss. Sometimes on purpose—deserters' records burned, mutineers stricken, clerks burying their own errors. Sometimes by pure accident of too much war and too little ink. But Willie Dunn was gassed during the retreat and died in my ward on a stretcher under a table because we'd run out of beds, and the chaplain who took his details was killed the next day with the notebook in his pocket. So William Dunn exists nowhere now but here." She touched the page under Edmund's frozen finger. "And in the third row east of the chapel."

Edmund Voss stood very still.

He thought of his aunt's kitchen. Of Willie, fourteen years old, laughing so hard at his own joke that he fell off the bicycle into a hedge. Of the last letter, which Edmund had read twice and answered in his best fountain hand, correcting nothing, because some part of him—even then—had understood that letters to soldiers were not correspondence but sacrament.

*I answered it promptly,* he thought, absurdly. As if promptness could reach the third row east of the chapel.

When he looked up, his eyes were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it, because hiding things suddenly seemed like the entire disease of which this war was only the worst symptom.

"How many?" he asked.

"In this book? Three hundred and six. Since April."

"Three hundred and six men whose mothers don't know."

"Some know. Some were told late. Some will never be told unless this book gets somewhere it can do damage." She closed the ledger gently, and he watched her hands protect it, and understood that he was looking at the truest document he had ever seen—truer than any census, any report, any column of figures he had ever certified with his signature. "Now you know what kind of man you are, Mr. Voss. You've read your page."

* * *

They sat on upturned ration boxes after that, because standing had become impossible for both of them, and the weak tea Brightwater's orderly had left steaming on the shelf had gone cold between them untouched.

"I could still report you," Edmund said. It needed saying aloud, in fairness to both of them. "Nothing has changed legally. You've confessed to falsification of military records. I've witnessed it."

"Yes."

"If I report you, Willie stays unrecorded. If I stay silent, Willie stays unrecorded. Silence and reporting arrive at the same grave." He turned his tea mug slowly, a full rotation, watching the leaves settle. "Which means the question isn't whether you're punished. The question is whether the rest of the three hundred and six get found."

Tamsin leaned forward. In the lamplight he saw that she was exhausted in the way that only people carrying secrets are exhausted—a weight distributed through the whole body, shoulders to spine to jaw.

"There's a woman in Amiens," she said. "Ada Quill. War correspondent. She's been fighting the censors since 1915—her dispatches come home gutted, half their lines blacked out. But she has been asking, publicly, in print, why casualty lists don't reconcile. She's made herself enough of a nuisance that the censors watch her, which also means she's credible. If this book reached her—if she published even fragments, names, dates, the arithmetic of the missing—there would have to be an inquiry. Corrections. Telegraphs to aunts in Clapham."

"The censors will never let it print."

"Not through normal channels. But she knows the press routes, the pouch systems, which officers take bribes and which take causes. She's the only door I can see." Tamsin met his eyes. "The problem is the road to Amiens runs through three checkpoints, and the ledger cannot travel by post anymore—I tested that in August and lost nine pages to the field censor's office. Someone has to carry it. Under a uniform, in a kit, in a ration crate. Past men whose job is to find exactly this."

Edmund set down the mug. His hands, he noticed, were perfectly steady—the steadiness of shock, which he recognized dimly from the one air raid he'd endured in London, the peculiar calm that arrives when the self you planned to be is destroyed and the next self hasn't yet been issued.

"You're asking me."

"I'm telling you the problem out loud, in a room with one door, because you're the only man at this station who has already read page forty-one and hasn't called for the provost marshal." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "I don't recruit, Mr. Voss. I just stop lying to whoever's already in the water with me."

Outside, the guns resumed. Three shells, a pause. The lamp flame bent and recovered.

Edmund thought of doors. Eleven years of doors, all of them honest, all of them opening onto rooms where people waited to be reduced to numbers. And here, at last, a number that refused to stay a number: *William H. Dunn, third row east of the chapel, tell my mum the dog'll be all right.*

"My train pass allows movement to Amiens for resupply liaison," he said slowly. "Signed by my own office. No one questions a census clerk's itinerary; no one has ever questioned a census clerk's anything—we are furniture." He looked up. "I can carry documents past a checkpoint better than you can, Sister. I look like exactly what I am, which makes me invisible. It's the one gift my life has given me."

"It's not a gift I'd wish on anyone."

"No," Edmund agreed. "But it's mine, and I'm spending it." He extended his hand across the cold tea. "One condition. When this reaches Ada Quill—when Willie's name appears in print, corrections issued, telegrams sent—I want to be the one who writes to my aunt. Not the War Office. Me. She deserves to hear it from family, even if the family news is the worst news there is."

Tamsin took his hand. Her grip was dry, strong, a nurse's grip—the grip of someone who held hands professionally at the exact moment holding on mattered most.

"Bargain struck," she whispered.

And then, because the moment demanded nothing more and the war allowed nothing more, they sat in silence together, two strangers bound now by three hundred and six names and one shared cousin, listening to the guns count down toward a spring none of them could yet imagine.

Neither of them knew—could not yet know—that within the hour, a runner would arrive at the clearing station with a signal flimsy that would change everything: rumors from GHQ that the armistice might come within weeks. And once peace was signed, every record in France would be sealed, crated, and shipped home to be buried in archives deeper than any trench.

The clock, though neither of them wore one that mattered, had begun to run.
