## 6. Three Checkpoints, One Truth

The rumor came in with the wounded, the way all news did at No. 14 Casualty Clearing Station—half-digested, bloodstained, and impossible to verify until it was either true or too late.

Tamsin Amberdale heard it first from a gunner of the 38th Battery who had taken a splinter through his shoulder and talked ceaselessly to keep from thinking about it. She was swabbing the wound while he held forth, and she had learned to let them talk; it was better than morphia for some men.

"They're saying it's weeks," he told her, wincing as she worked. "Weeks, Sister. Not months. The Americans are coming over in numbers now that'd stagger you, and the Boche are falling back like a tide going out. My cousin's with the Ordnance—he says the staff officers have started packing their own kit. Staff officers don't pack early unless they know something."

"Staff officers also said the war would be over by Christmas four years running," she said, and tied off the dressing.

"Not this Christmas." He caught her eye, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen from the wounded in a long time—not hope exactly, but its raw, frightened cousin. "This one feels different, Sister. You can smell it. Even the mud smells different."

She finished her rounds, and the words followed her down the ward like a stray dog. *Weeks.*

By evening she had heard it three more times—from an ambulance driver who claimed a signals man had intercepted something about an armistice commission; from the chaplain, who only raised his eyebrows when asked and refused to speculate, which from him was practically a proclamation; and finally, after lights-out, from Sister Brightwater herself, who came to stand beside her at the ward window while the stoves ticked and the snow came down soft and indifferent on the duckboards outside.

"You've heard it, then," Brightwater said. Not a question.

"Everyone's heard it."

"Mm." The older woman folded her arms, her breath fogging the black glass. "I'll tell you what I told the matron at Étaples before they moved me here. Peace is not a gift, Tamsin. It's a filing deadline."

Tamsin turned to look at her, but Brightwater kept her gaze on the window, her profile carved sharp against the faint glow of the hurricane lamp down the corridor.

"When the guns stop, everything stops with them," Brightwater went on. "The convoys stop. The mail stops moving freely. And the records—" she paused, choosing the word the way she chose instruments, "—the records get sealed. Crated up, indexed, shipped to some archive in London where no living hand will open them for fifty years, if ever. Whatever isn't written down before the armistice is signed will never be written down at all. The dead who aren't in the books by the eleventh of November—or whenever it comes—will simply never have died anywhere official. Their mothers will wait for letters that were never drafted. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Tamsin said quietly.

"Good." Brightwater pushed off from the window. "Then I suggest you stop standing about listening to rumors and start deciding what you intend to do about yours." And she walked away down the corridor, her boots striking the boards with the unhurried authority of a woman who had delivered her verdict and expected it obeyed.

* * *

That night, long after the last lamp was turned low, Tamsin knelt in the stores shed behind the surgical block with a candle stub cupped in her palm and the ration crate open at her feet.

The ledger lay where she always left it, beneath a false bottom of sacking and tins of bully beef: a plain exercise book, army-issue, its cover softened by handling, its pages crowded with her small, upright hand. Forty-one names. She knew the number the way other people knew their own age. Sometimes she woke in the night and counted them again in her head, a rosary of the erased.

Private Albert Fenwick, 2nd Royal Berkshires. Died 4 October, gas. Listed nowhere.

Lance-Corporal Denis O'Rourke, 1st Irish Guards. Died 9 October, wounds. Reported *deserted*, the word entered in the margin of the morning report by a clerk who had never seen him die screaming for his mother in Ward C.

And so on, down the pages, name after name, date after date, cause after cause, each entry witnessed where possible, each written in the flat declarative voice she had taught herself because grief made bad handwriting.

She did not read them tonight. She had learned that reading them at night was a door best left shut. Instead she sat back on her heels and made herself think the way Brightwater had commanded: not as a nurse, not as a sister mourning a brother whose name lived only in this book, but as a quartermaster thinks. As a woman planning a supply line.

*The records get sealed.* If the armistice came in weeks—if it even came at all—the clearing station would begin the slow bureaucratic evacuation that ended every war: wards closed, patients transferred, paperwork packed in numbered crates and consigned to the rear. Her ledger would become contraband in a country of crates. And she could not carry it out alone. That was the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the first name went into the book, and the gunner's rumor had made the sum due.

There were three walls between this shed and print.

She saw them clearly now, laid out like wards on a chart.

*The first is our own gate.* The aid convoy gate at the edge of the station, where every vehicle leaving for the railhead passed under the eye of a transport officer with a manifest and time to kill. Captain Rennie—a careful man, a resentful man, promoted sideways and bitter about it—who had begun cross-referencing manifests against personnel lists ever since a consignment of cigarettes had gone astray in September. He checked kit bags. He checked stretcher slings. He once made Sister Okafor unpack an entire ambulance because the count of blankets was off by two. Anything leaving this station in a vehicle would pass through his pale, patient hands.

*The second is the rail yard at Corbie.* Every road to Amiens ran through it, and the yard was a knot of military police, provost sergeants, and bored sentries with nothing to do but examine passes. A woman in a nurse's cape might pass unremarked; a parcel in her arms would not. Papers were the currency there, and hers were genuine but insufficient—a travel warrant for medical escort duty would cover her going, but not a package going with her, not if anyone thought to ask what was in it.

*The third is Amiens itself.* The press office—the Bureau of the Correspondents, lodged near the rue de la République in a requisitioned hotel with a censor on the ground floor and telegraph wires running out the back like ivy. She had never been inside it. She knew of it only by reputation and by a single letter, six weeks old, that sat hidden with the ledger: a reply from a correspondent named Ada Quill, typed on thin paper, unsigned beyond initials, in answer to a letter Tamsin had nearly not sent.

*You write that you hold names the official record does not,* the letter had said. *I receive forty such claims a week. Most are grief talking. Some are fraud. Two or three, in my experience, are true—and those two or three are worth more than everything the censors have given me in eighteen months. If your names are what you say they are, bring them to me before the war ends, because afterward no editor alive will print anything that reopens an account everyone is desperate to close. Come to the Bureau, ask for A.Q., and be prepared to be disbelieved thoroughly and at length. It is how we keep honest.*

She had read it perhaps a hundred times. *Bring them to me before the war ends.*

The candle guttered. Tamsin trimmed the wick with her thumbnail and looked at the crate, and the plan assembled itself in her mind with the cold clarity of a surgical procedure—each step incised, each risk named aloud in her head so it could not ambush her later.

Three checkpoints. Three doors.

At the convoy gate, the ledger could not travel as cargo. Cargo was searched. It would have to travel as something no officer thought to search—as part of a person. Wounded men were never searched; searching them was against decency and regulation both, and Rennie, whatever else he was, was punctilious about regulation. A kit bag belonging to a man on a stretcher, tucked beneath the canvas at his side, would pass if the man himself was believed. Which meant she needed a wounded man willing to lend his name and his blanket, and a driver willing to look elsewhere while it was done.

At the rail yard, papers were everything, and hers would not stretch far enough alone. But Pip Halloway—the orderly, sixteen years old, all elbows and bravado, who idolized her in the guileless way boys that age idolized any woman who bandaged their split knuckles without mockery—Pip pushed stretchers to and from the ambulance trains twice a week and had never once been asked for anything but a nod. No one searched a boy pushing a stretcher. No one even *saw* a boy pushing a stretcher; they were furniture, part of the landscape of the yard, as invisible as lampposts and twice as reliable. The thought of putting the ledger in Pip's hands tightened something in her chest until it hurt—but she made herself finish the thought anyway, because finishing thoughts was the discipline that separated a plan from a wish.

And at Amiens—the press office—there was no plan yet, only a name and a typed page and the memory of a voice she had heard once on a wireless broadcast, dry and unimpressed and utterly fearless: *Miss Ada Quill, who files from the Somme, though God knows how half her copy gets past the censor.* She would worry about the third door when the first two were behind her. One wall at a time. That was how you got through a night shift; it was how you got through anything.

She blew out the candle and knelt a moment longer in the dark, listening to the snow muffle the world.

Forty-one names in an exercise book. Against them: an empire's filing system, a censor's blue pencil, and a clock that had begun, somewhere east of here, to run.

* * *

In the morning she began, in the manner of all good conspirators, with tea.

She found Sister Brightwater in the kitchen annex at seven, before the day's first convoy of wounded, stirring a pot that had more water than leaf in it and radiating the serene menace of a woman fully awake while the rest of the world was not.

"The convoy goes to the railhead Thursday," Tamsin said. "Six stretchers, walking wounded, resupply run on the way back."

Brightwater stirred. "Captain Rennie will check the manifest."

"He checks everything."

"Yes." A pause, measured out in spoon-turns. "It would want to go with a man, then. Under a blanket. Men under blankets are the one cargo in France he hasn't the nerve to touch—his nerve failed him at Loos, I'm told, and has been very carefully rationed since."

Tamsin blinked. "You've thought about this."

"I think about everything, dear; it's why I appear so calm." Brightwater poured two cups and set one in front of her without asking. "Gunner Sallis, third bed, Ward B. Shoulder wound, healing clean, due for transfer Thursday regardless. He talks constantly, which means he listens constantly, which means he knows everything that happens in this station including, I should imagine, a fair amount he shouldn't. Ask him. If I'm wrong about him, I'll eat my cap."

"And the driver?"

"Corporal Vey. She lost a brother at Gallipoli and another at the Somme, and last month she drove a shell-shocked boy back from the aid post strapped under a tarpaulin because he couldn't bear the sky. Ask her too." Brightwater drank her tea, watching Tamsin over the rim with eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "You'll need more than a lift to the railhead, though. And more than a boy with a stretcher—I can see the boy in your face already, so don't pretend he isn't there. What happens at the other end?"

"Amiens," Tamsin said. "A correspondent. Ada Quill."

For the first time since Tamsin had known her, Sister Brightwater set down her cup with an actual sound, a small decisive click, like a lock turning.

"*Ada Quill.*" She said the name the way one pronounced a diagnosis. "The one the War Office tried to send home in 'sixteen and couldn't. The one whose column the men pass round the trenches folded up to the size of a prayer card." Something moved across the older woman's face—not a smile, quite; the place where smiles began. "Well. If it must leave this station at all, then that's where it ought to go. Though I'll tell you now what I'll tell you once more before Thursday, so you may hear it properly both times." She leaned forward, and the kitchen seemed to lean with her. "If any part of this comes apart—at the gate, at the yard, at her door—it will not come apart quietly. It will come apart on top of whoever is holding the book. Are you prepared for that? Truly?"

Tamsin looked into her tea. She thought of Albert Fenwick's mother writing to the War Office four times and receiving four different answers. She thought of Denis O'Rourke's margin-note, *deserted*, and the wife in Cork still waiting to learn whether to wear black or hope. She thought of her brother, and stopped herself thinking of her brother, because there was no time this morning for the thing underneath everything else.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'll do it anyway."

Brightwater nodded slowly, as if this were the correct answer on an examination. "Then we'll want the boy," she said, rising, and took the pot to the stove. "And Tamsin—"

"Sister?"

"If Rennie stops Thursday's convoy and starts pulling kit bags, you come to me before you do anything brave. Bravery is for emergencies. Planning is for the rest of the time." She glanced back, steam rising around her like weather. "Now drink your tea. The wounded will be here within the hour, and whatever we're about, the work doesn't stop for it. Nothing here ever stops. That's rather the whole trouble."

Outside, the snow had stopped. The light coming through the kitchen's grimy pane was thin and hard and clear, the kind of light that showed every crack in the glass, and Tamsin sat holding her warm cup and felt the fear in her chest change shape—cooling, contracting, taking on edges, becoming something she could aim.

Thursday. The convoy gate. Then Corbie, then Amiens, then a woman named Quill who promised disbelief and meant, by it, a hearing.

Three checkpoints, and one truth, and perhaps five weeks—perhaps fewer—in which to move it through all three.

She drained her tea and went to wake the wards.
