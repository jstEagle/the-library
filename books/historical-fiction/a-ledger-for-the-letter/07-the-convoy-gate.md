## 7. The Convoy Gate

The convoy gate at Bertincourt looked, from a distance, like nothing at all: two wooden posts, a striped barrier arm gone grey with rain, a sandbagged hut with a stovepipe breathing thin smoke into the November morning. Edmund Voss had counted four hundred and eleven such gates in his career as a census officer—gates of factories, workhouses, drill halls—and he had learned that any barrier, however humble, was really just arithmetic wearing a uniform.

This one, he thought as the ambulance jolted to a halt behind three lorries and a water cart, was arithmetic that could hang him.

The ledger rode twelve feet away, in the kit of a wounded man named Corporal Deel, who lay on the second stretcher of the first ambulance with a bandaged thigh and the serene expression of a man dosed to the eyebrows with morphia. Tamsin had sewn it into the canvas lining of the kit herself, at midnight, by the light of a shielded candle, her needle moving with the same unhurried precision she brought to tying off an artery. *No one searches a dead man's kit twice,* she had said. *They search it once for valuables, feel the spine of a book, and think it's a Bible. Every soldier carries a Bible.*

*And if they open it?*

*Then we learn something about ourselves very quickly,* she had answered, and threaded the needle again without looking up.

Edmund had not slept. He sat now in the cab of the ambulance beside Driver Fenwick—a broad, silent woman who smelled of engine oil and peppermints—and watched the transport officer at the barrier work through the lorries ahead of them with the methodical malice of a man who has found his life's purpose in other people's paperwork.

He was maybe fifty, this officer, with a moustache like a scrubbing brush and the collar badges of the Army Service Corps. He did not wave vehicles through. He made them stop, made drivers dismount, made them produce manifests in triplicate, and then—this was the part that tightened Edmund's chest—he read them. Actually read them. Line by line. Lips moving. Then he would look up at the lorry, count what was in it against what was on the page, and either grunt or not grunt. The not-grunt was worse.

"Fenwick," Edmund said quietly. "How long have you run this route?"

"Six weeks." She cracked her window an inch and exhaled peppermint. "Officer's new. Old one used to tap the bonnet and wave you past. This one—" she shrugged, a gesture that encompassed the whole catastrophe of France— "counts."

Of course he counts, Edmund thought. That is precisely the problem. He himself had spent nine years teaching clerks to count properly; he knew the type from the inside. A man who counted things was a man who noticed when numbers refused to reconcile, and somewhere under the floorboards of this convoy, wrapped in oilcloth inside a Bible-shaped bundle inside a morphia-sweetened corporal's kit, was a number that would never reconcile: forty-one names, none of which existed on any manifest in the British Army.

The lorries cleared the gate. The water cart. Now the ambulance ahead of theirs—a Ford with four sitting wounded—stopped, and the transport officer climbed onto its running board and spoke to the driver at length, then leaned in through the door and did something Edmund couldn't see. Counting heads. Of course counting heads. Four men went in, four men came out on paper, four men sat swaying in the back with their eyes closed.

Then it was their turn.

Fenwick braked. The barrier arm stayed down. The transport officer came around the bonnet at a walk, unhurried, a clipboard under his arm, and Edmund understood with sudden terrible clarity that everything he had been since Ypres—the neat ledgers, the reconciled columns, the reputation for incorruptible accuracy—had been rehearsal for exactly this moment, and he had no idea whether the rehearsal had prepared him or ruined him.

"Papers," said the officer.

Fenwick handed down her driving permit and the convoy chit. Edmund passed his own documents through the window: census warrant signed by the Deputy Adjutant-General's office, travel authority stamped at three headquarters, identity disc on its cord. The officer read all of it. Lips moving.

"Census officer," he said. Not a question. His eyes came up and measured Edmund over the rim of the clipboard—measured the civilian greatcoat, the ink-stained fingers, the spectacles fogging at the edges. "You're a long way from a desk, Mr. Voss."

"The war does not pause its paperwork because the roads are muddy," Edmund said. "Personnel movements must be verified at source. I am verifying."

"Verifying what, exactly?"

"Discharges and transfers out of No. 3 Casualty Clearing Station. There are discrepancies between station strength returns and the records held at the base." It was true. God help him, it was true; the discrepancy was forty-one names wide and currently riding in Corporal Deel's kit, but every word of it was true, and Edmund clung to that like a man clinging to a rail in heavy weather. *Tell the truth until you must lie,* Tamsin had said, *and when you must lie, lie about something small.* "A sergeant-major transferred last month whose transfer document was countersigned by an officer who had already been evacuated. Someone is signing for men who are no longer present. I am here to find out how many someones there are."

Something flickered across the transport officer's face—not suspicion, worse than that: recognition. Fellow feeling. The face of a man who had spent his own war chasing signatures that didn't add up.

"Bloody returns," he said, with feeling. "Half the army's on paper twice and the other half isn't on paper at all." He stepped back and rapped the ambulance panel with his knuckles. "Who's aboard?"

"Two stretcher cases for the base hospital," Fenwick said. "One walking wounded discharged to depot. One escort." She jerked a thumb at Edmund.

The officer opened the rear doors.

Edmund had planned, in the sleepless hours before dawn, to remain in the cab. Plans, he discovered, were for people whose hearts were not trying to climb out through their teeth. He heard the doors swing, heard boots on the tailboard, and before he had decided to move his legs were already carrying him around the back of the ambulance with his warrant held up like a lantern.

"Accompanying me," Edmund said. "I interview each man briefly—name, number, unit, date of evacuation. It takes ninety seconds per case and saves the base hospital six weeks of correspondence."

The transport officer looked at him. Inside the ambulance, the light was dim and smelled of carbolic and wet wool. Private Ansell, the walking wounded, sat hunched on the bench with his arm in a sling, watching the officer with the flat vigilance of a man who had learned that authority arrived unpredictably and always wanted something. On the near stretcher lay Sergeant Okafor, feverish, muttering at the ceiling of the ambulance as though arguing with it. And on the far stretcher—

On the far stretcher, Corporal Deel breathed slow and deep, bandaged thigh, kit bag tucked under the stretcher frame beside his hip where Tamsin had wedged it, close enough to be part of him. His eyes were half-open. They found Edmund's. And in them, beneath the morphia haze, was something alert and terrified and trusting, the look of a dog tied up in a room where strangers are searching the furniture.

Edmund felt the strangest sensation: that he was looking at himself.

"Name and number," the transport officer said, and put his hand on the kit bag to move it aside so he could see the stretcher tag.

Everything after that happened in the space of one breath, and Edmund lived the rest of his life inside that breath.

The officer lifted the kit. It swung clear of the stretcher frame, light—too light, a kit should weigh more—and for one absurd instant Edmund saw the whole machinery of his imagination run forward: the canvas slit open, the oilcloth, the sewn spine, the small dense book of names that did not exist, the field telephone, the wire to Amiens, the cell, the wall, Tamsin Amberdale's face when they told her. Forty-one men un-dead in a book and dead twice over in fact.

"Ninety seconds," Edmund repeated, stepping between the officer and the stretcher, and his voice came out not trembling but clipped, dry, bored—the voice of a man who had audited four hundred and eleven gates and found every one of them tedious. "Corporal. Name and number."

Deel blinked. Morphia moved through him like weather. "Deel," he said. "Corporal. Seven-two-nine... four-oh-one." He frowned, as if the numbers had been arranged on a shelf and someone had dusted them out of order. "Sir."

"Evacuated the eleventh, penetrating wound, left thigh," Edmund recited, reading from the stretcher tag upside down with the fluency of long practice. "Transfers to the base surgical division. Which is why," and here he turned and fixed the transport officer with a gaze he had practiced on sub-clerks for nine years, mild and absolute, "the kit travels with him. If you set it down at this gate, Corporal Deel will arrive at the base hospital without his effects, and I will spend my afternoon writing to your commanding officer asking why. I would rather not. You would rather I didn't. Let us both be spared."

The silence lasted three seconds. Edmund counted them, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

The transport officer set the kit back under the stretcher frame. Gently, even. "Effects go with the man," he agreed. "Regulation." He turned to Ansell. "Name and number."

Edmund stood in the swaying ambulance and watched himself from a great distance. *I lied,* he thought. Except he hadn't—that was the thing that frightened him most, the thing that kept surfacing in the following minutes like a body refusing to stay sunk. He had not lied. He had done something far more efficient than lying: he had arranged the truth. He had taken true facts—transfers, tags, regulations, correspondence—and pointed them like a weapon at a man who had no reason to doubt him, and the man had obeyed, because Edmund Voss had a face that made clerks believe in order.

Ansell gave his name. Okafor, fevered, gave three names, only one of which was his, and the transport officer wrote down the right one anyway, because fever was familiar to him. Fenwick produced a chit for the petrol drawn. The barrier arm went up.

"Mind the potholes past the crossroads," the officer said. "And good luck with your someones, Mr. Voss. If you find out how many there are, tell somebody. Half this army's a ghost and nobody will say so."

"We are in complete agreement," Edmund said, and shook his hand.

For four miles neither he nor Fenwick spoke. The ambulance droned. Rain began, fine as sieved flour, ticking on the canvas roof. Behind them, through the little observation hatch, Edmund could see Deel watching him, and after a while Deel raised one hand—an inch off the blanket, no more—and let it fall again, and that was all the conversation they needed.

"You're grey," Fenwick said at last. "There's a flask under the seat. Don't drink it all, it's got to last to Amiens."

Edmund found the flask. Tea, stewed, sweet enough to strip paint. He drank and watched the hedgerows slide past, torn and blackened, and tried to examine what had happened to him at the gate the way he would examine a column of figures: line by line, looking for the error.

Here was the error: he had enjoyed it.

Not the fear. The fear had been genuine and total, a white noise behind the eyes. But underneath the fear, in the exact moment the transport officer had believed him—had *trusted* him—there had been a click, a settling, like a ledger balancing to the penny. Nine years he had spent among figures because figures could not flatter, deceive, or disappoint; because a column that summed correctly was a small honest universe in a dishonest world. And now he had discovered that the same mind that loved an honest column could build a false one out of true parts and watch it pass inspection, and that some cold cellar of his nature had found the building of it *satisfying*.

"I can lie well," he said aloud, before he could stop himself.

Fenwick glanced sideways. "Everyone can, love. War's a teacher."

"No. You don't understand. I've spent my entire life being the man who catches liars. Reconciling. Auditing. My whole profession is founded on the belief that the truth leaves marks—arithmetic doesn't forgive, it only balances or fails to balance. And today I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Today I balanced a falsehood. Perfectly. To the regulation. And the regulation thanked me for it."

Fenwick chewed her peppermint and considered the road. "My husband was a bookkeeper," she said eventually. "Before. Used to say figures never lie. Used to say it like it comforted him." She changed gear as the ambulance took the rise toward the crossroads. "Figures don't lie. People do the lying. Figures just stand there while we decide what to call them. Same as guns. Same as God." She nodded toward the observation hatch, toward Deel, toward the twelve inches of canvas and oilcloth riding under the stretcher frame. "Question isn't whether you can do it, Mr. Voss. Question's what you'll do with the doing. Some men find out they're liars and get careful. Some find out and get useful. Sister Amberdale says you'll know which by Amiens."

"Sister Amberdale says a great many things with great certainty."

"Yes," Fenwick said, and smiled at the road ahead, and would not elaborate.

At the crossroads the rain thickened. The potholes, faithfully reported, arrived on schedule and tried to shake the ambulance apart, and Deel groaned once, and the kit bag shifted against the stretcher frame, and Edmund reached back through the hatch—his hand acting before any permission was granted—and steadied it, and left his hand there, resting on the canvas over the hidden spine of the book, for perhaps a mile longer than steadying required.

It was near dusk when the base hospital's lights appeared, a scatter of shaded lamps along a ridge. And with them, parked outside the gate, unmistakable even in the failing light, a staff car flying the pennant of the Deputy Adjutant-General's office—the office that had issued Edmund's warrant, the office to which, within the week, his full census report was owed.

Standing beside the car, checking a pocket watch, was Colonel Ravelsham himself.

"He's early," Fenwick murmured. "They said he wasn't due till Thursday."

Edmund looked at the colonel, and at the hospital, and at the ambulance's rear doors, behind which forty-one impossible names waited to be carried inside. He realized that he was about to shake hands with the man who had given him his career, and that he would have to do it wearing the face he had invented four hours ago at a wooden gate in the rain.

He straightened his collar. The face came easily. That was the frightening part—it came easily.
