## 8. Sister Brightwater's Kitchen

The kitchen at the rear of the clearing station had once been a dairy, and it still smelled of stone and cold milk and, faintly, of the cows that had been driven off three years ago to feed an army. Sister Brightwater kept a fire going in the old hearth no matter what the coal situation was officially declared to be. Tamsin had stopped wondering how she managed it. Some women could conjure supplies out of thin air and sheer authority; Brightwater simply looked at quartermasters until they found things.

Tamsin came through the door with Edmund Voss behind her, and the warmth hit her like a wall. She hadn't realized how cold she'd been until her fingers began to ache as they thawed.

"Sit," said Brightwater, without turning from the stove.

It was not a suggestion. Tamsin sat. Edmund hesitated at the threshold like a man unsure whether he'd been invited into a church or a court-martial, until Brightwater glanced over her shoulder and said, "You too, Mr. Voss. The chair by the woodbox. Mind the splinters."

He sat. Tamsin watched him do it — carefully, precisely, arranging himself on the broken chair as though it were an examination desk. Even now, even here, the census taker counted things. She saw his eyes flick to the rows of preserving jars on the shelf, the stack of enamel plates, the kettle. He couldn't help it. He was a man made of arithmetic, and she was beginning to suspect that this was exactly why he'd noticed what everyone else had managed not to see.

Brightwater set down three cups and poured. The tea was the color of weak amber and smelled mostly of hot water, but steam rose from it, and that alone felt like luxury.

"You look terrible," Brightwater said to Tamsin. It was said mildly, the way one remarks on the weather.

"I've been told."

"You've been told by people who were being kind. I'm telling you because it's true." She sat down across from them, folding her hands — those enormous, capable hands, scarred at the knuckles from years of boiling water and broken glass. "Now. Before you say anything, Miss Amberdale: I have known about your book for four months."

* * *

Tamsin's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Edmund looked between them. "Four months?"

"March," said Brightwater. "Perhaps early April. You hid it inside the flour sack behind the oat tins, which was clever, except that I do the inventory, dear, and a flour sack that never grows lighter does tend to draw attention." She took a sip of her tea, unhurried. "I didn't look inside for six weeks. Then one night we lost eleven men in the gas ward and I couldn't sleep, and I thought: if that woman is writing letters home at three in the morning, I'll let her be. And if she's writing something else—" She shrugged, a small economical motion. "Well. I looked."

"And you didn't report me."

"I did not report you."

"Why?"

Brightwater set her cup down with a soft click. For a moment her composure — that famous composure, the one the orderlies swore could stop bleeding by itself — flickered, and beneath it Tamsin saw something old and tired and grieving.

"Because my sister died at Loos," she said, "and for eight months the War Office insisted she had not. Missing, they wrote. *Missing.* As though she'd wandered off to find herself somewhere behind the lines. My mother wrote forty-one letters. Forty-one. She numbered them, because she was a schoolteacher and numbering things was how she kept from screaming." Brightwater's voice stayed level; only her hands betrayed her, tightening around each other until the scars went white. "The forty-second got a reply. A form. *Regret to inform.* No date of death. No place. No grave. Just a number where a person used to be, and my mother lived another two years waiting for a daughter who was already chalk on someone's tally."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall, the wards murmured their night sounds — moaning, coughing, the squeak of a stretcher wheel.

"So when I found a nurse keeping names that the morning reports had dropped," Brightwater went on, "I decided that God and the Matron-in-Chief could both forgive me for looking the other way. I restocked the flour myself so no one else would count it." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce. "That is why. Now tell me why there is a census man sitting on my woodbox chair at half past ten at night, and why Pip Halloway has been hovering outside that window for twenty minutes pretending to fetch water."

* * *

Pip came through the door with a bucket he clearly had not filled, his ears crimson.

"I weren't hovering, Sister. I was — the pump's frozen, see, and I thought—"

"You thought correctly that I keep a jug under the sink. Sit down and have tea." Brightwater poured a fourth cup before he'd finished sitting. "Well?"

Tamsin glanced at Edmund. He straightened, and she recognized the gesture — the little professional settling of shoulders he performed before delivering figures. But when he spoke, the numbers had gone out of his voice.

"There are seventeen men buried behind the east hedge," he said, "who do not exist according to the records I am required to reconcile. There is a ledger containing their names and others — I don't know how many others. I found it by accident. By counting." His mouth twisted. "I'm very good at counting. It's apparently all I'm good for. Except that last week I read one page of that ledger and found my cousin's name in it, and since then the arithmetic has stopped making sense to me."

"What do you want, then?" Brightwater asked.

"To get it out," Edmund said. "To Amiens. To Ada Quill. Before the armistice — if it comes — seals everything in a warehouse forever."

"And if it doesn't come? If they drag the war into another winter?"

"Then we try anyway."

Brightwater studied him for a long moment, the way she studied a wound before deciding whether to pack it or probe it. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her.

"The armistice will come," she said quietly. "My brother-in-law is at GHQ as a clerk. He writes to me when he can, which is against regulations and therefore reliable. There are men in silk hats in a railway carriage somewhere having conversations, and when those conversations end, every record in this sector gets crated, sealed, and shipped within seventy-two hours. After that, finding a single omitted name would take a solicitor, a Member of Parliament, and twenty years." She turned to Tamsin. "How far along is the route?"

Tamsin laid it out on the scrubbed table with a fingertip drawn through spilled tea: the aid convoy gate at the perimeter, the rail yard at Rouvroy, the press office in Amiens. Three checkpoints. The convoy gate was manifest-driven — a transport officer named Hessler who cross-referenced everything twice. The rail yard was military police and forged passes. Amiens itself would be censors' territory, though a press office had its own traffic, its own chaos.

"Hessler," Brightwater repeated. "He caught young Dr. Fenwick bringing in cognac last month. Made him empty his kitbag in the snow." Her eyes narrowed. "What's your plan for him?"

"A wounded man's kit," Tamsin said. "Nobody searches deep into a stretcher case's belongings. We move Private Ollerenshaw — he's being transferred anyway, blind in one eye, docile, sleeps through anything. The ledger goes flat against the bottom board of his kit, wrapped in oilcloth, under his spare shirt."

"And the pass for whoever walks beside the stretcher?"

"That's where it gets difficult."

"It's where it got difficult already." Brightwater rose, went to the dresser, and stood a moment with her back to them, one hand resting on the wood as though steadying herself. When she turned, she held a small oilskin packet, tied with string gone soft with age.

She set it on the table between the cups.

"My sister's papers," she said. "Identification card, travel permit, the letter from the matron at Étaples vouching for her character. Edith Brightwater. She was twenty-six when she died. I've kept these for three years because throwing them away felt like burying her twice." Her voice caught — just once, just slightly — and then resumed its level calm. "The photograph is close enough in age and coloring to pass for you in bad light, Tamsin. Edith was fairer, but nobody looks at photographs closely at midnight. The name is common enough. If you're stopped anywhere between here and Amiens, you are Edith Brightwater, volunteer nurse, traveling to rejoin her unit."

Tamsin stared at the packet. Something rose in her throat that had nothing to do with tea or smoke.

"Sister. I can't take—"

"You can and you will." Brightwater sat back down and picked up her cup as if they were discussing linen stores. "A dead woman's name is the one thing in this war the censors can't trace. Edith has been missing from the world for three years; she may as well be useful. My mother would have liked that. My mother liked usefulness above almost everything except honesty." She smiled then, briefly, terribly. "And I am giving you both, dear. Usefulness and honesty. Don't waste either."

* * *

Pip broke the silence, because Pip always did — the boy couldn't bear stillness the way other boys couldn't bear boredom.

"Begging pardon, Sister Brightwater, but if it's passes wanted for the rail yard, I could—"

"No," said Tamsin and Edmund together.

"You haven't heard the plan!"

"There isn't a plan in which you come," Tamsin said, "and that is final."

Pip set down his cup with elaborate care, which in Pip meant fury. He was sixteen and built like a whip handle, all sinew and freckles, with a cowlick that refused every regulation. He had carried stretchers through mud that swallowed grown men whole. He had held a dying sergeant's hand for four hours on a stretcher table because the morphine was gone and a hand was all they had left to give. And they spoke to him — all of them, even kindly — as though he were furniture that occasionally talked.

"I've pushed more stretchers than anyone here," he said. "Nobody looks twice at an orderly with a litter. Nobody *searches* an orderly. You know it's true, Miss Amberdale. You've watched them wave me past a dozen times."

"They wave you past because you're supposed to be here, Pip. Not on a road to Amiens."

"Then give me a reason to be on a road to Amiens and I'll be there." His jaw set. In the firelight Tamsin saw, with a strange lurch, not a boy but a young man assembling himself in front of her, plank by plank. "Sergeant Doyle died holding my hand. Eleven men in the gas ward. Mr. Voss's cousin. Your brother, miss — yes, I know about your brother, I've seen you write his name twice." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "Someone carried that ledger's worth of forgetting to all of them. Let me carry it back."

No one answered immediately. The fire ticked. Brightwater refilled Pip's cup, and the small ceremony of it — the tilt of the kettle, the rising thread of steam — somehow held the whole argument in suspension.

"We'll speak of it tomorrow," Brightwater said at last, in the tone that ended debates on wards and in kitchens alike. "Tonight, everyone eats. There's bread and there's dripping and there's a jar of onions Mrs. Collett smuggled me in October, and you'll all have some, including you, Mr. Voss, especially you — you look like a man who's been living on his own conscience, and there's less nourishment in that than in my tea."

* * *

They ate. It was absurd how good it was — stale bread softened in the heat, dripping salted heavily enough to make up for everything it lacked, onions sharp enough to bring tears that could be blamed on them. Edmund ate slowly, watching the room as though memorizing it, and perhaps he was; Tamsin was beginning to understand that this was how he loved things, by counting them, by fixing them in columns that could not be lost.

Brightwater talked while they ate — not of the route or the risk, but of nothing. Of Mrs. Collett's goats. Of the chaplain's disastrous sermons. Of the time in 1915 when she had served a visiting general weak tea in a chipped cup and he had complimented it and she had never let the station forget it. And Tamsin realized, somewhere between the second slice of bread and the third, what Brightwater was doing. She was building a room around them. She was making four strangers into something with walls and a roof, because she knew — had known for months, had known since Loos — that what they were about to attempt could not be survived by individuals. Only families got through nights like the ones coming. Families, or nothing.

When the plates were cleared, Pip fell asleep in his chair mid-sentence, his head tipping against the wall, the bucket still hooked over his arm. He'd fetched the water after all, at some point. Or claimed he had.

Brightwater draped a blanket over him without waking him, then stood looking down at the boy for a long moment.

"He'll do it whether we allow it or not," she said quietly. "You understand that. Better we choose the hour than he chooses it himself at dawn with no pass and no cover story."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"He's sixteen."

"He's what we have."

"Yes." Brightwater turned. In the low light her face was unreadable, and then it wasn't — grief and resolve and something fiercely tender, all of it passing like weather. "Then we do it properly. Tomorrow I'll write Edith a travel permit extending her leave. My hand is steady and my matron's stamp hasn't faded. And you, Miss Amberdale, will sleep — actually sleep, in an actual bed, for four hours, because whatever happens at that convoy gate, you cannot walk into it with your hands shaking."

Tamsin should have protested. Instead she heard herself say, "Yes, Sister," in the voice she'd used since her first day of training, obedient and small, and found that she meant it.

At the door, Edmund paused. "Sister Brightwater. Why help us? All of this — the papers, the food, the risk to yourself. You could lose your position. Worse."

Brightwater considered the question with her head slightly tilted, as though it were an interesting symptom.

"When Edith's form came," she said, "my mother pinned it to the kitchen wall. Not framed. Pinned. So that every morning she'd see what the government thought a person amounted to when the person was inconvenient." She gathered the cups onto a tray. "I have spent three years wanting to answer that form. This is my answer. Good night, Mr. Voss. Count carefully — it seems to matter what gets counted, after all."

* * *

Tamsin lay in the narrow bed in the nurses' quarters with Edith Brightwater's identity papers beneath her pillow, stiff as a psalm book, and did not sleep for the first hour despite strict instructions.

Through the frost-furred window she could see the east hedge, black against a sky going the color of dishwater, and beneath the hedge the seventeen mounds she had tended through autumn, weeded and squared and marked with crosses cut from ration crates. Men the reports had declined to remember. Tomorrow, God willing, some of them would begin the journey toward paper — toward print, toward mothers, toward kitchen walls where forms could finally be taken down and replaced with something true.

Below her window, a light still burned in the kitchen. Through the frosted glass she saw Brightwater's shadow move — washing up, banking the fire, and then, unmistakably, sitting down at the table again and remaining there, hands folded, in the posture of a woman keeping vigil. Keeping watch over a sleeping boy and a dead sister and a plan that would either honor them all or destroy them.

Tamsin closed her eyes.

She woke, what felt like minutes later, to shouting in the yard — the clang of the alarm bell, boots on frozen mud, and the voice of the duty orderly pitched high and cracking:

"Convoy's moved up! Gate inspection team arrived overnight — they're checking outbound transfers starting at dawn!"

Dawn. Not next week. Dawn.

Tamsin was out of bed with Edith Brightwater's name pressed against her heart, and downstairs the kitchen light was already burning brighter.
