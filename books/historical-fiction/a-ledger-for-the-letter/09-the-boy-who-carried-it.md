## 9. The Boy Who Carried It

Pip Halloway had learned early that the trick to being sixteen in a war was to be useful enough that nobody looked too closely at you. Useful like a spanner. Useful like a bucket. Nobody interrogates a bucket.

So he was the first one through the door of Sister Brightwater's kitchen when the voices went low and serious — because low, serious voices meant a job nobody wanted, and jobs nobody wanted meant jobs Pip could have.

He'd been hauling water for the boiler, sleeves soaked to the elbow, fingers gone red and stupid with cold, when he saw them: Sister Tamsin Amberdale and that census man, Voss, standing at the head of the long table where the maps of Amiens were spread out like a half-eaten dinner. The tea was going cold in its pot. Nobody was drinking it.

"—rail yard is the only way," Sister Amberdale was saying. Her voice had the flat calm Pip had come to know on her worst nights, the calm she wore like a splint over something broken underneath. "The road convoys are stopped at the aid gate now. Mail's guarded since the inspection. But the ambulance trains still run, and they still need orderlies."

"And the third checkpoint is at the press office in Amiens," Voss said. He had a face built out of ledgers, all ruled lines and careful sums, but his eyes kept flicking to the ration crate on the sideboard the way a man keeps touching a bruise. "Which means we get past it or we don't. There is no cleverness after that point."

"There's never cleverness," Sister Amberdale said. "There's only who's willing."

Pip set down his buckets with a clank that made them both jump.

"I'll carry it."

They turned. Voss looked at him the way officers look at boys — measuring him for a uniform he wasn't old enough to wear. Sister Amberdale didn't look at him like that. She looked at him like she knew exactly how old he was, which was worse.

"Pip," she began.

"No, hear me out." He wiped his hands on his apron and came to the table, planting both palms flat on the map of the rail yard, right across the sidings, because you couldn't argue with a boy whose hands were on the map. "You're a woman pushing through a rail yard at four in the morning, they'll want your papers, your orders, your reason. Mr. Voss, no offense, sir, but you've got *census* written all over your face. Any military policeman takes one look at you and starts asking questions just for the pleasure of it." He tapped the paper. "But me? I'm an orderly. I push stretchers. I push them day and night, up ramps, over mud, onto trains. Nobody searches a boy with a stretcher. Nobody searches the boy who's been carrying their mates all week. They pat his shoulder and tell him he's a good lad and wave him through."

"That's precisely why they'll be watching for it if they've been tipped off—" Voss started.

"Has anyone been tipped off?"

Voss's mouth did something complicated. "The matron has guards on the parcels. The matron does not yet know the ledger has legs."

"Then nobody's watching for me." Pip grinned, because grinning was free and it usually worked. "I've pushed four hundred stretchers since spring. I could do it asleep. I *have* done it asleep, probably. Ask anyone."

He expected Sister Amberdale to say no. She had a way of saying no that wasn't unkind but was absolutely final, like a gate closing. He'd already got his argument loaded — about how he knew every siding and every ramp, about how he was quick, about how he weighed nothing and could run if he had to —

Instead she looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted, some weight moving from one hand to another.

"You know what happens if you're caught," she said quietly. "Not to us. To *you*. You're not a soldier. You're a child in a hospital uniform carrying stolen documents through a military rail yard under shellfire. They won't shoot you, but they'll send you home in disgrace, and the story will follow you the rest of your life. *The boy who helped.*"

Pip felt the grin slide off his face, and underneath it he found — unexpectedly — something steadier.

"My brother Tom went missing at Loos," he said. "Two years ago. The letter came and it said *missing, presumed* — presumed what, they never even finished writing. My mum waited three years for a sentence to finish." He swallowed. "You told Sister Brightwater once, when you thought I was asleep in the kitchen — you said there were men the reports left out. Men nobody finished the sentence for. Well." His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed on anyway. "Tom might be one of yours. In your book. And if he is, then someone ought to carry it who knows what it costs to wait."

The kitchen was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere out in the wards a man coughed the deep wet cough that Pip had stopped flinching at months ago and now simply counted, the way you count bells.

Sister Amberdale reached out and put her hand over his on the table. Her fingers were cold and strong.

"The ledger goes inside a folded stretcher blanket," she said. "Under a patient. You'll have a real patient too — Corporal Dane, going down to Amiens for the eye hospital, poor soul can't see a thing past his own nose and won't notice anything. You'll be a boy doing his ordinary work, carrying one extra thing that weighs less than a loaf of bread."

"Yes, Sister."

"If anyone stops you—"

"I'm Pip Halloway, orderly, third casualty clearing station, taking Corporal Dane to the ambulance train, and here's my chit, and my mate wrote it so it looks proper." He'd rehearsed it already. He'd rehearsed it in his head forty times before he'd ever opened his mouth, because that was the other trick to being sixteen in a war: always arrive looking like you were sent.

Voss cleared his throat. "It should be noted," he said, in the voice of a man noting things against his will, "that this is the single most reckless proposal I have heard in two years of counting doors."

"Thank you, sir," said Pip brightly.

"It wasn't praise."

"It sounded like praise."

And there it was again — the almost-smile on Sister Amberdale's face, gone as fast as it came, but Pip saw it, and he banked it like a coal.

* * *

They gave him the afternoon to prepare, which Pip spent doing the most important preparation of all: nothing unusual whatsoever.

He pushed stretchers. He hauled water. He mopped where the mopping wanted doing and swore at the boiler until it behaved. He let Sergeant Mole bawl him out for leaving a wheel chock loose, and took it without cheek, because a boy who'd been shouted at recently looked like a boy with nothing to hide. Around him the clearing station went on with its winter business — the frost furring the guy-ropes silver, the smell of carbolic and coal smoke and the sweetish stink beneath everything that Pip had long ago stopped naming even in his own head.

At dusk he went to see Corporal Dane.

Dane sat propped in the corner cot of Ward C, bandages wrapped around his eyes like a boxer's, hands folded on his chest with the terrible patience of a man learning the world by sound. He was twenty-three and from Sheffield and before the gas he'd been a piano tuner, which he mentioned roughly nine times a day, and Pip loved him for it, the way you love anything that insists on continuing.

"Dane," Pip said, sitting on the edge of the cot. "How would you feel about a trip to Amiens?"

"They tell me Amiens exists," Dane said. "I'll take their word."

"Ambulance train tomorrow, four o'clock. Eye hospital. Proper specialists." Pip hesitated, then heard himself add, "I'm taking you down myself."

Dane's head tilted toward him, those bandaged eyes finding him anyway, the way they always did. "That so. Out of the goodness of your heart, Halloway?"

"Out of the fact that nobody else wants to get up at three."

Dane laughed, and reached out, and gripped Pip's wrist — lightly, carefully, the grip of a man recalibrating all his distances. "You're a good lad," he said. "Whatever it is you're not telling me, you be careful with it."

Pip's stomach dropped through the floorboards of himself. "I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." Dane settled back. "Piano tuners learn to hear what isn't played. Go on, get out. Wake me at half three."

* * *

At half past three in the morning the world was black iron and white frost, and the ledger was under his hands.

They'd done it in Brightwater's kitchen by candle stub: the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, flat as a hymnal, sewn into the fold of a stretcher blanket with thread Brightwater bit off with her teeth. Then Dane laid on top of it, six feet of oblivious Yorkshire wool and bandages, and the whole thing became — nothing. A stretcher. One of hundreds. The most boring object in France.

"Weight check," whispered Brightwater, lifting one end. "Even. Good. If it sags on one side, walk slower, don't adjust your grip."

"Sister," Pip said, "I've carried men heavier than this with their leg off."

"I know," she said. "That's why you'll be careful. Confident men drop things."

Voss stood apart by the door, holding a lantern he hadn't lit. Up close, in the dark, he didn't look like a census man. He looked like a man who had counted something he couldn't stop counting. "Halloway," he said. "If it goes wrong at the yard — if you're stopped — you know nothing. You found the ledger nowhere. It isn't yours, it isn't ours, it fell from the sky."

"Fell from the sky," Pip repeated solemnly. "Very good, sir. Right into a sewn-up blanket."

"Halloway."

"Sir?"

Voss seemed to wrestle with something. What came out, finally, quietly, was: "My cousin is in that book. Second page. I only learned last week." He paused. "Carry it like it's heavy."

And Pip, who had spent his whole life being told he was too young to understand things, understood that completely.

Sister Amberdale walked with him as far as the lane behind the laundry. She didn't say anything for a while. The stars were out hard and cold, the kind of sky that made the guns feel far away even when they weren't.

"Pip," she said at last. "Come back."

"It's a rail yard, Sister, not the Somme."

"Come back anyway." She pulled something from her pocket and pressed it into his hand: a small tin, dented, that rattled. "Boiled sweets. From my brother. He used to say courage runs on sugar." Her voice caught on *brother*, just barely, and smoothed itself over. "Eat one before the yard. Eat the rest after."

He ate one before the yard. It tasted of peppermint and somebody's kitchen a hundred years ago, and it steadied him more than any speech could have.

* * *

The trouble started at twenty past four, and it started with the sky.

Not rain — the night was clear as glass. It was the guns, away east, that had been muttering all night like bad weather deciding, and then, between the clearing station and the rail yard, they stopped muttering and started talking.

The first shell came down somewhere behind them, close enough that the frost jumped off the ground in a white sheet and the concussion slapped Pip's back like a giant's hand. Dane cried out — not from the blast, Pip realized, but from the dark of it, the blind man's terror of a world that moved without warning.

"All right!" Pip shouted over the ringing. "All right, Corp, I've got you! Just noise! Just noise!"

"Can't see," Dane gasped, absurdly apologetic. "Can't see which way—"

"You don't need to! I've got the eyes tonight!" And Pip laughed — actually laughed — because the alternative was to be sixteen and terrified, and laughing cost less.

He ran the stretcher along the trackside path he'd run a hundred times, wheels jolting on sleepers, breath smoking, the shells walking nearer and nearer with that horrible casual stride they had, *whump... whump... whump*, like something enormous crossing a room toward you. A flare went up over the yards ahead and turned the whole world into a photograph: rails shining like knives, the great humped shapes of the ambulance trains, the signal box with its windows boarded, and — small, absurd — a cat on a stack of timber, watching the fireworks with professional indifference.

Another shell. Closer. Earth and cinders rained down, pattering on the blanket, on Dane, on Pip's neck, and the stretcher handles bucked in his grip and for one lurching second the whole load twisted and he felt the ledger's corner shift against the canvas—

—and his hands did the thing Brightwater had drilled into him without either of them knowing it was drilling: they stopped. Everything stopped. The running, the panic, the boy. Just the hands, level, patient, keeping the weight even, keeping the weight safe, while the world detonated around a boy standing very still on a railway line holding a stretcher like it was the last solid thing in creation.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and Pip discovered he was already moving again, faster than before, and that he had stopped whistling somewhere back along the path without noticing, and that his mouth was saying something over and over, quiet, under the shellfire:

"Tom. Tom. Tom. Tom."

Not a prayer. A name. Names were what the whole thing was for.

He came down the ramp to the ambulance train platform with his ears roaring and his knees water and his heart going like a drummer boy's, and there was the train breathing steam, and there was the medical officer waving him up, and there was the ramp, and Pip pushed Corporal Dane aboard steady as Sunday, and handed him off to a burly RAMC sergeant with hands like hams, and stood there shaking in the steam-light while the train groaned and gathered itself.

"You all right, son?" the sergeant asked.

"Fine," said Pip, and found the tin in his pocket, and ate another sweet with hands that wouldn't quite behave, peppermint blooming through the terror like spring through frost.

The train began to move. Amiens lay forty minutes east through the frozen dark, and at the far end of the journey there was a rail yard checkpoint, a military policeman, a forged pass in Pip's breast pocket, and a ledger sewn into a blanket rolling eastward toward the press office like a slow, secret heartbeat.

Pip watched the clearing station lights sink behind them and touched the tin in his pocket — one sweet left after this, he calculated, and decided to save it for whatever came next.

He didn't know yet that at the rail yard, under the arc lamps, a military policeman with a clipboard was already checking passes against a list, and that the list had been updated at midnight.

He found that out at 4 a.m., and it nearly ended everything.
