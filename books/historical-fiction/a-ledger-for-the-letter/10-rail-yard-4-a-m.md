## 10. Rail Yard, 4 A.M.

The trouble with lying, Pip Halloway decided somewhere in the third hour of pushing a stretcher through the dark, was that it required you to keep being the person the lie described. He had told Sister Brightwater he wasn't afraid. He had told Tamsin Amberdale that a pass was only paper and paper couldn't hurt anyone. He had told himself, most convincingly of all, that sixteen was nearly seventeen and seventeen was nearly a man and men did not shake.

He was shaking now, but his hands were busy on the stretcher handles, so maybe nobody could tell.

The rail yard at four in the morning was a country unto itself. Steam lay along the ground like something drowned, waist-high and grey, and out of it rose the black shoulders of wagons, ambulance cars with their white circles painted dull by mud, flatbeds stacked with crates that might have contained rifles or rations or coffins—Pip had stopped trying to read the stencils months ago, because reading them meant knowing, and knowing was a weight a boy could not carry and still walk straight. Somewhere to the east an engine breathed in its sleep, chuffing softly, like a horse dreaming. The lamps on their iron posts made cones of yellow light, and between the cones the darkness was absolute, and everyone moved from cone to cone as if crossing a river on stones.

The stretcher weighed nothing. That was the joke of it. The man on it—Corporal Dawes, discharged, one lung gone to gas and the other going—weighed almost nothing either, nine stone of skin and apology, asleep under two blankets with his kit bag tucked against his hip. And inside the kit bag, wrapped in a spare shirt and a pair of socks that smelled of carbolic, lay the ledger.

Pip had held it exactly once, for eleven seconds, when Tamsin had transferred it from her coat into the bag at the last moment before they left Brightwater's kitchen. Eleven seconds was enough. He knew the weight of it now the way you know a stone you've stepped on in the dark: small, dense, final. A book of names. A book of men the war had swallowed and spat out without paperwork, so that officially they had never existed at all, and unofficially—the way that mattered, the way mothers mattered—they were still waiting at gates and windows for news that would never come unless a boy pushed a stretcher past a military policeman at four in the morning and said nothing clever and nothing stupid and nothing at all if he could manage it.

*No one searches a boy pushing a stretcher.* That had been his own argument, offered in the kitchen with the tea steam rising, feeling brave as a recruiting poster. Sister Brightwater had looked at him over her spectacles with an expression he couldn't read then and understood perfectly now: she had been measuring him, the way you measure timber before you ask it to bear a roof.

Ahead, at the mouth of Platform Two, stood the lamp and under the lamp stood the policeman.

Pip had seen military policemen before, dozens of them, red caps bobbing above crowds at every station from Rouen to the coast, and had thought nothing of them. But those had been policemen among people. This one stood alone, and alone he became a different animal entirely—a gate with legs, a question wearing a greatcoat. He was young, younger than Pip had expected, with a moustache that looked drawn on in pencil and hadn't decided yet whether to commit. His breath came out white. His hands were jammed in his pockets, and every few seconds he shifted his weight, boot to boot, the way men do when standing still hurts more than walking.

Tamsin walked beside the stretcher, on the side away from the lamp, and she had not spoken since the yard began. She wore her nurse's cape with the hood up and her face beneath it was the face she wore on the wards, which Pip had learned to read like weather: calm meant everything was fine; very calm meant everything was terrible and she had decided you didn't need to know.

Twenty yards. Fifteen. The policeman raised his hand—not sharply, wearily, the gesture of a man who had done this forty times tonight—and the white cone of lamplight took them all.

"Passes."

Pip's mouth went dry as chalk dust. He fumbled at his tunic with fingers gone stupid, and got the folded paper out, and held it up, and the policeman took it and opened it under the light and read it the way you'd read a letter from a solicitor: twice, slowly, lips moving slightly on the second pass.

It was a good forgery. Pip knew that because Sister Brightwater had made it herself, from a genuine pass she had altered with ink and patience and what she called *a steady hand and thirty years of forging doctors' signatures*. It named him as an orderly attached to No. — Casualty Clearing Station, conveying a discharged patient to — for onward transport. The blanks were filled in correctly. The stamps were convincing. Pip had watched her make it and had felt the thrill of it, the delicious criminal fizz of it, and had thought: *this is easy.*

But the policeman turned it over. And on the back, where there should have been nothing, there was the shadow of another stamp, half-erased with a razor blade, and the policeman's thumbnail found it and rested there, and Pip watched the thumbnail and forgot how to breathe.

"Where's this man posted?" the policeman asked, nodding at Dawes, who slept on with his mouth open, mercifully, beautifully unconscious.

"Home," Pip said. His voice came out an octave wrong, a boy's voice, a child caught in an orchard. "I mean—discharged. Blighty. Gas." He heard himself say it and winced. *Gas,* as if it were a town in Kent.

The policeman looked at him. Under the lamp his eyes were pale and tired and not unkind, which somehow was worse, because unkind eyes you could hate, and hating gave you somewhere to put the fear.

"And your pass," the policeman said, "is for a boy orderly, age eighteen, name of—" he squinted at the paper, though plainly he had already memorized it, "-Halloway, P. J. How old are you?"

The world narrowed to the size of that question. Pip heard the engine breathing to the east. He heard Dawes's ruined whistle of a snore. He heard, absurdly, the memory of his mother's voice saying *stand up straight when you're spoken to,* and he stood up straight, and lied again because there was nothing left but lying: "Eighteen, sir."

"You're not," the policeman said. Not cruelly. Almost sadly. "I've got brothers. I know eighteen when I see it. You're fifteen if you're a day, and this pass is forged, and that makes it my job to detain you, son, and send for the provost officer, and believe me—" and here he glanced down the empty platform, at the drowned grey dark, at the war itself, "-you don't want to be detained tonight. There are men in the guardroom who've been waiting since Tuesday for someone new to talk to, and none of what they say is pleasant."

A hand came down on the stretcher rail, beside Pip's own, and it was Tamsin's hand, and it settled there the way a hand settles on a fence rail, casually, belonging.

"There seems to be some confusion," she said.

Her voice was different. Pip had never heard it quite like this—ward-calm but pitched lower, slower, with a weariness woven through it so fine it read as authority. The policeman straightened. Nurses outranked nobody and everybody; a sister's voice at four in the morning carried the specific gravity of every hour of sleepless work behind it.

"Sister," the policeman said.

"This boy is my patient."

Pip's stomach dropped through the sleepers, through the ballast, through the earth. *Patient?* He opened his mouth and her hand pressed the rail, once, lightly, and he closed it again.

"Patient," the policeman repeated.

"He is sixteen years old," Tamsin said, "which his mother wrote on his enlistment papers and his recruiting sergeant wrote differently, because the difference between a mother's arithmetic and a quota is a boy in France a year early. He was brought to us three weeks ago with shell shock after Passchendaele—he was a stretcher-bearer before that, at thirteen months underage, and he has been carrying other people's wounds since he should have been carrying books. He does not sleep. He talks in his sleep, when he manages it. He startles at engines." She paused, and the pause was surgical. "You woke him at four in the morning, Corporal, and asked him questions in a loud voice about papers, and he answered badly, because answering well is precisely what his injury prevents. So I will answer for him. The pass is mine—I countersigned it—and any irregularity in it is mine, and you may take it up with Matron's office at clearing station number — at eight o'clock, when the telegraph lines open, or you may look at the patient in front of you and use your own judgement about whether a boy who has spent a year carrying dying men deserves to spend the rest of this night in a guardroom."

Silence. The engine breathed. Dawes snored his thin whistle, on cue, as if even his ruined lung had enlisted in the cause.

The policeman looked at the pass. Then at Pip. And Pip, understanding at last what was wanted of him—not a lie, worse than a lie, a truth worn as a mask—did the thing his body had been rehearsing for a year without his consent. He let his shoulders come forward. He let his hands tremble visibly on the stretcher handles. He let his eyes fix on the middle distance, on nothing, the thousand-yard stare he had seen on a hundred faces in the wards, and he let his lip go, just slightly, unsteady.

He did not have to act much. That was the terrible discovery. The fear was real; he only aimed it.

The policeman's face changed. Something behind the pencil moustache softened and aged ten years. He looked at Dawes, sleeping. He looked at the kit bag, and Pip felt the ledger's weight leap into his own chest as if the book had jumped bodies—but the policeman's glance slid off the bag the way glances slide off anything a nurse is standing over. You did not search a sister's patient. You did not search the space around grief. There were rules underneath the rules, and the policeman knew them.

"Get him home, Sister," the policeman said quietly. "Both of them. Before the next relief comes on—the lad on duty after me counts everything twice and writes it down three times." He handed back the pass. His thumbnail had left the erased stamp alone. "And Sister—" he hesitated, and for a moment the gate with legs was only a boy in a greatcoat, twenty-two maybe, cold, far from his own brothers, "-the boy carrying stretchers. Tell whoever let him do that that he did it well. Better than well. I've seen grown men come through this yard looking less steady."

"I'll tell them," Tamsin said.

They moved on. Ten steps. Twenty. The lamplight released them and the dark took them back, and Pip kept his broken-soldier shuffle until the platform curved away and the policeman was a smudge of red cap in a cone of yellow, and then another fifty steps beyond that, because he did not trust himself to stop performing.

Only when they reached the shadow between two ambulance wagons did Tamsin stop the stretcher and turn to him, and Pip braced for anger—for *you lied about your age to get here and you lied about your age to get through and how dare you risk everything*—but what was in her face was neither anger nor relief. It was recognition. She looked at him the way you look at a landscape that has quietly changed overnight: the same river, different water.

"You heard all that," she said.

"Yes, Sister."

"Don't 'Sister' me right now." She pulled off one glove with her teeth and pressed the bare hand briefly, hard, to his shoulder. Her fingers were freezing and they shook, just barely, just enough that Pip realized the calm had been a performance too, hers, running on rails laid over an abyss. "You understand what happened back there. What you did."

"I pretended to be cracked," Pip said. His voice came out strange, older, scraped. "I'm not. I mean—I am, a bit, everyone is—but I pretended to be more cracked."

"No." She shook her head once. "You stood in front of a policeman with a forged pass and a stolen book and a sleeping man, and you chose, in about four seconds, to become someone else entirely, and you did it well enough to fool a trained man whose whole trade is seeing through people. That's not pretending, Pip. That's soldiering. The worst kind there is—the kind where the weapon is yourself." She looked away, down the platform, toward Amiens, toward Ada Quill's waiting room, toward print. "I've been doing it for two years and I still hate it more than shelling."

Pip stood with his hands on the stretcher handles and felt something happen in him that had no words yet, a door swinging shut on one room and open on another. An hour ago he had been a boy doing a brave thing, and the bravery had been a costume borrowed from men. Now the costume wouldn't come off. Now he knew what the men knew: that courage was not the absence of shaking but the decision of where to point it, and that once you made that decision you could never fully be a child again, because children believed someone else would carry things. Nobody else was coming. There had never been anybody else coming. There was only the stretcher, and the dark, and the next checkpoint, and your own two hands.

"We'd better move," he said. "Before the relief comes on."

Tamsin looked at him a moment longer—filing him away, he thought, in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes—and nodded.

They crossed the rest of the yard in the drowned grey silence, Pip pushing, Tamsin guiding, Dawes snoring his faithful whistle, and the ledger riding against the corporal's hip like a heart in a borrowed chest. At the far gate a lantern burned and a transport sergeant checked them through with two bored ticks of a pencil, and the train for Amiens stood waiting with its boiler ticking as it cooled, patient as everything else in France, and they climbed aboard into a carriage full of sleeping wounded, and found seats, and sat.

It was only then, watching the yard slide backward into the dark, that Pip remembered the policeman's last words, the ones that hadn't been procedure at all: *there are men in the guardroom who've been waiting since Tuesday.*

Since Tuesday. Detained. Waiting.

And among the carriage's sleepers, in the corner seat nearest the door, a man who had boarded unseen at the yard's edge sat with his collar up and his hat low, and as the train gathered speed he lifted his head just enough for the passing signal-lamp to find his face—and Pip's blood went cold, because he knew that face, everyone at the clearing station knew that face, and it belonged to a man who was supposed to be fifty miles away and was supposed, above all, to know nothing about a certain ration crate, a certain book, and a certain boy pushing a stretcher at four in the morning.
