## 11. What Voss Reports

The requisition form arrived on a Tuesday, which Edmund Voss would remember afterward as the last ordinary day of his life.

It came by field post, folded twice, stamped by the Deputy Directorate of Manpower Statistics, Rouen, and it said in language of perfect bureaucratic blandness: *Mr. E. Voss, Census Officer (Temporary, Grade II), will forward to this Directorate not later than 18 November a consolidated return of all personnel counts conducted in Sector 14, including but not limited to medical establishments, with reconciliation schedules appended.*

Reconciliation schedules. He read the phrase four times, standing in the doorway of the billet he shared with two transport clerks and a stove that smoked when the wind came from the east. Reconciliation was his word. It was the word he had used in his first report from Lille, in the monograph nobody had read, in the lecture he'd given to three bored juniors in a Camberwell church hall before the war made census-taking sound quaint. *A count that cannot reconcile itself is not a count; it is an opinion.* He had believed that once the way other men believed in God.

Now the Directorate wanted his opinions, reconciled, by the eighteenth of November.

He sat down on the edge of his cot and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for nine days.

* * *

He had counted forty-one establishments in Sector 14. Dressing stations, clearing stations, base hospitals, convalescent depots, a dental annex housed in what had been a schoolhouse and still smelled faintly of chalk. At thirty-nine of them the numbers had behaved. Beds to admissions, admissions to discharges and deaths, deaths to grave registrations, graves to markers, markers to the burial returns that went back through channels to a registry office where some other meticulous man filed them in cabinets that would outlive them all. Thirty-nine establishments where the arithmetic closed like a well-made door.

And one—No. 23 Casualty Clearing Station, three miles behind the Passchendaele salient, a sprawl of canvas and duckboards and the smell of carbolic frozen into the mud—where the numbers refused.

He had gone there first in October with his ledger board and his pencil sharpened to a point no sane man would put near his eye. He had counted beds: two hundred and ten. He had counted admissions from the admission book: six hundred and forty over the reporting period. He had counted the dead.

That was where it broke. The morning reports—the daily state sheets signed by the adjutant and sent up the chain—recorded eleven deaths for the period. Eleven names, typed, initialled, tidy as buttons. But the burial party's own register, kept by a chaplain who had let Edmund copy it without being asked, recorded twenty-six. And the grave markers behind the station—Edmund had walked the rows himself in a wind that came straight off the flats and smelled of iron—numbered thirty-one.

Thirty-one crosses. Eleven names. A gap of twenty men who had died at No. 23 CCS and existed nowhere except in the earth and, apparently, nowhere else at all.

He had asked Sister Tamsin Amberdale about it in her ward, between one row of cots and another, and she had answered him with a steadiness he still turned over at night like a coin whose face wouldn't come right. Clerical error. Transfers misrecorded as deaths. Men evacuated who should have been marked discharged. It happens, Mr. Voss. War is not tidy.

And then, as she reached past him for a chart, the corner of something—a small black book, cloth-bound, its edge soft with handling—had shown itself in the pocket of her apron, and been withdrawn, and the conversation had moved on, and he had said nothing.

Nine days ago. He had said nothing for nine days, and every day the silence had weighed a gram more, until now it weighed enough to be measured by the Directorate of Manpower Statistics and returned to him as a form demanding reconciliation schedules.

* * *

His superior was Lieutenant-Colonel Hargreaves, and Hargreaves was not a stupid man, which was the worst of it. A stupid man could be managed. Stupid men wanted clean columns and no questions, and Edmund could have given them clean columns forever. But Hargreaves had spent twenty-two years counting things for the Army—mules, mess tins, men—and he had the particular alertness of a man who has caught many people lying about small numbers and therefore trusts no one about large ones.

"Voss." He didn't look up when Edmund came into the office, a Nissen hut in Rouen that managed to be both overheated and drafty. "Sit. Your sector return is late."

"It's due on the eighteenth."

"My reminder is today. I like my reminders early. It gives me time to worry." Hargreaves finished writing something, blotted it with unnecessary thoroughness, and looked up. He was a grey man with a boxer's flattened nose and reading glasses perched too low, so that he regarded the world over the top of them like a horse judging a fence. "Sector 14. Forty-one establishments. How does it stand?"

"Thirty-nine returns are complete and reconciled. I can submit them tonight."

"And the other two?"

"One is the dental annex. Trivial. The other—" Edmund heard the pause arrive in his own voice, half a second of open air, and hated it. "The other is No. 23 Casualty Clearing Station."

Hargreaves took off his glasses entirely, which was a thing he did perhaps twice a year. "Go on."

"There are discrepancies."

"What kind of discrepancies?"

"The kind," Edmund said carefully, "that arise under shellfire. Their admission book is maintained under conditions that—I inspected it personally—the adjutant keeps it in a dugout with a candle. Entries are made in haste. Deaths are recorded on morning reports that may lag the actual events by days, particularly during a push. Men die in transit. Men are buried by other units' burial parties. The ground there is—"

"I know what the ground there is." Hargreaves laid the glasses on the desk and aligned them parallel to its edge, a small compulsive squareness that Edmund recognized because it lived in his own hands. "I've had a signal from GHQ. They're preparing the final war establishment returns. When the armistice comes—*if* it comes, and don't repeat that I said if—the whole apparatus gets boxed. Every register, every state sheet, every grave registration goes into sealed cases and ships home to the Record Office. After sealing, corrections require a Board of Inquiry and a signature at directorate level or above. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the numbers become permanent."

"That the numbers become *history*, Voss. There's a difference, and you of all people should feel it in your teeth." Hargreaves leaned back. "So. Before they seal it, I want your reconciliation schedule for No. 23. Bed count, admissions, discharges, deaths, burials, markers. Every figure tied to a source document. If there are gaps, I want them itemized, quantified, and explained. If you can't explain them, you say so in writing, and I escalate it, and some poor devil at GHQ spends Christmas doing what you couldn't do in a month." He picked up his pen. "Can you close it?"

Outside, a lorry changed gear on the road with a sound like a cough. Somewhere beyond the hut a line of horses stamped, patient under their blankets. Edmund became aware that he was holding his cap in both hands and that his thumbs were pressing grooves into its brim.

Twenty men. Twenty men who had died and been carried out of the record by error or haste or something less innocent than either, and somewhere in the middle of that gap, folded into a nurse's apron pocket, a small black book that held their names—held them, he was now almost certain, because he had seen her write in it, seen the way she guarded it, seen the corner of it appear and vanish like a fish rising in dark water. She was keeping the true count. She was doing, in defiance of every regulation he had served for four years, exactly the work he had spent his life pretending mattered.

If he reported the discrepancy honestly, the machinery would grind into motion. Investigators would come to No. 23. They would interview the adjutant, the matron, the sisters. They would ask Sister Amberdale how she accounted for twenty unrecorded dead, and whether she had kept any private notes regarding them, and a woman who hid a ledger inside a ration crate would lie badly or bravely and either way she would hang for it—not from a rope, nothing so dramatic, but from the slow rope of a disciplinary board, a stripped rank, a discharge without character, a name entered in the one record that never forgives. And the ledger itself would be seized as evidence and vanish into a case box, sealed, shipped, and shelved, which meant the twenty men would be corrected on paper and forgotten in fact, their names locked in a file no mourner would ever see.

If he falsified the return—if he wrote *reconciled* against No. 23 CCS—he became the author of the omission. Not the clerk who erred, not the adjutant who hurried, but the man who saw the gap and smoothed it shut with his own hand. His monograph, his lectures, his little church hall gospel of the reconciled count—all of it would become the biography of a hypocrite.

"Voss." Hargreaves was watching him over the desk with those flat, patient eyes. "You've gone somewhere. Come back. Can you close the return?"

"Yes, sir," said Edmund Voss.

He heard himself say it the way a man hears a door latch behind him in an empty house.

* * *

He worked on it that night and the next, in the billet, by lamplight, while the transport clerks snored and the stove smoked and the wind worried the canvas. He assembled the thirty-nine good returns first, out of order, because he could not make himself touch No. 23 until the honest work was done. Column by column, source by source: the convalescent depot at Étaples, exact to the man; the dressing posts along the Ypres road, exact to the hour of transfer. His figures matched the registers the way a key matches a lock it was cut for. This was the only cathedral he had ever had, this agreement between what happened and what was written, and he built it faithfully, stone by stone, knowing what he intended to lay beside it.

Then he opened the folder for No. 23 and spread the documents across his blanket like a man dealing out a hand he had already decided to lose.

Bed strength: 210. Confirmed by inspection, 3 October.

Admissions, period: 640. From the admission book, copied in his own hand.

Deaths per morning reports: 11.

Grave markers on site: 31. Counted personally. He could still number the rows if he shut his eyes—seven, seven, seven, seven, three—a geometry he had walked twice to be sure, in wind cold enough to make counting a physical act, each figure standing over a mound the frost had turned to iron.

In the margin of his working sheet, in pencil, he wrote the question he had carried for nine days: *Where are the other twenty?*

And beneath it, because honesty was a habit that died slowly, he wrote the answer: *In a black book in Sister Amberdale's apron, if anywhere.*

He sat looking at those two lines for a long time. The lamp hissed. One of the transport clerks muttered something about a mare called Duchess and rolled over.

Here was the thing he had not told anyone, the thing he had barely told himself: he did not know if the omissions were sin or salvage. Clerical chaos swallowed men whole in this war—that much was documented, that much any census officer knew. A man died at a dressing station at midnight during a barrage; the sister who might have recorded him was elbow-deep in someone else's dying; the note never got made; the body went to the burial party with a tag and the tag went into the ground and the ground kept the secret better than any clerk. Twenty gaps could be twenty accidents. Or twenty could be policy—men dead of things no morning report wished to name, men whose deaths were inconvenient, men erased the way his cousin Gerald had been erased, *missing presumed*, a phrase that was not a record at all but a shrug dressed in uniforms.

Gerald. He hadn't let himself think the name in days, and it arrived now with its full weight, sitting down opposite him like a guest who knows he's unwelcome. Gerald Ellery-Voss, Second Battalion, listed missing since the spring, and Aunt Constance writing letters to the Record Office and receiving back the same courteous non-answer, the shrug in triplicate, until she stopped writing and started dying instead, quietly, of a grief that had no entry in any ledger. If Gerald was dead—and Edmund had known in his bones since June that Gerald was dead—then somewhere there was a gap in someone's return exactly the size of one man, and some officer like Edmund had looked at it and written *reconciled*, and a family had been left to guess for the rest of their lives.

He thought of Tamsin Amberdale's face when she had deflected him—composed, watchful, and underneath the composure something raw, the look of a person carrying weight in a place where no one can see it. He thought: *she is keeping faith with the dead, and I am asked to keep faith with a form, and the form will win, because the form always wins, unless—*

Unless a man decided otherwise. Unless a man committed the one act his entire training called unforgivable: he wrote down a number he knew to be false.

* * *

On the seventeenth of November he typed the return himself, on the Directorate's own machine, because a handwritten document invited scrutiny and a typescript invited filing. He typed steadily through the thirty-nine honest returns, and his heart behaved, and the keys rang out their small definite sounds, each strike a tiny verdict of *this is true*. That was the obscenity of it—the machine made no distinction. True and false struck the same note.

Then the sheet for No. 23 Casualty Clearing Station rose in the platen, blank, waiting.

He typed: *Bed strength 210, verified by inspection.* True.

*Admissions 640, per admission book.* True.

*Discharges and transfers:* the real figures, the ones he could defend. True, true, true. He was laying a roadbed of truth, and every true figure made the false one stronger, the way a forged banknote passes best among genuine ones.

Then he reached the line that mattered. *Deaths, period:* and his fingers hovered, and the lamp hissed, and in the pause he saw with terrible clarity the two futures laid out side by side like the rows of graves he had counted. In one, the truth went up the chain, and the machinery woke, and Sister Amberdale stood in front of a board with her black book produced as evidence against her, and the twenty men got their official correction and lost their names, and Hargreaves commended the diligence of Temporary Grade II Census Officer E. Voss, and Edmund went on counting things for the rest of his life with the specific hollowness of a man who once had a chance to matter and filed it.

In the other, he typed a number that was a lie, and the lie traveled up the chain wearing truth's uniform, and the books were sealed, and the gap closed over twenty men like water, and no investigator ever knocked at No. 23, and a nurse kept her liberty and her forbidden book and whatever stubborn sacrament she was performing in the dark—and Edmund Voss became, permanently and by his own hand, the kind of man he had spent four years measuring.

There was no third future. He looked for one. He was a thorough man; he looked thoroughly. There was no way to report the gap and protect her, no clever phrasing that would raise the question without summoning the wolves, no intermediate number that was honest. Arithmetic admitted no half-truths. That had been his creed. That was why the betrayal, when it came, had to be total.

He typed: *Deaths, period: 11, per morning reports. Burials and grave markers correspond. Return reconciled.*

Correspond. He stared at the word. Thirty-one markers against eleven reports, and he had written *correspond*, and the sentence was so smooth, so professionally cadenced, that he felt a moment of pure vertigo—as if he had looked up from the page and found himself standing in a different man's body, a man with the same name and the same careful hands who lied in complete grammatical sentences.

He signed it. His signature came out steady, which frightened him more than trembling would have. He dated it. He placed it in the folder with the others, squared the edges against the desk, tied the tape in the regulation bow, and addressed the cover to the Deputy Directorate of Manpower Statistics.

Then he sat down again and wrote, separately, on a plain sheet, a memorandum in his smallest hand:

*Sir,—I have the honour to submit the consolidated return for Sector 14. All establishments reconcile within acceptable margins of error. I would add, for whatever value the observation carries, that the conditions under which these counts were obtained varied greatly, and that no return, however reconciled, can be more truthful than the records upon which it rests. Respectfully, E. Voss, Temp. Census Officer, Gr. II.*

It was not a confession. It was not courage. It was the equivalent of a man leaving a door unlocked behind him as the burglars leave—a gesture so small it could mean nothing, so deliberate it could mean everything. He read it twice, and understood that he had written it not for Hargreaves but for himself, a single splinter of honesty lodged in the timber of the lie, so that someday, if anyone ever pulled the return apart, they would find it and know that the man who signed it had known. Had known, and chosen, and paid for the choosing in the only currency he possessed, which was the corruption of his own life's work.

He became what he counted. The thought came to him with the flat certainty of a completed sum. For four years he had been the instrument that fixed men in ledgers—present, absent, dead, transferred—and now he had used the instrument's full authority to fix twenty men in oblivion, and the fixing was done, and no future remorse would unfix it. What was written was written. He had built his whole faith on that proposition. Now he knew its other face.

He sealed the memorandum inside the folder, and the folder inside the cover, and dropped it in the field post box outside headquarters at 0640 on the eighteenth of November, hearing it land on the pile below with a sound far too light for what it contained.

The armistice rumor was everywhere that morning—in the cookhouse, in the stables, in the mouths of men who had no right to know anything. *Any day now. They're signing. It'll be over by Sunday.* And Edmund stood by the post box in the grey cold and understood what he had actually mailed: not a report but a clock. Once peace was signed, the records sealed, every day of delay was a day shaved off the life of a certain black book and the names inside it. Whatever Tamsin Amberdale intended to do with her forbidden count, she would have to do it soon. Days, not weeks.

He turned toward the motor park, where a car was being fueled for a run up the line toward Sector 14, toward Amiens, toward the corridor of checkpoints between here and the press offices where correspondents waited behind censors' walls. He had told himself he was finished—that he had bought her safety at the price of his integrity and the transaction was closed.

But his feet carried him toward the car all the same, and he heard himself ask the driver, in a voice that sounded remarkably casual, "Which way are you routed? Only I've an inspection outstanding at No. 23."

The driver consulted a chit. "Via Amiens, sir. Press section wants a lift up."

Edmund looked north, where the road ran white with frost toward the rail yards and the gates and the men with stamps and manifests, and felt the fear arrive on schedule, punctual as one of his own returns.

"Good," he said. "I'll come along."
