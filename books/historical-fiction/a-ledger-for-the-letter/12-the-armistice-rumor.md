## 12. The Armistice Rumor

The rumor arrived the way weather arrives on the flats—first as a change in the light, then as a pressure behind the ears, and finally as a fact too large to argue with.

Tamsin felt it before she heard it. She was changing a dressing on Private Ockham's leg, her fingers working the gauze with the economy of long habit, when she noticed that the ward had gone quiet in the wrong way. Wards were never quiet; they moaned and muttered and coughed through the small hours like old houses settling. But this was a different silence—the held-breath kind, the kind that gathers around a man reading a letter.

Sister Mowbray stood at the end of the row of beds with a newspaper folded to a quarter of its size, which is what people did with newspapers that contained either very good news or very bad.

"Well?" said Ockham, from under his bandages. "Out with it, Sister. Some of us haven't got all winter."

Mowbray looked up. Her face did something complicated. "They're saying it's close," she said. "The signing. Days, some of them say. The German delegates are already across the lines."

The gauze in Tamsin's hands went still. Somewhere down the ward a man laughed—a short, disbelieving bark—and someone else began to sing, three notes of a music-hall tune before a nurse hushed him. Ockham's eyes had gone bright and wet above his dressing.

"Days," he repeated. "After four years."

*Four years,* Tamsin thought, *and eleven days for you, since they carried you in.* She finished tying off the bandage, pressed his hand once, and walked out into the corridor before anyone could see what the news had done to her face—because it was not joy there, or not only joy. It was arithmetic.

If the armistice came in days, the records would be sealed within weeks. Sealed, crated, shipped to the War Office, indexed by men whose whole talent was making things vanish into other things. Every name in her ledger that wasn't already inked onto an official form would become a ghost twice over—dead once in the mud, dead again in the filing. And Edmund's route through the checkpoints, mapped and argued over at Sister Brightwater's table until the candle guttered, would close like a door.

She found him in the records hut, because of course she did. He was at the trestle table with his census forms squared to the edge of the wood, checking figures against figures with his fountain pen uncapped, and even now—even now, with the world possibly ending within the week—he flinched slightly when she came in without knocking, as though she'd disturbed an equation.

"You've heard," he said. It wasn't a question. News traveled through this place faster than blood through a body.

"I've heard." She shut the door. The hut was cold enough to see their breath. "How long does sealing take, once it starts? Truly. Not what the manual says. What happens."

Edmund set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the forms, and Tamsin understood that this was how he bought himself time to think—that tidiness was his thinking made visible.

"Manifests first," he said. "Then verification counts. Then the crates go to the rail yard, and after that they're in the War Office's hands, and no power on this earth gets them open again inside five years. If the signing is Monday—" he glanced at the calendar on the wall, its days crossed out in pencil, "—the last sensible day to move anything past the checkpoints is Thursday. Possibly Friday, if the trains are late, and the trains are always late."

"Thursday," Tamsin said.

"At the outside." He looked at her then, properly, and she saw that underneath the census-taker's composure he was as frightened as she was. It steadied her, oddly. Fear shared was fear halved—or at least fear witnessed. "Ada Quill needs two clear days to set type once she has it. Which means the ledger leaves here Tuesday night. Pip carries it through the yard Wednesday, before dawn. That was always the plan."

"That was the plan when we thought we had a month."

"The plan doesn't change. Only the price of failing does." He said it flatly, without drama, and went back to his figures, and Tamsin stood a moment watching the pen move—*this man falsified his life's work for us,* she thought, *and now he squares his pens*—and left him to it, because there were dressings to change and hours to count down.

*

The second half of the rumor arrived at supper, carried on a different wind.

Matron Honorius Quayle had come over from the base hospital at Rouen on an inspection tour of auxiliary units, and she brought with her the particular atmosphere of a chapel in which something has recently died: incense and disapproval in equal measure. She was a tall woman built like a piece of municipal architecture, gray throughout—gray uniform, gray hair pinned in a gray net—and she had the reputation, which preceded her by three days, of having reduced two orderlies and a chaplain to tears in a single afternoon.

She took supper in the sisters' mess, which was itself an omen, and Tamsin was pouring the weak tea when Quayle's voice rose over the clink of tin plates—not loud, but carrying, the voice of a woman who had learned that volume was vulgar but audibility was power.

"—irregularities," she was saying, to Sister Brightwater, who sat opposite her with the serenity of a millpond. "I have reviewed the admission returns for this station for October and November. They do not reconcile with the burial returns filed through the chaplain's office. There are men in your graves who do not appear in your ledgers, Sister Brightwater, and men in your ledgers who appear nowhere else. I call that irregular record-keeping."

"Do you," said Brightwater, and passed the bread.

"I call it more than irregular." Quayle set down her fork with a click. "In twenty-six years I have seen every species of clerical sloth, and I know what it looks like when it is not sloth at all. Someone at this station has been keeping accounts of their own. Unofficial accounts. And unofficial accounts, in wartime, are indistinguishable from espionage."

The tea urn hissed. In the pause Tamsin heard her own heart, quick and traitorous.

Brightwater buttered her bread, unhurried, a woman who had fed ten thousand men through worse moments than this. "Matron Quayle," she said pleasantly, "you have tasted my tea. Would you call it espionage as well? Because it is certainly criminal."

"This is not a jesting matter."

"No," Brightwater agreed. "It isn't. It's arithmetic, which I understand you also practice. Men die quickly here and paperwork moves slowly, and between the quick and the slow, names drop. I should think a census professional such as yourself would find the phenomenon unremarkable. Mr. Voss could explain it to you—he's been counting our doors all month, and hasn't once accused me of treason."

Quayle's gray eyes moved—slowly, deliberately—around the mess, and Tamsin kept her own gaze on the tea urn with the fixed innocence of a woman watching water boil. But she felt the inspection land on her like a hand on the shoulder, pause, and move on.

"There will be a formal inspection," Quayle announced, to the room rather than to Brightwater. "Tomorrow morning. Every ward, every store, every office. Records, correspondence, private effects. I am authorized to open any parcel, any locker, any book." She rose, gathering her papers. "And Sister Brightwater—you will make available to me whatever it is you imagine I will not find."

She left. The mess exhaled. Down the table, young Nurse Corrigan began, shakily, to laugh, and stopped herself, and looked terrified, and Brightwater reached over and squeezed her wrist once, briefly, the way one checks a pulse.

"Tamsin," Brightwater said quietly, not turning her head. "Walk me to the stores."

*

They walked. The frost had come down hard that evening and the duckboards rang under their boots like a struck marimba, each step a small clear note in the dark. Overhead the sky was doing its winter trick—stars sharp as bayonet points, and beneath them, far off toward the line, the stutter of guns that everyone now listened to differently, knowing it might be the last music of its kind.

"She'll find it," Tamsin said. "Whatever you say in front of her, Honorius Quayle finds things. She found contraband morphine at Rouen hidden in a piano. She found a whole deserter living in the linen store for six weeks. She has a nose for it."

"Mm." Brightwater walked with her hands clasped behind her back, breath streaming. "And where is it tonight?"

Tamsin hesitated. Then: "Where it's always been. The ration crate."

"No." Brightwater said it immediately, without heat. "That was sound in October. It isn't sound tomorrow. A matron conducting a records inspection will inventory every ration crate against the supply manifest—it's the first thing they teach them, because rations are where theft lives. Your crate will be opened by nine o'clock." She stopped walking. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and voice. "So. Where does a thing hide, Tamsin, when every hiding place is about to be searched?"

Tamsin stood on the ringing boards and thought. She thought about pianos and linen stores and morphine, about the matron's gray thoroughness, about the way a searching mind works—hungry for concealment, delighted by discovery, suspicious of everything shut. And then she thought of what her brother Tom used to say, back when they were children playing at soldiers in the orchard, whenever she hid and he sought: *You always look in places, Tam. You never look at things.*

"In plain sight," she said slowly. "It hides in plain sight. In something so ordinary, so official, that opening it proves nothing."

"Go on."

"A hymnal." The idea arrived whole, the way the best ones did—as if it had been waiting. "The chapel shelf. Every station has hymnals; nobody counts hymnals; nobody opens hymnals except at services, and we haven't had a service in a month. A book bound in black cloth, spine worn, sitting among thirty others." She was almost laughing, the hysteria-adjacent laugh that lived close behind her sternum these days. "She'll run her finger along the shelf and feel spines and move on. Books are furniture, Sister. Furniture is invisible."

"It's not invisible if she pulls it down and leafs through it."

"She won't leaf through it if it looks like a hymnal down to the last detail. Which means I spend tonight making it look like one." Tamsin was already moving, the plan assembling itself under her hands the way dressings assembled, step by necessary step. "A proper cover—hymn titles in gilt on the spine, page edges trimmed, a flyleaf with a presentation inscription, *Presented to No. 14 Casualty Clearing Station by the parishioners of St. Aldhelm's, 1915.* And the ledger goes in the middle, sewn in, so the pages fan like psalms. She can open it anywhere and find singing in it."

Brightwater regarded her for a long moment. Frost silvered her eyebrows. When she spoke, there was something in her voice Tamsin hadn't heard before—not the brisk command of the head nurse, but something older and more tired and more fond.

"My sister Ruth," she said, "was a schoolmistress in Norfolk. Hid her brother-in-law in the hay loft for eight months in '15, a conscientious objector, while the village constable drank her tea every Sunday and complimented her garden. Do you know her rule? *Never hide a thing where you'd be afraid to have it found. Hide it where being found costs you nothing.*" She turned back toward the mess. "A hymnal on a chapel shelf, Sister Amberdale. Being found costs you nothing. Being read—" the faintest pause, "—well. We'll pray she reads only the psalms."

*

That night the clearing station slept badly, and Tamsin did not sleep at all.

She worked in the chapel by candlelight—chapel being a generous word for the requisitioned barn with twelve benches and a shelf—and she worked with a bookbinder's patience she hadn't known she possessed. She boiled paste from flour. She cut gilt paper from a chocolate box into letters. She copied hymn numbers onto ribbon markers. Twice she heard boots in the yard and put out the candle and stood in absolute darkness among the benches with her heart going like a stretcher-bearer's, listening, and twice the boots passed on.

Around three she paused, ledger half-sewn into its false psalms, and let herself have the thought she'd been refusing all evening.

*It might work. It might actually work—and the war might actually end.*

Both at once. Both true. And she found, examining the feeling honestly, that hope and dread were not two things in her but one thing, braided, indistinguishable, the way grief and love had turned out to be one thing the night she lost the ward. You could not pull them apart without tearing something. The rumor of peace and the rumor of inspection had arrived in the same twenty-four hours, and they were the same rumor really: *everything is about to change, and you are not ready.*

She thought of Edmund saying *Thursday, at the outside,* and of Pip Halloway practicing his stretcher-face in the corridor mirror—sixteen years old and learning to carry death professionally—and of Ada Quill somewhere in Amiens with her blocked columns and her cynicism wearing thin. She thought of the names. Two hundred and six of them now, in her cramped upright hand. Each one a man the war had tried to misplace. If the inspection failed, they'd be misplaced forever.

She finished the sewing. She set the book on the chapel shelf, third from the left, spine flush with its neighbors, and stepped back, and looked at it until her eyes ached, and it was nothing. It was a hymnal. It was furniture.

Dawn was graying the east when she came out, stiff with cold, and found Edmund waiting by the chapel door, cap in his hands, breath steaming, awake in the way of a man who has spent the night composing speeches to himself.

"I heard about the inspection," he said. "Corrigan told me. Tamsin—if she takes the ledger, she takes the evidence and us with it. Perhaps I should take it out tonight instead. Risk the road early rather than—"

"And lose two days' margin, and carry it through the yard in daylight?" She shook her head. "No. It stays on the shelf. It survives the morning. And tomorrow night we move."

He looked at her a moment. "You're certain."

"I'm certain of the number of hours," she said, "and I'm done pretending certainty about anything else." She nodded toward the east, where the gray was warming. "Pray for a delayed train, Mr. Voss. Pray for fog. Pray the Germans dawdle over their signatures."

Behind them, across the yard, the bell over the receiving tent began to ring—wounded coming in, the night's harvest arriving—and down the ward a man was singing again, three notes, four, undeterred. And at the far gate, small in the early light, a gray figure was advancing with a leather dispatch case and a face like a verdict: Matron Quayle, ahead of schedule, come to count their doors.

Tamsin drew a breath that hurt, straightened her apron, and went to meet the morning.
