## 13. Inspection at Dawn

Sister Edith Brightwater had learned long ago that the secret to standing still was not stillness at all. It was motion so small and so practiced that no one thought to call it anything but calm: the smoothing of a sheet already smooth, the straightening of a chart already square, the folding of hands that had nothing left to hold.

She performed all three now, in the grey hour before dawn, while the frost on the ward windows turned the lamplight into something like lace.

Matron Hallowell—no relation to their Pip, thank God, though the coincidence of the name had made Tamsin laugh once, bitterly, in a way Edith had not cared for—was due at first light. The telegram had come at nine the previous evening, unsigned but unmistakable in its bureaucratic perfume: *Inspection of records and wards, 0600 hours, No. 14 Casualty Clearing Station. All personnel to be available for interview.*

*Irregular record-keeping.* Someone had written those words and sent them down the chain like a stone dropped into a well, and now the ripples were coming back up, and everything they touched would be wet.

Edith had known about the ledger since March.

She had never said so aloud—not to Tamsin, not to God, not even to herself in the privacy of her own head, where she kept her confessions filed under headings like *Necessary* and *Later*. She had simply begun, without announcement, leaving things where they needed to be left. A lamp burning late in the linen room. A key to the chapel that had gone conspicuously unmissed. An extra ten minutes of quiet on Sunday evenings when the wards slept and Sister Amberdale walked toward the chapel with a ration crate held the way other women carried infants.

You did not ask a woman what she carried into a chapel at night. Not in this war. You only made sure the door was unlocked.

Now the door's discretion was about to be tested by someone whose whole profession was doors.

* * *

At half past five, Edith stood in the chapel.

It was a poor thing as chapels went—a requisitioned barn with a plank altar and forty hymnals bought surplus from a school that had lost its boys to the same war. The hymnals were shabby, water-stained, their covers swollen from damp. Which was precisely why the disguise worked. One more swollen, water-stained book among forty was invisible. It was arithmetic, really, and Edith had always trusted arithmetic more than prayer, though she offered both most mornings.

The ledger sat third from the left on the second shelf, its spine re-lettered in her own hand two nights ago with ink she'd aged with tea and a candle flame. *HYMNS A&M — No. 3.* Inside its covers were not hymns but names—two hundred and eleven of them now, each one a man the morning reports had declined to remember—and Edith found a certain bleak fitness in the substitution. What was a hymnal, after all, but a book of words meant to be spoken over the dead? They had merely cut out the middle verses.

She touched the spine once. Then she took her hand away, because touching it again would be the beginning of fussing, and fussing was the one sin Matron Hallowell could smell through walls.

"You'll want to breathe," she told the empty chapel, in the voice she used on young nurses before their first amputation. "Breathing is permitted."

The chapel did not answer. Chapels rarely did. But the light was coming up through the east window—the glass long since shattered and replaced with oiled canvas, which turned the sunrise into something soft and amber and almost merciful—and Edith squared her shoulders and went out to meet the morning.

* * *

Matron Hallowell arrived at six o'clock exactly, which told Edith everything. Punctuality of that caliber was not discipline; it was appetite. A woman who arrived at six exactly wanted you to know she could have arrived at five fifty-nine.

She came with an escort—a captain from the records office, dry as a file folder—and she came down from the motor car in a coat too fine for the front, wool the color of wet slate, and she looked at the casualty clearing station the way a housekeeper looks at a kitchen she has been sent to inspect for weevils.

"Matron." Hallowell extended a gloved hand.

"Matron." Edith shook it. Hallowell's glove was kid leather, unscuffed. Edith wondered how many miles of corridor a hand had to walk before the leather learned honesty.

"You keep a small establishment," Hallowell said, surveying the wards with eyes that counted without appearing to count. Edith recognized the technique because she used it herself. You did not look at a thing; you let the thing be seen by you, peripherally, and then you asked a question whose real subject was elsewhere.

"We are what the army requires us to be," Edith said pleasantly. "Small enough to move, large enough to bleed."

Something flickered across Hallowell's face—the briefest tightening at the corner of the mouth, distaste or recognition, Edith couldn't tell. "We will begin with the records room."

"Of course. May I offer you tea first? It's poor, but it's hot."

"I do not take refreshment during inspections."

"No," Edith said, leading the way. "I imagine one couldn't, and stay sharp."

Behind her, she heard the captain murmur something to his clipboard. She did not turn around. Turning around was fussing.

* * *

They spent forty minutes in the records room, and Edith watched a professional work.

Hallowell did not read files; she interrogated them. She pulled admission ledgers at random and cross-checked them against theatre logs, against stretcher-bearer returns, against the burial register, moving with a fluency that was almost musical. Twice she stopped, ran a finger down a column, and asked a question so precise that Edith understood she was not searching for the ledger at all—or not only for the ledger. She was searching for the shape of its absence. A gap in the numbers. A night where admissions exceeded deaths reported and no one could say why.

"There," Hallowell said, tapping the page for the eleventh of October. "Fourteen admissions overnight, twelve recorded discharges. Where are the other two?"

"Transferred," Edith said. "Convoy to Rouen. The transfer chit would have been raised by the night sister."

"The night sister being?"

"Sister Amberdale. She keeps meticulous records—you'll find her chits in perfect order."

And there it was, the terrible elegance of the thing: Tamsin's records *were* in perfect order. That was the whole art of the ledger. The official books balanced because Tamsin made them balance, and the truth lived somewhere else entirely, in a book dressed as a hymnal, and the two accounts never touched. You could audit one forever and never find the other, because the other was not hiding in the numbers. It was hiding outside them.

Hallowell studied her for a moment. Her eyes were pale, unhurried, the eyes of a woman who had learned that people filled silence the way water fills a crack.

"You've been matron here how long, Sister Brightwater?"

"Since the station opened. Fourteen months."

"And before that?"

"Queen Alexandra's, Étaples. Before that, I trained at St. Thomas's. Before that—" Edith smiled, a small, true smile, because the next part was true and she had decided long ago to spend her truths carefully but completely. "Before that I kept house for my father, and before that I had a sister named Constance who died at nineteen of scarlet fever, and I mention it only because you are going to ask me eventually whether I understand the importance of accurate records, and I want to save us both the trouble. Yes, Matron. I understand better than most. I have buried everyone whose records I ever kept."

The silence that followed had texture. The captain's pen stopped.

"That is not what I was going to ask," Hallowell said at last, and for just a moment the housekeeper fell away and Edith saw something else underneath—tiredness, perhaps, or the memory of a brother, a son; every woman at the front had one, and grief was the one uniform that fit everyone.

But then the moment closed, the way moments did, and the glove went back on, metaphorical and otherwise.

"The wards," Hallowell said.

* * *

They walked the wards together, and Edith discovered that the hardest part was not lying. She had made her peace with lying sometime around the Somme, in the specific form of telling dying men they'd be fine when they wouldn't, and telling mothers their sons died painlessly when sometimes they hadn't. Lying was a clinical skill. She administered it in measured doses.

The hard part was Pip.

Pip Halloway was pushing a mop along the central aisle with the exaggerated innocence of a boy who had been coached for exactly this moment and could think of nothing else. He had polished the same four feet of floorboards to a mirror shine. His ears were scarlet.

"Do you need something, orderly?" Edith asked mildly.

"No, ma'am. Just—mopping. Ma'am."

"Carry on."

He carried on, mopping air, and Edith moved on before Hallowell's attention could settle on him like a bird of prey deciding. Later, she would tell him he'd done beautifully. Later, she would also tell him that a boy who mopped one spot for twenty minutes might as well hang a sign on it. There was time for both lessons. There had to be, because there was a war on and boys kept arriving, and some part of Edith Brightwater had quietly appointed itself responsible for making sure as many of them as possible lived long enough to become men worth knowing.

In the third ward, Hallowell stopped at a bed near the window and read the chart hanging at its foot. The man in the bed was asleep, or pretending to be; his name was Corporal Dengate and he had a hip wound and the particular stillness of a man listening hard.

"This patient," Hallowell said. "Admitted the ninth. Your ledger shows him admitted the tenth."

"A clerical error," Edith said. "I corrected it in red. You'll see the amendment initialled."

"I see it." Hallowell did not write anything down. That frightened Edith more than writing would have. A written note was a fixed accusation you could answer. An unwritten note was a knife a woman kept in her sleeve until she chose the moment.

They finished the wards. Hallowell interviewed two nurses briefly—both steady, both dull, both chosen by Edith that morning for their gift for dullness—and inspected the dispensary, the linen store, the mortuary tent with its two occupants awaiting the burial party. At the mortuary she paused longer than anywhere else, reading the register of deaths line by line, and Edith stood beside her and felt the cold come off the ground into her boots and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *If it ends here, it ends here. But it does not end today.*

Because here was the arithmetic Edith trusted above all others. Two hundred and eleven names. Every one of them a man the army had misplaced, misfiled, or deliberately mislaid. If the ledger was found, Tamsin would be court-martialled, Edmund Voss would go down with her, Pip would be flogged back to some home he'd fled, and Edith herself—well. Edith was fifty-one years old and had been ready for consequences since 1914. Consequences and she were old acquaintances. They exchanged cards at Christmas.

But if the ledger was destroyed, two hundred and eleven men would die twice, and the second death would be the permanent kind, the kind Edith had watched happen to her sister Constance, whose grave had taken thirty years to find because a parish clerk in 1889 couldn't spell.

So: if it ends here, it ends here. But not without a fight, and not without cost to the other side.

"The chapel," Hallowell said.

* * *

Edith had prepared for this the way she prepared for hemorrhage: knowing it would come, refusing to know when.

"Of course," she said, and walked beside the matron across the yard, past the laundry lines where sheets hung frozen into sculptures of themselves, past the ambulance park where Driver Okonjo was checking a tire with tremendous concentration and did not look up, past everything and everyone who had spent the night knowing and pretending not to know, which was, Edith reflected, its own kind of family. Families weren't only people who loved you. They were people who lied for you with their posture.

Inside the chapel, the morning light lay across the shelves in amber bars, and the forty hymnals stood in their rows like a choir waiting for the downbeat.

Hallowell walked the length of the room slowly. She looked at the altar. She looked at the window. She looked at the shelf.

Edith folded her hands and became perfectly, professionally still—the stillness of a woman with nowhere to hide and therefore nothing to fear, which was the deepest cover there was, because it was true. She genuinely had nowhere to hide. She had decided, walking across the frozen yard, that if Hallowell reached for that book, Edith would step between her and it, physically, and take whatever followed. There was no plan beyond that. Plans were for people with exits. Edith had instead a certainty, which weighed less and traveled farther.

Hallowell stopped at the shelf.

Her gloved hand rose.

It hovered—one inch, perhaps two, from the spines—and Edith watched it hover and did not breathe and did not move and felt, beneath the serenity, her own heart going like a boy running with a message.

Then, from the doorway behind them, the captain cleared his throat.

"Matron. The motor's required at the rail head by eight. And there's the matter of the interviews at Number Six station—we're behind schedule."

Hallowell's hand came down.

Not on the book. On the shelf's edge, resting there a moment, fingers drumming once, twice—a small, deliberate percussion, a message of her own, though addressed to whom, Edith could not say. Then she turned.

"Your wards are clean, Matron Brightwater. Your records are tidy. Tidier than most." Her pale eyes rested on Edith's face for a long, unreadable moment. "Almost remarkably tidy."

"We do our best with what we're given."

"Yes," Hallowell said. "That is precisely what concerns me."

She drew on her glove more snugly—it had slipped, minutely, during the reaching—and walked out of the chapel into the white morning. At the door she paused, without turning.

"One more measure, effective immediately. Outgoing mail and parcels from this station will be opened and read at the adjutant's office. All of it. Effective within the hour." Now she did turn, and her face was composed and courteous and utterly without mercy. "A precaution against irregularities. You understand."

"Perfectly," Edith said.

The motor car crunched away across the frost, and the yard exhaled, and Edith stood alone in the chapel with the light and the books and the sound of her own heartbeat slowing from gallop to march.

She crossed to the shelf. She took down *HYMNS A&M — No. 3*, and held it a moment, feeling through the boards the weight of two hundred and eleven names, and understood with cold precision what the matron's parting words meant: the old routes were dead. No parcel, no mailbag, no ration crate would pass the gate now without being opened to its bones.

Which meant the ledger was now a prisoner in a prison that would soon be sealed entirely—because when the armistice came, and it was coming, everyone whispered it was coming, the records would be crated and shipped and locked in some archive where mistakes became history and history became stone.

Edith set the book back in its place among the hymns, aligned its spine with the others, and went to find Tamsin.

There were perhaps days left. Possibly hours. And every road out of this station now ran through a pair of opened hands.

She found Tamsin at the ward door, white-faced, having watched the whole inspection from the dispensary window.

"Well?" Tamsin whispered.

Edith smoothed a wrinkle from her apron that wasn't there.

"She nearly took it," Edith said. "And then she didn't. And I cannot decide, Sister, whether that was mercy, distraction, or a cat playing with something it fully intends to eat later. So we are going to assume the worst, and we are going to move the book today—because as of this morning, nothing leaves this station by post, by parcel, or by convoy gate." She met Tamsin's eyes, and let fifty-one years of composure soften, just slightly, into something warmer and more afraid. "We will need another way. And we will need it before the bells."
