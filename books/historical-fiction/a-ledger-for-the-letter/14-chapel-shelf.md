## 14. Chapel Shelf

The notice went up on the mess board at four o'clock in the afternoon, pinned under the same brass thumbtack that had held the Christmas roster and, before that, a warning about gas drill. *Inspection by Matron-in-Chief Hargreave, 0600 hours tomorrow. All wards, stores, and quarters to be prepared for review.*

Tamsin read it twice, standing with her tin mug going cold in her hand, and felt the words settle over her like snow falling into an open grave.

*All wards, stores, and quarters.*

Not the chapel. She read it a third time just to be sure. The chapel was not listed. The chapel was never listed; it belonged to God and the chaplain, and neither of them was subject to the administrative machinery of the Royal Army Medical Corps.

Her heart did not lift. It only began, very quietly, to hammer.

* * *

She found Brightwater in the linen store, folding sheets with the economy of a woman who had folded ten thousand of them and intended to fold ten thousand more before the war took her hands. The lantern hung low between them. Outside, the frost had come early and hard, and the canvas walls of the store crackled like paper whenever the wind leaned on them.

"You've seen it," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"I've seen it." Brightwater set a folded sheet on the pile, squared its corners with two fingers, and looked up. In the lantern light her face was all planes and patience, a face built for sieges. "Hargreave herself is coming. Not one of her deputies. That tells you how seriously someone in Rouen takes your little book."

"It's not my little book anymore. It hasn't been since Voss read his cousin's name off page eleven."

"No," Brightwater agreed mildly. "It's everyone's little book now. Which makes it heavier, not safer." She reached for another sheet. "Where is it?"

"The chapel. Where it's been since Tuesday."

"And where it will stay." Brightwater finished the sheet and turned from the pile at last, giving Tamsin the full weight of her attention—the attention that had steadied orderlies through their first amputations and sisters through their first deaths. "Listen to me, Amberdale. Tomorrow morning you will be exactly what you always are: competent, punctual, slightly too thin, and entirely unremarkable. You will answer every question put to you in four words or fewer. You will not look at the chapel door. You will not look away from the chapel door either—that's worse. You will look at Hargreave the way you look at any matron, which is to say the way one looks at weather."

"And if she goes into the chapel?"

Brightwater was quiet a moment. Somewhere beyond the canvas, a stretcher-bearer coughed, deep and rattling, and moved on.

"Then we learn whether God still does field promotions," she said.

* * *

They spent the evening preparing, because there was nothing else to do and because preparation was its own kind of prayer. Tamsin walked the wards with a lamp, checking that every blanket lay at regulation angle, that every chart was clipped to its frame, that the morphia ledger balanced to the last grain against the stocks. It was absurd work—Hargreave would find something regardless; Hargreaves always did—but the absurdity of it steadied her hands.

Pip Halloway waylaid her outside Ward C, breath steaming, eyes too bright. "Sister. Sister, if she comes near the stores, I could—"

"You could do nothing."

"But if I moved it out, just for the morning, I know a culvert behind the latrines—"

"Corporal Halloway." She used the rank deliberately, and watched his shoulders come down half an inch. "If you are caught moving anything tomorrow, you are caught carrying it. Do you understand? As long as that book sits where it sits, it belongs to nobody. The moment it moves, it belongs to somebody. We are not going to hand that woman a somebody."

He chewed on that, jaw working, sixteen years old and trying to grow into courage faster than his bones were growing into him. "And if she finds it anyway?"

Tamsin looked past him, toward the dark shape of the chapel at the end of the ward path, its tarpaulin roof furred white with frost, the small cross nailed above its door catching what light the stars allowed.

"Then it finds itself," she said. "Go to bed, Pip."

* * *

She did not go to bed. She told herself she was doing a final round, and perhaps she even believed it, but her feet carried her along the duckboards to the chapel door, and she let them.

Inside it smelled of paraffin and pinewood and the ghost of incense some prewar chaplain had insisted upon. Eleven men could sit in it if they were friendly about it. A harmonium no one had tuned since 1915 stood against one wall like a wounded animal. And on the shelf beneath the small east window, wedged upright between the hymnals where the sisters kept them for Sunday services, sat a book bound in dark cloth, its spine stamped in faded gold letters: *Hymns Ancient and Modern.*

Tamsin had done the binding herself, three nights ago, by candlelight, with paste boiled up from flour and a hymnal cover sacrificed from the damaged crate. Inside, the ledger looked like nothing at all—just pages ruled and filled, ninety-one names in her careful hand, each one with a date, a ward, and the reason the morning report had let it fall: *clerical omission. returned to unit in error, died en route. deserted, unrecorded. shot at dawn, entry suppressed.* Ninety-one men who had passed through this clearing station and out of the war's memory, except that a nurse with a pencil had refused to let them go.

She took it down now and held it against her chest, feeling its weight, which was nothing, which was everything.

"Tom," she said softly, to the empty chapel, to her brother wherever he was—not missing, never missing, only dead and mislabeled. "Tomorrow they count everything. Everything but you."

The harmonium ticked as it cooled, or perhaps as it warmed; it made noises no one had ever explained. Tamsin slid the ledger back between the hymnals, flush with their spines, and stepped back to look at the shelf as a stranger would see it. A row of identical dark bindings. One gold-stamped title among several. Nothing.

It would have to be enough. It was all there was.

* * *

At ten minutes to six the next morning the frost was still on the duckboards and the whole clearing station stood at a kind of attention it had not achieved since the King's cousin had passed through in '16. Tamsin stood at the head of Ward B with her hands folded, wearing her cleanest apron and her most forgettable face.

Matron-in-Chief Hargreave arrived precisely on time, which surprised no one. She was a tall, grey, unhurried woman with a voice like a filing cabinet and eyes that inventoried whatever they touched. She came up the ward path flanked by the commandant and by Matron Deele, the rival from the base hospital whose letter—*irregular record-keeping, unaccounted personnel, possible falsification of returns*—had brought all this down on them like weather.

"Sister Amberdale." Hargreave stopped before her. Up close her face was older than her uniform suggested, seamed around the mouth with lines that might have been carved by disapproval or by grief; it was impossible to tell, and Tamsin suspected the ambiguity was deliberate, a fortification.

"Matron-in-Chief."

"Your returns show forty-two beds. I count forty-six frames."

"We erected four extra in October, ma'am, during the push. They were never struck. The returns were corrected verbally to the quarter-master but I believe the paperwork—" Four words or fewer, Brightwater had said. Tamsin gave her six and hated each one.

"Hm." Hargreave wrote something in a leather notebook without looking at it. Then she walked the ward.

And here was the thing about being inspected that no one warned you of: it took forever and no time at all. Hargreave lifted blankets. She opened chart folders and read them upside down, which somehow made them more legible to her. She asked a wounded sergeant from the Lancashires how he was fed, and listened to his answer with such perfect courtesy that he told her more than he meant to, including a complaint about the tea that made Deele's mouth tighten. She counted the morphia stock aloud. She ran her finger along the top of the highest locker and examined it against the light.

Through all of it, Tamsin stood where she was told to stand and performed the great labour of looking calm. It was the hardest nursing she had ever done—keeping her own pulse down, keeping her face a smooth and professional surface, while somewhere behind her ribs ninety-one names waited in the cold like men in a trench waiting for a whistle.

Hargreave finished Ward B. She finished Wards A and C. She inspected the kitchen, the linen store, the dispensary, the officers' quarters, the laundry with its steam and its sanctity. At the stores she paused over the ration crates and had two of them opened, and Tamsin watched the empty cavity where a ledger had once hidden for three weeks feel, impossibly, like a wound reopening—there was nothing there, and still she nearly swayed.

Then the party came up the path toward the chapel.

Deele saw it first. Of course she did. Her chin lifted, and she said, in a voice pitched to carry, "The chapel, Matron-in-Chief? For completeness."

The chaplain, a mild, exhausted man named Reverend Alcott who had been forewarned by nothing and everyone, materialized at his own door with his keys already in his hand. "Of course, ladies. Mind the step; the frost gets into the boards."

The door opened. Cold light fell across the harmonium, across the eleven chairs, across the small altar with its tin cross.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the path and did not look at the chapel door, and did not look away from it, and understood suddenly why Brightwater's advice had contained both instructions. Both were impossible. She managed them anyway, the way soldiers manage walking upright under fire—by refusing to think about it.

Hargreave crossed the threshold. She stood a moment in the centre of the floor, turning slowly, taking in the room the way she had taken in the wards. Her gaze touched the harmonium. The chairs. The window with its painted-over glass. The altar.

The shelf.

Tamsin's heart stopped—not figuratively; she felt the actual stagger of it, a missed beat like a foot missing a stair—and then Hargreave stepped to the shelf, drew out a hymnal at random, opened it, sang two bars of the first line she saw, in a surprisingly true alto, and put it back.

"They're kept well," she said to Alcott. "Better than most."

"I have good sisters, ma'am."

"Mm." Hargreave closed the chapel door herself, gently, like a woman shutting a lid on something she wished to keep. And then she turned to the commandant, and her voice changed—the filing-cabinet voice, the voice of returns and regulations—and the morning tilted sideways.

"I am satisfied with the clinical standards here," she said. "Wards are clean, stocks balance, discipline is sound. However." A pause, measured out like medicine. "In view of the correspondence received regarding record-keeping, and pending full reconciliation of personnel figures, I am directing that all outgoing mail, parcels, and documents from this station be subject to censorship inspection, effective immediately. A postal orderly will be posted at the gate. Nothing leaves by post or parcel until my reconciliation is complete. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly, ma'am," said the commandant.

Tamsin heard it the way one hears a shell coming: clearly, from far enough away to understand exactly where it would land, and much too close to move.

* * *

Afterward, in Brightwater's kitchen—a real kitchen, brick-floored and wood-smoked, the one room in the clearing station that answered to no schedule—the three of them sat with cups of weak tea and said nothing for a long time.

Pip had been dismissed to his duties and was no doubt listening at the door instead. Edmund Voss arrived twenty minutes after the inspection party left, snow in his hair, having run—actually run—from the census office on the far side of the camp, and Brightwater poured him tea without asking and pushed bread-and-dripping at him until he ate.

"She searched the stores," Tamsin said. "She opened the crates. And then she stood in front of the shelf, and she sang a hymn, and she walked out."

"Perhaps she didn't see it," Edmund offered.

"She sees everything. That's her profession, same as yours." Tamsin turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "No. She saw it. She chose the wards, the kitchen, the dispensary—everywhere else—to make us think she might. And then she posted a guard on the mail, which is what she actually came to do. The inspection was theatre. The cordon is the operation."

Edmund set down his cup. He had changed in the weeks since the ration crate; the meticulous, faintly resentful clerk had been sanded away, and what remained was leaner and less certain of himself and far more dangerous. "You're saying she knows the book exists."

"I'm saying she suspects it strongly enough to seal every road out of this camp. Every road she can think of." Tamsin pressed her palms flat against the table, feeling the grain of the wood, grounding herself in something solid. "Which means we have to use the road she isn't thinking of."

"There isn't one," Brightwater said flatly. "Gate, rail siding, ambulance route, mail. All covered now, or coverable within a day. Hargreave is thorough."

"The post office in Amiens is covered. The rail yard is covered. The aid convoy gate is covered." Tamsin looked up. "But Ada Quill doesn't receive things. She takes them. Hand to hand, at the press office, through the press channel—which runs on different paperwork entirely, military press passes, correspondent chits. Hargreave controls the medical mails. She has no authority over the press wire office, and by the time she applied for it, the request itself would land on a desk in Amiens, where Miss Quill reads every document that crosses it."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the guard at the gate could be seen from the kitchen window, stamping his feet, a new figure in an old landscape.

"That's not a road," Brightwater said at last. "That's a thread. And threads break."

"Everything breaks. Roads break too—we've just watched ours break this morning." Tamsin heard her own voice steady itself, and marveled a little at it, the way one marvels at a comrade's courage. "But a thread only has to hold once."

Edmund was staring at the table, and when he raised his head there was something grim and resolved in his face that she had learned, in recent weeks, to be afraid of—in others, and lately in herself. "I have leave accrued," he said. "Four days. Unspent since August."

"You'd be carrying it through the rail yard checkpoint with your own name attached," Brightwater said.

"I know."

"And if you're taken, the book dies with you, and so does your career, and possibly considerably more than that."

"I know that too." He almost smiled. "I used to count doors, Sister Brightwater. Doors, windows, beds, heads. I have recently become very interested in the ones that open."

Tamsin looked from him to Brightwater to the frosted window, where the new sentry stamped and blew on his hands beside a mail sack already filling with letters that would go nowhere fast. Somewhere in Amiens, a blocked and furious correspondent was running out of days, and somewhere in this camp, a matron-in-chief was running out of patience, and somewhere above both of them the armistice was either rushing toward them or receding, no one knew which.

"We'll need a pass," Tamsin said. "A real one, or one good enough to die for. And we'll need it tonight."

Brightwater refilled the tea, unhurried, siege-hearted, and reached for the key that hung on the nail behind her—an old key, worn bright, that Tamsin had seen a hundred times and never once asked about.

"Then drink up," she said, "while I tell you about my sister Edith."
