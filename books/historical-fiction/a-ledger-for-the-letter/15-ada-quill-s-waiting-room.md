## 15. Ada Quill's Waiting Room

The waiting room of the *Continental Daily Mail* bureau in Amiens smelled of cold pipe smoke and wet wool, and Ada Quill had come to think of it as the truest room in France. Everything outside it was performance—the salutes, the communiqués, the maps with their tidy arrows crawling toward tidy victories. But here, in a cramped anteroom papered with rejected dispatches and the ghosts of stories no censor would let live, the war admitted what it was: an administration of grief with a publicity department.

She sat at the corner desk by the window, because the window had a crack in the glass that whistled when the wind came off the Somme, and she found she could not write unless something in the world was admitting to its wounds.

Three months. That was how long it had been since anything of hers had crossed the channel unbutchered. Three months of copy returned to her in red pencil—*not verified*, *not relevant to morale*, *not at this time*—until she had begun to keep the rejections in a drawer, filed like specimens, evidence of a second war fought in ink. The censors did not fear lies; lies were their currency. They feared particulars. A name was a particular. A name with a face and a mother's address in Croydon was a weapon.

So they struck the names out, and Ada collected the strikes, and waited for the thing that would make striking impossible.

"You're still here," said Halloran from the doorway, coat on, hat dripping. The bureau chief, a kind man worn down to pragmatism. "The wire says there may be movement at the front within the fortnight. Everyone's positioning."

"Movement," Ada repeated. She turned a page of her notebook without looking at it. "You mean the armistice."

"I mean nothing of the sort, and you'll print nothing of the sort." He said it gently, which was worse. "Ada. When it comes—and I'm not saying it will—you understand what happens to your kind of story?"

She understood perfectly. Armistice would be a door closing, not opening. Records sealed, shipped home, indexed into oblivion. Every discrepancy she'd chased for two years—the men who vanished between the casualty reports and the grave registrations, the wards that emptied overnight without a corresponding entry anywhere—would be locked behind the double seal of victory and bureaucracy. *We won; don't sully it.* History was written by the victors, but the paperwork was written by clerks, and clerks were never on anyone's side.

"I know," she said. "Which is why I need it before the bells, not after."

Halloran sighed and left, and Ada went back to waiting, which was the profession under the profession. Correspondence, her editor had told her in 1914, meant correspondence—a keeping of accounts, a faithful answering of letters. Nobody had mentioned that most of the job would be learning to sit still while the world refused to answer back.

* * *

They came at four o'clock, through rain that had turned to sleet somewhere over the cathedral spires. Ada heard them before she saw them: the uneven percussion of boots on the bureau stairs, one set marching, one set dragging, and between them a third tread light and quick, almost running.

Then Tamsin Amberdale was in her doorway, and Edmund Voss behind her, holding his hat like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at.

"Sister Amberdale," Ada said, rising. "And Mr. Voss. You look like a telegram that's been walking for days."

"We have one," Tamsin said. "Of sorts." She glanced down the corridor, then closed the office door with the deliberate care of someone shutting a ward curtain around a dying man. "How much time do we have before someone comes through that door?"

"Fifteen minutes, if the tea trolley behaves. Sit. Both of you." Ada pulled the second chair from beneath the desk and studied them while they settled. She'd met hundreds of people carrying secrets; they had a posture, a compression, like letters sealed too tightly. This woman had it in her spine. The man had it in his hands—he kept smoothing the brim of his hat as though it were a document requiring his signature.

"You wrote to me," Ada said. "Twice. Unsigned. About omitted names in casualty returns."

"I wrote carefully."

"You wrote well. I nearly traced you twice anyway. The postmark did half the work; the phrasing did the rest. 'Men absent from the morning report'—nobody writes *absent from the morning report* except someone who reads morning reports the way other people read scripture." Ada sat on the edge of her desk, arms folded. "Now. You've brought whatever it is through three checkpoints, if the mud on this gentleman's boots has anything to say about the rail yards. So before the tea trolley comes, tell me what you want from me, because I can promise you exactly what I can do, and I won't waste your courage on promises I can't keep."

Tamsin reached into her coat and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with surgical tape. She laid it on the desk between them the way one lays down a card that ends the game.

"Names," she said. "Two years of them. Men who died in my ward and appear in no record anywhere. Men listed as deserters who died under my hands with more honor than the officers who signed the lists. Men erased because erasing them was tidier than explaining them." Her voice didn't shake. That was the frightening part, Ada thought. It had gone past shaking. "I want them printed. All of them. Before the records are sealed."

Ada looked at the bundle and felt the old cynicism rise in her like scar tissue. She had been brought bundles before. Diaries, letters, photographs smuggled in boot heels—every one of them either unverifiable, unpublishable, or already superseded by a fresher atrocity. Grief made people certain their sorrow was the exception. She'd learned to be gentle about it and immovable underneath.

"May I?"

Tamsin nodded once.

Ada untied the tape. The oilcloth fell open on a ledger—not a diary, not a scrapbook, but a proper ledger, the kind quartermasters kept, with ruled columns and a spine cracked from use. The handwriting inside was small, upright, and mercilessly neat. Each entry occupied two lines: name, rank, regiment. Then date, place, manner of death. Then, in a narrower column, the reason the man had gone unrecorded—written in the same flat hand, as though she were noting a temperature.

*Pte. Alfred Denning, 2nd Royal West Kents. Died 14 March, gas, Ward C. No entry: reported wounded-in-transit, convoy lost in reorganization.*

*L/Cpl. Harold Voss, 7th Buffs. Died 2 June, wounds, Ward B. No entry: posted missing prior to admission; death never reconciled.*

Ada stopped.

Her finger stayed on the third line of that entry longer than reading required. The rain ticked against the cracked glass. Somewhere downstairs the telegraph clattered its idiot, faithful rhythm.

"Voss," she said.

"My cousin," Edmund said quietly, from his chair. He had gone very still. "Harold. My mother's sister's boy. We were told missing in June. There was—" He stopped, started again. "There was a form. One form, and then nothing. My aunt writes to the War Office every month. They answer every fourth letter with the same sentence."

"He died in my ward," Tamsin said. "On the second of June. I held the pen that should have entered him, and the entry went nowhere, and I have spent two years understanding why and one year doing something about it."

Ada turned pages. The ledger was thorough in a way that made her chest hurt—it wasn't a protest, it was an audit. Dates cross-referenced against convoy manifests. Ward numbers matched to bed counts. In the back, a section of corroborations: statements from orderlies, a chaplain, a driver, each signed, each dated. Someone had built a case. Not a memorial. A case.

"This is verifiable," she heard herself say, and was surprised by her own voice. "This is—Sister, do you understand what you've done? You haven't written testimony. You've written *accounting*. The one language the War Office cannot call sentimental. If even a tenth of these entries reconcile against the grave registration units—"

"They reconcile against all of them. I checked before I wrote. That was the discipline. I only ever recorded what I could prove, and I only ever proved what I recorded." Tamsin's composure bent, finally, just slightly, at the edge. "It took longer that way. Some nights I wanted to write everything, all of it, every face. But a ledger that can be dismissed saves no one."

Ada set the book down and walked to the window, because she needed a moment and would rather spend it looking at sleet than at strangers. Twenty-two years in journalism had taught her the precise weight of paper. Most paper weighed nothing. This weighed like ballast.

Here was the arithmetic she ran automatically, the professional calculus, the armor: *Unverifiable sources. Anonymous correspondent. Seditious content in wartime. Libel exposure. Prison.* Every column came up red. And then beneath the columns, quiet as a clerk's footnote, another calculation she hadn't run in years: *A woman named Harold's name so his mother could stop writing letters to a wall.* 

"How many," she asked, turning back. "In total."

"Four hundred and eleven," Tamsin said. "As of last night."

"Four hundred and eleven mothers."

"Some are fathers. Wives." A pause. "One is a boy of sixteen standing in your corridor pretending to be a stretcher-bearer, because he carried this book through a shell barrage at the rail yard and I'd thank you not to tell him it was foolish."

"The boy stays out of it," Ada said. "If this goes wrong, he was never in this building. That's not negotiable; that's the first law." She came back to the desk and put both palms flat on the ledger, feeling its warmth—or imagining she did. "Now listen to me, both of you, because time is the whole story now."

She counted it off on her fingers, briskly, because briskness was how she kept her hands from shaking.

"One: the moment the armistice is signed, every field record in this theatre begins the sealing process. I've seen the orders—they're drafted, waiting for a signature. Forty-eight hours after the guns stop, this book becomes contraband history. Two: the censors hold my copy until they've killed it, which means I cannot send it through channels. It goes into type directly, here, on our own press, and it ships in the diplomatic pouch with the Thursday edition—which means I need final copy by Wednesday night. Three:" she looked at Tamsin squarely, "it cannot be anonymous. An unsigned list is a rumor. Rumors die. It needs a named witness willing to stand behind every entry, which means you, Sister, in print, under your own name, knowing what the military will call you afterward."

Silence. The radiator knocked. Down the hall, someone laughed at something, the ordinary laughter of the ordinary world.

"I watched my brother die," Tamsin said at last, "and then I watched them write him out of the world. I have been a coward about my own name for two years. I won't be one about theirs."

"Good," Ada said, and hated how easily it came out, and meant it completely. "Because here's the last of the arithmetic. Even if everything holds—even if the press runs, the pouch flies, the censors sleep—this prints hours after the signing, at best. Maybe minutes. Maybe after. It will not stop the war. It will not punish anyone. By the time it's read, everyone responsible will be decorating themselves in London."

"I know," Tamsin said. "That's not why."

"No," Ada said. "It isn't, is it." She looked down at the ledger—at Harold Voss's name sitting patiently in its ruled column, waiting two years for a reader—and felt the scar tissue crack straight through. Four hundred and eleven. Not statistics, not a trend piece, not *questions raised in the Commons*. Names. Names were the only ammunition that never expired; they could wait out any government, any censor, any century. All they needed was someone to fire them once.

"Mr. Voss," she said. "You've been very quiet. In my experience, quiet men in bureaus are either useless or indispensable. Which?"

Edmund Voss looked up, and Ada saw what Tamsin must have seen somewhere on the road: a man who had spent his life counting things discovering what counting was for. "I falsified a census report yesterday," he said. "My life's work, and I falsified it to protect this book. I expect you can use a man who knows exactly how the reconciliation tables work—who can prove, figure against figure, that these numbers aren't sentiment. They're arithmetic. I'm told I'm good at arithmetic."

Ada smiled for the first time in three months, and it felt like frost breaking.

"Yes," she said. "I rather think I can."

She pulled fresh copy paper from the drawer, uncapped the ink, and set the first clean sheet before her. Outside, the sleet thickened against the glass, and the whistle in the crack rose to a thin sustained note, like something held breath too long finally letting go.

"Now," Ada Quill said, "we work. And God help us all, we finish before the bells—because I have just realized what day the pouch leaves."

Tamsin went pale. "What day?"

"Thursday," said Ada. "Armistice or no armistice, the pouch leaves Thursday morning. And today is Tuesday."

* * *

Downstairs, the telegraph chattered on, faithful and blind, ticking out the war's last official sentences. Upstairs, in a room that smelled of cold smoke and wet wool, a correspondent dipped her pen, a nurse opened a ledger, and a census taker began, at long last, to count what mattered.

None of them heard the front door below, or the wet boots on the stair, or the knock—two sharp raps—that stopped the room like a stopped heart.
