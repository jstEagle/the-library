## 16. The Long Road to Amiens

**Tamsin**

The patient they were escorting was named Corporal Ellis Fenwick, and he was the most useful man in France, because he had a discharge chit, a missing left foot, and an unshakable conviction that he owed his life to Sister Amberdale personally.

"Two of us for one of him," Edmund had said at the gate, presenting the papers in that flat census-taker's voice that made everything sound like arithmetic. "Escort duty. Amiens and back."

The transport officer had looked at Tamsin's face—she'd made sure it was grey with fatigue, which took no effort—and stamped the chit without reading it twice. That was the whole trick of moving something past authority: give authority something duller than itself to look at. A wounded corporal is dull. A ledger sewn into the lining of a greatcoat is not.

Now they were forty miles out from the clearing station, wedged into the back of a supply lorry between crates of condensed milk and a stack of stretchers, and Corporal Fenwick was asleep with his head lolling against Tamsin's shoulder, breathing through his mouth like a boy. The road jolted under them. Frost furred the canvas at the tailgate. Somewhere ahead, guns still worked the line at Albert, a sound so constant by now that she felt it less in her ears than in her teeth.

Edmund sat opposite, knees nearly touching hers in the narrow space, holding a clipboard he wasn't reading. He'd been holding it since dawn as if it were ballast keeping him upright.

"You can put that down," she said. "Nobody's going to count you out here."

"I'm aware." He set it on his knees anyway.

The lorry swayed around a shell hole's shoulder. Fenwick murmured something in his sleep—*mum*—and Tamsin shifted so his head found her collarbone instead of the crate edge.

"He thinks I saved him," she said quietly. "I held a basin while they took the foot. That's all I did. But you tell a man at twenty-two that the leg has to go, and whoever's face he sees when he wakes up becomes the face of mercy. It isn't fair to them, being grateful. It weighs more than they know."

Edmund watched the sleeping corporal for a moment. "My mother used to say gratitude is a debt people pay to the wrong person because the right one won't take payment."

"That sounds like something a woman said to a small boy who was counting things."

"It was exactly that." He almost smiled. The almost was new on him; she was learning its geography—the slight loosening at the corner of his mouth, the way his eyes went to hers and then away, disciplined even now. "She died in 'fifteen. Influenza. There was no ledger for her either. Just the parish register, and my father couldn't bring himself to write the entry, so I did it. I was fourteen. I wrote her dates in my best hand and thought: this is what remains. Fifty strokes of ink and then nothing."

"And so you became a man who counts," Tamsin said.

"And so I became a man who counts." He looked down at the clipboard, turned it over, squared its edges against his knee. "I told myself accuracy was a kind of honour. Every person accounted for, every door, every hearth. If everyone is counted, no one vanishes. That was the creed. And then I stood in your ward and found out how easy it is for the count to lie. Not because the numbers are wrong. Because someone decides, before the counting starts, who deserves to be counted."

The guns went on. Fenwick slept on.

"My brother," Tamsin said.

It came out before she decided to say it, which was how the true things always came. She had rehearsed this confession for three days—on the ward, in the wash tent, walking the duckboards at night—and now that it arrived she found she didn't want the rehearsal. She wanted the raw version, the one only spoken once.

"Thomas. Thomas Amberdale. He was two years younger than me and could not sit still for a sermon or a meal or a winter. He enlisted at seventeen with forged consent our father signed without looking, because looking would have meant stopping him." She kept her voice level, the nurse's voice, the one that reported wounds without flinching. "In the spring I was posted within reach of his sector. I got word he was coming through the clearing station, wounded but alive, and I waited two days for him. On the second night there was a push and the wounded came in like a tide with debris in it. And he was among them. Not among the living ones."

She felt Edmund go very still across the narrow space, the way men did when they understood that moving might end the sentence.

"I identified him myself. I held his hand—it was warm, they're warm for hours, nobody tells you that—and I wrote his name on the tag and pinned it, and I signed the form, and I thought: whatever else happens, he will be counted. His mother will get a telegram with a true word in it. Died. Not missing. *Died.*"

"But the word was missing."

"The word was missing." Her throat closed and opened again around it. "A clerical collapse—they'd moved three hundred tags that night, the adjutant told me later, a rush, a mix-up, a form misfiled. He was very sorry. Sorry is free in the army. My mother got a telegram saying Missing Believed Killed, and she spent eleven months believing the believed part. She wrote to every bureau. She walked to every train station in Kent where returning men came through, holding his photograph, asking boys coming off the trains, have you seen him, have you seen him. Eleven months. Then a parcel came back from a depot with his effects, and inside was the tag with my own handwriting on it, and she knew I had known, and she never—" 

Tamsin stopped. Fenwick stirred; she adjusted the blanket over him with hands that were perfectly steady, because they had learned steadiness as a survival trait years ago and would keep it long after the war forgot them.

"She never said a word against me," she finished. "That was worse. She just began setting two places less at table instead of one, and she stopped writing to me except at Christmas. She died in February. I got leave too late." She looked at Edmund straight on. "So now you know why the ledger exists. It isn't defiance. Defiance is too grand a word. It's arithmetic. Somebody counted wrong, and somebody has to count right, and it turns out the army will not do it, so a nurse does it in pencil in a ration crate."

Edmund didn't say anything for a long time. The lorry ground up a hill, gears complaining.

"When I asked to see a page," he said finally, "in your ward. You thought I was going to report you."

"I hoped you weren't. I prepared for both."

"I want you to know what I actually saw when I read it." He spoke carefully, like a man laying out evidence at his own trial. "I saw my cousin's name. Arthur Voss. We grew up four houses apart in Croydon. He called me Neddy until he was nineteen and decided it was undignified. He's in your book, Sister, because some clerk decided a man who was carried off the field by stretcher-bearers who were themselves killed ten minutes later was *deserted in the face of the enemy*, and it was easier to write that than to find out otherwise. His father hanged himself over the word. I found that in a letter from my aunt, folded into my kit, the same week I found your ledger. Two pieces of paper, and one of them was true."

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said, and meant it the way she meant it over bodies—with her whole weight behind it.

"So am I. And here is the cowardice I have to confess, since we're trading tonight." He laughed, briefly, without any humour in it. "You think mine was hesitating at the gate, or lying to the transport officer. It wasn't. I lied well—that frightened me, discovering I could, but it isn't the shame. The shame is earlier. The first day, when I reconciled your headcounts against the grave markers and the numbers wouldn't meet, I knew something was being hidden. And I chose to believe the simplest explanation—that you were incompetent. Sloppy record-keeping. I wrote *discrepancies noted, follow-up required* in my notes and felt righteous about noting them. I gave myself permission to think the worst of you because thinking the worst was safe. If you were merely careless, I didn't have to care."

"That's not cowardice," Tamsin said. "That's Tuesday. Everyone does that."

"Everyone does that," he agreed, "and the world is full of Tuesdays, and that's how the names get lost. Not by villains. By everyone, on ordinary days, choosing the version that requires nothing of them." He met her eyes. "When I asked to read the page, I told myself I was investigating. When I struck the bargain with you, I told myself I was managing risk. It took me until the convoy gate—until I heard my own voice lie smoothly to a captain with a manifest—to admit what I was actually doing. I was trying to buy back a name. One name, Arthur Voss, and I'd have settled for one. And then I thought: how many people out there are owed one? How many Neddies are standing at gates all along this road, willing to trade anything?"

The lorry topped the hill and the valley opened below them—mud fields stitched with frost, a shattered orchard standing like a congregation, the far smoke of Amiens itself, grey against a grey sky.

"Then don't settle for one," Tamsin said.

* * *

**Edmund**

They changed lorries at a crossroads where a military policeman waved them through with the boredom of eternal repetition, and after that they walked the last miles into Amiens behind Corporal Fenwick's wheelchair, which Pip Halloway—waiting at the rail yard with a borrowed chair and a grin he hadn't quite earned yet—pushed with tremendous ceremony.

"No one looks at a nurse, a clerk, and a boy pushing a cripple," Pip had declared, and then gone scarlet at his own phrasing while Fenwick roared with laughter from the chair. "Corporal, sir, I didn't mean—"

"You meant it exact," Fenwick said. "Push on, lad."

Now the boy ranged ahead with the chair, showing off his steering, and Edmund and Tamsin walked behind through streets that had been shelled and rebuilt and shelled again, shopfronts boarded with timber and painted with the names of the shops that used to be there, so that a street could be remembered while it was rubble. He found that unbearably moving and said so, and Tamsin looked at the boards a long moment.

"They do that so people can find their way home," she said. "Even when home is a hole in the ground. You need the name painted somewhere."

He understood she was not talking about shops.

They lodged that night at a convent school converted to a rest billet, in a corridor of iron cots separated by curtains, and after lights-out they talked in whispers across the aisle, the way children at a boarding school might, except about things no child should know.

"Tell me about Croydon," she whispered.

"Why?"

"Because we may die tomorrow at a press office checkpoint, and I've confessed my dead to you and you've confessed yours, but I don't know what your house looked like. I refuse to be shot alongside a stranger."

"We're not going to be shot. Ada Quill says the press office is the least guarded building in France because censors trust paper more than they trust people."

"Tell me anyway."

So he told her. About the semi-detached house with the hedge his father trimmed into rigid obedience; about the smell of his mother's bread on Saturdays; about Arthur teaching him to smoke behind the gasworks and both of them sick under the hedge; about the summer of 1913 when Arthur had a girl named Lily and lent Edmund his good coat for a dance and Edmund had spilled punch on the sleeve and hidden the coat for a week rather than confess, and Arthur had never found out—or had found out and said nothing, which was somehow the same kindness.

"Lily married someone else in 1916," Edmund said. "Arthur heard about it in a letter and laughed and said good for her, life's short. He was right about the second part."

Tamsin was quiet. Through the thin curtain he could see the shape of her sitting up, knees drawn in.

"I had someone," she said at last. "Before the war. A curate, God help us both, named Harold. He proposed the week before I started nursing training, and I said I needed to finish the course first, and then the war came, and he joined up as a chaplain to be near the men, and he was killed carrying a stretcher at Loos—a chaplain, they gave him a rifle range target practice and then sent him out unarmed among dying men." Her breath caught and steadied. "I keep his last letter in my kit. He wrote that when the war ended he hoped I would look at him again the way I looked at the work. Isn't that a terrible thing for a man to write? To know he's competing with a vocation and losing gracefully."

"Is he competing?" Edmund asked, and then immediately: "Forgive me. That's not a question I'm entitled to."

"It's a question I invited." The curtain rustled; her silhouette turned toward his cot. "No. He isn't competing, because Harold is dead, and the dead don't compete, they just accompany. But I'll tell you what frightens me, Edmund, since we're being thorough about confessions. I have spent five years making myself into a person who can hold a basin and write a name and feel precisely as much as the work allows and no more, because feeling more gets nurses killed—your hands shake, you miss a vein, you weep at the wrong moment. And now there is a ledger, and a road, and you, and I can feel the discipline fraying, and I don't know whether that's the beginning of becoming a person again or the beginning of dying."

The guns were distant here, a mutter rather than a voice. Somewhere in the convent a clock ticked with obscene domesticity.

"What do you want it to be?" he asked. "The fraying. Beginning or ending."

"That depends entirely," she said, "on what happens tomorrow, doesn't it."

"Yes," he agreed. "And on the day after."

"Don't," she said softly. "Don't build past tomorrow. I've watched too many people make plans across a week and bury them by Friday. Tomorrow we deliver the ledger. After that—" He heard her exhale, the sound of a woman deliberately closing a door on herself. "After that there's tea, and we see who's still alive, and we count that."

"That's a bleak arithmetic."

"It's the only honest one I know."

He lay in the dark and listened to her breathing slow toward sleep, and he understood that she had answered him completely, and that the answer was neither yes nor no but *survive first*—and that this, from her, was not rejection but the largest offer she knew how to make. He turned it over the way he'd once turned over coins to check their dates, and found no flaw in it, and no comfort either, and fell asleep holding both facts.

* * *

**Tamsin**

Morning came white and hard. They dressed in silence, checked the greatcoat lining where the ledger rode flat against Edmund's chest—he had insisted on wearing it himself, arguing that a nurse escorting a patient would be searched sooner than a bored clerk—and ate the convent's black bread and chicory coffee standing up, like soldiers, though none of them were quite that anymore.

Fenwick insisted on coming to the press office square. "I'll wait by the fountain," he said. "Man with one foot sitting in a wheelchair attracts no attention at all, and if you come out running, I want to know which way to point the shouting."

Pip walked with them as far as the Rue des Trois Cailloux, then stopped, suddenly formal, and shook each of their hands like a small businessman concluding a merger. "If it goes wrong," he said, "I've got the copy of page one sewn in my cap. Brightwater's idea. So it can't all be lost."

"Nothing is going to go wrong," Tamsin said.

"No, miss," Pip agreed cheerfully, and ran.

The press office stood on the corner where two streets met, a tall narrow building with sandbags at the ground floor and, above them, windows that had been repaired with new glass in old frames. A single sentry at the door. A queue of correspondents and officers waiting to submit dispatches. Ordinary. Almost aggressively ordinary.

Ada Quill would be inside, behind some desk, waiting on a column that had been strangled by censors for months. The ledger was warm from Edmund's body. The bells of the cathedral, when they rang the hour, sounded closer than usual, or perhaps that was only the morning, or perhaps—

Tamsin stopped on the pavement. Across the street, outside the censor's entrance, a staff car stood with its engine running, and two men in the uniform of the Provost Marshal's office were speaking to the sentry, and one of them was holding a piece of paper up to the light.

She touched Edmund's arm. He followed her gaze, and she watched him go pale by degrees, the colour leaving his face like tide off a flat beach.

"That's a warrant description," he said quietly. "Or a circulation notice. They're checking faces at the door, Tamsin. Someone has described us to someone."

The bells rang nine. The sentry looked up, directly across the street, directly at them.
