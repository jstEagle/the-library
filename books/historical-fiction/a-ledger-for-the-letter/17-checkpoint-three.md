## 17. Checkpoint Three

The rain began an hour out of Amiens, a thin grey sifting that turned the road to porridge and made the escort's mackintosh shine like something gutted. Edmund Voss sat in the back of the ambulance with his hands folded on his knee and did the thing he had done all his life when he could not afford to feel: he counted.

Mileposts. Eleven since the last crossroads. Raindrops per square foot of window, roughly, if one assumed even distribution. The breaths of Corporal Deene, their discharged patient, asleep against the canvas wall—fourteen to the minute, steady, mercifully unremarkable. Tamsin sat across from him with Deene's kit bag between her boots, and she was not counting anything. She was watching the road as though it were a wound she intended to dress.

They had rehearsed the story twice in a barn outside Corbie while the driver smoked and pretended deafness. A discharged man going home to Amiens for demobilization processing; a nurse escorting him; a civilian clerk—Edmund had produced his census credentials, laminated and absurd—attached for administrative verification. Every document genuine except the purpose. It struck him that his whole life had been training for this: years of making paperwork agree with reality, and now three days spent making reality agree with paperwork.

"You're doing the numbers," Tamsin said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Your lips move. You're counting something."

Edmund closed his mouth. Outside, a shattered orchard went by, trees amputated at the waist, still standing because no one had bothered to finish them.

"It steadies me," he admitted.

"And what does it tell you?"

"That we are eleven miles from the last checkpoint and that I have lied successfully at two of them already." He looked down at his hands. "Which frightens me more than being caught."

She considered this with the same level attention she gave a pulse rate. "Why frightens you?"

"Because I used to be a man who couldn't. Lie, I mean. At school they called me Honest Voss, which was not affectionate." He almost smiled. "I built a career on it. Counting things and reporting them exactly. And now I find the machinery works just as well in reverse. Same attention. Same precision. I told that transport officer at the convoy gate that Deene's kit contained surgical dressings, and I gave him the inventory from memory, and my voice never moved half a tone. He believed me completely. I could have told him the moon was paper and he'd have written it down."

Tamsin reached across the kit bag—not quite touching his hand, but near enough that he felt the warmth of her knuckles through the cold—and said nothing, which was its own kind of absolution.

The ambulance slowed.

Through the rain-streaked rear window Edmund saw the checkpoint: a timber barrier across the road, sandbags furred green with damp, a Nissen hut with a stovepipe breathing smoke, and beside it a larger building with a painted board that read PRESS CENSORSHIP OFFICE — AMIENS — BY AUTHORITY. Two military policemen under an oilcloth awning. A queue of dispatch riders. And beyond the barrier, up a cobbled lane, the grey bulk of the town itself, where somewhere Ada Quill waited in a room full of killed columns and unsent cables, keeping a page of his cousin's name like a coal banked against morning.

* * *

The MP who waved them to a halt was young and bored, but the man who came out of the censorship office was neither.

He was perhaps fifty, comfortably upholstered in a staff captain's coat, with the ruddy, well-fed face of a man the war had declined to inconvenience. He stood under the awning reading a signal flimsy, and when he glanced up at the ambulance his eyes passed over Tamsin, over the sleeping corporal, and stopped on Edmund with the small mechanical click of a lock finding its key.

"Good God," the captain said. "Voss. Edmund Voss."

The world narrowed to a point. Edmund heard himself say, "Captain," in a voice borrowed from 1911, from a lecture hall, from another life.

"Bramwell-Hurst." The man descended the steps, hand extended, delighted. "We were at the School of Economics together—you wouldn't remember, you never remembered anyone below first in class, and I was reliably fourth. You used to mark the attendance register for old Professor Lachlan. Devastatingly thorough. Half the college hated you for it."

"I'm surprised you remember me at all."

"Oh, everyone remembers the man with the ledger." Bramwell-Hurst laughed at his own phrase, and Edmund felt the word go through him like shrapnel. "What on earth are you doing out here? Don't tell me they've finally sent someone to count the war."

"Personnel verification," Edmund said. "Census section. Escorting this discharged corporal to processing, with Sister Amberdale."

Bramwell-Hurst peered past him into the ambulance. His gaze touched Tamsin's face, the kit bag, the blanket-wrapped figure of Deene, and returned. There was nothing predatory in it—that was the terrible thing. He was simply a man doing arithmetic he had done ten thousand times, and he had done it quickly.

"Well," he said. "You'll want stamping, then. Come inside out of the wet. All of you—no, leave the patient with the driver, there's a good fellow. Just papers."

* * *

The censorship office smelled of tea, tobacco, and carbon paper. Clerks bent over desks beneath hanging electric bulbs, stamping and sealing the war's correspondence into obedience. Bramwell-Hurst led them to a side room furnished with a deal table, two chairs, and a framed photograph of the King looking mildly apologetic.

He took their papers. He read them properly—which no one at the other checkpoints had done—turning pages over, checking dates, holding Tamsin's nursing credential up to the bulb as though truth might be visible by transmitted light.

"Corporal Deene," he said. "Discharged, medical category C. Going forward to Amiens for final processing." He set the papers down and laced his fingers. "And your cargo, Sister? Ambulances don't run this route empty-handed. What's in the kit bags?"

"Dressings," Tamsin said. "Personal effects for discharge processing. Nothing requiring your stamp."

"Mm." Bramwell-Hurst smiled without warmth. "You'd be astonished what doesn't require my stamp. That's rather the whole of my job, finding the things people believe don't require it." He rose, crossed to the door, and opened it. "Wait here, both of you. I'll have a look at the vehicle. Procedure. You understand."

The door closed. Tamsin's face did not change, but Edmund saw her thumb press white against her forefinger—the only tell she permitted herself, learned, he supposed, across four years of wards where panic was contagious and therefore lethal.

"He'll find it," Edmund said. "It's in Deene's kit. Under the dressings. Wrapped in oilcloth."

"I know where it is."

"If he finds it, he finds it with your name on the manifest and mine on the escort order. Both of us."

"Yes." She met his eyes. "Say what you like, then. I won't confirm anything."

"Tamsin—"

"No." Quiet as a pulse counted down. "You've done your part. Two checkpoints. More than anyone asked. If it comes apart here, it comes apart on me. I carried it into every ward for two years; I can carry the blame out of one room."

He wanted to argue and found he had no argument, only terror wearing his face. He sat very still and listened to the rain on the roof and thought, absurdly, about Professor Lachlan's attendance register, and how he had once ruled a line through the name of a boy who had died over the Christmas holidays because no one had told the college, and how he had felt then—nine years ago, a lifetime ago—that the worst thing in the world was a name on a list that no longer matched the world.

The door opened.

* * *

Bramwell-Hurst came in alone. He was carrying no kit bag. He was carrying nothing at all, and that frightened Edmund more than an arrest would have.

He set a key down on the table, deliberately, where they could see it was not a handcuff key. Then he sat, took out a tin of cigarettes, offered them around, and was refused by both of them with the synchronized reflexes of the guilty.

"The kit bag is on the table in the next room," he said pleasantly. "Under a dressing roll wrapped in oilcloth, there is a book. Bound boards, cloth spine, hand-ruled columns. Names." He let the word sit. "A great many names. Cross-referenced, if I'm not mistaken, against casualty lists—against the omissions in casualty lists. Do you know how long it's been since anyone brought something interesting into this office? Months of love letters and black-market cheese requests. And then a book of ghosts walks in under a corporal's spare shirt."

Tamsin said, "I've never seen that book."

"No," Bramwell-Hurst agreed. "I don't imagine you have. And you, Voss? Have you seen it?"

Every instinct Edmund possessed—the schoolboy's, the clerk's, the coward's—screamed the same instruction: deny, deflect, survive. He opened his mouth to do all three.

And then he thought of his cousin. Walter Voss, of the 8th Norfolks, who had played the violin badly and loved it sincerely. Who had gone missing at Gallipoli, and whose name their grandmother had entered in the family Bible in pencil, so it could be erased if better news came, and had never been erased because better news never came. Walter, whose name Edmund had read three days ago in a ration crate in a freezing ward, in Tamsin's careful upright hand, with a date and a place and the single annotation *witnessed*, and had understood in one vertical drop of the stomach that his entire profession—the whole sacred apparatus of counting and recording—had walked past his cousin and recorded nothing.

"I've seen it," Edmund said. "I've read it. Page forty-one. My cousin is on page forty-one."

Bramwell-Hurst raised his eyebrows, genuinely interested, the way a lepidopterist is interested. "Well. There's honesty. I wondered which of you would spend it first." He leaned back, chair creaking. "Now. Let me save us all some unpleasantness, because I liked you at college, Voss, and because I am, despite appearances, a reasonable man in an unreasonable posting. Here is the situation. In"—he consulted his watch—"some number of days, possibly hours, there will be an armistice. Everyone upstairs knows it and no one will say it. When it comes, every record in France gets sealed, crated, shipped home, and forgotten by Christmas. Including that book. Unless it becomes news before it becomes archive. You understand the difference. News is read. Archive is dust."

"We understand it perfectly," Tamsin said.

"Of course you do; you've been driving toward it for days. So here is my offer, and I'll make it once." He tapped the table with one finger, each tap a clause. "The book stays here. Officially, it was intercepted—irregular record-keeping by hospital personnel, a minor scandal, handled internally, names protected. Unofficially, it goes into my safe until the sealing orders arrive, and then into a crate, and then into the sea of dust. No one is harmed. No one is charged. Sister Amberdale returns to her station with a reprimand at worst. And you, Voss—" he smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the worst thing yet— "you write a short statement. One column's worth. 'A nurse at a clearing station kept unauthorized records causing distress to bereaved families.' Signed. Published in the garrison bulletin. Denounce the book, denounce the method, walk out that door with your career not merely intact but advanced—I will personally see your promotion through, and I mean that literally; I owe favours in London. One paragraph of ink, Voss, and you go free, and the book goes quiet, and in five years nobody remembers any of it. That is the shape of mercy in the year 1918. Take it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere a clerk's typewriter knocked away like a distant machine gun.

Edmund looked at the key on the table. At the photograph of the King. At Tamsin, whose face had gone still in the particular way he had learned meant she was holding herself together with discipline alone, and whose eyes were telling him—he realized with a strange, floating clarity—absolutely nothing. She would not ask him. She would not beg. She had decided in the barn at Corbie that his soul was his own jurisdiction, and she was honoring the border.

His whole life he had been the man who marked the register accurately. Who counted doors and reported doors. And he saw now, with the terrible simplicity of a sum finally balancing, that accuracy had never been the virtue—only the instrument. The question was always what the count was *for*. Bramwell-Hurst counted too. Bramwell-Hurst's books balanced beautifully, and they were full of the same silence that had swallowed Walter Voss and four hundred others, silence dressed in carbon paper and stamped and sealed and shipped to the sea of dust.

One paragraph. One lie, published under his own name, in exchange for everything.

"No," Edmund said.

Bramwell-Hurst sighed, not angrily. Almost regretfully. "No?"

"No. I won't write it." His voice was steady, and he noted its steadiness the way he noted all data, distantly, as though it belonged to someone else. "Those names are the only true record those men will ever get. Their mothers were told *missing*. Their wives were told *missing*. Some clerk's error or somebody's convenience erased them, and that book is the last place in the world where the erasure is undone. I will not put my name on the erasure. I spent my whole life learning to make records true. It seems a poor time to stop."

"You understand," Bramwell-Hurst said gently, "that this is not the brave option you appear to think it. You're a civilian clerk obstructing military censorship in a war zone, in possession of—well, we'll find the right charge; there's always a right charge. You'll be detained. Possibly weeks. Possibly longer if the armistice disappoints us. And the book still ends up in my safe either way, because it's on my table and you're about to be in a cell. So you will have lost, Voss. Both of you. Entirely."

"Then I'll have lost entirely," Edmund said, "for the right side of the sum."

Bramwell-Hurst studied him for a long moment, and something moved behind the comfortable face—not respect, Edmund thought; something older and sadder, the look of a man seeing a door he had shut on himself thirty years ago and hearing, faintly, knocking.

"Pity," Bramwell-Hurst said, and stood. "Sergeant!"

The door opened. The young MP from the barrier filled it, rain dripping off his cape.

"Take Mr. Voss into custody. Room at the back, the one with the cot. Charge sheet: unauthorized removal of official records, obstruction of censorship, and we'll see what else. The sister is free to go—her papers are in order and the patient's waiting." He turned back, and for a moment his voice dropped out of the officer register into something almost private. "The book stays in my office tonight. That much I'll grant you—it sleeps indoors, out of the wet. Draw whatever comfort from that you can."

* * *

They let him and Tamsin have thirty seconds in the corridor while the sergeant fetched the paperwork, and it was the strangest thirty seconds of Edmund's life: standing arm's length apart under a flickering bulb, unable to touch, speaking in the flat code of strangers because the sergeant was six feet away pretending to fill in a form.

"The driver knows the address," Tamsin said, low and fast. "Rue de la République, above the bakery, the blue door. Ada Quill. If the book leaves this building, it has to be tonight, before the sealing orders. I'll get it out. However I have to."

"They'll search you."

"They searched me at every checkpoint between here and heaven. I'm still carrying everything that matters." Her eyes were bright and dry and furious. "You knew Bramwell-Hurst would offer you the way out, and you refused it before he finished the sentence. Why?"

Because of Walter, he wanted to say. Because of a pencil name in a family Bible. But what came out was simpler, and truer, and it cost him more:

"Because you taught me what the count is for."

For one moment her hand came up and gripped his forearm, hard, through the wet wool—a nurse's grip, clinical and fierce, taking his pulse as if to confirm he existed—and then the sergeant cleared his throat and said, "Mr. Voss?" and the moment ended the way moments do, without ceremony.

They walked him down the corridor. At the cell door he paused and looked back, and she was already turning away toward the rain and the ambulance and the blue door in Amiens, her shoulders squared under the wet cape like a woman walking into a ward she intended to save.

The door shut. The bolt went home. Edmund Voss sat down on a narrow cot in a censorship office in Amiens, listened to the rain, and began, helplessly, automatically, to count—the seconds until the armistice, the steps between this cell and the press office, the minutes left in which a book of names might become news instead of dust.

Somewhere above him, a church clock struck the hour, and he did not know which hour it was, only that there were fewer of them than there had ever been before.
