## 18. Eleven Minutes Past Eleven

The cell block was a grey building on the Rue des Jacobins with a door that did not slam so much as *decide* to close behind Edmund Voss. Tamsin stood in the street and watched it do exactly that, watched the two gendarmes take his arms at the elbows the way one handles a piece of furniture being moved rather than a man being taken, and heard herself say—calmly, absurdly—"You have the wrong ledger."

She did not say it loudly. She said it the way she said everything in that last week: at a volume calculated not to be noticed.

One of the gendarmes glanced at her. The other didn't. The door finished its decision, and a bolt went home somewhere inside the stone like a full stop.

Then it began to rain, because of course it did. It had rained on her brother's stretcher and it had rained on the ward the night she lost all eleven of them, and it would rain, she suspected, on the day they buried the century itself if anyone thought to hold a funeral for it.

Tamsin pulled her collar up and counted what she had left.

*The ledger.* In its oilcloth wrapping, inside the canvas haversack slung across her body where a nurse's kit bag would sit, unremarkable as a bandage roll. Ada Quill's address memorized twice over: the *Continental Dispatch* bureau, third floor above a printer's shop off the Place de l'Hôtel de Ville, Amiens. A press pass forged by Pip Halloway with more conviction than skill—the ink still smelled faintly of the kitchen stove he'd dried it over. And four hours, perhaps five, before the censor's office opened its morning postbag and discovered that whatever trap they'd set for Edmund Voss had caught only half of what they were hunting.

She turned away from the cell block and walked. Not fast. Nurses learn early that running draws eyes; walking, bent slightly forward, carrying something heavy, draws nothing at all.

* * *

Amiens had been shelled into a kind of honesty. Whole streets had lost their faces; you could see into parlours from the pavement, wallpaper hanging like flayed skin, a clock on a mantelpiece still ticking to nobody. Tamsin moved through these opened rooms of other people's lives without looking directly at them—it felt like trespass even now, even here—and cut south along the river, past the cathedral whose great west front rose out of the drizzle sandbagged to the eyebrows, scaffolding climbing its towers like ivy grown desperate in the dark.

Somewhere ahead, bells.

Not yet. Not the bells that mattered. These were just a workaday quarter-hour from some parish church with a cracked bell and a tired ringers' bench, marking three o'clock or thereabouts in the grey afternoon, and Tamsin's heart seized anyway, lurched up into her throat like a startled bird, because every bell between here and the armistice was going to do that to her. Every bell until the real one. She put her hand flat against the wall of a ruined house and breathed until the bird came down again.

*Eleven minutes past eleven,* Pip had said that morning, grinning around a crust of bread in Sister Brightwater's kitchen. *That's when they say it'll be signed. Eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. Someone in Paris has a sense of theatre.*

*Or arithmetic,* Brightwater had said, passing the tea. And then, quieter, to Tamsin alone, while Pip argued with the driver about tire chains: *He's clever, your census man. He'll talk them in circles.*

*Talking in circles is how they catch you,* Tamsin had answered. She had been right, which gave her no satisfaction whatsoever.

She crossed the river at the iron bridge, where a sentry checked papers without interest—her nursing pass, genuine, the one document in her possession that had never needed forging—and the rain thickened. Ahead the town thinned toward the printing quarter: low brick buildings, ink-smell in the gutters even through the wet, windows papered against blast. Somewhere among them, third floor, a woman named Ada Quill had spent fourteen months having her dispatches returned to her in ribbons of blue pencil, and had told Tamsin in a voice sanded down to bare wood: *Bring me names I can verify and I will get them printed if I have to set the type myself and bribe the compositor with my mother's silver.*

There would be soldiers outside the press office. There might be censors inside it. There would certainly be questions, and Tamsin had learned in the last three days—learning it properly now, walking this street, with Edmund sitting in a cell because he had finally refused to lie—that she could answer questions. That under sufficient pressure some seam in her split open and let out a voice she hadn't known she owned: level, reasonable, faintly bored, the voice of a woman who has seen worse than whatever you're threatening her with and found it untidy.

It was Edmund's voice first. She had stolen it from him across a ration crate in a freezing clearing station, watching him lie beautifully to a transport officer about a wounded man's kit, and understood that lying, like tourniquets, is a skill you hope never to need and are grateful to have once you've practiced.

* * *

The printer's shop was shuttered. Above it, a narrow stair, and at the top a door with a card gone soft-edged with damp: CONTINENTAL DISPATCH — BUREAU. Underneath, smaller, someone had pencilled *Ring twice. Ring a third time only if you mean it.*

Tamsin rang twice.

A slot scraped open at eye height. One eye appeared in it, grey, bloodshot, appraising her in a single sweep—uniform, haversack, mud, face.

"Nurse," Tamsin said. "I'm expected. I telephoned from the aid station Tuesday."

"You telephoned a number that is watched," the eye said. It belonged, now that she adjusted, to Ada Quill—smaller than her byline, older than her reputation, hair pinned in a style that had surrendered sometime around 1916. "Which means either you are very stupid or very careful. Come in before the stairwell decides for us."

Bolts. Chains. The door opened inward onto a room that smelled of cold tea, cigarette smoke, and hot metal—a composing room pressed into service as a newsroom, cases of type along one wall, a wire machine ticking softly to itself in the corner like a nervous animal, papers everywhere in strata, geological, years deep. Two men looked up from a table buried under galley proofs and went back to work with the studied blindness of people who had decided long ago not to know things.

Ada Quill shut the door, threw both bolts, and held out her hand—not for greeting but for the haversack.

"Not here." Tamsin kept the strap across her body. "Not until we've agreed what happens if I don't walk out of this room."

Something flickered behind the grey eyes—surprise, quickly filed away. "Sit down, Nurse Amberdale." She indicated a chair with a broken spindle, mended with wire. "Tea?"

"I know what your tea is like. Yes."

Ada poured two cups from a blackened pot on a spirit stove, and the tea was exactly what Tamsin expected—warm brown water with ambitions—and she drank it anyway, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Your census man," Ada said. "Edmund Voss. They took him at the checkpoint on the Péronne road at ten minutes past nine. My stringer saw it from the café opposite. He went quietly. He also, before they reached him, dropped a parcel into the café's letter box addressed to me. Do you know what was in it?"

Tamsin's chest tightened. "No."

"Three pages. Copied in a hand so neat it looks engraved. His cousin's service record. William Alfred Voss, private, listed as transferred to a base depot he was never sent to. With a covering note." She recited it without looking at the paper, and Tamsin realized she had read it many times. "*'If I am detained, the rest is with Nurse Amberdale, who does not lie well and therefore tells the truth, which is worth more.'*"

Tamsin looked into her cup. The tea trembled very slightly, a fine ring of ripple traveling rim to rim, and she stilled it with both hands around the warmth.

"He wrote that on the train," she said. "I saw him writing. He told me it was a letter to his aunt."

"Was it?"

"It was true," Tamsin said. "That's better than a letter."

Ada Quill sat back. For a long moment the only sounds were the wire machine ticking and the rain finding new ways into old roofs. Then she said, without any softness at all, which was somehow kinder:

"Now tell me what happens if you don't walk out of this room."

* * *

Tamsin laid it out the way she'd rehearsed it on the road, counting checkpoints instead of sheep. If she were arrested, the ledger went nowhere—no dead drops, no confederates, no brave boy pushing stretchers into rail yards ever again; Pip Halloway was sixteen and had done enough and would do no more, and she said this in a voice that closed the subject like a ward door. If she were arrested, the ledger stayed on her body, and her body would be searched within the hour, and the ledger would be destroyed as irregular documentation by an officer following regulations, and no one would ever be punished for it, because that was the genius of regulations—they committed the crimes so no one had to.

"So print it tonight," she finished. "Before they come for me tomorrow. Before the armistice seals everything in crates marked DO NOT OPEN UNTIL THE INQUIRY—an inquiry that will be convened after the records are sealed and will conclude, after due deliberation, that no inquiry is warranted."

"And if I refuse? If I think your pages are unverifiable sentiment?"

"Then I take it to the *Matin*, or the Americans, or a church newsletter if I must, and you can explain to your editor why the *Dispatch* missed the only story of this war that will still be read in fifty years."

One of the men at the proof table snorted into his coffee. Ada didn't turn her head, but something in her shoulders shifted—a loosening, almost like laughter.

"Names I can verify," she said. "That was the price. Bring me the ledger."

Tamsin unslung the haversack.

Her hands were slower than she wanted them to be. Four days of carrying it had taught her fingers its weight, its corners, the particular way the oilcloth crackled, and unwrapping it in front of a stranger felt like opening her brother's effects box all over again—watching a stranger lift out his watch, his lighter, the photograph with the crease through her own face, handling him carelessly, handling him *wrong*. But Ada Quill did not handle it wrong. She unwrapped the oilcloth as if it were linen over a wound, drew the ledger out—a school exercise book, bought for two sous, its marbled cover warped with damp—and opened it flat on the table with both hands, and read.

The room went quiet in a way that had weight. Even the wire machine seemed to tick more softly.

Page after page. Names in columns, in Tamsin's small upright hand: name, rank, regiment, date of death, place, and—in a final column added after the first week—*why omitted*. *Clerical error, duplicate file.* *Reported missing; body unidentified; file closed.* *Desertion alleged, withdrawn, allegation retained.* *Ward destroyed by shellfire; morning report burned; not re-entered.* Page after page, forty-one names by October, sixty-three by November, and each one a man Tamsin had washed, or bandaged, or held down while the morphine went in, or sat with through the last hour because someone should be reading to him and the chaplain was elsewhere.

Ada stopped at one entry. Her finger rested beside it without touching the ink.

"Corporal Daniel Ferrow," she read aloud. "Died of wounds, fourth of October. Omitted: *transferred to Casualty Clearing Station 12; transfer never acknowledged; assumed alive at depot; depot assumes CCS error; no one files.*" She looked up. "I interviewed a woman in Luton in June. His mother. She gets his pay book every quarter and a form asking why he hasn't written."

"That's the column," Tamsin said. "That's the whole column. Print those mothers next to these lines and no censor in France can stop it, because there's nothing in it but the truth and their own paperwork."

Ada Quill closed the ledger gently, both hands, like closing a door on a sleeping child. When she spoke, her professional cynicism was gone and what remained was younger, rawer, dangerous.

"Compositors work through the night. We go to press at six. The armistice edition—if it's signed—will be on the streets by noon." She paused. "And if it isn't signed, if they delay it again, we run it anyway, under the headline *These Men Died*. Which may be the braver edition." She stood. "Give me the page with Voss's cousin. I want his note set beside it, verbatim."

"It stays with me until it's in type," Tamsin said. "You can copy it. You can't have it."

For the first time, Ada Quill smiled. It changed her whole face, made her look like someone who had once been reckless and could be again. "Nurse, you and I are going to get on famously."

* * *

They worked through the evening by lamplight while the rain gave up and the fog came in off the Somme instead, folding the town into wool. Tamsin copied names aloud; a compositor named Bresson, who had lost two sons and said so exactly once, set them in metal with hands that shook only on the letters of the youngest ones. Ada drafted and redrafted the column, cutting every adjective with the ruthlessness of a surgeon, keeping only nouns and dates and the military's own phrases turned back on themselves—*assumed alive at depot*—until the column read like a charge sheet written by the war against itself.

At some point bread and cheese appeared. Tamsin ate without tasting. At some point she slept, twenty minutes, sitting upright, and woke with a start reaching for a pulse that wasn't there.

And at seven minutes past eleven in the morning—she would remember the clock on Ada's desk, its face cracked, its hands stubborn—all the bells in Amiens began to ring.

Not the cracked quarter-bell. All of them. The cathedral first, that vast bronze avalanche rolling over the rooftops, and then every parish church stumbling in after it, and then steam whistles from the yards, and horns, and shouting, and doors banging open all down the street, and a sound rising underneath it all that Tamsin had never heard in four years of war and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a voice in another room: people laughing in the open air without fear.

Ada was already on her feet, hand pressed flat on the desk, head bowed as though the bells were weather breaking over her. "It's signed," she said. "My God. It's actually—" She straightened, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand in one brisk motion, businesslike even at the end of the world. "Bresson! The front page. Pull the war story. It leads, but it leads *second*."

Tamsin went to the window. Below, the shattered street was filling with people—French civilians emerging from cellar doors, British soldiers standing in the road with their caps off, an old woman in the doorway of the ruined house opposite raising both arms to the rain-washed sky. Somebody was singing. Somebody was sobbing. Both songs braided together and neither one wrong.

The eleventh hour of the eleventh day. Eleven minutes past eleven, and the guns were stopping.

It should have been the happiest minute of her life. It was, in part—she felt it move through her, huge and slow, grief and relief inseparable, indistinguishable, one tide. She thought of her ward, eleven beds empty in one night. Of Daniel, chalked name on a slate, erased. Of Pip's grin. Of Brightwater's weak tea poured out like communion. Of Edmund Voss standing in a doorway in the rain saying *I find I don't want to count anything anymore unless it counts for them.*

Of Edmund Voss, this morning, ten minutes past nine, taken at the checkpoint on the Péronne road.

The bells rang on, glorious, merciless, and Tamsin stood at the window with tears running freely down her face and her jaw set like a splint, and understood the exact shape of this victory: the war was ending, and he was still in a cell, and the column would not save him by itself, because the men who had arrested him were the same men whose signatures she had spent all night setting in type.

"They'll process him with the general release," Ada said behind her. "Armistice amnesty. Paperwork takes days—"

"We don't have days. Once peace is ratified, his arrest becomes a footnote in a sealed file, same as the rest." Tamsin turned from the window. Down in the street, a military police lorry ground past through the celebrating crowds, bound, she would have bet her life, for the Rue des Jacobins, to move its prisoners somewhere quieter before the world got too happy to notice. "Where do they take detainees from the press checkpoint?"

Ada's smile came back, and this time it had teeth. "Nurse Amberdale. How much do you know about the interior of a military prison?"

"Nothing," Tamsin said. "But I'm told I don't lie well. So I'm going to go tell the truth to whoever holds the keys—and you're going to make sure today's edition makes it impossible for them to keep either of us quiet."

Below, the bells were still ringing. She picked up her haversack and started for the stairs.
