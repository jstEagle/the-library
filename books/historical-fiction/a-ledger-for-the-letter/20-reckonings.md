## 20. Reckonings

The road back from Amiens smelled of wet ash and woodsmoke, the way November roads did now, as if the whole country had been quietly burning for four years and had finally been allowed to lie down.

Tamsin rode in the back of a supply lorry wedged between crates of condensed milk and a wounded sergeant who slept with his head against her shoulder and his bandaged hand curled in his lap like something he'd forgotten he was carrying. She didn't mind. The weight of him was ordinary weight. After eleven minutes past eleven on the eleventh of November—after bells, after strangers weeping in the streets of Amiens, after walking a ledger through a door she had expected to be thrown out of—she wanted nothing so much as something heavy and human to lean against.

The armistice had held for two days. Two days of silence along the front so profound that men reported hearing birdsong in the shattered trees, though the birds were probably dead too and it was only their ears inventing mercy. Ada Quill's column had run that morning in three papers at once—*The Ledger for the Letter*, she had called it, because Ada could never resist a pun that cut—and already, Tamsin knew from the telegraph office, the War Office had issued a statement promising an inquiry into "irregularities of record-keeping affecting certain personnel."

*Certain personnel.* As if her brother were a filing error. As if Edmund's cousin were a smudge of ink.

She watched the fields go by, brown and pocked, and touched the folded newspaper in her coat pocket until the edges softened under her thumb.

The lorry turned off the main road onto the lane she knew by its ruts. The casualty clearing station rose out of the grey—the long marquees, the cookhouse chimney bleeding thin smoke, the chapel with its crooked cross nailed over the door, and beyond them all the cemetery, row upon row of white markers standing in the frost like a congregation that would never disperse.

Home. The word arrived unbidden and she let it stay.

The sentry at the gate recognized her before she showed papers—which were, for once, entirely her own—and something moved across his young face that might have been salute or apology. Word travelled faster than lorries. Everyone here had heard about the column by now; she could feel it in the way the orderlies paused mid-task as she passed, in the way Sister Ainsley in the receiving tent looked up from a chart and pressed her lips together as if swallowing something that wanted very badly to be said aloud.

They let her walk through without stopping. It felt less like welcome than like parting water around a stone.

* * *

Matron Havelock was in her office.

Tamsin stood in the doorway longer than courtesy required, because there was no protocol for this moment and no script. This was the woman who had ordered the inspection at dawn. Who had posted guards on the mail sacks. Who had sat across from Brightwater with that terrible patient stillness while her fingers walked the chapel shelves, close enough to touch the ledger disguised as a hymnal, and had drawn back at the last inch—not fooled, Tamsin understood now, never fooled, but choosing. Or perhaps not choosing yet. Perhaps only deferring.

Matron Havelock was perhaps fifty-five, built like a piece of field artillery herself, with iron-grey hair pinned so severely it looked load-bearing. She had come out from England in '14 with a reputation for extracting perfect compliance from chaos, and she had done it, mostly, and everyone who served under her feared her in the comfortable way one fears weather.

Except that this morning, when Tamsin entered, Matron Havelock was sitting at her desk with a newspaper open in front of her, and she was not reading it. She was looking at it the way one looks at a wound—one has dressed on someone else.

"Sit down, Nurse Amberdale."

There was a second chair. There had always been a second chair. Tamsin had simply never been invited to it before.

She sat. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, an orderly laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first she'd heard in weeks—and the sound of it seemed to make the room smaller and quieter.

"You went to Amiens," Matron Havelock said. Not a question. "With the Quill woman's people. You took the record out through the press censor's office while Sergeant Doyle was sitting on the mail bags I told him to sit on." She folded the paper closed with exaggerated care, aligning the edges. "You made me look like either a fool or an accomplice. I haven't decided which is worse."

"Yes, Matron."

"Don't *yes, Matron* me. I've had forty-eight hours of yes-matron from every officer in the chain of command and I find I've developed an allergy to it." She leaned back. For the first time Tamsin noticed how tired she looked—not the theatrical exhaustion of authority but the genuine article, the kind that lived behind the eyes and didn't wash off. "Do you know what I was doing, Nurse, when you were smuggling your little book past my guards?"

"No, Matron."

"I was writing to the Records Office. Again. The ninth letter since March." She opened a drawer, took out a sheaf of carbons, and dropped them on the desk between them like a hand of losing cards. "Private Daniel Ferrow. Died of wounds, October 1916, listed as recovered and returned to duty. His mother has written to me eleven times asking where her son is buried, because according to London he isn't. Corporal Whitcombe—desertion struck from the roll after the court found shell shock. The correction was approved in April. It has not been entered. Sergeant Okafor—" Her voice caught, just slightly, a hitch in machinery, and she covered it by squaring the carbons against the desk edge. "Sergeant Okafor died in the gas attack in March and appears in no report whatsoever. I wrote. I wrote and wrote and wrote. Do you know what came back?"

"A receipt," Tamsin said quietly. "Form W-3421, acknowledging correspondence received."

"*A receipt.*" Matron Havelock almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see. "So when I found a hymnal on my own chapel shelf that weighed four ounces too much, and when I chose—yes, Nurse, I *chose*—to post guards instead of opening it, understand that it was not mercy. It was arithmetic. I calculated that a nurse with a notebook would do more damage to that building in London than forty years of my letters. And I hated you for it. I want that on the record. I hate neatly."

The stove ticked. Outside, the frost was beginning to burn off the marquees, and the light through the small window had gone from grey to the colour of weak tea.

"And now?" Tamsin asked.

"Now," Matron Havelock said, "read the column."

She pushed the newspaper across the desk. Tamsin unfolded it with hands that were not quite steady. She had read it twice already—in the telegraph office, then again by candlelight with Edmund's shoulder against hers—but reading it here, in this office, under these eyes, was different. The ink seemed fresher. The names seemed louder.

Ada had printed ninety-one names. Ninety-one men the official rolls had swallowed. At the top, above all of it, she had set a single line: *These are the men the war preferred to forget. Let us decline the preference.*

And beneath each name, where space allowed, a line or two. Private Ferrow, died October 1916, beloved of his mother in Leeds, who is owed a grave. Corporal Whitcombe, restored to honour by court finding, restoration unentered. Sergeant Okafor, who sang bass in the ward on Sunday nights and whose death saved the men in Tent Six because he insisted they move the stove.

Tamsin read her brother's entry. Thomas Amberdale. *Listed missing, August 1915. He was not missing. His sister held his hand.*

She hadn't known Ada was going to include that. She had read it the first time with her heart slamming and her eyes burning, and she read it now the same way, because some sentences do not lose their force with repetition; some sentences are load-bearing.

When she looked up, Matron Havelock had taken off her spectacles, and without them her face was suddenly older and more defenceless, a fortification with the scaffolding down.

"Ninety-one," the matron said. "You collected ninety-one names while I collected receipts."

"It wasn't a competition, Matron."

"No." She replaced her spectacles slowly. "It was a war. We were both fighting it. We simply couldn't see we were on the same side, because you were twenty-six years old and insubordinate, and I was fifty-four years old and correct." She stood. From the cabinet behind her—locked these many months, guarded, the subject of whispered speculation among the nurses—she withdrew a flat parcel tied with regulation string. "Stand up, please."

Tamsin stood.

"These were confiscated in September," Matron Havelock said. "Under standing orders regarding irregular correspondence. There were—let me recall—fourteen items. Letters. A photograph. Your brother's last letter, which you will notice I did not read, because I am a matron, not a monster, whatever the wards may say." She held out the parcel. "They should have gone to London with the sealed records. They would have been filed. Filed things are lost things, Nurse Amberdale—you know that better than anyone now."

Tamsin took the parcel. It was lighter than she remembered and heavier than anything.

"I'm not asking forgiveness," Matron Havelock said. "I don't believe in it. I'm asking you to take these home when home exists, and to read them aloud somewhere, someday, to someone, because paper in a drawer is exactly the problem we've spent this whole war demonstrating." She straightened her cuffs with two sharp tugs. "The inquiry convenes in January. Quill's column has seen to that. They'll want witnesses. They'll want the ledger itself, and they'll want—" a pause, the machinery catching again— "they'll want testimony from the matron who nearly burned it. I intend to tell them everything. Including the chapel shelf. Especially the chapel shelf."

"Why?" Tamsin asked. It wasn't defiance. It was genuine puzzlement, and maybe the matron heard that, because something in her face eased by a single degree.

"Because being wrong in private is vanity, and being wrong in public is evidence," Matron Havelock said. "I have been vain long enough. Now go. And Amberdale—" Tamsin stopped at the door. "The photograph. The one of you and the boy at the seaside. I looked at it once. Only once. I'm telling you so you know I owe you a debt, and I pay debts."

* * *

She didn't open the parcel in the office corridor. She didn't open it in the ward, where Pip Halloway spotted her and came limping down the aisle on his crutches shouting her name loud enough to turn every head, and she had to hold him up when his embrace overbalanced him, and Sister Ainsley pretended furiously to adjust a screen. She didn't open it at supper, where Brightwater fed her bread and dripping and weak tea and asked no questions at all, which was Brightwater's highest form of eloquence.

She waited until full dark, until the generator coughed its nightly rhythm and the lamps settled low, and she walked alone up the path to the cemetery.

The frost had come back with nightfall. The white markers glowed faintly, each one holding its little pool of moonlight. She found the new rows first—October's dead, the ones from the bad week—and then, as she always did, she walked to the far corner where the ground was older, where the markers had weathered to the softness of sea-worn stone.

Thomas wasn't here. Thomas was somewhere east of Ypres under a wooden cross with a number, or under nothing, or under earth that had been shelled so many times the notion of *under* had become philosophical. But she stood at the corner anyway, because grief needs furniture, and she sat down on the cold grass with her back against the fence post, and she untied the string.

Fourteen items. Letters from home, mostly—her mother's careful round handwriting, her father's terse postscripts added sideways in pencil. A letter from Thomas himself, dated July 1915, six weeks before the end: *Tell Tam the sea at Boulogne is the exact colour of the tin her paints came in, she'll know the one.* She had read that sentence perhaps five hundred times. She read it again. It did what it always did, which was to open the floor of the world and let her fall a little way before catching her.

The photograph was last. Two children on Brighton sand, squinting into sun that no longer existed anywhere except in silvered paper. Thomas mid-laugh, hair everywhere. Herself, seven years old, gripping his sleeve with both hands as if the tide might take him.

*I looked at it once,* the matron had said. *Only once.*

Why had that undone her more than any apology? Because it meant the matron had stood in this same cold room with this same small square of light and had understood, in one glance, exactly what confiscation meant—not irregular correspondence, not contraband, but a child gripping her brother's sleeve forever, seized and boxed and filed. And had chosen, in her iron way, to keep it safe rather than surrender it to the drawer. Grace doesn't always look gentle, Tamsin thought. Sometimes it looks like a woman who hates neatly, doing one untidy thing.

She sat in the cemetery until her fingers went numb, holding the photograph, letting herself cry—not the efficient crying of the ward, silent and rationed, but the other kind, the kind she had forbidden herself since August 1915, the kind with sound in it. Somewhere across the compound a gramophone was playing, tinny and distant, a dance tune, absurd and holy. The war was over. The war was over and her brother was still dead and the two facts refused to reconcile, and no form W-3421 would ever reconcile them, and that was the truth she had been carrying since she was twenty-two years old, and tonight, at last, she set it down on the frost and let it rest beside her like a sleeping man.

When she finally stood, stiff with cold, she noticed the figure waiting at the gate of the cemetery path. Edmund. Capless despite the cold, hands in pockets, having clearly stood there for some time out of some census-taker's scruple about intruding.

"You're back," he said, as if reporting a count.

"I'm back."

"The inquiry letter reached Ada this afternoon. January the ninth. They want the ledger produced." He hesitated. "They also want the nurse who kept it."

Tamsin looked down at the photograph still in her hand, then back at him—at this meticulous man who had falsified his life's work for the sake of true ones, who had traded his freedom for ninety-one names and gotten both back in the space of a week.

"Then we'd better decide," she said, "what we're going to tell them about the chapel shelf."

Edmund's eyebrows went up. "You know about the chapel shelf?"

"Matron just confessed to it." She tucked the photograph inside her coat, over her heart, where it had lived before and would live again. "Come inside, Voss. There's tea. It's terrible. Brightwater will pretend she didn't put brandy in it, and Pip will pretend he isn't listening, and for the first time in four years—" she looked back once at the white rows glowing in the dark, each marker keeping its small light, each name, now, accounted for— "for the first time in four years, nobody here has to lie about anything."

She started down the path, and he fell into step beside her, and behind them the cemetery held its silence the way a page holds ink—patiently, permanently, waiting to be read.
