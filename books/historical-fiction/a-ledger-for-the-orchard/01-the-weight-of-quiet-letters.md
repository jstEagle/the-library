## 1. The Weight of Quiet Letters

There is an art to composing a lie in movable type, and I learned it before I learned my letters properly.

The trick is in the spacing. A truth set well sits comfortable on the page, each word shouldering its neighbor like men who've worked the same quay for years. A lie set badly — and most official lies are set badly, because they are written by men who think ink is a formality — leaves gaps you could drive a cart through. The eye stumbles. The reader feels it without knowing why: *something here doesn't fit.* My father taught me this when I was nine years old, standing on a crate at the composing stone, and he taught it as a technical matter, a craftsman's observation, nothing more. "Read the measure, Domi. If your words don't sit right, your reader knows it before his mind does."

He did not say: *and when the governor's proclamation lies, set it so beautifully that no one notices the gaps.*

But that was the lesson underneath the lesson, the way there's always a lesson under a lesson in our trade.

That February morning — the ninth, though the date meant nothing yet; dates only acquire meaning afterward, like names on stones — I was setting the Governor's Proclamation on Grain Reserves and Public Order, four hundred copies, rag paper, the good black ink because His Excellency's lies deserved better materials than most people's truths. The proclamation announced that grain stores in the river district were "ample and equitably distributed," which was true in the sense that the governor's own cellars were ample, and equitable in the sense that everyone equally had none.

I set AMPLITUDE first. Then EQUITABLY, which has too many letters for its honesty. My fingers knew the cases by touch — capital case above, lower below, the pi row at the far end where the odd sorts live, asterisks and daggers and the long s we were forbidden to use anymore because some clerk in the capital had decided it looked seditious, or old, or whatever else clerks decide things look when they have nothing better to fear.

The shop smelled the way it always smells in deep winter: ink and cold glue and the tallow of the lamp Aldous insisted on burning even at midday, because his eyes had begun demanding light the way a drunk demands drink — more and more, for less and less return.

He was at the press, adjusting the platen by feel, his face six inches from the mechanism. Forty-eight years old and he squinted like a man reading through river water. He'd been doing it for two years now, the squinting, and for eighteen months he'd been pretending not to do it, and for six months we'd both been pretending not to notice him pretending, which in our family constituted open communication.

"Dom." He didn't look up. "Your third line's drifting."

"It's not drifting. It's justified to the measure."

"It's drifting." He came over anyway, though coming over meant navigating the shop by memory and the tips of his fingers along the frame, and stood squinting down at my galley. "There. After RESERVES. You've got a gap wide enough to post a sentry in."

"That's the wordspacing His Excellency deserves."

"Dominic."

"I'll fix it."

He waited. That was his method — not the raised voice, never that, not in seven years, not since Mother — just the waiting, patient as a press screw, until the silence did the work a shout would have done faster and worse.

I slid the spaces out with the point of my bodkin and set the line tight. Happy? I didn't say. He heard it anyway. We were fluent in each other's unsaid things; it was practically our mother tongue.

* * *

You need to understand about the shop before anything else happens, because everything that happens later happens because of the shop.

Hartwell & Son it said on the sign, though there was no son and hadn't been since Tomas Veyra walked out of it three years ago carrying his coat and his grievances and, as far as the record showed, his life — the record being a coroner's report Aldous signed as witness, a drowned apprentice pulled from the winter weir, identified by a scar on the left forearm and a belt buckle stamped T.V. I was fourteen when they fished him out, and I remember thinking even then that the buckle was wrong, that Tomas kept his buckle polished like a church brass and a week in the weir would have eaten it dull. But I thought it the way children think things, quietly, and put it away in the drawer where our family kept its quiet thoughts, which was nearly full.

Aldous Hartwell was a printer the way some men are priests — not a trade but a sentence. His father had printed pamphlets against the last war; his grandfather had printed hymnals for a congregation that got deported anyway. We printed what paid: proclamations, bills of lading, funeral notices for people whose funerals the state sometimes arranged ahead of schedule, and once a year, a small almanac whose astrological predictions grew more cautious every year until they consisted entirely of "changeable weather."

And under all of it, invisible, we printed the silences.

Every shop in this city keeps silences. It's the local industry, really — the river brings goods, the garrison takes them, and what's left over is silence, which we manufacture and export in quantities exceeding all other trades combined. The rules of the shop, never written down because writing them down would be the one way to break them:

One. Set what you're given. Don't improve the lie, don't worsen it, don't add a single letter of your own. The censors read proofs the way priests used to read entrails, hunting for omens.

Two. Keep no archives. What's printed today is ash by Thursday. (My father broke this rule every day of his life, but that comes later.)

Three. If a man asks you to print something after dark, you are closed. If he asks again, you have always been closed. If he asks a third time, you have never been a printer at all, you are a cobbler, look at these boots.

Four. And this one Aldous said aloud exactly once, the night the soldiers took the Kessler brothers' shop apart plank by plank on suspicion of printing hymns in the old tongue: *What you know, keep behind your teeth. Teeth are the only press they haven't learned to search.*

I was nineteen now, seven years at the stone, and I could set a page of type blind — a useful joke, given how my father was going — and I had never once broken rule four. I want that on the record. Whatever else I became that winter, in February I was still a woman who kept her teeth shut.

* * *

The censor came at noon, as censors do, at hours calculated to interrupt.

His name was Fenn, and he was not a monster, which made him worse. Monsters you can hate cleanly. Fenn was a gray, apologetic little man with chilblains on his knuckles and a genuine love of typography — he'd trained, rumor held, at a scriptorium before the printing bureaus swallowed the old copying houses — and he read our proof sheets with the pained attention of a man editing poetry he despised.

"The measure on the second paragraph," he said, tapping the grain proclamation. "It runs long."

"His Excellency's text runs long. I can't cut His Excellency."

"No. No, of course." He adjusted his spectacles. "Perhaps... tighter leading. To suggest compression of thought."

"To suggest," I said, "that the paragraph is shorter than it is."

Fenn looked at me over the spectacles, and for a moment — half a heartbeat — something moved behind his face that might have been sympathy or might have been warning. In this city those two expressions are printed from the same plate.

"Young woman," he said, "you have your father's hands."

It was meant as a compliment. Ink-stained, nicked, a burn scar across the left thumb from the hot metal — yes, I had his hands, and his eyes, and presumably someday his squint. I thanked him. Fenn gathered his copy, wished the shop continued prosperity in terms suggesting prosperity was a disease currently going around, and left into the snow.

Aldous emerged from behind the press. "What did he want?"

"Tighter leading. To suggest the lie is smaller than it looks."

"Hm." He turned back to the press. "Do it, then. We're not martyrs to typography."

No, I thought. We're just its undertakers.

I don't want to give the impression I spent my days seething. Seething is a luxury; it uses fuel needed elsewhere. Mostly I felt what I imagine a ferryman feels — competent, necessary, and privately aware that the river is deeper than his pole. Four hundred copies of the proclamation went out that afternoon. I set them beautifully. Every line sat snug as a family in a one-room flat, every margin sang. If any citizen of this city read that grain was ample and believed it, the craftsmanship was partly mine, and I have carried that particular weight ever since, and will carry it, and deserve to.

That evening I burned the day's spoilings in the stove, per rule two, and watched the words AMPLITUDE curl and go. Outside, the river lay locked under ice so thick the garrison drove supply wagons across it, and the whole city seemed to hold its breath the way the river did — not dead, just *waiting*, pressure building under the surface, nobody saying so out loud because rule four applied to rivers too.

* * *

We were cleaning the press when the knock came.

Not the street door. The loading door — the big one on the alley side where the paper deliveries came, the door we kept barred after dark per rule three and every other sensible rule besides. Ten o'clock at night, snow coming down like the sky was settling accounts, and someone knocking at that door soft and steady, three strikes, pause, three strikes, like a man who had strength either to knock or to run but not both.

Aldous's head came up. I watched him listen — the whole body listening, the way animals do — and I saw something cross his face I had never seen on it in nineteen years. Not fear exactly. Fear I knew on him; I'd seen it the night of the Kesslers' shop. This was older. This was grief arriving early, before its appointment, still wearing its traveling coat.

"Papa?"

"Stay at the stone," he said, which meant nothing, because there was nothing to set.

Three knocks. Pause. Three.

He unbarred the loading door himself, and the cold came in like an unpaid debt, and with it a man — young, maybe my age, swaying on his feet, one arm pressed against his side in a way that meant blood even before I saw it seeping between his fingers. Snow had gotten inside his coat and melted there; he steamed faintly, like a horse brought hard into stable.

"They told me—" His voice cracked, restarted. "They said Hartwell. The printer on Tanner's Lane."

"This is a cobbler's," my father said. Rule three, deployed instantly, automatically — and then the man lifted his head, and the lamplight found his face, and whatever my father saw in it stopped the lie in his throat like a hand on a bell rope.

The stranger worked his bleeding hand inside his coat, wincing, and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and he held it out with both arms the way you hand over something that weighs far more than paper.

"For you. From—"

"Who sent you?" Aldous's voice had gone strange. Thin. The voice of a man hearing water under ice.

"He did. He wrote them himself, he made me memorize the route twice, he said—" The man swayed again, caught himself on the doorframe, left a red smear on the wood that I would scrub out the next morning and think about for months. "He said to tell you the buckle was real, but the rest wasn't. He said you'd understand."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Somewhere out on the frozen river, deep under the white, the ice groaned — a long sound, tectonic, the sound of a thing that has held as long as it can.

My father took the oilcloth packet. His hands, those famous steady hands, were shaking.

"Domi," he said, without turning around, "bar the door."

And I did — but not before I looked past the stranger into the falling snow of the empty alley, checking, the way seven years of rules teaches you to check, whether anyone had followed him.

No one had. Yet.

Inside the oilcloth, I would learn within the hour, were eleven letters sealed in wax. Eleven letters in a handwriting I knew as well as my own — the hand that had corrected my spacing at that same composing stone, that had drawn me alphabets in the condensation on the window glass, that belonged to a dead man.

Tomas Veyra wrote a beautiful hand. The dead write slowly, but they write.
