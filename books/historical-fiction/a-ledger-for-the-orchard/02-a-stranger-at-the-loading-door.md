## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The cold came early that year, the way bad news always does — before you'd finished bracing for it.

I was in the back of the shop sorting type by candlelight because my father's eyes could no longer tell a *p* from a *q* after sundown, and he refused to admit it, and I had learned long ago that letting him win small arguments about his eyesight was cheaper than winning large ones about everything else. Outside, the river had begun its annual argument with itself, ice forming along the edges while the middle still moved, black and impatient. The whole city sounded like that lately: frozen at the borders, churning underneath. You just had to know where to listen.

It was past eleven when the knock came.

Not a customer's knock. Customers knocked at the front door, twice, politely, and waited to be ignored until morning. This was the loading door — three slow raps, then silence, then two more, like someone who knew the rhythm of the place but wasn't certain it remembered him.

My father looked up from the proof sheet he was pretending to read. In the candlelight I watched something move across his face that I hadn't seen there since Mother died: not fear exactly, but its older cousin, the one that arrives first and waits.

"Expecting anyone?" he asked.

"From you? Never." I set down my composing stick. "From God, occasionally."

"Dominic."

"I'm going. I'm going." I took up the iron poker, because I am sentimental but not stupid, and went through the storeroom toward the loading door.

The wind off the river hit me when I cracked it open, carrying the smell of ice and coal smoke and something else — blood, faint and coppery, the way a print shop teaches your nose to find ink, a stranger's blood teaches it to find this. The man on our step was half-collapsed against the doorframe, one arm pressed to his side, snow gathering on his shoulders like he'd been standing there long enough to become part of the architecture. Younger than me by a few years, maybe. Dark hair matted with melt. Eyes that did the thing I recognized instantly, the thing I did myself every day in front of Captain Reiner's men: they were watching me watch them, calculating how much truth each of us could afford.

"Please," he said. His voice was rough as unsized paper. "I need Aldous Hartwell. Only him. Please."

"He's asleep."

"He isn't." A ghost of a smile. "Printers never sleep. They just rest their eyes between catastrophes."

That was true enough to be annoying. Behind me, I heard my father's uneven footsteps crossing the storeroom, and when I turned, his face had gone from candlelit to candlelit-and-drained, all the color gone out of it like a page left too long in the wash.

"Let him in," Aldous said quietly.

"Papa—"

"Let him *in*, Dominic."

I stepped aside. Later I would understand that I had crossed some kind of line in that moment without knowing it — that the man bleeding on our step was already rearranging the furniture of all our lives. But at the time I only noticed the details, because noticing is what I do instead of speaking. The stranger's boots were wrong for a laborer and wrong for an officer and right for neither, which meant they were chosen. His coat had been torn, not worn. And under the smell of blood, faintly, unmistakably, he carried the scent of a print shop — oil, solvent, the metallic ghost of lead — which meant whoever he was, he was ours. One of the trade.

He got three steps into the storeroom before his knees made the decision for him. My father caught one arm; I caught the other; together we lowered him onto the bench by the drying racks, which has held heavier burdens than men, though rarely more dangerous ones.

"Water," my father said. "And bring the good cloth, not the rags."

"The good cloth," I repeated, "for a stranger bleeding on our floor at midnight. Papa, if Reiner's men come through that door, we'll want a story better than 'we were feeling charitable.'"

"We will want a story," my father said, kneeling beside the stranger with his hands already working at the torn coat, "and we will have one. Bring the cloth, Dominic."

I brought the cloth. I also brought the poker and set it within reach of the bench, which my father pretended not to see and the stranger did see, and something passed between us — not quite approval, but its practical cousin.

The wound was a knife cut along his ribs, deep enough to bleed impressively and shallow enough, thank whatever saints govern couriers, to miss anything that mattered. He bore the cleaning of it without a sound, which told me he'd done this before, or had it done to him before, and neither option comforted me.

While I worked, my father stood apart, holding a candle, and I watched him watch the boy's face. There was a strange tension in him, the look of a man reading a document he knows by heart and waiting for the forgery to show itself.

"You have a name?" Aldous asked.

"Ilan."

"Just Ilan?"

"For tonight." He glanced at me. "For her, for a month, if she likes."

"She stays," my father said, in the voice he used for settling accounts with suppliers. It was not a negotiation. "She sets type better than I do and lies better than either of us."

"That's not the compliment you think it is," I said, pressing the bandage home. Ilan hissed.

"How did you find us?" my father asked. And then, softer, in a voice I barely recognized, the voice of a much younger man: "Who sent you?"

Ilan looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached inside his shirt — slowly, showing his hand empty except for what he meant to draw out — and produced an oilcloth packet, tied with waxed string, sealed at both ends. It was stained dark in places. Some of that stain was blood, and I remember hoping most of it was.

"These are for you," Ilan said. "From Tomas Veyra."

The packet sat in my father's hands and did nothing, and everything happened anyway.

You have to understand what that name did in our house. For three years it had been a closed ledger, a debt marked paid in the ugliest currency. Tomas Veyra, my father's apprentice — the brilliant one, the one whose hand could cut a letter finer than any founder in the city, the one who ate at our table and argued politics with Papa over the composing stone until my mother threatened to set them both in pi type. Three years ago the news had come down from the capital like weather: Tomas Veyra, dead. An accident on the road north, they said. The guild had held a memorial. My father had printed the memorial notices himself, standing at his own press for six hours without speaking, and I had watched him pull each sheet and understood, in the wordless way children of quiet men understand things, that grief was only part of what was breaking him. There was guilt in it too. There was something he wasn't saying, and I had learned the family art and never asked.

And now a stranger sat on our bench, sewn shut with our thread, saying the name aloud like a match struck in a room full of lamp oil.

"That's impossible," I said, because someone had to say it. "Tomas Veyra is dead. There were notices. Papa printed them."

"Yes," my father said. Still staring at the packet. "He did."

"Papa—"

"*Dead*," my father said again, very gently, in the tone of a man correcting a proof, "is a word we print when the governor requires it. I have printed it for living men before. I have printed it for dead ones too. It is a word like any other, Dominic. It goes where it is paid to go."

The candle flame leaned in the draft from the loading door, which I had not latched, and none of us moved to latch it.

Ilan shifted on the bench, wincing. "Open it," he said. "Or don't. But I've carried those letters four hundred miles, Master Hartwell, and I'd rather die having delivered them than die having failed to, so whichever you choose, choose quickly, because there are men behind me who are slower than I am but more numerous."

My father broke the seal.

I won't pretend I didn't read over his shoulder. I am my father's daughter and a printer's cutter besides; privacy, in a shop of twelve feet by forty, is a theoretical discipline. The handwriting hit me before the words did — that slanted, urgent script, letters leaning forward like runners, the crossbars slashed hard. I had seen it a thousand times on job tickets and margin notes and once, memorably, on the back of a pancake. A hand doesn't change in three years. A hand doesn't change in a grave.

*Old man,* the letter began. *If this reaches you, it means Ilan made it further than I ever did, which should surprise no one, since he can walk quietly and I never could.*

I heard my father breathe. Once. The way a press breathes between pulls.

*I am alive. I know what you were told. I know you printed it — I even know which font you used, because you would use the ugly one, the one you save for lies you hate. I need you to listen now, because the thing I ran from has grown teeth, and the ledger I saw with my own eyes — the real one, not the one the governor published — must go downriver before the ice breaks. Names, old man. Payments. Every signature on the forgery, priced and dated. If it reaches Velmora, the guild can print it, and every lie in this city comes apart like wet pasteboard. If it doesn't — *

There was more. There was a great deal more, and my father read it all, and I watched his face while he did, and what cracked open there was not shock alone. Shock is quick. What moved across my father's features was slower and worse: seven years of a locked room opening all at once, and everything he'd kept folded neatly inside it spilling onto the floor where I could finally see it.

Guilt, I thought. So that's what it looks like on him. Like exhaustion. Like age arriving all in one evening.

"Master Hartwell." Ilan's voice, careful. "Are you well?"

"No," my father said. It seemed to surprise him to say it. He laughed — a small, terrible sound, like a press catching. "No, I am sixty-one years old, and a dead man is writing to me in his own hand, and I am well. Sit down, Dominic. No. Stand. Stand there and hold the candle higher, I can't—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. When he took them away, he was my father again, mostly, the way a rebuilt wall is mostly a wall.

"There are men behind you," he said to Ilan. "How many days?"

"Two. Maybe three, in this cold."

"And they know you're coming here?"

"They know the route. Not the stops." Ilan's eyes flicked to mine, then away. "Probably."

"'Probably,'" I repeated. "I love this plan already. Tell me, does it get better?"

"It gets worse," Ilan said, with the honesty of the truly exhausted. "But the worse parts involve the river, and you don't have to hear them yet."

My father folded the letter with the reverence he usually reserved for first proofs. He did not put it down. He held it, and I saw his thumb move across the slashed crossbars once, twice, the way a man touches the face in a locket.

"Tomas," he said, to no one, to the letter, to a grave that had turned out to be empty. "You stubborn, brilliant, *ungrateful* boy."

There was a whole history in the insult. I could hear the shape of it, the way you hear the shape of a locked drawer by the way the cabinet sits crooked. Seven years of silence, three years of mourning, and it all came out as *ungrateful boy*. My father's anger had always been like his type — cast small, set tight, capable of bearing enormous weight.

"Papa," I said. "What aren't you telling me?"

He looked at me then, really looked, the way he hadn't since I was a child — measuring me, the way you measure a piece of type before you set it, deciding whether it fits the line. Outside, the wind pushed at the loading door. Somewhere downriver, the ice groaned, that long complaint it makes when the pressure underneath gets to be more than the surface can bear.

"Light the stove," my father said finally. "Both of you are staying. We have work tonight."

"What work?"

He tucked the letter into his coat, over his heart, like a man arming himself.

"The kind," he said, "that gets people hanged. Or worse. Light the stove, Dominic. And then bar the loading door — properly, this time. From tonight onward, we are no longer a shop that keeps silent."

I went to do as he said. And if my hands shook a little at the bolt — well. Cold hands make poor witnesses, and I had already decided, somewhere in the last quarter hour without announcing it to myself, that whatever my father was not yet telling me, whatever dead man's business had bled onto our floor and into our house —

I was in it now. Ink dries fast. Blood dries faster. And some pages, once pulled, cannot be unprinted.

The stove caught. The wind rose. And two streets away, though none of us knew it yet, a lantern was being lit in the customs house — the first of many that would spend the winter looking for us.
