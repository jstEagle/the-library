## 3. What the Corpse Wrote

A man's handwriting does not die with him. That is the first thing I thought when I saw the letters — before I felt the cold of the loading door still on my skin, before I noticed the blood on the stranger's sleeve or the way my daughter was looking at me as though I had grown a second face. A hand is a signature deeper than any name. You spend years teaching a boy to cut a serif clean, to let the downstrokes breathe, to give his capitals just enough arrogance to be legible across a crowded page — and then he vanishes into an official death, and three winters later his hand walks back through your door in a stranger's pocket.

Tomas Veyra's hand. Unmistakable. The *y* that kicked like a mule, the crossed t's set at a slight upward slant, as if every word he wrote were climbing toward something. I would know it in the dark. I have known it in the dark, these three years, in the letters I never wrote and the apologies I composed at two in the morning on the backs of spoiled proofs and burned.

"Read them," Ilan said. He said it the way men say *trust me* — which is to say, it achieved the opposite.

I read them.

I should tell you about the boy first, though. The dead deserve their chronology observed, even when they turn out to be badly misfiled among the living.

* * *

Seven years ago — no. Let me be honest, since honesty has apparently decided to ambush me in my own shop: six years and eleven months ago, because I have counted, God help me, I have counted like a creditor — Tomas Veyra came up from the cellar with ink on his knuckles and a look on his face I had seen only twice before in thirty years of printing. Once on a journeyman who later emigrated to Velmora and opened a bookshop and never spoke of why. Once on myself, aged nineteen, holding a pamphlet whose truth was worth more than my life and knowing it anyway.

He had been setting the governor's tribute edition — the commemoration of the Orchard Bridge "unrest," the one where forty-one souls were recorded as having perished in a regrettable accident of crowd and current. Forty-one. I set that figure myself, year after year, the way you set the date on a calendar. It becomes furniture. It becomes nothing.

Tomas had been given the garrison's source documents to work from — a courtesy, Reiner's office called it, proof of our loyalty that we typeset their own account rather than let some less careful house do it. And Tomas, being Tomas, had *read* them. This was his genius and his ruin: the boy could not help reading. He read everything. He read the shipping manifests we printed for the grain merchants and deduced which aldermen were being paid off. He read hymnals for the heresy hidden in the margins. He once corrected my Latin at fifteen, politely, which I forgave, and correctly, which took longer.

"They don't add," he told me that night, standing in the middle of the shop with the proofs fanned in his hands like losing cards. "Master Hartwell. The bridge tolls. The burial registers. The parish lists. They don't add up to forty-one. They add up to—" and he stopped himself, because even then he understood that some numbers are not spoken aloud indoors.

"How far short?" I asked, which was the coward's question, the merchant's question, the question of a man calculating exposure rather than horror.

"Short by ninety," he said. "And the ledger that reconciles it exists. I've seen its title page. It's kept in the governor's own counting room."

I want to record what I did next with precision, because precision is the only penance available to a printer. I did not argue. I did not ask him what he intended. I walked to the window, checked the street the way I had taught him to check it — both directions, unhurried, a man admiring the weather — and then I told him the truth as I understood it then, which was this: that a press is a tool, not a torch; that truth without distribution is just grief with good grammar; that he was twenty-two years old and brilliant and therefore precisely the sort of man the empire noticed first.

"You can print it," I said, "and be dead by Thursday, and the type will be melted by Friday, and no one will ever know there was anything to melt."

"Then someone should make them melt it slowly," he said. "Someone should make them afraid of the alphabet."

You see what I was working against. You cannot out-argue a sentence like that. All I could do was what I did: I gave him money from the shop's false bottom, the good coat from its hook, and a route downriver I had prepared years earlier for a contingency I prayed would stay hypothetical. I told him to write to no one. I told Marta he'd run off with a Velmora actress, and she looked at me with such contempt that I understood she hadn't believed me and had chosen, generously, to let me keep my lie intact rather than force me to build a better one.

And then, when the garrison came asking — because they always come asking, Reiner's predecessors, Reiner himself, patient as rust — I told them the apprentice had been dismissed for theft. Small theft. Sordid, believable, unworthy-of-defense theft. It was the best lie I knew how to build: a small ugly truth-shaped thing that explained his absence, humiliated him safely, and closed the inquiry. They believed it. They always believe a story that lets them feel superior.

Three months later the official word came back from downriver: drowned at the ice break near Kessel's Ferry. No body recovered — bodies rarely are, on that river; the water keeps what it takes. The parish register recorded it. I printed the notice myself. I set his name in obituary type — the small somber face we reserve for the dead — and I remember thinking, with a clarity I have spent three years trying to drown: *this is the last line anyone will ever set about him.*

I was wrong. That is the entire content of this chapter, if you want it in a single line of type. I was wrong, and here were the letters to prove it.

* * *

The present, again: the stove ticking, the wind worrying the shutters, Dominic standing very still with her sleeves rolled and her forearms inked to the elbow like any compositor's, watching me read with the expression she has worn since childhood when she believes herself unobserved — that fierce inventory-taking stare, cataloguing me for flaws and openings alike. She gets it from her mother. God rest Anka, who married a printer and learned to read proofs faster than I did and never once let me forget it.

The letters were four. Written in exile over three years, apparently hoarded until a courier could be found mad enough or desperate enough to carry them. The hand was Tomas's but changed — heavier now, the ascents shortened, a hand written by lamplight in cold rooms by a man conserving ink and strength alike.

The first letter was old, dated within weeks of his flight. It said only: *I am alive. Do not search for me. Do not answer this.* It was, I understood immediately, a test — mailed to see whether the shop was watched, whether an answer would come. We answered nothing. I hope to God no one else did either.

The second was dated two years past. It described, in the dry voice of a man writing an inventory, what he had found in the governor's counting house before he fled — not the massacre ledger itself but its shadow: requisition orders, payment drafts, a schedule of disbursements initialed by men whose initials I recognized because I had set them in official proclamations for a decade. He wrote: *They paid for the arithmetic, Master. Someone bought the difference between ninety and forty-one, and bought it in installments.*

The third letter named names. I will not reproduce them here — not because I fear the page, but because Dominic was standing close enough to read over my arm, and there are things a father still flinches to show his daughter, and a list of traitors that includes two garrison officers and the deputy customs magistrate is one of them.

The fourth was recent. It said: *I hold the original. It must reach the guild in Velmora before the ice breaks, or it reaches no one. I cannot carry it myself; they watch the roads for my face. Send someone the river will not remember.*

*Send someone the river will not remember.* Even in extremis, the boy could cut a phrase. I put the letters down and found my own hands unsteady — a compositor's hands, steady through tremors and fevers and the fine palsy creeping in around my failing eyes, unsteady now.

"He's alive," Dominic said. Not a question. She had read enough over my shoulder.

"By all appearances." My voice came out level, which cost me something. Level voices always cost me something; it is the family trade.

"The whole city mourns him. Marta wears black for him. There's a— Papa, there's a *memorial plaque* at the parish."

"I'm aware of the plaque," I said. "I contributed to it."

And there it was, the guilt, surfacing exactly as I knew it would — not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a drowned body rises: slowly, unwillingly, in stages, each stage worse than the last, until the thing you sank is floating face-up in the middle of your life for everyone to see. Six years and eleven months of telling myself I had chosen the only survivable course. Six years and eleven months of a small ugly lie doing its modest, decent work of keeping a shop open and a daughter fed. And underneath it all, the truth I had never let reach the surface: that I had been given one chance in my life to be the kind of man my trade pretends printers are — the smugglers of truth, the midwives of revolution, all that flattering folklore we tell ourselves over the composing stick — and I had weighed it against a roof and warm bread and chosen the roof, and dressed the choice up as wisdom ever since.

Dominic was still looking at me. "You knew he might be alive," she said. "When the notices came. You didn't believe the drowning."

It wasn't a question either. My daughter does not deal in questions when statements will wound more efficiently. I considered lying to her — briefly, professionally, the way one considers a cheap font for an expensive job — and discarded it. She had heard the letters read. She had watched my face during the third one.

"No," I said. "A river that keeps bodies is convenient for the empire. I didn't believe it. Believing it would have required evidence, and I had helped manufacture the absence of evidence, so I was hardly positioned to demand it."

"Why?" And now the question, finally, the one I had been rehearsing answers to since the night I handed a boy a coat and a route and my complicity. "Why send him away instead of printing it? You print their lies every day. You hate every one of them. Why not one true thing?"

Because I was afraid. Because the shop was your inheritance. Because your mother had just buried her second child and I could not bury her too. Because courage is a currency and I had calculated my holdings and found myself poor. Any of these would have served; together they were simply true, and truths in quantity are harder to dodge than one well-aimed lie.

"Because I chose the shop over the truth," I said, "and I have been paying interest on that loan ever since. Now the debt has walked in through the loading door wearing someone else's blood, and I suspect the terms have gotten worse."

* * *

Ilan — the stranger, the courier, the man with the sea-soaked cough and the soldier's habit of sitting facing the door — watched this exchange between us with the wary attention of a man checking the soundness of a bridge before committing his weight to it. When Dominic went to bolt the front shutter and bank the stove, he leaned toward me and spoke low.

"You set his death notice yourself. I saw it in the archive copies. Good notice. Convincing."

"Thank you," I said, and discovered that praise for that particular job sat in my chest like a swallowed nail.

"He needs the ledger moved. I need your shop — not your presses." He glanced toward the back room where Dominic had gone. "Your craft. Forged passes, altered manifests, a duplicate bill of lading. Things that look born, not made."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I go to another house, and I pray they're half as good, and the ledger probably burns with me in an alley." He shrugged. "But you won't refuse. I've read your work, Master Hartwell. A man doesn't set forty-one deaths a year for a decade without keeping a private ledger of his own. Yours is written in guilt. Guilt pays well, in my experience. It wants redemption and it overpays."

I ought to have resented that. Instead I admired it — it was typesetting of a kind, that speech, each clause set to justify, the argument composed with an eye to exactly how it would read. Exiles learn composition the hard way.

"The ice breaks in five weeks," I said. "The river gives us perhaps four of usable passage after preparations. Three checkpoints stand between here and Velmora, and the governor's men are not stupid, merely well-fed." I looked down at the fourth letter, at the climbing hand, at the *y* kicking its mule-kick across the page. "Tell your master he has his shop. Tell him—" I stopped, because there was no sentence I could finish that wouldn't betray more than it carried.

"Tell him yourself," Ilan said. "When it's done. He said you'd say that. He said—" and here the courier permitted himself the ghost of a smile, the first I'd seen on him, "—he said the old man will apologize with typography. He'll rebuild your trust in type specimens and forged documents because words frighten him and craft doesn't. So let him craft."

From the back room came the sound of my daughter pulling the case drawer — the deep one, the one with the hidden floor I had not opened in seven years. She had found it. Of course she had found it; she was mine, raised among secrets stored in furniture, and I had perhaps taught her too well the grammar of concealment and not nearly enough of confession.

"Papa," she called, her voice carrying that new note it had taken on tonight — not the weary competence I knew, but something sharper underneath, ore showing through the seam. "How much lead do we have? And Papa — how soon can you teach me to forge a customs seal?"

I looked at Ilan. Ilan looked at me, eyebrows raised, a man revising his assessment of the cargo.

"Five weeks," I said to the room in general, and to the ghost in particular. "God forgive us, we'll need every one of them."
