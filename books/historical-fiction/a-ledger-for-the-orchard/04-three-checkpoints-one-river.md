## 4. Three Checkpoints, One River

I have been told I explain things well. This is a lie I encourage, because a man who explains things well is assumed to be honest, and honesty is the most useful disguise I own. My face is not trustworthy — too many scars arranged without architectural sense, one of them pulling my left eyebrow into a permanent expression of doubt — so I have learned to make up for it with voice. Calm, reasonable, slightly bored. The voice of a man discussing freight rates, not treason.

I used that voice on the Hartwells in their kitchen at four in the morning, over cold tea and a candle burned down to a puddle.

"Three checkpoints," I said. "One river. And a calendar that hates us."

Aldous Hartwell sat with his hands flat on the table like a man pinning down something that might fly away. His daughter stood by the stove with her arms crossed, watching me the way a compositor watches a line of type she suspects contains an error — patiently, thoroughly, and with the quiet certainty that she'll find it.

Let me describe the problem as I described it to them, because the shape of a thing matters when you're deciding whether to die for it.

The ledger — Tomas Veyra's ledger, the true one, the one the governor paid eleven men to unmake — was not in this city. It was two hundred versts south, in a cellar in Kolvary, under a floor that a very careful woman pretended to store pickled cabbage above. Tomas could not bring it north himself; his face was known from here to the salt mines, and there are men in Kolvary who would sell their mothers for the price of his capture. So the ledger had to come to us, or rather, had already begun coming to us, traveling in pieces inside bales of wool, sewn into coat linings, memorized in the head of a deaf-mute carter who repeated it to no one because he couldn't.

Wait. That last part isn't true yet. Let me keep the order straight; a courier who scrambles the sequence dies scrambled.

Here is what was true: the ledger existed. It was coming north by stages. It would arrive within the week, in whatever form we had arranged — and I will come to the form, because the form was Aldous Hartwell's contribution and it was, I confess, better than anything I'd planned. And once it arrived, it had to leave again. Immediately. South by river, to Velmora, to the printers' guild there, before the ice broke.

Everyone nods at "before the ice breaks." It sounds poetic. Let me make it mechanical instead, because poetry doesn't move cargo.

This city sits on the bend where the Vell narrows and deepens. For most of the winter, the river is a road — a road the governor owns, patrolled, taxed, sealed. Nothing moves on it that he doesn't know about. But for perhaps ten days at the end of winter, the ice rots. It goes gray and honeycombed, groans at night like an old man climbing stairs, and then one morning it cracks along the current line and the whole frozen highway becomes a river again. Boats can run. More importantly, boats can *leave* — quickly, at any hour, without anyone on the wharf having time to think about whether they should.

After the ice breaks, the river belongs to everyone for a few weeks, until the governor remembers he owns it. Before it breaks, every boat is inspected, every manifest read aloud, every hold probed with iron rods. We needed the ledger aboard a boat in the window between "ice rotten enough to trust" and "garrison awake enough to notice." That window was closing. That was the calendar.

And between the shop and any departing boat stood three doors, each one built by a different kind of man, each one requiring a different kind of lie.

* * *

"The customs house first," I said, and drew a line in the spilled tea with one finger. "Every shipment leaving the city passes through the customs quay upstream of the bridge. Clerk examines your manifest, weighs your goods if he's feeling diligent, probes anything that weighs wrong. The clerks there are not soldiers. They're worse — they're *bureaucrats*. A soldier can be bribed or rushed. A bureaucrat has all morning."

Dominic spoke for the first time in an hour. "Which means?"

"Which means the paperwork must be perfect and boring. Not interesting-boring, which is its own kind of interesting. Perfectly dull. A widow shipping her husband's effects downriver to family, say. Funeral goods. No one wants to open funeral goods. Grief is the best wrapping paper in the empire." I looked at her when I said it. She didn't flinch. Most people flinch when you suggest they play a corpse's next of kin; it touches something. She just nodded slowly, filing it away somewhere behind those ink-stained hands.

"There's a clerk named Brann," I went on, "who has been at the customs house nineteen years and has been passed over for promotion eleven times, which makes him either corruptible or fanatically honest, depending on which wound is fresher. We don't approach him. We approach nothing. The trick at the customs house is to be so ordinary that his eye slides off you like rain off waxed canvas."

"And the second door?" Aldous asked. His voice had gone rough. He'd been quiet a long time, and I understood why. Every word I said made the thing more real, and real things, in his experience, got people killed. I'd learned since arriving that his experience was extensive.

"The garrison bridge." I moved my finger downstream through the tea-puddle map. "The only road off the customs quay crosses the bridge, and the garrison holds both ends. Soldiers, not clerks. They don't read manifests; they look at faces. Captain Reiner has them trained to watch for exactly the things we'd want to do — people who don't make eye contact, people who make too much of it, packages carried wrong, shoes worn by someone walking a route they haven't walked before." I tapped the table. "You cannot bribe your way past Reiner's men. Half of them believe in the empire, and the half that don't are watched by the half that do. What you can do is give them something else to look at."

"A diversion," Dominic said.

"A diversion," I agreed. "Which requires a person willing to stand in front of soldiers and be interesting. Interesting, at a garrison checkpoint, is a career decision with limited longevity." I let that sit. I am not proud of how I let it sit. Part of recruiting people is letting them see the cost clearly, and part of it — the part I dislike about myself — is letting them volunteer anyway.

"And the third?" Aldous again. He was leaning forward now despite himself.

"The governor's private wharf." I heard my own voice flatten, because this was the part I hated saying out loud. "Every other dock on the river will be watched after the first two checkpoints report anything unusual. But the governor keeps a private landing below the fort walls, for his own barges. It's guarded lightly — his household guard, not the garrison — because the governor does not trust the garrison, and the garrison knows it, and this mutual allergy is the only crack in the whole machine. If a boat leaves from the private wharf, it's presumed to be *his* business, and no soldier alive asks the governor's business."

"So we steal his dock," Dominic said. Something in her voice had changed. Not excitement — I want to be fair to her, it wasn't excitement, it was the sound of a person discovering that an impossible problem has one solution and the solution is insane.

"We borrow his dock," I corrected. "We put a boat there with papers suggesting it's a consignment for one of his own officials in Velmora. Which means forging the governor's household paperwork, which means—" I gestured around the room, at the type cases stacked against the wall, the press under its dust sheet, the whole sleeping animal of the shop, "—it means the finest counterfeiter of official documents in this city. Who is officially just a printer with failing eyes."

All three of us looked at Aldous.

He looked back at us for a long moment, and then he did something I did not expect. He laughed. Quietly, mostly breath, the way men laugh at funerals when the deceased would have appreciated the joke. "Seven years," he said. "Seven years I've set the governor's proclamations with a steady hand and kept my opinions in the pi case. And now you want me to use the same hand to forge his seals."

"I want you to consider it," I said. "Consideration costs nothing."

"It costs everything," Dominic said. She hadn't moved from the stove, but her voice had dropped into a register I'd learn to recognize later — the register she used when she'd already done the arithmetic and found the sum unbearable but correct. "You're asking him to stop being silent. After seven years of silence keeping this shop alive."

"I'm asking him to spend the silence," I said. "There's a difference. Silence saved up does no one any good. Spent at the right moment—"

"Don't lecture me about spending," she cut in, and for a second the compositor was gone and something younger and angrier stood in her place. "My father spent seven years teaching me that the safe thing and the right thing are different rooms in the same house, and you never get to live in both. Don't tell him his hoard is worthless now that you need it."

The candle guttered. Nobody moved to trim it.

I should record, for accuracy, that she was right and I was wrong in every particular except one. I did need Aldous's silence spent. But the way I'd said it — the way I'd walked into their grief and started inventorying it like cargo — that was the old Ilan, the courier who treated people as route segments. Three years of exile had supposedly taught me better. Apparently they hadn't taught me better enough.

* * *

We argued until the windows went gray. Not loudly — the Hartwells don't seem to do loud, even in extremis — but thoroughly, the way water argues with stone. Aldous raised objections in the manner of a man testing load-bearing walls. The ice might break early. The guild in Velmora might already be compromised. Eleven names signed the forged ledger; any one of them might guess the true one still breathed. Each objection I answered, and each answer cost me something, because honest answers in that trade are always expensive.

Dominic objected differently. She asked questions designed to find the flaw, not to defeat the plan. How heavy was the ledger? Could it pass for something a widow would actually ship — and what did widows ship, exactly, and would she need to learn to cry on demand, because she'd never managed it even when it was warranted? Where would the diversion at the bridge come from, and did I already have someone, and if I did, why wasn't that person carrying the ledger themselves instead of trusting strangers?

That last one stopped me.

"Why *are* you trusting us?" she asked. "You've known us for one night. You were bleeding on our loading door six hours ago. Either you're a fool, or you've got no one else, and fools don't survive long enough to carry letters for dead men."

"Ilan," Aldous said mildly, "the lady has found the flaw."

I could have lied. I considered lying; the reflex arrives faster than thought, like a hand catching a falling glass. But there was something in the way she waited — not triumphant, not hostile, just *waiting*, the way she probably waited over a bad line of type — that made lying feel like handing a counterfeit coin to a mint inspector.

"No one else," I admitted. "The network that got Tomas out is scattered or turned. Two couriers went south in autumn; neither arrived. Everyone I trusted in this city is either dead, arrested, or someone I trusted *too much*. Your name came up because Tomas wrote it. 'If the letters fail, go to the shop. Aldous kept me alive once; he'll hate doing it twice.'" I spread my scarred hands. "So yes. I'm trusting you because a dead man vouched, and because I have run out of alternatives, and because—" I hesitated, and decided the hour was too late for strategy, "—because you set type for the governor's lies every day and haven't gone mad, which suggests a discipline I could use."

Silence. The gray light crept across the floorboards.

Then Aldous stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and went to the corner where the oldest type case lived, the one with the false bottom I'd noticed on arrival and been polite enough not to notice. He came back with a sheet of paper, yellowed, folded eight times.

"If we do this," he said, "we do it properly. Here is the watermark pattern of the governor's household manifests, from memory, because I printed the originals for six years before they took the contract in-house." He laid it on the table between us like a man laying down a card he'd been holding for seven years. "Now tell me again about the widow."

It was nearly settled, then. Nearly. We had the route, the disguise, the forgery, the deadline. We had a widow who didn't exist yet, a diversion with no volunteer, and a boat that existed only as an idea with mooring fees.

What we did not have — what none of us knew yet, what wouldn't walk through the loading door until the sun was fully up and the city stirring — was the sound of boots in the street outside. Measured boots. Many of them. Stopping.

Dominic reached the window first and looked down through the gap in the shutters, and I saw her shoulders change.

"Soldiers," she said quietly. "At the corner. An officer." A pause, and then, in a voice carefully emptied of everything: "He's looking at our sign."
