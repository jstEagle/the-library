## 5. The Governor's Compliment

The proclamation I had set the week before — the one declaring the grain harvest *abundant* and the people *contented* in a winter when children were boiling bark for soup — was still pasted to the customs house wall when Captain Reiner came to compliment us on it.

That is the thing about this city. The lies are not merely permitted here; they are *framed*. And the men who hang the frames come around personally to shake your hand.

I heard him before I saw him, because you always do. Boots with a certain weight of authority, two escorts who walked half a step behind in the way of men paid to be scenery. Aldous heard it too. He was at the stone with his loupe in one eye, squinting at a piece of ten-point Caslon like it owed him money, and his shoulders did that small settling motion — a man arranging himself into respectability the way you'd smooth a bedsheet.

"Company," I said.

"I have eyes, Dominic."

"You have one and a half."

"And it sees fine enough." He set down the loupe. "Front room. Be pleasant."

I am always pleasant. It is my second-best skill after setting type upside-down without looking, which is a trick every compositor learns eventually and no one outside the trade ever appreciates. Pleasantness is what keeps a print shop alive in a city where paper itself has become suspicious. You smile. You agree the harvest was abundant. You ink the governor's face so many times he begins to feel like family, an ugly cousin you're obliged to feed at every holiday.

The bell over the door — I'd been meaning to oil it since autumn; now I was glad I hadn't — announced Reiner with a jangle entirely beneath his dignity.

He was younger than his reputation. That's the first thing I noticed, and I resented noticing it. Reputation had made him forty and granite-jawed, a man carved by the Empire out of some imperial quarry. In person he was perhaps thirty-three, with a scholar's pale forehead under the garrison cap and eyes that moved through our front room the way a customs clerk's finger moves down a manifest: item, item, item, nothing missed.

"Hartwell & Daughter," he read aloud from the painted glass of the inner door, though he must have known the name before he crossed the square. "Though I understand the daughter does most of the work these days."

"The daughter," Aldous said from behind me, "is modest enough to let her father claim otherwise."

"Modesty." Reiner smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, issued by the same quartermaster as the boots. "A rare commodity this season. May I?"

He gestured at the shop generally, already walking into it, which answered the question for him. His escorts stayed at the door. Scenery, but armed scenery.

He admired everything. That was the unnerving part — not menace but appreciation. He picked up a printed funeral notice and read it with genuine interest, tilting it toward the window light. He ran a fingertip along the edge of the composing stone and looked at the ink on his glove the way a man checks a wine against its label. He paused before the framed specimen sheet Aldous kept on the wall, thirty years of typefaces arranged like portraits of ancestors.

"Beautiful work," he said. "Truly. The guild should be proud. *I* am proud." He turned. "It is precisely why the Governor asked me to visit."

Here it comes, I thought, and reached for the pleasantness like a tool off the rack.

"The new press ordinances," Reiner said. "You'll have seen the circular. Registration of all matrices above twelve point. Weekly submission of press runs. A licensed man present during all commercial printing." He said it lightly, the way you'd describe weather arriving. "Some shops are treating it as an insult. I prefer to think of it as the Empire acknowledging quality. One regulates what one values. No one inspects mud."

"No one prints mud either," Aldous said mildly. "Though I've set some proclamations that came close."

There was a silence with edges on it. I watched Reiner decide whether my father's joke was an old man's foolishness or a test, and I saw him decide — correctly, damn him — that it was both.

"Your father," he said to me, "has a reputation for exactly that kind of honesty. It's why the shop survived the troubles seven years ago when others closed." His eyes came back around to me, and stayed. "You would have been young then. Fourteen? Fifteen?"

"Fifteen."

"A formative age." Something shifted in his voice — a half-step down, a door opening onto a warmer room than I wanted to enter. "And you learned the trade at his side. All of it? Casting, cutting, composition?"

"She can cast a matrix better than men I apprenticed for six years," Aldous said. There was something underneath the pride, a wire pulled taut. My father was doing arithmetic I couldn't see, and the sums were coming out wrong.

"I believe it." Reiner stepped closer to me — not improperly, nothing anyone could name, just inside the radius where politeness becomes pressure. "May I?"

He took my right hand before I decided whether to offer it. Turned it palm-up in the gray window light like a page he meant to proofread.

Ink lives in a compositor's hands the way sin lives in a conscience: scrubbing removes the surface and never the truth. The cuticles black. A stain in the whorl of the left thumb where the roller passes daily, ten thousand times a year, until the skin itself takes the color permanently. My father called them working hands. Marta Veyra once said, in a kinder lifetime, that they looked like hands that had made a promise to something and kept it.

Reiner held mine and read them.

"These are not," he said, "a lady's hands."

"No," I agreed. "They're mine."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and for one unguarded moment he was just a tired young man in an old cold shop. Then the moment folded itself away, filed somewhere I couldn't reach, and the captain returned.

"Ink-stained like a typesetter's. Like a cutter's." His thumb moved once across the heel of my palm, reading the callus there, the one that comes from the burnisher, from hours of smoothing metal. Most women in this city had never held a burnisher. Most women in this city would not know what that callus meant. He knew. "Your father says you cut your own sorts."

"When the case runs short."

"And engrave."

"Ornaments," I said. "Initials. Nothing worth registering."

"Everything is worth registering," he said pleasantly. "That is rather the entire philosophy of the new ordinance." He released my hand. My skin kept the shape of his grip a moment longer than I liked, the way paper holds a fold forever after you've creased it.

He turned back to Aldous. "The Governor wishes me to convey his confidence. Hartwell & Daughter will find compliance rewarded. Priority contracts. The municipal printing, perhaps — there is talk of consolidating." A smile. "Loyalty is noticed in this city, Master Hartwell. So is its absence. You've built something fine here. It would be a shame—" he glanced, casually, terribly, at the type wall, the cases stacked floor to ceiling, each one a small locked drawer of alphabet, "—if anything happened to it. Fires happen. Accidents. Winter is careless with old buildings."

"Winter is careless with everyone," Aldous said.

"Yes," said Reiner. "Isn't it."

* * *

They left. The bell jangled their departure, which was the last honest sound in the shop for some while.

Aldous stood at the window watching them cross the square, his breath fogging the glass, one hand pressed flat against the frame as if holding the building shut. I went back to the stone and stood in front of the job I'd been composing — a wedding announcement, innocuous as bread — and discovered my hands were shaking. Not much. Enough. The tremor of a hand that has been held by the wrong person.

"He read my hands," I said. "Like a ledger."

"Mm."

"He knows what I am. What this shop actually is. The cutting, the casting—"

"Dominic." My father turned from the window. In the flat February light his failing eye looked almost healed, both of them fixed on me with terrible clarity. "Listen to me. A man like that does not make visits. He makes *records*. Today he wrote us into a file. Everything he praised, he measured. Everything he admired, he priced." He came and took my ink-stained right hand in both of his own — the same gesture, gentler, and yet it was the same gesture, and we both felt the echo. "From tomorrow, the engravings come out of your case. The private work stops. Whatever you've hidden in the pi fonts, whatever you keep under the third drawer—"

"There's nothing—"

"Don't." He said it softly, and the softness was worse than shouting. "Seven years ago I made a choice about what gets hidden in this shop and what gets shown, and I chose wrong, or I chose right, and I have spent every day since not knowing which. I won't have you learn the answer the way I'm learning it."

"What answer?"

He didn't say. He squeezed my hand once, hard, the callus against the callus, cutter to cutter, and went back to his loupe and his Caslon and his squinting, and the shop settled around us like snowfall, quiet, heavy, concealing everything it touched.

But I stood at the stone with the wedding announcement half-set and thought about the way Reiner's thumb had crossed my palm. Not a soldier's touch. An auditor's. Somewhere in the garrison, in a file with our name at the top, there was now a line describing my hands, and lines in files have a way of becoming warrants.

Fear sat in my chest, cold and specific.

And underneath it — I tested it carefully, the way you test ice before committing your weight — something else. Something harder and more awake than fear. He had held my hand to prove I was dangerous.

Fine. Let the record show he read it correctly.

That night I did not take the engravings out of my case. Instead I cut a new initial, small, sharp, unregistered, and hid it where only a cutter would think to look — and if that was defiance, then Captain Reiner could add it to his file in his own fair hand, because my hands were going to write faster than his.
