## 6. Lessons in Disappearing

There are two ways to teach a person anything. The first is to explain it, which is what most teachers do, and which is why most lessons evaporate like water off a hot platen. The second is to put the thing in their hands and say nothing until they've nearly ruined it.

I had spent seven years teaching my daughter the first way and pretending it was the only way. On the night after Ilan Veyra's letters lay open on my desk — Tomas's handwriting crawling across the pages like a man writing from the bottom of a river he was supposed to have drowned in — I decided it was time for the second way.

"Dominic," I said, "fetch the long case. The one with the false back."

She looked at me the way she looks at everything now: as though she's already read it twice and found one error. "We don't have a case with a false back."

"We do. You've been setting type out of it for four years."

I watched that land. I watched her do the arithmetic — every job, every official proclamation, every wedding announcement and shipping notice that had passed through her hands from that case — and arrive at the sum: *my father has been lying to me, competently, for years.* Her face did something complicated. It always does something complicated. It's the Hartwell inheritance; we keep our faces like we keep our type cases, everything sorted and nothing on top.

"The third one from the window," I said. "Bring it here. And bring a lamp."

* * *

The shop at midnight is a different country than the shop by day. By day it smells of ink and oil and the cold iron patience of the press. By midnight it smells of memory, which is very similar but harder to wash off your hands.

Dominic set the case on the composing stone between us, and I put my palm flat against its lid. My eyes are going — I'll not pretend otherwise; I set my last clean page of body text by feel and faith some months ago, and Dominic has quietly been correcting my errors ever since, thinking I don't notice, which is its own small kindness and its own small insult — but my hands still see better than my eyes ever did. Hands remember.

"Feel along the left wall," I said. "Inside. Where the dividers join the frame."

Her fingers went in. She is a cutter, my daughter; her hands know wood the way a blind man knows faces. I heard her breath change when she found it — the seam, hairline-fine, running where no seam has any right to run.

"There's nothing there," she said. "It's just the joint."

"Press the lower corner toward you and lift."

The false panel came up with the soft sigh of wood that hasn't moved in three years, and beneath it lay the shallow drawer of my other life. Not papers — paper burns, paper betrays, paper has testimony written all over it. Beneath the panel lay *type*. A full font of it, cast in an alloy slightly softer than our working metal, each character cut by hand by a young man who had been dead for three years by official record and was, according to the letters currently hidden inside my coat lining, inconveniently alive.

"What is this?" Dominic asked, though I could see she already knew. That's the trouble with teaching someone to think like a printer: they stop being able to un-think.

"That," I said, "is a pi font."

"Pi fonts are just sorts without homes. Castoffs. Every shop has a drawer of them."

"Yes. And every censor in the empire knows it, which is why it works." I took up one of the characters — a lowercase *e*, but cut wrong, the counter opened too wide, the serif on the wrong foot — and held it to the lamp. "Look closely. Every glyph in this case is wrong. Deliberately wrong. Cut so that no compositor would ever use them by accident, because they'd look like errors, and compositors hate errors more than they love their own mothers."

"So if a message were set in these—"

"Then anyone pulling a proof would see a line of gibberish with bad kerning and move on. But set the same message in *proper* type alongside it, and the pi sorts act as—"

"—as a key." She turned the misfit *e* over in her fingers. "A confirmation. Proof the message is genuine, proof it's from whoever owns the font."

"Tomas cut that case the winter before he ran. Took him eleven weeks. He complained about it every single day." My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it go; there was no one in the room but my daughter and a lamp, and the lamp keeps confidences. "He said it was the most tedious work I'd ever assigned him. I told him tedium was the price of survival. He said survival was overrated."

"And then he proved his own point."

"He did rather."

She was quiet a moment, turning sorts in her fingers, and I watched her arrive somewhere I couldn't follow. When she spoke again her voice was level in the way mine goes level when I'm angriest, and I recognized the family resemblance with something between pride and grief.

"You taught him this. You taught him all of it. And when he found what he found — the ledger, the forgery, all of it — you taught him how to disappear instead of how to stay and fight."

"I taught him how to survive," I said. "Fighting was available separately. He declined the package."

"And you?"

"I kept the shop open."

It is a poor answer. It has been a poor answer for seven years, and I have polished it nightly the way one polishes brass, until it shines enough to pass inspection. Dominic looked at me across the composing stone, and I saw that she was deciding — not whether to forgive me, but whether to file the question away for a night with better light. She filed it. Hartwells file things.

"Show me the rest," she said.

* * *

The rest took until the small hours, and I will record here, for whatever ledger finally gets the truth of my life, that teaching my daughter was the finest work I ever did in that shop, including the year I printed the almanac that saved the grain merchants' contracts and made our name.

I showed her the compartments. There are four in that room and she had known about none of them, which pleased me shamefully and depressed me thoroughly in equal measure, since it meant I'd raised a careful girl in a house built on carelessness — or rather, on concealment, which is carelessness wearing a good coat. The kick-plate under the stone sink holds a foot of depth behind a board that looks load-bearing. The frame of the old proofing press has a cavity in the leg wide enough for a sealed letter, accessible only when the press bed is cranked fully back. And the type cabinet by the door — the one she'd assumed was empty since the Kessler job — has a drawer that opens six inches and stops, unless you lift the runner, at which point it opens six inches more into a space exactly the size of a folded quarto.

"Why the Kessler cabinet?" she asked. "It's the least used thing in the shop."

"Because censors search where the work is. They pull drawers by frequency of use. An empty cabinet is invisible precisely because it should be full." I tapped the runner. "Remember: hide things where absence is expected. Never where presence would be natural."

She wrote it down. Actually wrote it, on the margin of a spoiled galley proof, in her quick slanted hand. I felt something turn over in my chest that I chose not to examine, because examining it would have meant admitting I was preparing her for something, and preparing her for something meant admitting what the something was, and there were letters in my coat lining that made the word for that something too large to say aloud in a room with thin walls.

Then I showed her the inks.

Water-soluble ink is the oldest trick in the trade and the best, because its genius is its modesty. Mix a fugitive ink — iron gall cut with gum and a whisper of the right salt, or the commercial preparations if you have money, which we did not, so we had chemistry and stubbornness — and print your true text in it beneath or beside the innocuous text in standard ink. The censor reads the standard text. The censor's clerk stamps the sheet. And the recipient, at leisure, in private, runs a damp cloth across the page and watches the permanent-looking words dissolve away like a lie at dawn, leaving the truth underneath.

"It fades?" she asked, bending over the test sheet I pulled from the drying rack — a shipping notice, ordinary as bread, with faint ghost-lines beneath the freight manifest.

"It vanishes. Completely, in minutes. Which is also its flaw: you get one reading. No archives, no evidence. If the reader isn't paying attention, the message dies with the water."

"So it's ink for people who trust each other."

I hadn't thought of it that way. I said so. She looked surprised — genuinely surprised, my daughter who is surprised by nothing — and I understood that she had expected me to be embarrassed by the question, and my not being embarrassed unsettled her more than any answer could have.

"Trust is the whole trade, Dom," I said. "Type is just trust made heavy enough to hold still."

* * *

We worked. God, how we worked. Somewhere past two in the morning I stopped being a father explaining tricks and started being a cutter working beside a cutter, and the difference was like the difference between a rehearsal and a performance.

I had her set a practice message in the pi font — something harmless, a line from the shipping notices — while I composed the same words in proper type. Then I had her pull proofs both ways and compare, hunting the discrepancies, learning to read the wrongnesses the way Tomas had learned to write them. She was slow at first. Then she wasn't. There is a moment in every apprentice's progress when the hands stop asking permission of the mind, and I saw it happen in her at half past three, her fingers walking the case without looking, selecting the misfit sorts by touch alone because she had already memorized their individual wrongnesses.

"You cut these yourself once," she said suddenly. Not a question.

"Some of them. Tomas cut most. We split the alphabet like brothers splitting a pie — he took vowels and the tall letters, I took the round ones and the descenders." I turned a *g* in my fingers, my own work, twenty years younger than my eyes. "You can tell ours apart. He cut angry. Look at the terminals — he bore down hard when he was furious, and the graver bites deeper."

"And what were you feeling when you cut this one?"

I considered the little *g*, round and mild, its tail curled like a sleeping dog.

"Satisfied," I said. "That was the problem, Dom. I was satisfied. A man with a shop, a daughter, a trade, a city that mostly leaves him alone if he leaves it alone. Tomas had talent and nothing to lose, so he lost it all on principle. I had everything to lose, so I counted it every night like a miser and called the counting prudence." I set the *g* down in its compartment, in its exact place, because a man should return his own sins neatly to their drawer. "When he came to me with what he'd found in the governor's records — when he stood right there, where you're standing, with the proof in his hands and his eyes burning like a boy who's found God — I told him to take the river road north and not to write."

"And he went."

"He went. Because he was the better man, and better men obey worse ones when the worse ones hold the purse strings." I closed the false panel. It seated with that soft sigh again, and the case became, once more, an innocent piece of furniture. "Seven years I've told myself that keeping the shop alive was keeping *something* alive — some ember, some possibility. And perhaps it was. But I'll tell you what it actually was, since we're being cutters tonight and cutters deal in what's real: it was fear. Fear with a trade mark and a steady income."

Dominic was quiet for a long time. The lamp burned. Outside, the river ice groaned — it had begun doing that this week, a low complaining sound like the whole winter clearing its throat, and everyone in the city pretended not to hear it because hearing it meant admitting the thaw was coming, and the thaw meant the checkpoints opening, and movement, and change, and all the things this empire punishes.

"Father," she said at last. "The letters. The plan Ilan described. You're thinking of sending me."

"I'm thinking of a great many things. Most of them are variations on locking you in the cellar."

"But the one you keep coming back to—"

"The one I keep coming back to," I admitted, "is that you are the best cutter in this shop, which means you are the best cutter on this street, which means — God forgive me — you may be the only person in this city who can do what needs doing without getting caught. And I hate that thought the way I hate damp cold: continuously, and without cure."

She absorbed that. Then she picked up the pi-font *e* again — Tomas's *e*, angry and open-mouthed — and held it up to the lamp between us.

"Then teach me properly," she said. "Not the way you taught the apprentices. Not the way you taught me bookkeeping and manners. Teach me the way you taught him. All of it. Even the parts you're ashamed of."

"Especially those," I said. "Those are the load-bearing parts."

And so we began again, and this time I held nothing back — the dead-drop conventions disguised as delivery schedules, the cipher of the classified advertisements, the way to fold a document so it hides in the spine of a bound book, the signal marks compositors leave for each other in the margins of galleys, a language older than either of us and known, we prayed, to fewer and fewer living men. She learned fast and asked better questions than Tomas had, though I'd have bitten my tongue off before telling her that. Once, near dawn, she set a line in the pi font without consulting the key, purely from memory, and read it back to me perfectly, and I had to turn to the type rack and pretend to be checking distribution because my eyes had gone unreliable in a way that had nothing to do with my age.

It was full light when we finished — grey river light, the kind that makes even a print shop look penitent. Dominic washed the ink from her hands at the stone sink, and I watched her, and I thought: *there. Whatever happens now, that is done, and done well. She carries the trade now, the true trade, the one underneath the trade.*

She dried her hands and hung the cloth, and was halfway to the door when the knock came.

Three knocks. Measured. Official. The kind of knock that has paperwork behind it.

Through the frosted glass of the street door I could make out a figure in garrison grey, standing very straight, and my stomach performed a maneuver I hadn't felt since the year they searched Kessler's bindery.

"Dominic," I said quietly, sliding the lamp into shadow, "the pi case. Second shelf, under the ruined quires. Now."

She moved without a word. And Captain Reiner knocked a fourth time, patient as frost.
