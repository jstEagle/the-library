## 7. The Widow Veyra

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that grief teaches you, and Marta Veyra had always been better at it than I was.

She lived three streets east of the tanneries, in a house that smelled of lye and rendered fat no matter the season, because the tanneries owned everything downwind of themselves including, apparently, the air inside other people's homes. I had been there twice before in my life. Once as a child, when Tomas still worked for my father and the two households traded dinners like families do — bread for pickles, gossip for gossip. And once, three years ago, when the news came that his boat had gone over at the Sorrel Bend and his body had never surfaced. I had carried a covered dish that day. Cabbage. It was a terrible choice; everyone knew Marta hated cabbage. But my father's hands were shaking too badly to cook, and mine were too young to know what you bring a woman whose husband has drowned without leaving her so much as a boot to bury.

I knew better now. You bring nothing. Anything you carry becomes an object she has to decide what to do with, and every object is a small argument about whether the dead man is coming back to claim it.

So I came empty-handed, which in Marta's ledger was its own entry, and not a flattering one.

* * *

The walk took me past the customs house, which I made a point of doing — Ilan's route ran through it eventually, and a courier who avoids looking at a thing is a courier who has already told on herself. So I strolled by with the unhurried insolence of a woman out buying thread, and I counted what there was to count: four guards at the river door instead of three, a new iron grate over the manifest window, and Clerk Dossin at his post behind the glass, chewing, always chewing, like a man paid by the jaw.

Then I let my feet take me where they had been wanting to go since the night Ilan fell through our loading door trailing blood and sealed letters.

Here is the trouble with secrets: they are heavy in inverse proportion to how many people may carry them. A secret shared by a whole city weighs nothing. A secret held by one girl in a print shop presses down on her like a type case left open on her chest, every letter a small cold weight spelling out something she cannot say.

Tomas Veyra was alive. I had held his handwriting in my hands. I knew the way he crossed his t's like a man signing a confession, the way he looped his y's into little gallows. Dead men do not write letters. Dead men do not apologize for the trouble they cause or ask after the health of their old master in a hand that shook only where the words got difficult.

And his widow swept her step each morning believing he was at the bottom of the Sorrel Bend.

* * *

Marta opened the door before I finished knocking. She had always done that — heard you on the stairs, decided about you while you were still deciding about yourself.

"Dominic Hartwell." Not a greeting. An indictment filed under the correct name.

"Marta." I made myself meet her eyes. They were brown and level and had not softened one degree in three years. "May I come in?"

"You may stand on the step. It's swept."

It was. It always was. I sometimes thought Marta swept her step the way my father set type — as an act of defiance against entropy, against everything that wanted to come apart.

"I won't keep you long."

"No," she said. "You never did."

That was true and unfair in equal measure, which is the recipe for most true things said between grieving women. After the drowning, we had come once more — Father and I, with money this time, folded into an envelope because Marta would have thrown coins back through the window. She had refused the envelope too. She had stood in her doorway with her arms crossed and told my father that the Hartwells had taken enough from her without adding charity to the account.

Taken enough. I had spent three years reading that sentence in every tone available. Taken his apprenticeship when he left? Taken his good name when the story went round that he'd been drinking, that he'd overloaded the boat, that a man who drowns at the Sorrel Bend alone at night was probably running from debt or worse? The gossips had needed only a week to make Tomas's death a moral lesson, and my father — my careful, silent, complicit father — had said nothing in his defense. Had let the story settle over the shop like river fog. Safer that way. Silence was safer. Silence was always safer, until it wasn't, until it curdled into something you had to smuggle past checkpoints sewn into a shroud—

But I'm ahead of myself. That night I knew only fragments. What I knew was this: letters in a dead man's hand, a stranger bleeding on our floor, my father gone white as fresh paper. What I did not yet know filled a ledger. I was working up the courage to ask.

"Your father sent you?" Marta asked. "Or did you come on your own account?"

"On my own."

"Hm." She considered me the way a proofreader considers a page she already knows contains an error and is simply waiting to see how bad it is. "You've got ink on your wrist."

I looked. Of course I had ink on my wrist. I had ink on my wrists the way other women had freckles. "Occupational hazard."

"So was the river, they say. For your father's apprentices." She stepped back from the door — not an invitation, exactly, more a decision that arguing on her own doorstep was beneath her. "Come in, then. Stand on the mat. Don't touch anything."

The house was exactly as I remembered it and entirely different. Same table, same chairs, same crooked shelf of Tomas's old books that Marta had never sold even in the lean winters. But everything had the look of a room maintained rather than lived in — dust permitted nowhere, comfort invited nowhere. A shrine without candles. Or a museum of a woman who had stopped expecting tomorrow to contain anything new.

"Sit," she said, and did not sit herself.

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." She folded her arms. "Say what you came to say."

And there it was, the whole problem of my visit laid out flat on her clean table: what *had* I come to say?

I want to be honest here, since honesty is supposedly what this chapter of my life was teaching me, though God knows the tuition was steep. I had told myself I was going to Marta to find out things. Whether Tomas had said anything strange before he left. Whether he'd kept papers, letters, a second set of books — anything that might corroborate Ilan's claims or explain why my father had aged ten years in one night. That was the sensible mission, the courier's mission. Reconnaissance dressed as condolence.

But underneath it, in the cellar of myself where I keep the things I don't inspect often, I think I went because the secret was crushing me and Marta was the one person alive with a right to share its weight. I think some traitorous part of me believed that if I could just put the truth down in front of her — *he's alive, Marta, he's alive and writing to us* — then it would stop being a stone in my chest and become, instead, a gift.

That is the lie people tell themselves about truth-telling. That it's generosity. Sometimes it is only offloading cargo onto someone who hasn't agreed to receive it, and calling the splash a blessing.

* * *

"Tomas," I said.

Her face did something complicated and fast, like ice shifting on the river — cracks radiating out from nothing visible, the surface holding, mostly. "Don't."

"He was your husband—"

"Don't." Louder. A dog started up somewhere down toward the tanneries. "Three years, Dominic Hartwell. Three years you people leave me alone, and now you stand in my house saying his name. Why? What does the shop want with me now?"

"Nothing. The shop doesn't want anything."

"The shop always wants something." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Your father taught half this city to read and the other half to be afraid of what they read. He wants something. Everyone from that shop wants something. So ask."

I asked. Not the question I'd rehearsed — the safe one, the one about old papers — but the one underneath it, the one that had been sitting on my tongue since the night of the stranger.

"Do you know why he left?"

Her arms uncrossed slowly. "He drowned."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I have." She turned away from me, busied her hands with a kettle that didn't need moving, and spoke to the stove. "He came home on a Tuesday. He was quiet — quieter than usual, and Tomas was never loud, but this was a different quiet. Like the house after a storm. He kissed me and he said he'd be back before the ice broke and he took the boat out and that was the end of it. The end. No note. No reason. Your father—" her voice caught on something sharp and swallowed it, "—your father came to me afterward with his hat in his hands and told me Tomas had been troubled lately, restless, talking about going south. As if that explained it. As if a man leaves his wife over *restlessness*."

"Maybe he did talk about going south."

"He talked about lots of things." Now she turned around, and her eyes were wet and furious, which is the worst combination, because it means the anger is doing labor it shouldn't have to. "He talked about type faces and river levels and whether the moon affected the catch. If he'd talked about leaving, I'd have known. I knew him, girl. Whatever drove him out onto that water, it wasn't wanderlust, and whatever your father knows about it, he's kept shut tighter than his strongbox for seven years, and I have sat in this house every single day knowing that."

The kettle ticked as it warmed. Outside, the dog gave up. Somewhere a cart went by, iron-shod wheels on frozen cobbles, the sound this city made instead of birdsong in winter.

Seven years. Not three. She thought the silence began with the drowning, but the drowning was only when it became unbearable — the silence itself was seven years old, older than her widowhood, older than the story about restlessness. My father had chosen silence about Tomas long before Tomas vanished. And now I knew — or suspected with the certainty of someone holding a dead man's handwriting — what the silence had been about: whatever Tomas found, whatever made him flee across the river with forged ledgers burning holes in his coat, my father had known first. Had helped, maybe. Had watched him go and locked the door behind him and called it protecting the shop.

And Marta had spent seven years sweeping a clean step for a man who wasn't coming home, hating the wrong death.

"Marta," I said carefully. "If there were things — things about that Tuesday, things about why he went — that couldn't be told yet. Things where telling them would put people in danger. Would you want to know anyway? Or would you want to wait until telling was safe?"

She looked at me for a long time. The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

"You know something," she said.

"I know some things."

"You know something about Tomas." Not a question anymore. Her voice had gone very quiet, the way the river goes quiet just before the ice starts to groan. "You walked in here with it under your tongue. I saw it the moment you opened your mouth."

"I can't—"

"*Can't.*" She laughed, one dry syllable with no humor in it anywhere. "Do you know what can't means in this house? Can't means the same thing it meant when your father stood on this step with his hat in his hands. Can't means *won't*, wrapped in tissue paper. Seven years of can't, Hartwell. His can't, and now yours. It's hereditary, apparently, like the ink."

"I came because I thought—"

"I know what you came for. You came to see if I'd break." She advanced a step, and I will swear to my grave the temperature of the room dropped. "You came to look at the widow and measure how much she could bear, the way your father measures everything, the way you're being trained to measure everything — what can be printed, what must be buried, who can be told, who must be lied to. Well, measure me, then. Look at me. Three years I've mourned a man who is apparently not dead, because the daughter stands in my kitchen with a secret shaped exactly like his name, and do you know what I'll do with that knowledge, Dominic? Nothing. Nothing, because I'm strong enough to hold whatever you're too much a coward to give me, and strong enough to wait you all out. That's your measurement. Take it back to the shop."

Every word landed. I stood there and let them, because dodging them would have cost more than absorbing them, and because — this is the shameful part — some cold practical corner of my mind was taking notes even as the rest of me bled. *She suspects he's alive. She'll say nothing. She'll wait. She is dangerous but disciplined.* Even gutted, I was cataloguing. My father's training runs deeper than ink.

"I'm sorry," I said. And I was. I am. Sorry is such a small coin for such large debts.

"Sorry." She examined the word and found it counterfeit. "Go home, Dominic. Tell your father the widow Veyra sends her regards. Tell him—" and here her voice finally broke, cleanly, like type snapping under the press, "—tell him I still set two places at midwinter. Out of habit, I tell myself. Out of habit."

I left. On the step, I paused, and every honest bone in my body voted to turn around and say it — *he's alive, he wrote to us, there's a ledger, there are names* — and every prudent bone voted otherwise, and I am sorry to report that prudence has always had the better organization.

I walked back toward the river with my empty hands and my full chest, past the customs house, past Dossin still chewing, past everything that couldn't be told yet.

That was the first lie I ever chose deliberately. Not a lie of words — I hadn't spoken one — but a lie of walked-away steps and unsaid names, and it weighed more than any false proclamation I ever set in type, because those I could blame on the censor's office. This one had my fingerprints all over it.

At the shop, my father looked up from his bench, read my face the way he read proofs, and asked nothing.

"We'll need to tell her soon," I said. "Before the ice breaks. Whatever else happens — she gets told."

Aldous Hartwell nodded slowly, and took off his spectacles, and rubbed the bridge of his nose the way he did when a line of type wouldn't justify. "Soon," he agreed. And then, quieter, almost to himself, in the voice of a man rereading an old error he'd hoped was fixed: "Though soon has a way of turning into never, in this trade."

Behind him, on his desk, lay one of Ilan's sealed letters — unbroken wax, a dead man's looping gallows-y's — and beside it, where he surely knew I'd see it, a fresh travel pass, blank except for the signature, awaiting a name.
