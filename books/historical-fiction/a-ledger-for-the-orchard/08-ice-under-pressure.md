## 8. Ice Under Pressure

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a courier learns, and it has nothing to do with miles.

You count doors instead. Exits. The number of steps from the bed to the window in every room you've ever slept in. The weight of the man behind you on a dark street — his gait, whether his boots are army-issue, whether he's drunk or hunting. I had been doing this arithmetic for six years now, since Veyra handed me my first packet and told me that a courier who trusts anyone is already dead, merely awaiting delivery.

So when Aldous Hartwell poured me tea on the third morning of my convalescence and asked, with the gentlest inflection, how my leg was mending, I performed the usual calculations. One door to the street, bolted. One to the loading yard, barred. A cellar I hadn't seen but could hear — the shop above had a trapdoor somewhere; you could tell by the way the floorboards sang differently over one corner of the kitchen. Two exits, then, if you counted the one they were hiding.

And two people between me and them.

The old man moved like a man who had spent seven years teaching himself not to be seen. That was the first thing wrong with him. Printers are loud creatures by trade — the clatter of the press gets into the bones, into the voice, into the way a man sets down a cup. Hartwell set down his cup without a sound. He'd learned quiet the way soldiers learn to sleep sitting up: because staying alive required it.

The daughter was worse. Or better, depending on which side of her you intended to end up on.

Dominic moved through that shop like she owned it and was ashamed of owning it — wiping ink from surfaces that were already clean, straightening galleys that didn't need straightening, always within arm's reach of some implement that could crack a skull or start a fire. When she looked at me it was never longer than a glance, and never less than a full accounting. I knew what she was doing because I did it too. We were both counting.

"You'll want to keep the bandage dry," Aldous said, gathering my bowl. "River water's bad for wounds. Full of everything the city wants to forget."

"That why you live here?" I said. "Away from the forgetting?"

It was a probe, and a clumsy one, and I watched him take its measure without letting anything show. "I live where my press is," he said. "Everything else is geography."

He left me alone with the stove and the ache in my leg, and I lay there doing what I do, which is suspecting everyone, methodically, the way other men pray.

Because here was my problem, laid out as neat as type: someone in the network had talked. Three weeks ago, a safe house in Kessel Row went quiet — not raided, just *quiet*, the kind of quiet that means everyone left in a hurry or didn't leave at all. Then old Brenner stopped answering drops. Then a message I sent came back to me with one word changed — a single transposed letter, small enough that a tired reader would miss it, deliberate enough that no honest mistake makes.

Someone was reading our mail. And the only thread that still ran unbroken from the burned end of the network to Tomas Veyra's letters ran through this shop, through this limping old printer and his silent daughter, through me and my ruined leg propped up on their kitchen bench like a delivered parcel.

Tomas trusted them. Tomas, who had smelled the forgery in the governor's ledger from across a room, who had fled rather than sign his name to a lie — that man had trusted the Hartwells with his life's evidence and his life's handwriting. But trust, I have learned, is a currency, and men have been bought cheaper. And grief, I have also learned, makes excellent cover. If you wanted to plant an informer inside a resistance cell, you could do no better than install a father and daughter who had genuinely loved the dead man.

I turned all this over while the stove ticked and the shop overhead murmured with the small sounds of work. And beneath the suspicion, unwelcome as a splinter, sat another thought: that these two had taken me in off the ice with a wound and a packet of treasonous correspondence, asked nothing, reported nothing, and fed me soup that was mostly water out of a larder that clearly couldn't afford generosity.

Suspicion is easy. It's the cheapest virtue there is. What cost something was the growing suspicion that I might be wrong about them, and that being wrong about them in the direction of trust was exactly how couriers ended up in the river.

* * *

I tested Dominic on the fourth day.

Not her — that's not accurate, though it's what I told myself. I tested the situation. The distinction matters to men like me only after everything has gone wrong.

She came down at midday to change my bandage, moving with that economy of hers, scissors and linen laid out in advance like instruments before surgery. She worked in silence, which I had learned was her native tongue, and I broke it deliberately, the way you break ice to see how thick it is.

"I leave tomorrow night," I said. "Or the night after. The leg's decided for me — it won't hold a long walk, so I'll go by water."

Her hands didn't stop. Good. Most people's hands stop.

"Downriver," she said. Not a question.

"To Velmora." I let the name sit there. Then, watching her profile the way a card player watches a face: "There's a woman I'm meant to meet before I go. A contact. She keeps the toll records at the customs house — a widow, gray shawl, chipped front tooth. I'm to give her a token and she'll arrange passage past the first checkpoint."

All of it invented. Every word. There was no widow, no token, no arrangement — the real route existed only in my head and in a letter sewn into my coat lining. I had built the fiction that morning out of plausible parts, and now I laid it out before her like bait before a fish whose reactions I needed to read.

Here is what I was looking for: recognition. If the Hartwells were the leak — if word of the network's methods had passed through this house — then the shape of my lie might snag on something true in her memory. A flicker. A question asked too precisely. Even silence of the wrong texture.

What I got instead was worse than recognition, and better, and much harder to read.

She tied off the bandage, smoothed the knot flat with two fingers — a careful person's gesture, a craftswoman's gesture — and said, "The customs house doesn't keep widows on the toll rolls. My father ships consignments through twice a year. The clerks are all men, and the senior one counts everything twice because he was cheated once and has never recovered."

Not recognition. *Correction.* She had caught my lie not because she knew the truth of the network but because she knew the truth of the building — the dull, granular, irreplaceable knowledge of a local, and I had failed to salt my fiction with it.

For three full seconds neither of us moved. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs the press frame creaked as Aldous leaned his weight against it.

Then she looked at me — really looked, that level gray accounting look — and I watched her arrive, in real time, at what I had done. Her jaw tightened. Not fear. Something closer to insult, banked and controlled.

"You wanted to know if I'd heard that story before," she said quietly. "If I'd been fed it."

"The thought crossed my mind."

"And?" She folded the linen scraps into a precise square. "What did your mind conclude?"

"That either you're innocent," I said, "or you're very good."

"And which does Ilan—" she used my name like a tool, turning it over to find the flaw in it "—believe?"

"Neither," I said. "Both. I believe in checking."

Something moved through her face then, fast, like weather — anger, mostly, and under it something that might have been relief, which surprised me until I understood it. She had spent years around men who decided things about her without testing them: too young, too female, too quiet to matter. Here, at least, was one who took her seriously enough to lie to her.

"My turn, then," she said.

"I don't follow."

"You checked me. Now I check you." She stood, wound the linen square into her apron pocket, and fixed me with the same flat gaze her father used on crooked type. "The man who brought you here — the stranger at the loading door, bleeding, half-frozen. You knocked. He answered. Why did he open the door for you, Ilan? In this city, on that night, with the patrols thick as flies — why would a careful man open his door to a bleeding stranger?"

And there it was, my own trick come home, because the answer was a thing I had managed never to say aloud in this house: *because I gave him Tomas Veyra's name, and the man went pale as paper and let me in anyway.*

She didn't know that part. She'd been asleep, or pretending to be. All she knew was that her father had broken seven years of discipline for a stranger, and strangers who cause such breaks deserve scrutiny.

"He carried letters," I said carefully, "that your father recognized."

"From a dead man."

"Yes."

She held my gaze a moment longer, weighing whatever she found there, and then she nodded once — a transaction concluded, terms accepted, balance struck — and climbed the stairs without another word.

I lay back and admitted to the ceiling what I wouldn't admit to her: that she'd beaten me. Not at deception — at honesty. She had answered my lie with truth so exact it functioned as a rebuke, and then she had demanded her own accounting and gotten a half-answer, and she had known it was a half-answer, and accepted it anyway, provisionally, the way you accept a promissory note from a man whose credit you haven't verified yet.

Brittle. That was the word for it. Trust between strangers is always brittle — it doesn't bend, it holds until it doesn't, and when it fails it fails all at once, like river ice, without warning and with terrible consequences for anyone standing on it.

But brittle is not the same as false. I had spent six years learning the difference, and everything in that kitchen — the corrected customs-house detail, the controlled anger, the transactional nod — rang true. Which left the leak somewhere else in the network, which left me no safer than before, and which left, God help me, the beginnings of something that felt uncomfortably like respect.

* * *

That evening Aldous came down alone, carrying a lamp and a flat wooden case I hadn't seen before.

He set the case on the bench between us and didn't open it. His hands rested on the lid — big hands for a printer, scarred at the knuckles, steady despite the tremor that came and went with his eyesight.

"You tested her today," he said.

"She told you."

"She tells me everything that matters. Not everything — everything that *matters*." He said it without reproach, which somehow made it heavier. "I want you to understand something, courier. Whatever game you're playing with false widows and gray shawls — play it at me. Play it at the walls. She has spent seven years keeping this family alive by noticing things and saying nothing. Do not teach her that noticing gets punished."

"It wasn't punishment," I said. "It was verification."

"It was a test, and she passed it better than you did, and now you know it, and now she knows you know it." He opened the case at last.

Inside, packed in wool, lay type. Not the shop's ordinary stock — these were worn, mismatched faces, a jumble of pi characters and ornaments and letters from half a dozen defunct fonts, the kind of assortment no legitimate printer would keep sorted and no careless one would keep at all. A compositor's shadow alphabet.

"Tomas cut some of these," Aldous said. "Before he ran. The rest are older than both of us. There are messages in this case that three governors' censors never found, because they weren't looking at type — they were looking at paper, at ink, at seals. Men search for what men would use themselves." He closed the lid gently, the way you close a coffin. "The leak in your network. Whoever it is. When you find them — and you will, men like you always do — remember that they will not be the person who caught your mistake today. Liars build convincing details. Only someone telling the truth corrects you with the boring particulars."

He picked up the lamp and paused at the stairs.

"There's bread in the tin," he said. "And tomorrow, if your leg allows it, my daughter will want to talk routes. Real ones. She knows this river better than any pilot alive, and she has been waiting four days for you to stop testing her long enough to ask."

He climbed the stairs. I sat with the wooden case between my knees, feeling its weight, thinking about ice — how it looks solid right up until the moment it isn't, how the river underneath never stops moving no matter how still the surface seems.

Above me, faintly, I could hear the two of them talking. I couldn't make out the words. I didn't need to. It was the tone that mattered — low, quick, collaborative, two voices working the same problem — and it was aimed, unmistakably, at getting me and my packet downriver alive.

Which raised the last question, the one I'd been circling for days and could no longer postpone: because tomorrow we would plan the route together, and planning together meant trusting together, and trusting together meant that when the leak finally surfaced — and it would surface, leaks always do — one of us might discover we had vouched for the wrong person at the worst possible moment.

I slept badly, which was normal. What wasn't normal was waking at dawn to the sound of the loading door bolting back, and finding Dominic standing in the gray light with snow melting on her shoulders and an expression I had not seen on her before.

"We have a problem," she said. "There's a garrison officer asking after wounded strangers. Captain Reiner. He's coming here — Father saw him turn onto our street."
