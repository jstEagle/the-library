## 9. The Ledger Described

My father read the way he set type: slowly, with his lips moving, as though the words might bite him if he rushed. That night in the back room of the shop, with Ilan's wound freshly stitched by my own unsteady hands and the shutters barred twice over, Aldous held Tomas Veyra's letters at the distance his failing eyes demanded and read them aloud for the third time.

I had asked him to stop after the second reading. He hadn't. There is a thing some men do when they are frightened, which is to keep working the problem like a stubborn bolt in a frozen nut, and my father was that kind of man. I am apparently also that kind of man, though I would thank you not to say so to my face.

"The date," Aldous said, tapping the page. "Note the date. He wrote this on the fourth of October. Three years ago almost to the week."

"He waited three years to send word?"

"No." Ilan spoke from the corner where we'd propped him on a grain sack, his leg stretched out before him like something he'd borrowed from a taller man. "He sent word every year. The letters never arrived."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a patrol passed on the cobbles, boots keeping the empire's time.

"Read it again," I said. "From the part about the bridge."

Aldous adjusted the letter, squinted, and began.

---

*To Aldous Hartwell, if this finds you, and to no one else, because no one else is left who will believe me.*

*You taught me that a page cannot lie unless a hand makes it lie. I have spent two years learning how true that is, and what it costs to prove it.*

*I have seen the ledger.*

*Not the one they published — the one you and I both set type for, the official account, forty dead, regrettable excess, closed inquiry. I mean the true one. The governor's own clerk kept a parallel book, because powerful men are sentimental about their crimes; they like to look at them. It records the ninth of November, the year you know. Not forty souls. Three hundred and eleven. It lists them by village, by name where names were known, and — here is the part that will make your hands shake, old man — it lists the payments.*

---

Aldous stopped there. Not because his voice failed, but because mine did something between a breath and a laugh, the ugly involuntary kind, and he wanted to see whether I'd broken.

"I'm fine," I said, which was the second lie I told that night. "Keep reading."

*Every man who signed the forgery is entered in it,* Tomas wrote. *The customs assessor Voss received four hundred crowns for certifying the cargo manifests that moved the bodies out as 'quarantine goods.' The magistrate Kessler took six hundred for closing the inquiry. The garrison quartermaster Brandt supplied the powder and billed it as target practice — target practice, Aldous, in November, with the river already freezing. And above them all, the deputy commander of the garrison itself, Captain Reiner's own patron—*

"Reiner," I said. The name came out of me flat and cold, like a coin dropped on stone.

"You know the name," Ilan said.

"I set his proclamations twice a month. He visited our shop in the autumn. He complimented my father's loyalty while looking at me the way a man looks at a horse he intends to buy." I wrapped my arms around myself, mostly because my hands had begun doing something embarrassing without permission. "He has patron?"

"Everyone has a patron," Ilan said. "That's the whole architecture of the empire. Nobody does evil alone; they do it in committees, with receipts."

*Tomas goes on,* Aldous said quietly, resuming before I could say anything else stupid. *The ledger names fourteen officials in total. Fourteen signatures on the forgery, fourteen entries in the true book, each with sum and date and witness. And at the bottom, the final entry, dated three days after the massacre: payment authorized by the governor's office, countersigned—*

Aldous's voice stopped again. This time it wasn't courtesy.

"Countersigned by whom?" I asked.

He turned the letter toward the lamp, then toward himself, then back, as though the words were playing hide-and-seek in his failing sight. "It's smudged. Water damage." He lowered the page slowly. "Dominic, the ink is smeared exactly where the name should be. Every other line on this page is clean."

We looked at each other. Then we both looked at Ilan, who wore the expression of a man who had been waiting all evening for someone else to notice the trap laid in the middle of the floor.

"Tomas did that deliberately," Ilan said. "He's careful. He writes in code where the codes matter, and here—" He gestured with his chin at the letter. "Here he wants only one reader to know the name. The rest of us get a blank."

"And who is the one reader?" I asked, though I already knew. My father's face had gone the color of the cheap newsprint we used for tax notices.

"Me," Aldous said.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about my father, if you are to understand any of this: Aldous Hartwell had spent seven years being a very good coward. Not a lazy one — cowardice of his quality requires discipline, the daily practice of small silences, the craft of knowing which proclamation contains which lie and setting it beautifully anyway. He had raised me inside that discipline. He had taught me, without ever once saying it aloud, that survival is a trade like typesetting: you learn the rules, you respect the rules, and the rules keep you breathing.

And now his dead apprentice's ghost was sitting in his shop telling him, through water-damaged paper, that the single most dangerous name in the province had been written down once, in invisible ink or lemon juice or whatever trick Tomas had learned in exile, and that the key to it lived in Aldous Hartwell's memory.

"What did Tomas leave for you," I said. Not a question. I've found that questions give people room to lie, whereas statements just hang there until they confess.

Aldous looked at the stove for a long moment. Then he got up, went to the type cases against the far wall — the old ones, the ones from before my time, stacked under canvas like coffins waiting for occupants — and pulled out the case marked PI. Discarded sorts, broken ligatures, the typographical junkyard where letters go when no sentence wants them.

He reached into the third row and came out with a slip of paper folded eight times, small as a communion wafer.

"Seven years ago," he said, "three days before Tomas ran, he handed me this and made me swear on Mother's grave that I wouldn't open it unless he was confirmed dead. Officially dead. In writing." His mouth twisted. "Three years ago they sent us the confirmation. I sat in this room with the letter in my hand and I couldn't do it. Because opening it meant he was really gone, and because—" he laughed, a terrible dry sound, "—because I was afraid. Old habits."

"You're opening it now," I said.

"I'm opening it now." He worked the folds apart with fingers that shook, and I noticed, with the peculiar clarity fear grants, that his nails were clean of ink for once, and that this somehow frightened me more than anything else in the room.

The paper held one line, in Tomas's unmistakable hand — the same hand as the letters, the same forward-slanting urgency, the *t*s crossed like fence rails:

*The countersignature is the name you printed and then burned. You know whose it is. You burned the forme yourself, the night I told you what the ledger held.*

My father sat down on the stool very carefully, the way old men sit when their legs have made an independent decision to resign.

"I don't remember," he whispered.

"Yes, you do," said Ilan from the corner, gently. "Tomas said you would say that first."

* * *

I want to be fair to my father here, and fairness requires admitting that I spent a full hour wanting to shake him until the memory fell out like a loose sort from a cracked case. But anger is a poor reader; it skips lines. So while he sat staring at nothing, I took the letters and read them myself, start to finish, aloud, because there is a particular magic in saying terrible things out loud — they become real, and real things can be fought, unlike the shapeless ones.

The third letter was the longest. In it Tomas described the true ledger physically, the way a man describes a woman he loves badly:

*A quarto volume, perhaps two hundred pages, bound in grey calfskin with the governor's cipher stamped blind on the cover — the same cipher as on his private correspondence, which is why no customs officer will ever open it; it looks like his own dispatches. The entries run chronologically. First the massacre itself: dates, units deployed, ammunition expended — Brandt's requisitions match shell for shell. Then the payments, entry by entry, cross-referenced to the forged publication, with the exact discrepancies noted in the margin in the clerk's own hand. Then the disposal: three barges, weighted, sunk above the falls — except the ice broke early that spring, and one barge surfaced, and forty-one bodies came out of it instead of the twelve the record claims, and someone paid to have that discrepancy drowned too. The last quarter of the book is the dangerous part. Names. Promotions purchased. Judges appointed. A whole provincial government, bought and receipted, going up to the countersignature.*

*I am sending it downriver before the ice breaks, because after the thaw the governor's inspectors work the water and the window closes till winter. It cannot go by road; the roads are Reiner's. It must go by boat, through the three checkpoints, hidden in plain freight, carried by someone with no history — no politics, no debts, no face the garrison knows.*

*It must be carried by someone the shop trusts absolutely, because the guild in Velmora will not act without the shop's vouching.*

*I know what I am asking. I watched you teach your daughter the trade through the window of the composing room. She has your hands.*

I stopped reading. My ears were hot. Seven years of being quiet, of being furniture, of being *the daughter who sets a beautiful page but asks nothing* — and Tomas Veyra, dead three years, had looked through a window once and seen the whole architecture of me.

"He has my hands," I repeated. "What does that even—"

"It means," my father said, and his voice had changed, come up out of wherever it had fallen, "that he thinks you're the courier."

The stove ticked. The patrol had passed. Ilan shifted on his grain sack and winced and didn't apologize for the noise.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. I'm the person who stays. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement — you stand in front, I stand behind, and nothing happens to either of us because neither of us moves." I heard my own voice climbing and hated it. "You want me to carry a seditious document past the customs house, the garrison bridge, and the governor's *private wharf*, down a river that is currently solid enough to walk on and will soon enough be solid enough to drown in, into a city where the printers' guild may or may not contain a traitor—"

"How do you know about the checkpoints?" Ilan asked.

I stopped. Cold walked up my spine on small feet.

"Because I listen," I said. "Because I set the garrison's shipping notices twice a month and I read everything I print, which is more than the officers who sign them do. Because I am not actually furniture, whatever the arrangement says."

Silence, of the loaded variety. Ilan studied me with new arithmetic happening behind his eyes, and I understood suddenly that I had just made myself either invaluable or dangerous, and that from where he sat those might be the same category.

"The guild contact," he said at last, carefully. "You're right to worry. We've lost two couriers this year. I don't yet know why." He looked at my father. "But she's right about the river too. After the thaw, the inspections double. If the ledger moves, it moves now, in the last weeks of solid ice, when everyone's traffic is slowed and the guards are bored and cold and looking at their feet."

"Bored guards make mistakes," I said.

"Cold guards make mistakes," Ilan corrected. "Bored ones make *thorough* mistakes. They invent games to pass the time, and the game is usually searching things."

* * *

We talked until the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and what we built in that candlelight was less a plan than a skeleton of one, all bone and no flesh, but bones hold a shape at least. The ledger would come downriver with the ice. It would travel as funeral freight — death is the one cargo the empire respects, having manufactured so much of it — and it would pass the three checkpoints wearing grief like a disguise.

"And if you're taken," my father said. It wasn't a question either. We had graduated past questions somewhere around midnight.

"Then the ledger dies with me," I said, "and Tomas died for nothing, and Marta buried a living man, and three hundred and eleven people stay numbers instead of names." I flexed my ink-stained fingers and looked at them. Tomas had called them *his* hands, meaning my father's, but he was wrong. They were mine. They had set ten thousand lies in beautiful alignment, and there was a symmetry to the idea that they might now carry one truth out of the city, crooked and smuggled and alive. "I've been practicing silence for seven years, Papa. I'd like to find out what I sound like."

My father looked at me for a long time across the guttering candle. Something moved in his face — pride and terror fighting for the same ground, and losing jointly to exhaustion.

"There's one thing you haven't asked," he said finally.

"There's a great many things I haven't asked. I've been rationing."

"The name. On the countersignature." He held up the little folded paper, Tomas's seven-year-old secret, and his hand was steady now, which frightened me all over again. "If we're doing this — if you're going to risk your life carrying that ledger — then you deserve to know whose name is at the bottom of every page of it. Whose name I printed and burned."

"Papa—"

"Aldous Hartwell," he said, "does not appear anywhere in the forgery. Do you understand? I checked, years ago, when Tomas first told me. I combed every line of that proclamation looking for my own ruin, and it isn't there. Which means whoever countersigned the massacre payments chose to keep the shop's name out of it." He swallowed. "Someone in that chain of fourteen protected us, Dominic. Someone signed away three hundred and eleven lives and then reached down the list and struck one printer's name free. And I have spent seven years not letting myself wonder *why*, because the answer to why is always the same in this city."

Outside, the river groaned — a long, deep sound, ice shifting against ice, the sound of a thing under pressure deciding whether to hold or break. All three of us went still and listened to it, and none of us said what we were thinking, which was that the river and the city and the three of us were all in the same condition.

It was near dawn when the knock came.

Three measured raps at the loading door, spaced like a military tattoo, followed by a voice I knew from a hundred proclamations — smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who has never once needed to raise it:

"Hartwell. Open the door. Captain Reiner, garrison gendarmerie. Routine business."

At four in the morning, there is no routine business.

My father blew out the candle. In the dark, I felt him press the folded scrap of paper into my hand, close my fingers around it, and whisper, so quietly it barely counted as sound:

"*Burn it, and remember you never read it.*"

Then he went to answer the door, straightening his coat as he went, arranging his face into seven years of practiced loyalty — while behind him, in the dark, I stood holding the most dangerous blank space in the province, listening to the bolts slide back one by one.

Routine business had arrived.
