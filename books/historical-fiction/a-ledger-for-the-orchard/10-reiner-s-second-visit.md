## 10. Reiner's Second Visit

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which told me everything before I opened the door.

Official men do not visit print shops in the evening, when honest work is done and the lamps are lit and a captain might be offered a chair and a glass of something warming. Official men come in the morning, when the shop smells of ink and sleeplessness, when your hands are already stained black past the wrist, when you have been caught — as we had been caught every dawn for seven years — in the act of setting type. It is much harder to look innocent at your own bench than it is behind your own door.

I knew the knock too. Three raps, evenly spaced, patient. A man who knocks like that has never once in his life wondered whether he would be admitted.

I wiped my hands on my apron out of habit, though of course it made no difference; the ink had become part of my skin years ago, worked into the whorls of my fingers like a second set of prints, and then I opened the loading door to the grey morning and Captain Reiner standing in it with two soldiers and a folded paper he held the way other men hold flowers.

"Fräulein Hartwell." He smiled. He had good teeth and used them often, the way some men use a knife they are proud of sharpening. "You look well."

"I look tired," I said. "It's morning."

"It is indeed." He stepped inside without being asked, boots ringing on the flagstones, his men fanning out along the walls with the bored competence of men who had done this many times and expected nothing interesting and hoped, privately, for something otherwise. "I've brought you something. Consider it an honor, really. Most shops in this city will never be searched at all."

"How generous," I said. "Shall I put out saucers?"

He laughed — a real laugh, I'll give him that, warm as a stove — and handed me the paper. The warrant was written in the governor's own office hand, that slanted, self-satisfied script I had set into proclamations often enough to recognize it from across a room. *Seditious type. Unauthorized plates. Materials prejudicial to public order.* The phrases were so familiar I could have composed them blindfolded, which was precisely the point. We printed them. The empire wrote its accusations in our alphabet.

"My father is upstairs," I said. "His eyes are bad. He'll want me to read it to him."

"Of course." Reiner looked around the shop with the unhurried appetite of a man in a gallery. "A remarkable place. You know, I've always admired printing. There's something noble in it. Order out of chaos. Every letter in its case, every word where it belongs." He picked up a composing stick from the bench and turned it over, weighing it. "If only everyone were as orderly."

* * *

My father came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and I watched him arrange his face on the way — not fear, exactly, but a mild, dignified vagueness, the expression of an old craftsman whose eyes had failed before his loyalty ever could. He had worn that face for seven years and it fit him like his own coat.

"Captain," he said. "An honor."

"Herr Hartwell." Reiner took my father's offered hand and held it a moment longer than courtesy required. "Your reputation precedes you. Finest compositor in the city, they say. Even the governor says so."

"The governor is kind."

"The governor is thorough," Reiner said pleasantly. "That's why I'm here. There's a great deal of unauthorized printing moving through this city lately, Herr Hartwell. Pamphlets. Lists. Things with names in them that ought not to be named." He let that sit in the cold air of the shop. "You'll want to help us find where such things are made."

"We print what the censor approves," my father said. "Nothing else. You know our work, Captain. You've complimented it."

"I have. And I meant it." Reiner turned to his men. "Begin with the type cases."

And so they began, and I learned that morning what a search truly is: not violence, but time. They went through the cases drawer by drawer, letter by letter in some places, holding sorts up to the light as if sedition might be written on the face of a lowercase g. One of them ran his thumb along the racks of pi type, the broken sorts and ornaments we kept in a battered case by the window, and I felt my heartbeat change address, moving up into my throat.

Because there it all was. Not hidden — hidden things invite searching — but disguised. The compartment my father had built into the floor beneath the stone slab where we mixed ink, sealed so perfectly that I myself had forgotten its edges until the night Ilan arrived. And in the pi case, mixed among the genuine broken sorts, the letters of the message we'd been keeping for Velmora, set in a cipher only a cutter would see. If Reiner had been a printer, we'd have been dead by breakfast. But Reiner was a soldier, and soldiers read faces, not fonts.

So I gave him a face.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I record it now because afterward I could hardly believe it myself. While his men rattled the cases and my father stood with his hands folded like a man waiting out weather, I walked — walked, mind you, with the unhurried stride of a woman who has nothing on earth to conceal — to the shelf above the finishing bench, and I took down the box of my own engravings.

They were mine. Seven years of them, cut in secret, in the hours after closing: woodblocks I had carved with images no censor would ever pass. The customs house with its windows like closed eyes. The bridge at dusk with soldiers' silhouettes. My mother's face, once, rendered in lines so fine they were almost prayer. I had never printed more than proofs of them. They lived in that box the way some people keep letters from the dead — taken out, touched, put away. Proof that somewhere under the apron and the silence there was still a person who saw.

I carried the box to the workbench, in full view, and I began — deliberately, audibly — to pry up the loose board beneath it, where I kept a smaller cache of things I could not explain: three unregistered blocks, caricatures, one of them unmistakably the governor himself drawn with the body of a fat spider.

"What are you doing?" Reiner asked.

I looked up at him with what I can only describe as the face of a cornered girl, and I let my hands shake. Just enough. A tremor, not a palsy. "Captain," I said, "if you're going to find them anyway —"

He crossed the room in four steps and lifted the board himself.

The spider-governor stared up at him from the block. So did the caricature of Captain Reiner, which I confess I had cut the previous autumn after his first visit, giving him the neck of a heron and the eyes of a fish. Beside them, a satirical broadside proof, months old, mocking the new press restrictions.

There was a silence in the shop long enough to grow roots.

Reiner picked up the block with his own face on it and studied it with an expression I will be studying for the rest of my life. His men had gone still. My father had gone very carefully blank, the way a man goes when he understands that his daughter has just thrown herself onto the wire in front of him.

"These," Reiner said at last, "are yours?"

"They're nothing," I said. "Foolishness. I cut them at night. I never printed them — you can see, there's no edition, no distribution, the paper is proof stock from our own shelves—"

"You drew the governor as a spider."

"He's difficult to flatter," I said, and immediately wished to die, and saw — God help me — the corner of Reiner's mouth move.

He set the blocks down on the bench, one by one, arranging them neatly, the spider, the heron, the broadside. "Do you know what the penalty is for caricature of imperial officials?"

"I can read the statutes," I said. "We print them."

Another silence. Then he laughed again, that warm stove of a laugh, and shook his head almost fondly. "Look at her hands," he said to his men. "Ink to the elbow. This one doesn't make pamphlets, gentlemen, she makes art she hasn't the courage to show anyone." He tapped the spider block. "This is a girl's private spite, not sedition. Sedition has readers."

He confiscated the blocks anyway — of course he did; a man does not walk away empty-handed from a morning's work — and wrote a receipt for them in beautiful bureaucratic script, and informed me that the matter would be noted but that, given my youth, my father's loyal record, and the evident absence of any *distribution*, formal charges would be spared. "But Fräulein Hartwell." He leaned close, and his voice dropped into something without warmth in it at all. "Artists who graduate from carving to printing rarely survive the apprenticeship. Keep your knife on wood, and only wood."

Then, because the morning had produced nothing else, he ordered the search finished anyway, and it was finished, and the compartment under the inkstone slab slept on beneath a soldier's boot heel while he stood on it discussing the price of paper with my father.

* * *

They left at noon. The door closed. The boots faded down the frozen lane. And my father and I stood in the wreckage of the shop — drawers half pulled, type scattered, the loose board gaping where my spiders had lived — and neither of us spoke for a full minute.

Then he said, "The engravings," and his voice broke in the middle of the word.

"They were going to find something," I said. "Better they found mine."

"Dominic — those were yours. Seven years of —"

"Yes." I busied myself straightening the pi case, because if I stopped moving I would cry, and crying takes time we didn't have. "And now they're evidence in a captain's desk drawer, and he thinks he knows exactly what I am: a frightened girl who draws pictures she's ashamed of." I slid the last sort home. "Let him think it. A man who thinks he's seen your secrets stops looking for them."

My father looked at me for a long moment — with his ruined eyes, which somehow saw more clearly just then than they had in years. "Your mother," he said quietly, "used to say you'd end up either hanged or famous."

"She didn't mention the third option," I said. "Running the shop alone."

I was still putting the cases to rights when I heard the footsteps come back up the lane — quick ones, uneven, the runner's lurching tread I had memorized in a single frozen night — and Ilan slipped through the loading door white-faced, snow in his hair, and said the words I would carry downriver with me like ballast:

"They've taken two couriers at the guild hall in Velmora. The contact there — the one we were to meet — he's the informer. The whole route is burned, Dominic. Every safe house between here and the river."

Outside, the ice on the river groaned like something waking.
