## 11. Betrayal in the Guild Hall

There is a particular smell to a printers' guild at night — lamp oil and lead dust and the sour ghost of old ink — and I have always found it comforting. That should have been my first warning. Comfort is a luxury, and luxuries in this city are bait.

I arrived early. I always arrive early; arriving late is how couriers die, because a man hurrying through the dark stops thinking and starts obeying his feet. So there I was, twenty minutes before the appointed hour, standing across the lane from the Velmoran Guild Hall — no. Forgive me. The guild hall in our own city, the one on Tanner's Row, with its crooked lintel and its windows fogged yellow like old teeth. The Velmora guild was the destination. This was only the last handshake before the journey, the place where Sera and Dov were to receive their final instructions from Master Corvin himself, the guild's second compositor, our man inside the trade for eleven years.

Eleven years. I want you to sit with that number the way I have had to sit with it every night since. Eleven years of Corvin passing messages in misprinted almanacs, of corrections that weren't corrections, of errata lines that said more than any pamphlet ever dared. When Aldous Hartwell named him as the anchor point of the whole plan, I had not questioned it. Why would I? You do not question bedrock. You build on it, and you forget that even bedrock can be undercut by water.

The snow had stopped an hour before, which meant the streets were loud with that particular hush snow makes, the kind that carries footsteps too far and muffles everything else. Sera and Dov were already inside — they'd gone in through the delivery yard with the ledger's duplicate casing, a dummy consignment of hymnals, God help us all, because nothing says *nothing to see here* quite like psalms. I was to give Corvin the final signal, confirm the ice reports, and then take the long way home and let the professionals do the professional part.

I remember checking my watch. Nine minutes to the hour. A cat crossed the lane with the insolent confidence of a creature who has never once been arrested. I remember envying it.

Then the door of the guild hall opened, and light spilled out, and I saw the silhouette of a man in a garrison greatcoat, and my body understood what had happened a full three seconds before my mind consented to.

* * *

Three seconds is either nothing or everything, depending on whether you've spent them well. I spent mine stepping backward into the recessed doorway of the cooperage next door, where the shadows were deep enough to drown in, and pulling my collar up, and becoming, insofar as a man can become, furniture.

Two soldiers came out, flanking a third man between them. Not Corvin. One of ours — I couldn't see his face, but I knew the roll of his shoulders, the way he walked like a man whose hands were bound behind him and who had decided not to make it look easy. That would be Dov. Dov walked everywhere like that.

Behind them came Sera, escorted, her chin up, her breath steaming in little furious clouds. She looked around the street as she passed — not for rescue, don't be romantic — but for me. Checking whether I'd been taken too. And when her eyes swept over the doorway where I stood, she did something that I will carry to whatever grave is eventually dug for me: she looked directly at me and did not blink. Did not slow. Did not so much as tilt her head. Just that flat, level stare, one heartbeat long, that said as clearly as any message set in pi type: *you are not here, I never saw you, now be worth it.*

Then they turned the corner toward the garrison, and the street was empty except for me and the cat, who had watched the entire proceeding with what I can only describe as professional detachment.

I stood in that doorway for the better part of an hour, doing arithmetic. Two couriers taken. The ledger itself was not with them — we had split it three ways weeks ago precisely against this eventuality, and what Sera carried was the decoy casing, hymnals and hollow hope. But the *plan* was with them. The names. The checkpoints. The customs house clerk we'd bought, the bridge schedule, the safe houses downriver. All of it lived in two heads that were, at this moment, being introduced to Captain Reiner's lamplight.

And Corvin. Corvin who had arranged this meeting. Corvin who had insisted the couriers come through the guild hall rather than bypassing it, because "the guild is stone, boy, and stone doesn't talk." Stone doesn't talk. Stone gets talked *to*, apparently, or stone gets replaced by something softer and more cooperative.

Here is the ugly truth about betrayal, which no ballad ever gets right: you almost never find out by catching the traitor in the act. You find out afterward, by replaying everything, and discovering that the traitor has been in every important room you ever entered, holding the door.

* * *

I went to ground in the tannery district, in a room above a chandler's shop that smelled so violently of tallow that no garrison dog could ever pick anything else out of the air. I lit no lamp. I sat on the floor with my back against the wall and made lists.

List one: what do they know?

Sera knew the customs house plan. Dov knew the bridge timings. Between them, if Reiner was thorough — and Reiner was thorough the way frost is thorough, getting into every joint — he now possessed the skeleton of the entire operation. He did not possess the ledger. He did not possess Tomas's letters, which had never left the print shop, sewn into the lining of things I will not describe here because some habits of secrecy outlive their usefulness. But he possessed enough to burn the river route from end to end.

List two: how did it happen?

Not Corvin. I kept circling back to it. Corvin was the contact, yes, but Corvin had been our man since before I could shave, and men don't survive eleven years inside a guild under imperial watch by cracking now. Something else had happened. Someone else had been squeezed. Which meant the rot might go deeper than one guild hall, and every name I trusted was suddenly a name I had to re-weigh, like coins bitten to test for lead.

And underneath both lists, in letters I refused to write but could not stop reading, a third list: *who pays for this?* Because Sera had a sister. Dov had a wife who thought he dealt in hymnals. And tomorrow morning, when the garrison paraded its catch or didn't, when the questions started in rooms without clocks, those two people were going to learn exactly what a courier's silence costs, and whether they could afford it.

I am not a sentimental man. Sentiment in this work is a leak you drown through. But I sat in the dark above the chandler's shop and I will confess that for a while I allowed myself the luxury of grief, rationed strictly, like everything else in this city.

* * *

Near midnight, I moved. Staying anywhere past the first sleepless hours is how you wake to boots on the stairs. I crossed the rooftops south — slowly, because the snow on the slates was old and crusted and announced you like a town crier — and made my way toward the river to see the damage with my own eyes.

What I saw confirmed the arithmetic. The customs house was lit like a festival. Soldiers on the quay, more than the night shift had any reason to require, and a clerk — our clerk, the one we'd bought with three years of careful favors — standing at a table under a lantern, going through manifests with the exaggerated diligence of a man performing innocence for an audience. Every barge being boarded. Every crate being opened. Every widow's shroud, I would have wagered my remaining teeth, being unwrapped by hands wearing white gloves.

The bridge was the same story doubled: a chain of lanterns across the span, traffic reduced to a trickle, and at the center of it, unmistakable even at distance by the way everyone stood slightly straighter when he passed, a garrison officer conducting inspections personally. I couldn't see his face at that range. I didn't need to. Reiner doesn't delegate the things that matter to him.

So. The original route: burned. Both checkpoints lit up like stage sets, waiting for a courier who would never be foolish enough to walk into them — or worse, waiting because they knew the courier *had* to be foolish enough, because the route was the route, and where else was there to go?

That was the question, wasn't it. Where else was there to go.

I crouched behind a stack of empty barrels on the tanner's quay and let myself think the forbidden thought all the way through, because a plan you refuse to finish thinking is a trap you build for your own foot.

The river was the only road. Everyone knew it, including Reiner, which is why he was strangling the public crossings. But a strangled road still has one gate nobody guards properly, because nobody is mad enough to use it: the governor's private wharf. The one stretch of water where the garrison doesn't inspect, because inspecting the governor's own traffic would require a garrison officer willing to ask His Excellency awkward questions, and careers in this city are shorter than winters.

Getting onto the governor's wharf required papers no courier could forge convincingly. It required standing in plain view of the governor's own household staff. It required walking through the enemy's front parlor carrying the single most dangerous document in the province.

It required, in short, a suicide. Or someone so thoroughly burned, so obviously hunted, that audacity became the only remaining camouflage — because Reiner would be hunting shadows in dark alleys, and no shadow walks in through the front door wearing a legitimate face.

And then, sitting among those barrels, smelling the tanneries and the frozen river and my own fear sweating through wool, I thought of Dominic Hartwell.

I thought of ink-stained fingers held steady under Captain Reiner's gaze. Of a woman who had spent seven years learning silences the way other people learn languages, and who had, in the space of a few weeks, learned to break them one at a time, in order of increasing danger. I thought of her father's hands guiding hers over a composing stick, teaching her that a page is a weapon and patience is the trigger.

She could pass as a shipping agent's clerk. She had the hands for it — literally, the ink-stained hands of the trade. She had the nerve. God forgive me, I had tested that nerve myself, with false information and watched her hesitate exactly correctly, hesitate the way a truthful person hesitates, and I had believed her, and believing her had been the last easy thing I'd done.

It would mean asking her to walk into the governor's wharf with a forged ledger sewn somewhere on her person while the garrison tore the city apart looking for exactly such a person. It would mean telling Aldous Hartwell, whose eyesight was failing and whose guilt about Tomas was a wound he'd stopped pretending wasn't there, that the price of the truth had just gone up again.

It would probably cost them everything. I had watched tonight what it cost people who merely carried the message.

But the alternative was that Tomas Veyra stayed dead on paper forever, that the massacre stayed a footnote in a forged ledger, that every name signed to that forgery kept signing, comfortable and warm, until the ice broke and spring came and nothing changed, the way nothing had changed after the last spring, and the one before that.

The cat from Tanner's Row appeared on the quay wall — or some cat; in this city they all share the same insolent soul — and looked at me with that expression of professional detachment, as if to say: *well?*

"Well," I said aloud, to the cat, to the river, to no one. "There's the wharf."

The ice groaned out in the channel, a long low sound like the world clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

I pulled my collar up and went to find the Hartwells.
