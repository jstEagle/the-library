## 12. Father and Daughter, Cutting Type

There is a particular hour of night — I have met it perhaps four thousand times in this shop — when the lamp burns low enough that every shadow on the wall looks like a decision you failed to make. My father used to say that hour was when the type spoke to you. My father said a great many comforting things that were lies, which is, I suppose, where I learned the trade.

Dominic had lit two lamps anyway. She said my eyes were done pretending.

"Your eyes are fine," I told her, because a man must defend something, even if it's only his own failing equipment.

"Your eyes are seventy years old and squinting at a ten-point body like it insulted your mother." She pulled the composing stick from my hand with the gentle brutality she reserved for me alone. "Sit. Watch. Learn something."

I sat. I watched. I learned, as I have been learning for seven years, that the child I thought I was protecting had become the craftsman protecting me.

* * *

The work itself was simple, which is to say it was the kind of simple that kills men. A duplicate title page for *The Orchard Ledger of Velmora Province, 1841* — Tomas's ledger, the true one, waiting upstairs under the floorboards wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse we'd rather not look at. The governor's men knew the official edition by heart; if Dominic was stopped, she'd need a copy that matched, page for page, down to the printer's slug at the foot. And travel passes: one widow, Marta Veyra's name would not do — no, we had settled on a fictional aunt, a Frau Hessler of no fixed address and convenient grief — traveling downriver to arrange her husband's burial before the thaw made the graves unkind.

Forgery, when you do it honestly, is just printing with your conscience turned to face the wall.

"Cut the serifs thinner here," I said, leaning over her shoulder despite myself. "Reiner's clerks have seen our work a hundred times. They know Aldous Hartwell cuts a fat serif because his hands shake and he's too proud to admit it."

"Then cut thin," she said, "and be someone else tonight."

That was the whole philosophy of the thing, laid out in six words. Be someone else tonight. I have been someone else for seven years — a man who did not write to his apprentice, who did not ask questions at the customs house, who set the governor's proclamations with the same care he set hymnals because care was the only armor the shop could afford. Tonight I would be someone else again: an accomplice. It felt, shamefully, like coming home.

She worked. I watched her hands move over the graver, and they were steady as a metronome, and I thought: *Aldous, you old coward, you spent seven years teaching her silence when she was born knowing how to speak.*

* * *

Somewhere past midnight she asked the question I had been dreading since Ilan bled on our loading door.

"Papa. Tell me about the night Tomas left."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the river groaned under its ice — a long, tectonic complaint, like the city clearing its throat before saying something unforgivable.

"There isn't much to tell," I said, which was the first lie, and she let it sit there between us, unchallenged, the way she lets everything sit until it rots or ripens. She went on cutting. The graver whispered through the lead. I lasted perhaps four minutes.

"He came to me on a Tuesday," I heard myself say. "Late. The same hour as this, near enough. He had the ledger pages in his coat — copied ones, the forgery next to the truth — and his hands were shaking so badly he could barely lay them on my bench. He said, *Master Hartwell, read this.* So I read it."

I had read it. God help me, I had read it, and I had understood it, and I had done the arithmetic that has governed my life ever since: one shop, one daughter, one dead wife buried out at Kesselhof; against them, a massacre of three hundred souls and a governor whose signature sat at the bottom of a lie like a fly in amber.

"And?" Dominic said. She didn't stop cutting. That was her mother in her — Marta-like, though I'd never say so — the ability to make a man confess simply by refusing to interrupt him.

"I told him to burn it," I said. "I told him whatever he thought he'd found, it wasn't worth what it would cost. I told him—" My voice did something embarrassing, and I let the lamp fill the pause. "I told him that a printer's first duty is to live long enough to print tomorrow."

"And he said?"

"He said, *Then whose duty is it to print the day after?*" I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing. "Seventeen years old and he handed me my own trade back like a coin I'd dropped."

The graver kept whispering. Thin strokes. A stranger's serifs.

"I didn't turn him in, if that's what you're asking. I want that understood between us, even now, even tonight. I never gave his name to anyone." I rubbed my eyes, which were useless and honest about it. "But I didn't warn him either. Reiner's predecessor came round three days later asking after a runaway apprentice — they already suspected, you see, they only wanted confirmation — and I stood in this very doorway and I said, *Boy ran off in the spring, owed me money, good riddance.* And then I went inside and I took his tools out of the rack and I put them in the cellar, and I told myself that was grief. Seven years I called it grief. Grief is what you feel when death takes something from you, Dominic. What I felt was relief. Relief is what you feel when you've thrown something overboard and the boat floats."

She set the graver down. Carefully, the way she does everything — aligned with the bench edge, blade toward herself, a habit I taught her back when teaching her things was still my privilege rather than my penance.

"You were afraid," she said.

"I was a father."

"Those aren't different answers."

"No," I said. "They aren't. That's the whole obscenity of it."

* * *

We worked on. There is a mercy in craft that conversation cannot offer: the work requires all of you, and so it takes the parts of you that are bleeding and puts them to use. She forged the passes; I set the title page twice over, once clean and once deliberately aged — tea-stained, sun-faded at the edges, a document that had lived hard years in a provincial office. Forgery is mostly acting, and acting is mostly believing. You cannot print a lie well unless for the duration of the impression you hold it to be true. Every counterfeiter worth hanging knows this. So does every newspaperman. So, I suspect, does every governor.

"Tell me the rest," she said around two o'clock, without looking up.

"The rest?"

"After he left. What happened to *you*. You've never said. You just got quieter, year by year, like a room being emptied of furniture."

I considered lying. I am very good at it; it is, as established, my trade. But there is something about watching your child cut steel letters by lamplight for a cause that may hang her — something about the small economies of her movements, the way she blows the lead dust away like breath on a window — that makes a man want to leave one honest object behind, even if it's only a sentence.

"The quiet wasn't an accident," I said. "After Tomas, I decided the safest shop was a silent one. No opinions in the type. No names in the margins. I set what they paid me to set, and I did it beautifully, because beauty is deniable. And then I taught you the same. Every rule. Every silence. *Don't meet the clerk's eyes. Don't correct their proofs. Don't keep copies.* I told myself I was keeping you alive." I turned the composing stick over in my ruined hands. "And then Ilan walked through that door with letters in a dead boy's hand, and I watched you read them, and do you know what I saw in your face? Not fear. Fear I know how to read; I've been fluent in it for decades. It was recognition. You looked at those letters like a woman finding a door in a wall she'd been told was solid. And I realized I hadn't built that wall to keep you safe. I built it because it was easier than telling you the truth about me."

"Papa—"

"Let me finish. An old man gets few chances to finish; everyone keeps interrupting him with kindness." I set down the stick. "Tomas wrote in his letter that he never blamed me. Did you read that part? *He did what fathers do,* he wrote. *He chose his child. I only wish he'd told me, so I could have chosen differently too.* Well. That's generous, and Tomas was always generous, and it's also wrong. I didn't choose you over him, Dominic. I chose my fear over everything, and dressed it up in your name afterward, because fear is more respectable wearing a daughter's face. If the ledger costs us everything — the shop, the name, the bones of us — then understand it won't be because you carried it downriver. It'll be because I spent seven years paying interest on a debt that only grows."

For a while she said nothing, and the river said plenty, and the lamps burned.

Then she picked up the aged title page, held it to the light, and said: "Your kerning's off on 'Province.' Third line. You've been crying on your work again, old man, and your eyes are lying to you about the spacing."

I looked. She was right. She is always right about the spacing.

"Fix it, then," I said, "and while you fix it, tell me why we're forging a pass for a woman who doesn't exist instead of telling the truth to one who does."

She went very still. "Marta."

"Marta Veyra believes her husband is dead. She believes it because we let her believe it — because *I* let her believe it, seven years of Sundays, standing next to her in church while she mourned a living man, because telling her felt dangerous and I have made a religion of feeling safe." I took off my spectacles, which helped nothing, but a man needs a gesture. "You will go to her. Tomorrow, before anything else. Not after the checkpoints, not after the ice breaks, not when it's convenient. Tomorrow. Because you are going to carry that ledger down a river lined with men who would kill you for it, and I will not have you doing it with a lie strapped to your back alongside the truth. One burden at a time. That's how we survive the crossing."

"She'll hate us."

"She'll hate us either way. Better she hates us for a truth than for a lie discovered. Lies discovered curdle. Truths, even cruel ones, at least keep their flavor." I managed something that might charitably be called a smile. "Besides — she kept Tomas's riverboat papers. Ilan said so. A widow doesn't keep a dead man's papers unless some part of her refuses to believe the dead part. Go give that part of her the news it's been starving for."

* * *

Near dawn we finished. Two passes, flawless. A title page aged forty years in four hours. A duplicate ledger cover that would pass within arm's reach of the man who commissioned the original — provided the man looking at it wanted to see what he expected, and Reiner's clerks, like all clerks, see chiefly their own expectations.

Dominic locked the passes in the false bottom of the type case — my father's trick, older than both of us, the shop's one inheritance of honest dishonesty — and then she stood there a moment with her hand flat on the case lid, as if taking its pulse.

"Papa," she said. "When this is done. When the ledger's in Velmora and the ice is gone and — whatever comes, comes. Will you teach me the rest? Everything you held back. The pi fonts, the soluble inks, all the tricks you buried with your courage. I want all of it. Even the parts you're ashamed of. Especially those."

It was, I think, the first time in my life I have been offered forgiveness before I finished committing the crime.

"Aye," I said, and my voice cracked like the river outside. "Though fair warning: most of what I know is how to survive quietly, and you seem determined to die loudly."

"Someone has to," she said. "You raised me to notice things. It seemed wasteful not to use it."

She kissed the top of my head — she has to bend now to do it, when once she stood on a stool — banked the lamps, and went up to sleep two hours before dawn. I stayed below a while longer, as old men do, touching the cases, the stone, the press my grandfather bought with money I prefer not to investigate. Tomorrow she would walk to the Widow Veyra's door carrying the truth like a live coal. And I would stay here among my silent letters, keeping the fires low and the type warm, an accomplice at last and a coward no longer — or at least a coward with better employment.

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, closer to breaking.
