## 13. Marta Learns the Truth

There is a particular kind of cowardice that dresses itself as kindness, and I had been wearing it like a good coat for three years.

I stood outside Marta Veyra's door on the Salt Lane with my breath steaming and my heart doing something unprofessional in my chest. The morning was the color of dishwater, the kind of gray that makes a city look like it's already given up. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled over frozen cobbles, and a fishmonger was arguing with the ice about his stock, losing gracefully.

Three times I raised my hand to knock. Three times I put it back in my pocket like a sensible woman.

The trouble with telling the truth is that you only get to do it once, and after that it stops being yours. I had carried Tomas Veyra's name in my mouth for days now — alive, exiled, writing letters in that slanted, impatient hand I'd have known anywhere, because half of it I'd learned at his elbow — and every hour I kept it there, it got heavier. Ilan had said nothing about Marta. The plan had no line item for her. Plans rarely account for the people who were loved by the dead.

But I had sat across from her two weeks ago and watched her hate me with her whole grieving body, and I had let her. I had walked out of her house carrying the truth like stolen goods, and it had been sitting against my ribs ever since, cold and irregular, like a stone sewn into a coat lining.

Which, as it happened, was roughly where the actual ledger was going to go.

I knocked.

* * *

Marta opened the door before the second knock finished — she'd always had that trick, hearing intent through wood — and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as inventory. She took in my face, my coat, the absence of anything in my hands worth stealing, and arrived at some private conclusion that did not flatter me.

"You," she said.

"Good morning, Marta."

"It isn't." But she stepped aside, which was more than last time. Last time she had held the door like a shield and spoken to me through the gap, narrow as a confession slot.

Her house smelled of tallow and dried lavender and something faintly medicinal — she'd taken in mending since the widow's pension stopped coming, and the baskets of other people's torn shirts sat stacked along one wall like a small paperless archive of the city's wear. She didn't offer me a chair. She sat herself, hands folded over her apron, and waited, because Marta Veyra had never in her life made things easy for anyone, including me. Especially me.

"I've come to tell you something," I said. "And I need you to hear all of it before—"

"No."

"You don't know what—"

"I know what men say when they start that way. They want absolution first and information second." Her chin came up. "You'll do it in the other order or not at all."

So much for the speech I'd rehearsed on Salt Lane. All four versions.

"Tomas is alive," I said.

The room went very still. Even the fire seemed to lean in. Marta's face did not change — not at first, not visibly — but I saw her hands tighten on her apron, knuckles rising like knuckles through dough, and I understood that whatever was happening inside her was happening at a depth where faces are useless.

"Say it again," she whispered.

"He's alive. He fled the city. He's been in exile these three years, and he wrote—" I stopped myself. Letters. Proof. The governor's forged ledger. None of it was mine to spill; some of it wasn't even mine to know. "He sent word. Through a friend. He's alive, Marta."

She rose from her chair without hurry, which frightened me more than if she'd lunged. She walked to the mantel where a small tin frame held the official notice — KILLED IN THE COLLAPSE OF THE RIVERSIDE WAREHOUSE, BELOVED CITIZEN, etc., the state's poetry of erasure, set in type my father's shop hadn't even printed, thank God, though someone's had — and she touched the frame with two fingers, lightly, the way you touch a burn to see if it still hurts.

It still hurt. You could see it.

"Alive," she said to the notice. Then she turned around and the grief had gone through some alchemy into its cousin, and her voice when it came was quiet in the way a river under ice is quiet. "Three years, Dominic. Three years I washed other women's shirts so I could keep this house he died for. Three years I lit a candle on the anniversary and told God I forgave Him. Three years your father stood in this doorway — in this doorway, girl — with his hat in his hands and his eyes on the floor, and never once said *he might be alive*. Never once said *we don't know*. Never once gave me the mercy of a question mark."

"Marta—"

"And now you come, the daughter, with your ink-stained fingers, to hand me the question mark three years late and call it news?" She picked up the nearest basket of mending and threw it at me. It was soft and full of collars and hit me in the chest and dropped to the floor, and somehow that was worse than if it had been a chair. "Get out."

"He couldn't tell you. If anyone knew he lived—"

"If anyone knew!" She laughed, and it was an awful sound, bright and cracked. "Do you know what I would have done with that knowledge? Nothing. Nothing! I'd have gone on washing and lighting candles and being the Widow Veyra, because that's what the truth required of me, and I'd have done it *gladly*, because I'd have known he was breathing somewhere, and every shirt I stitched would have been stitched toward him instead of away from him. Do you understand the difference? No. Of course you don't. You Hartwells understand silence the way priests understand Latin — fluently, and to keep the congregation out."

That landed somewhere below the ribs and stayed there.

"They took everything from him," she went on, quieter now, which was somehow louder. "His name, his trade, his future. And then they took his death and gave it to me like a gift, and I *opened* it. I wore mourning for a living man. I said kaddish over a heartbeat." Her voice finally broke, just at the edge, and she mastered it the way my father mastered a bad line of type — by pressing down harder until it held. "What kind of friend lets a woman do that?"

"The kind who's afraid," I said. My own voice surprised me. "The kind whose father watched the governor forge a ledger that killed forty-one people and chose to keep his shop open rather than be the forty-second name. The kind who has spent seven years learning that the price of staying safe is paying interest on your silence until it costs more than the original debt." I swallowed. "We're cowards, Marta. My father and I. We've been cowards so long we call it craft. And I'm here because I'm trying — God knows how badly — to stop."

She stared at me. Outside, the fishmonger had won his argument with the ice, or lost it, hard to say; either way the shouting stopped.

"Forty-one," she repeated.

"Forty-one. And the names of everyone paid to pretend otherwise are written in a book that's going to reach Velmora before the ice breaks, if I have anything to do with it. Which is why I came today, and why I should have come weeks ago, and why I'm standing in your mending."

Marta looked at me for a long moment. Then she bent down, picked up the scattered shirts one by one, folded them badly on purpose, I think, and set the basket on the table.

"Sit down," she said. Not kindly. But she said it.

* * *

We drank chicory that tasted like punishment, and she made me tell her everything I could — not the routes or the checkpoints, which I kept behind my teeth, but the shape of it: the letters, the exile, the true ledger, the lie of the warehouse collapse. She listened the way she did everything, with her spine straight and her judgment suspended but not adjourned.

When I finished, she was silent for a while, looking at her hands.

"He used to set type standing up," she said at last. "Did you know that? Wouldn't take a stool. Said a man should feel tired honest." A ghost of something crossed her face that was almost a smile and decided against it. "He talked about your father's shop like it was a cathedral. And about your father—" She stopped. Started again. "Your father taught him everything except when to stop talking. That was his flaw, Dominic. Not courage. Courage he had to spare. His flaw was believing the truth could be said out loud and survive the saying."

"And yours?"

"My flaw," she said, "is that I married him anyway."

Then she got up, went to the bedroom, and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone gray with age. She set it on the table between us like a card played face-down.

"What's that?"

"His riverboat papers. His master's license, the boat registration, the cargo manifests — everything from when he ran freight upriver before he apprenticed to your father. The *Lark*. She's still moored at the tanner's slip. I've paid the mooring fees every year since he 'died' because I couldn't bear to let them sell her." She untied the string with fingers that trembled only slightly, and I will never know whether that was grief or fury or simply cold. "A dead man's boat. Registered to a dead man's name. Nobody inspects the property of the dead, Dominic. The dead are the safest citizens in this empire."

I looked at the papers and then at her, and I understood what she was offering before she said it, because I was holding the same thought she was: a route downriver needs a vessel, and a vessel needs papers, and the checkpoints ahead of us were built to catch smugglers, not widows shipping their husbands' effects.

"I can't take those."

"You can and you will." She pushed the bundle across the table. "Not for you. For him. If he's alive, then he'll need a way home someday, and I'd rather it be on a boat with his own name on the papers than crawling through the mud with someone else's." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were dry, which cost her more than tears would have. "But hear me, Dominic Hartwell. This is not forgiveness. Forgiveness is earned over years and I haven't begun the work. This is a loan. Against the truth. And the interest rate is brutal."

"That sounds like something Tomas would say."

"It sounds like something I learned from thirty years of watching men handle money." She almost smiled. Almost. "One condition. When this is finished — when the ledger is printed and read and the governor is choking on his own signatures — Tomas comes to this door himself. Not a letter. Not a courier. Him. In person. He owes me three years of anniversaries and he will pay them in coin I can see."

"He will," I said. "Or I'll drag him upriver myself."

"And one more thing." She stood, and gathered the mending basket, and became practical the way wounded people do, folding the moment away into some inner drawer. "Take the shroud too. Top shelf of the wardrobe. His grandmother's linen. If you're going to ship a widow's goods downriver, you'll want proper cloth — and no one searches a shroud twice."

I froze. "How did you—"

"I wash for half the customs clerks' wives, girl. I know what moves down that river and how it's wrapped." For the first time that morning, Marta Veyra smiled fully, and it was a terrible, magnificent thing, like weather breaking. "You're not the only one in this city who knows how to hide things in plain sight. I've simply had better practice. Grief is excellent camouflage. Everyone looks away from it."

I stood to leave, the oilcloth bundle heavy under my arm, heavier than any ledger. At the door I paused, because some debts require acknowledgment aloud.

"Marta. I'm sorry. For the three years. For the question mark."

She didn't turn from her mending. "Bring him home," she said, "and we'll discuss your apology. Until then, consider yourself in arrears."

* * *

Outside, the gray had thickened toward snow. I walked fast, clutching the papers, thinking about interest rates and question marks and the difference between stitching toward a man and away from him.

By the time I reached the tanner's slip, the snow had started properly, settling on the deck of the *Lark* like a shroud of its own. She was smaller than I remembered — a stubby freighter with a patched smokestack and paint gone the color of old porridge — but she sat in the water with a certain stubborn competence I recognized.

A boy was unloading eel traps nearby. He glanced at me, at the boat, at the snow.

"She yours?" he asked.

"Not yet," I said. "She belongs to a dead man."

He shrugged and went back to his traps, satisfied. And I climbed aboard the *Lark* for the first time, holding a dead man's living papers, and began to plan how a widow ships a shroud past three checkpoints before the ice breaks.
