## 14. Checkpoint One: The Customs House

Grief, I learned that morning, is the most forgiving of costumes. It requires no corsetry, no padding, no practiced accent. You simply stop sleeping properly for a week and let your face do what it wants.

I had been rehearsing widowhood since the second bell after midnight. Marta had coached me in it with a ruthlessness I hadn't expected from her — though perhaps I should have. Seven years of mourning had made her an expert in the wardrobe of sorrow, and she dressed me in it now piece by piece: the grey wool dress with its high, joyless collar; the black veil that turned the world into something seen through dirty river ice; the wedding band she pulled from her own finger and pressed into my palm without ceremony.

"Mine's worn smooth," she said. "Yours would look new. New rings ask questions."

She didn't say whose death I was borrowing. She didn't have to. We both understood that I was to be a widow shipping her husband home downriver — and that somewhere in Velmora, if Tomas still breathed, he was a widower who didn't know his wife had lent his grief to a stranger. The arithmetic of it sat wrong in my chest, but there was no time to be delicate. The ice would break when it pleased, and it did not consult our schedules.

The shroud itself was Aldous's masterwork, and I do not use that word lightly. My father had spent forty years hiding things inside other things — messages in pi fonts, water-soluble inks, compartments cut into type cases so precisely that a garrison inspector once ran his thumb along the wood and found nothing but grain. But the shroud was different. It wasn't cleverness for its own sake. It was tenderness weaponized.

He had taken a length of good linen — funeral linen, the kind that costs more than a month's wages because grief makes people stupid with money — and he had unpicked its lining with a surgeon's patience. Then he'd sewn the ledger flat against the inner panel, page by page, wrapped first in oilcloth against the damp, the stitching done in a pattern so ordinary no eye would catch it. When he finished, he held it up to the lamp and said, quietly:

"A man's last shirt should carry something true. Most carry nothing at all."

Then he'd folded a body-sized shape of straw and old clothing into the center, wound the whole thing in rope the way rivermen do, and stepped back.

"There," he said. "A dead man who weighs about as much as one ought to." He looked at me over his failing eyes, and for a moment I saw how much this cost him — not the sewing, but the sending. "Customs men don't like touching the dead, Dominic. Superstition runs thicker than law on the waterfront. But they'll look. Reiner has them looking at everything now. So you let them look, and you give them nothing to find, and you keep your hands steady even when you want to scream. Can you do that?"

"I've been keeping my hands steady since I was eleven," I said. "It's the rest of me that wobbles."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a blessing we had time for.

* * *

The customs house squatted at the foot of Rope Street like a fat man at a funeral he disapproved of. Grey stone, green shutters, a clock that had run four minutes slow since before I was born — which meant that half the official business of this city was transacted four minutes behind God's schedule, and nobody had ever thought to correct it. Smoke stood up from its chimneys in perfectly straight columns, as if even the smoke here were afraid to drift without permission.

I came down through the fish market with the shroud on a handcart, and the market parted around me the way water parts around a pilaster. That was my first lesson of the day, confirmed: the dead are a passport. Women touched their foreheads as I passed. A fishwife pressed a copper into my hand "for the journey," and I took it, because a widow who refuses charity is a widow with something to hide. The coin burned in my pocket like a coal.

At the loading steps, where the barges knocked their tired hulls against the ice-sheathed pilings, a queue had formed. Widows mostly — the river had been hungry this winter, and it always preferred the poor — each with her coffin or her shrouded bundle, each clutching papers, each wearing that particular expression of people waiting to be processed by an empire: patient on the surface, screaming underneath.

I joined them and became furniture.

That was the discipline of it. Not courage exactly — courage implies choice, heat, the blood up. This was colder than courage. This was the same thing that lived in my hands when I set a proclamation I knew to be lies: a small, locked room behind the eyes where the real Dominic sat with her arms folded, watching everything, feeling nothing she could afford to feel. I had spent seven years building that room. I had hated it every day. Now it was going to save my life, and I hated it a little less, which frightened me more than the soldiers did.

The line moved. A sergeant worked the queue with a list and a voice like a boot heel. Name. Cargo. Papers. Next.

When my turn came, I gave them the name we had chosen: Elena Marsh, of the parish of St. Aubin's, shipping her husband's remains to his mother's people below Corvel. The papers were beautiful — Aldous had forged them himself, and there is a particular pride in watching your father commit flawless crimes with the same hands that taught you letters. The parish seal was slightly imperfect, deliberately, because perfect seals are the tell of forgers. He'd thought of everything. He always had. It was the thinking-of-everything that had kept us alive seven years under a governor who read everything, and it was the failure to think of one thing more that had cost us Tomas.

The sergeant barely glanced at me. Veiled women all look alike, and veiled grieving women all look alike in the way that makes officials want to be elsewhere. He stamped. He waved me toward the steps.

And that was when the clerk saw me.

* * *

I never learned whether the clerk had a name. In my memory he is only a pair of eyes above a ledger desk set up on the landing — a narrow man, maybe thirty, with the complexion of someone who worked indoors by choice and the hands of someone who counted other people's sorrows for a salary. His ink-stained fingers were elegant. His suspicion was not. Suspicion in a clerk should be professional, a coat he wears and removes. His fit him like skin.

He stopped me three steps from the gangplank.

"Widow Marsh." He said it the way you test a coin against your teeth. "St. Aubin's parish."

"Yes, sir." I kept my voice low and roughened, the way Marta had taught me — grief does something to the throat, she said, and officials know the sound of it. "Forgive me, sir, I'm expected on the *Corvel Maid* before—"

"You're expected when the customs house finishes with you." He extended a hand, and a soldier beside him took the cart handles. "Papers again."

He read them slowly. Too slowly. Around me the queue shuffled and muttered, and I understood suddenly that this was a performance staged for an audience: the clerk wanted the queue to see him thorough. There had been orders from the garrison, everyone knew it — Reiner had locked down the river two nights past, and every barge, coffin, and crate was being handled with new and insulting care. Somewhere downstream a courier was supposedly carrying seditious paper, and Captain Reiner had announced, in effect, that he would unwrap every corpse in the city before he'd let it pass.

Which meant, of course, that he had ordered corpses unwrapped.

"The shroud," the clerk said. "Open it."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Quiet behind my eyes, where the locked room was; loud everywhere else — the groan of ice against the pilings, the mutter of the queue, my own heart doing something undignified against my ribs.

"Sir," I said, and let a tremor into it — calculated, measured, a tremor with exactly one note of fear and none of guilt. "My husband is in there."

"Your husband is cargo, madam, until he clears customs." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty I knew how to face. Procedure was a wall with no handholds. "There are regulations. Paper moves. People move paper. Dead men are remarkably convenient vessels for both."

Somewhere in the crowd, a woman gasped — genuine outrage, God bless her, the fishwives of this city would riot faster over desecrated dead than over massacred living, and the clerk knew it, and did it anyway, because his orders outranked their outrage.

I could refuse. I could weep, cling, make a scene, invoke the priest of St. Aubin's — all of it bought time and none of it bought passage, and time was the one cargo I couldn't afford to lose. The *Corvel Maid* would cast off within the hour. After her, the next boat south was days away, and the ice had begun to talk at night, that deep grinding mutter along the banks, the sound of a river deciding.

Or I could do the thing that every instinct screamed against.

I could let them open it.

"Please," I said, and my voice cracked on the word — genuinely, I hadn't planned that, some part of me that wasn't acting remembered that I had shipped my father's whole life downriver inside a lie, and the crack was real, and it was the best forgery in the room because it wasn't one. "Be gentle with him. He was gentle with everyone."

The clerk hesitated. For one full second, procedure and decency fought visibly across his narrow face, and I watched procedure win, because procedure always wins, that is its entire purpose.

The soldiers laid the shroud on the trestle board the porters used for coffins. One of them — young, freckled, hating this — began picking at the knots. My knots. Aldous's knots. Every loop had been tied the way a grieving wife would tie them, clumsily, hurriedly, and I prayed to whatever saint governs forgers that the boy's fingers were too cold to notice how well the linen beneath was sewn.

The knot gave. Another.

I did not watch. Watching is a confession. I watched the river instead, the grey plates of it shifting downstream, and I thought about type. About how a compositor holds a letter upside-down and backwards and must see it whole anyway, must trust the form she cannot directly read. That was me now: I could not see what the soldiers saw. I could only hold the shape of it in my mind and trust the craft.

Linen rustled. Straw shifted — the false weight of the false husband, packed to answer exactly the question no one should ask.

The clerk reached into the folds.

His fingers were six inches from oilcloth. Six inches from three years of a dead man's testimony, from names that reached into the garrison itself, from the payment entries that would turn this frozen city into a bonfire come spring. Six inches from my father's life's work and Tomas Veyra's life's evidence and Marta's stolen wedding ring and my own neck.

And then he stopped.

Because the boy had pulled the linen back far enough to show what lay at the top of the fold: a dead man's face. Or rather, the top of a head — greying hair combed by Aldous's careful hand, waxen skin, eyes closed with two coins' worth of dignity. My father had built the illusion to be seen only in pieces, and the pieces were devastating. The boy crossed himself. The freckled soldier crossed himself. Two women in the queue were openly weeping, and one shouted, "Shame! Shame on you!" and the murmur turned ugly, and the clerk stood there with his elegant fingers hovering over a stranger's dead husband while an audience of the bereaved pronounced judgment on him with their eyes.

Superstition, Aldous had said, runs thicker than law on the waterfront. He had bet the ledger on it. He had sewn the truth beneath a face, because no man in this city — clerk, soldier, or governor — would put his hands on a dead man's mouth.

The clerk withdrew his fingers as if the linen had bitten them.

"Cover him," he said shortly. "Madam. Your papers are in order." He stamped them hard enough to crack the wax, thrust them at me, and turned to the next case with the expression of a man who intended to wash his hands before lunch and possibly forever.

I rewrapped nothing — the soldiers did it, hastily, crossing themselves again, and the boy retied the knots badly, which was fine, because we would untie them ourselves aboard and retie them properly before Corvel. I took the cart handles. My hands were steady. Of course they were steady. They had been trained by the best silences in the city.

Only when I reached the gangplank did the trembling start, deep in the knees, behind the veil, invisible — and I walked up the plank into the smell of pitch and wet rope and let it shake me privately, one plank-step at a time, like a debt being paid in installments.

* * *

The *Corvel Maid* took her passengers the way a tired animal takes a load: without enthusiasm, without refusal. I claimed a place in the stern cabin among the freight — a widow rides cheap when she ships her own dead — and settled onto my trunk with the shroud lashed beside me, close enough to touch, close enough to defend.

Through the stern window, the customs house shrank. The clock said four minutes slow. The smoke stood straight. The queue went on processing grief, one stamp at a time.

Marta's ring dug into my finger where it was a size too small, and I was grateful for the pain. Pain meant I was still a person and not yet entirely a vessel. I flexed my hand, ink-stained even now, scrubbed raw but never quite clean — Reiner had noticed those hands once, and lingered over them, and I had thought about that every day since. Hands like mine were supposed to set type, not carry revolutions. Well. Type carried revolutions often enough. Why shouldn't the hands?

The mooring lines came off. The deck tilted. Beneath the hull, the river muttered its ice-talk, and the pilot swore softly at the floes riding down from the north like slow white cavalry.

We were moving. Checkpoint one lay astern, and ahead lay the garrison bridge, and beyond it the wharf where the governor kept his private boats and his private suspicions.

I looked down at the shroud and rested my palm flat against the linen, over the place where the pages slept.

"Hold together," I whispered to the dead man who wasn't there. "Both of you."

Upstream, faint over the water, came a sound I told myself was only the ice — a long, rolling crack of thunder out of a cloudless sky. But the pilot heard it too, and stopped swearing, and stared north with his mouth slightly open.

The river, I thought, was beginning to break early this year.
