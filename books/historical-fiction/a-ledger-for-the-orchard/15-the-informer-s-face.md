## 15. The Informer's Face

There is an art to following a man through a city that wants you dead, and the first rule of it is this: never follow him.

I had learned that rule in Velmora, years ago, from a printer's devil named Oskar who could shadow a bishop across three parishes and be home for supper. "Walk beside them, Ilan," Oskar told me once, cleaning his nails with a compositing knife he absolutely should not have been using for that purpose. "A man looks behind him when he's afraid. He never looks at the man walking next to him carrying the same loaf of bread."

So on the morning after the guild hall burned two of our people into the governor's ledgers as *apprehended*, I bought a loaf of bread and walked beside Petrik Sorin.

He was younger than I'd expected. That was the first obscenity of it. When Ilan-the-courier thinks *informer*, he imagines a certain type — a soft-bellied clerk with wet eyes and a taste for other men's ruin, someone you could hate cleanly, the way you hate a tax. Instead I got a boy of perhaps nineteen, narrow through the shoulders, walking with his collar up against the river wind and his hands jammed in his pockets like any apprentice with an hour to waste before the garrison bell rang the quarter.

He stopped at a chandler's stall. He bought tallow candles — four of them, cheap ones. He haggled over the price of the fourth with the particular shameless ferocity of someone who has counted every coin twice. Then he walked on toward the garrison barracks, eating nothing, carrying his candles like a boy going home to a mother who would scold him for spending too much.

I wanted, very badly, for him to be wrong. For me to be wrong. For the trail of small catastrophes that had followed our network since midwinter — the courier taken at Velmora gate, the safe house emptied a day before the search, the guild hall meeting betrayed — to belong to some other face entirely.

But I had spent three days confirming it, and three days is enough time to stop hoping and start knowing. The candle shop sat directly across from the garrison's side door. He went in through it without being challenged, which no candle-buying apprentice in this city does unless the door knows his name.

* * *

The others wanted him dead by sundown.

We met in the back room of a dye-house, where the reek of mordant covered conversation the way snow covers a corpse — thoroughly, and only until spring. There were six of us around a table scarred with acid burns: Bren the bargeman, old Sefa who ran messages between laundries, the Kessler twins who looked alike enough to alibi each other through any checkpoint, a young woman named Ruzena whose brother was one of the two taken in the guild hall, and me.

"He's a rat," Bren said, laying one enormous hand flat on the table. "You step on rats."

"You step on rats in your own cellar," Sefa said. "In the street, a stepped-on rat tells every other rat where your feet have been." She turned her milky eyes on me. "Well, Ilan? You found him. You've been chewing on it three days. Give us the taste of it."

I gave them what I had. The name. The age. The candles. The side door. And then the part I'd dug out last, from a parish register I'd had no business reading and read anyway: *Petrik Sorin, born 1829. Brother: Jozef Sorin, born 1821.*

Jozef Sorin. One of the ninety-one names the governor's forged ledger had struck from the record of the mill square — the massacre we were carrying the true account of downriver in a shroud sewn around a widow's funeral goods. Ninety-one dead men written out of existence so that nine officials could sleep in clean sheets.

"His brother," I said, "was in the square that day."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when grief walks in wearing work clothes.

"So was half the city," Bren said at last. "My cousin lost a son there. You don't see me selling couriers to Captain Reiner."

"No," I said. "You're selling them to me instead, which is nearly as dangerous, but better paid in conscience." A few grim mouths twitched. Wry humor is the currency of conspiracies; we are all broke and spend it anyway. "Here is my argument. If we kill Petrik, Reiner loses an informer and gains a martyr's brother — because within a week the whole riverside will know the Hartwell network murdered Jozef Sorin's little brother, and every grieving family on the embankment will believe it. We'd be doing the governor's printing for him. Free of charge."

"And if we don't kill him?" Ruzena asked. Her voice was flat as a press bed. Her brother had been in custody eleven days. Everyone in that room knew what eleven days meant, and no one said it, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"Then we do something harder," I said. "We show him what he sold."

* * *

Dominic Hartwell argued with me about it for an hour, later that night, in the cold room above the print shop where the smell of ink lived in the plaster the way sin lives in a confessor.

"You want to hand the ledger to the man who burned our route," she said. She stood with her back to the window, so the lamplight caught only the edge of her — ink-stained fingers, jaw set like a locked type case. "You want to open the shroud in front of him."

"I want him to read three names," I said. "Three pages. Not the whole book."

"How do you control what a man reads?"

"You don't. That's rather the point."

She looked at me then, fully, and I saw what Aldous Hartwell must have seen every day for seven years: a woman who had survived by mastering silence and resented every syllable of the tuition. "Ilan. He gave up Oskar's route. People are in Reiner's cells because that boy wanted a warm coat and a pass through the gates."

"I know what he did."

"Do you? Because you talk about him like a problem in composition. Set the type, pull the lever, see what comes out. But men aren't pages. Sometimes the impression comes out wrong and you can't strike it and reprint."

"No," I agreed. "But sometimes you can make a second edition."

That landed. I watched it land — watched her flinch slightly, the way people do when they hear their own private arithmetic spoken aloud. Dominic Hartwell had spent the winter calculating costs. So had I. We were both, in our different ways, bookkeepers of the unspeakable.

"Why does it matter to you?" she asked finally. Quieter now. "You didn't know Jozef Sorin."

"No," I said. "But I knew a boy once, in Velmora, who informed on his own cell to keep his mother out of prison. They hanged him. And his mother was arrested the following month anyway, because Reiner's predecessor had already decided her name was useful." I turned my cup in my hands. It was chipped, and the chip always caught my thumb, a flaw I had memorized the way you memorize flaws in things you cannot afford to replace. "Reiner collects informers the way you collect type. He doesn't use them because they're good. He uses them until they're used up. Petrik has maybe two months left in him before he becomes a liability with a name worth arresting. We can hang him or we can spend him. Only one of those buys us anything."

Dominic was silent a long moment. Outside, the river groaned under its ice — that long tectonic complaint that had become the city's heartbeat all winter, pressure building beneath a surface that pretended to be solid.

"Marta should be there," she said at last.

"Marta—"

"Jozef Sorin was a mill hand. Marta's Tomas organized the mill hands. She knew him. She buried a husband who isn't dead, Ilan; she knows precisely what it costs to lose someone the official way." Her eyes were very steady. "If we're going to break a boy open, we ought to have someone present who understands what's inside him."

And that, more than any argument I'd made, was how I knew the plan would work. Because it was no longer mine. It belonged to the ledger now, and the ledger had opinions.

* * *

We took him on Thursday, in the hour between the lamplighter's rounds and the garrison patrol, on the stairwell of a tenement off Tanner's Row where Petrik had a room he presumably believed was secret. Nothing is secret. That is the first lesson of conspiracy and the last comfort of tyrants: they believe their own secrets too.

Bren held his arms. The twins held the door. And I held the lamp up to his face and let him see exactly who we were, because terror recognizes its creditors faster than it recognizes its friends.

Petrik Sorin did not beg. I'll give him that much, and I gave it grudgingly, the way you pay a debt you resent. He shook — God, he shook, a fine constant tremor like a leaf in rain — but he kept his mouth shut and his eyes open, and he looked at each of us in turn as though memorizing us for a report he'd deliver from whatever came after dying.

"You're the courier," he said to me. Voice cracking on the second word. "From the bridge job. They said you were tall. They were wrong."

"They say a lot of things at the garrison," I said. "Do they also tell you what happens to their informers in spring?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man who has done sums he doesn't share.

"We're not going to kill you," I said, and felt Bren's grip shift behind him — disagreement, expressed in knuckles. "We're going to read to you."

Marta Veyra stepped out of the shadow of the landing, and I will be honest: I had argued against her coming, and I had been wrong, and I have rarely been so glad to lose. She was a small woman gone grey before her time, dressed in the brown of permanent mourning, and she carried the three loose pages wrapped in oilcloth the way other women carry bread home. Petrik stared at her and something in his trembling changed key.

"I know you," he whispered. "You're— you're Veyra's wife. Jozef worked for your Tomas. In the mill. Before."

"Before," Marta agreed. Her voice was dry as old paper. "Open the cloth, Ilan."

I unwrapped the pages. Three sheets, copied in Dominic's careful hand from the true ledger sleeping somewhere south of here, sewn into a shroud, riding downriver inside a dead woman's coffin goods. On each sheet, a column of names. Dates. Amounts paid. Signatures.

"This is the governor's ledger," I said. "Not the forgery. The true one. The one that records what actually happened in the mill square on the ninth of October, four years ago. Ninety-one men dead. Not forty. Not 'a regrettable disturbance.' Ninety-one. And here—" I turned the first page toward the lamp "—is the payment schedule for every official who signed the document saying otherwise."

Petrik's eyes went to the page the way drowning men go to light.

"Find Jozef," Marta said softly.

His lips moved. His finger rose, hovered, shaking worse than the rest of him. Then it came down on the third line of the fourth column and stayed there as if pinned.

*Sorin, Jozef — mill hand, second shift. Deceased 9 Oct. Payment authorized: 200 crowns, disbursed to Capt. M. Reiner, garrison fund, for suppression of claims.*

They had priced him. Two hundred crowns for a life, itemized, filed, countersigned. His brother — who had taught him to fish the millrace, who had given him his first pair of boots when their father died, whose boots Petrik was almost certainly wearing at that moment because the poor inherit everything and call it luck — his brother had a *line item*.

The sound Petrik made was not a word. It was the noise a beam makes before it gives — a long structural complaint from somewhere below language. He slid down the wall until Bren's grip was the only thing holding him upright, and he wept the way nineteen-year-olds weep, completely, gracelessly, with his whole body, and none of us looked away because looking away would have been a mercy and we hadn't come to be merciful.

"I didn't know," he gasped. "They told me — they said it was printers' sedition, they said you were foreign agents, they said—"

"They said, they said." Marta's voice didn't rise at all, and that was worse than shouting. "Boy. My husband has been dead three years. Officially. I have mourned him officially. Do you know what it means to grieve a lie? To set flowers on a grave with nothing in it? Your brother is in that ledger because powerful men needed him to be nowhere. And you helped them put other names alongside his."

"I needed—"

"You needed candles," Marta said, glancing at the parcel still clutched in his fist, four cheap tallow candles he'd haggled over like a boy. "Yes. I know what needing looks like. Needing is honest. Selling is not. But listen to me, Petrik Sorin, because I will only say this once." She leaned close, and the lamplight made her grey eyes burn like wet slate. "My husband is alive. He is in exile, and he wrote that ledger's testimony with his own hand, and it is moving downriver right now, tonight, past your masters, toward Velmora. When it arrives, ten thousand copies will be printed and every name in it will be read aloud in the square — including yours, if you are still theirs when it lands. *Informer, Petrik Sorin.* Is that how you want Jozef remembered? As the brother of a footnote in the governor's service?"

The silence in that stairwell lasted a year.

Then Petrik Sorin lifted his head, and his face had changed — not cleansed, nothing cleanses that fast, but *turned*, the way a boat turns, slowly and then all at once, from one bearing to another.

"What do you need," he said.

* * *

What we needed was the garrison bridge, and that is a story with blood in it, so let me set this scene plainly before it turns.

We sat him on the stairs and told him everything he could safely know: that a courier would cross the bridge within days disguised among the funeral traffic, that Reiner himself was inspecting, that the network needed one pair of eyes inside the checkpoint who could count patrols and report the gaps. He gave us the inspection schedule from memory — Reiner's rotation, the relief times, the fact that the captain preferred to inspect at dusk because he believed fear worked best in failing light. He talked until his voice wore out, and then he sat there in the dark with his candles on his knees like an offering, and asked the question I had been waiting for since the shroud came open.

"The courier crossing the bridge," he said. "Who is it?"

Nobody answered. Dominic's name existed in that stairwell the way gunpowder exists in a powder magazine — present, essential, and never to be spoken near a flame, however recently converted.

"It's the printer's daughter," Petrik said quietly. "Isn't it. The one whose hands are stained like a compositor's. Reiner talks about her. He noticed her hands." He looked up at me, and his young face was terrible with understanding. "He's hunting her already. He just doesn't know it yet. And I'm the only person in this city who can stand next to him on that bridge and point him the wrong way."

"Yes," I said.

"He'll figure it out. Eventually. Whatever I do on that bridge, afterward he'll know."

"Almost certainly."

Petrik nodded, slow, absorbing the shape of his remaining life the way you absorb the shape of a coffin — by lying down in it mentally first. Then he picked up his candles and stood, and Bren, watching me, waited for my signal to take him back into custody or turn him loose, and I confess I hesitated one heartbeat longer than a professional should.

Because here is the thing I did not say aloud, then or ever: I had tested him. Before the dye house, before the stairwell, I had floated a false route past him through a laundry contact — invented, poisoned, a road to nowhere — and I had watched it reach Reiner's desk within a day, and I had known. And even now, standing converted in the dark with his brother's name fresh off his lips, some cold clerk in the back office of my skull was still keeping his account open, still entering debits, still unwilling to write *paid in full* across a debt that large.

Trust, in my trade, is not a feeling. It is a risk you price.

"Thursday," I told him. "Dusk inspection. You'll wear a blue scarf. You'll stand where you always stand. And when the moment comes, Petrik — you'll know the moment — you'll give Reiner a diversion worth believing."

"What kind?"

I looked at Marta. Marta looked at the ledger pages, folded again into oilcloth, already gone from this place, already traveling.

"The kind that goes wrong," I said. "That's the only kind Reiner believes."

He went down the stairs and out into the frozen dark, a boy-shaped debt walking toward a bridge, and behind him the river groaned under the ice like something enormous turning over in its sleep, getting ready to wake.

Two nights later, at dusk, Captain Reiner took up his post on the garrison bridge, and a blue scarf moved along the rail to meet him — and the moment came, exactly as promised, and it went wrong in ways none of us had budgeted for.

But that is the next page.
