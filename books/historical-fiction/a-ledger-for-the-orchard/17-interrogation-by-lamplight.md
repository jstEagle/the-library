## 17. Interrogation by Lamplight

There is a particular quality to pain that I did not learn in the resistance, despite what our pamphlets implied. The pamphlets spoke of endurance as though it were a muscle you could train, like carrying sacks of grain or memorizing routes. Nobody told me that endurance is mostly bookkeeping — a running tally of what you can afford to lose next.

By the second hour, I had lost my boots. By the third, most of my dignity. By the fourth, I was negotiating with God, whom I had previously considered a rumor spread by frightened people.

The room they'd taken me to sat beneath the garrison bridge itself, which struck me as either a joke or an economy. Why waste cartage on a prisoner when the cells come furnished? The ceiling wept river water in slow, patient drips, as if even the architecture had been instructed to wear me down one drop at a time. A single lamp burned on a table, turned low, and Captain Reiner sat beside it reading papers with the unhurried attention of a man checking his accounts before supper.

They had worked me over twice already, and both times by professionals — quiet men who apologized with their elbows and never raised their voices. It was the silence that got under my skin. Shouting I could have hated. Hatred is fuel. But these men handled me like cargo, and there is nothing to burn in being freight.

"Water," I said, because it seemed wise to want something small. Give them a victory. A man who refuses everything looks like a man protecting something enormous.

Reiner didn't look up. "He wants water."

One of the professionals brought it. I drank. My hands shook so badly that some of it went down my chin, and I decided to count that as washing.

"You understand my position," Reiner said mildly, still reading. "I have a city where everyone knows something and no one knows anything. And now I have you — a courier with river calluses and a Velmoran accent he's been sanding down for years. You are, at present, the only thread I hold." He set down his papers at last and looked at me with genuine, almost friendly curiosity. "So let's not make this tedious. Where is the ledger?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't. Nobody ever does. In eleven years of service I have yet to meet a single man who knew what I meant." He rose, stretched like a farmer after a long ride, and nodded to the professionals. "Again, then. Gently. He's more use to me whole."

I will spare you the inventory of the next hour. Not out of delicacy — I've never much trusted delicacy; it's what people perform instead of courage — but because the details belong to me, and I find I'm possessive about them. Suffice to say the body keeps books too, and mine began writing entries I hadn't authorized. Somewhere in the middle of it I heard myself making sounds I didn't recognize as mine, and a distant part of me — the part that had survived three years of exile by being practical — noted with interest: *so that's what I sound like when I stop being useful.*

* * *

Here is what matters: they asked questions in the wrong order.

That's how I knew they had nothing. An informed interrogator asks about people first — names, faces, safe houses — because information has a shape and you can feel for its edges. Reiner's men asked about *things*. Papers. Documents. A ledger. They wanted confirmation of a rumor, which meant the rumor had arrived without its contents attached.

Someone had talked, then. Someone in the network had whispered that the true ledger existed, but not where it slept or who carried it. That narrowed the traitor considerably, and I lay on the wet stones afterward doing the grim arithmetic while my ribs filed their complaints. Petrik? No — Petrik had been ours for barely two days, and his conversion had the raw, bleeding look of the genuine article. The guild contact, then. The one whose route I had watched burn from a doorway in the Guild Hall district. He had known the plan existed. He had not known I'd already rerouted it through a widow's funeral goods and a woman with ink in her knuckles.

Which meant Dominic Hartwell was past the customs house by now, or very near it, and every hour I kept this lamp-lit room supplied with my stubbornness was an hour bought at retail and sold wholesale.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out broken and wet, and both professionals paused to look at me with something like professional respect.

Reiner looked up from his papers again. "Something amusing?"

"You," I said. My voice sounded like a gate that needed oiling. "Eleven years. And you're still asking prisoners questions you could answer yourself if you read your own ledgers properly."

It was a stupid thing to say. I want that on the record. Pain makes poets of us all, and most of them bad ones. But Reiner tilted his head, and I watched — with the strange clarity that arrives when you've got nothing left to protect but the timing — as the thought took root behind his eyes.

"The customs house," he said slowly. "Tuesday's manifests." He stood, crossed to the door, and spoke to the guard outside. "Bring me the burial shipments. Every one since Tuesday. Coffins, shrouds, funeral goods going downriver."

The door closed. Reiner returned to his chair and studied me the way a man studies a lock he's decided is worth picking.

"A shroud," he said, almost conversationally. "It's always a shroud, isn't it? Or a false bottom in a coffin. Some pious trick. You people love your funerals — you get so much use out of grief." He smiled, and it was the first honest expression I'd seen on him all night, which made it worse than any blow. "But here's what you don't understand, courier. I don't need to find the ledger anymore. I only need to close the river. A document is a prisoner too. It cannot swim."

My heart did something painful that had nothing to do with the professionals' handiwork.

* * *

They left me alone after that, chained by the ankle to a ring in the wall, and the lamp burned low and the ceiling dripped its idiot metronome, and I conducted the full audit of myself.

Assets: one working eye, mostly. Both hands, technically. A tongue that had so far refused to earn its keep. Knowledge that Dominic had a head start measured in hours, and that hours were about to become the rarest currency on the river.

Debts: everything else.

I thought about Tomas Veyra, whose handwriting had started all this — letters smuggled across borders in the linings of ordinary coats, describing a massacre in numbers so precise they could only be read off the original page. Tomas, who had chosen exile over silence and paid for it with a name, a wife, a grave with no body in it. I had carried his words for three years and told myself I understood their weight. Lying on those stones, I understood it better. His weight was this room. His weight was every room like it, in every city the empire governed, occupied night after night by men who would not say the names.

And I thought about Aldous Hartwell, whom I'd met exactly once, briefly, in a print shop that smelled of ink and lamp oil and old fear. A man who had spent seven years perfecting the art of the closed mouth. I had judged him for it. Exiles judge everybody; it's the only property we're issued. But sitting in the dark with my own silence stretched around me like a shroud — there's that word again, Reiner wasn't wrong about us and funerals — I began to suspect that Aldous's silences hadn't been cowardice at all. They'd been type cases. Compartments. Places to keep dangerous things safe until the press was ready.

His daughter was the press. I'd seen her hands. I'd watched her lie to Reiner's face with her pulse steady as a compositor setting a line.

"Hold," I said aloud, to nobody, in the typesetter's word for keeping space where something will go. My voice cracked on it. I said it again anyway. "Hold."

Because that was the whole trade, wasn't it? Everything I'd ever believed about resistance reduced to a single instruction. Hold the line. Hold the space. Hold until the ink sets, until the ice breaks, until the words can stand on their own without you holding them up. Endurance isn't heroism's loud cousin. It's the quiet work underneath, invisible, unsung, the labor of staying unbroken long enough to matter.

I tested my chains, found the ring loose in its mortar — old building, river water, rot — and noted it without hope. Hope is a supply you ration. If the mortar gave, good. If not, I'd hold anyway.

* * *

Near dawn — or what I guessed was dawn; the room offered no opinion — Reiner came back.

He looked different. Not rested — I doubt he'd slept — but settled, the way a man looks when a suspicion has hardened into a decision and he's finally free to act on it. He dismissed the guards. He crouched down to my level, which cost him something, I think, and which I appreciated in the way you appreciate a debt you intend to collect later.

"I've been thinking about you," he said. "About what you are. You're not a fanatic — fanatics sing. You're not a mercenary — mercenaries price themselves. You're a courier. Which means somewhere downriver there is a person, or a place, waiting for something that isn't coming unless you arrive." He paused. "Unless it already arrived."

I kept my face the way Aldous had taught his daughter to keep hers: a locked shop with the shutters drawn and nothing worth stealing visible through the cracks.

"So here is what happens now," Reiner continued. "As of this hour, the river is closed. No traffic passes the garrison bridge without my signature. The governor's wharf is sealed to everything but his own consignments. Every manifest from the customs house for the past week is on my desk, and I am reading each one personally." He stood, brushed off his knees, and looked down at me with something I was surprised to recognize as regret. "If your ledger exists, courier, it is currently sitting in a box somewhere between here and Velmora, in a country that is shrinking around it by the mile. And when I catch it — when, not if — I will come back to this room, and we will finish our conversation."

He walked to the door. Stopped. Without turning:

"For what it's worth — you held longer than most. I'll note it in your file. It's the only kindness the empire stocks."

The door shut. The lamp guttered. The ceiling dripped.

I lay back against the wet stone and let myself feel the size of what he'd done. The river closed. The bridge sealed. The wharf locked to all but the governor's own boats — and somewhere ahead of the closing net, a woman dressed as a widow with a shroud sewn around her ribs, drifting toward checkpoints that were, even now, turning from doors into walls.

She'd be at the garrison bridge by tomorrow. And Reiner himself would be standing on it.

I pulled at the loose ring until my fingers bled, and the mortar gave a grudging quarter-inch, and I settled into the slowest, most patient work of my life: becoming a free man one crumbled handful of plaster at a time.

Above me, through the floor, I heard boots — many boots, moving fast, and a shout rolled down from the street, muffled but unmistakable in its direction:

*"Fire! The printer's shop on Chandler's Lane — fire!"*

The ring gave way all at once, and I didn't even feel my wrist wrench, because I was already running up the stairs into the smoke-colored morning, toward the glow on the river, knowing — knowing the way you know your own heartbeat — whose shop was burning, and who had stayed inside it.
