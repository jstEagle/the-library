## 18. The Shop Burns

I have set type for executions, tax proclamations, and the governor's birthday ode — that last one a masterpiece of flattery in which I compared him to an oak, which I still consider my finest act of treason, since everyone who read it knew exactly what oaks are for: felling. But I never once printed my own death notice. It seemed a waste of good paper.

The soldiers came at dusk, which I thought theatrical of them. Fire at dusk shows better against a darkening sky. Someone in the garrison has a sense of composition. Perhaps Captain Reiner himself; he always struck me as a man who appreciated the visual arts, provided they ended in ash.

There were six of them, plus an officer whose name I didn't catch and didn't need to. They came through the front door without knocking, which is the privilege of boots, and the officer unrolled a paper with the word AUTHORIZATION stamped across it twice, as if once hadn't convinced even him.

"Master Hartwell," he said. "By order of Governor's Writ, this establishment is seized pending investigation into seditious materials."

"Seditious," I repeated, tasting the word. "You mean the almanac? The one with the frost tables?"

"The investigation will determine what is seditious."

"Ah. So you'll know when you find it." I nodded sagely. "That's how my father taught me to proofread, too. Read it until you find the error. The trouble is knowing when to stop."

He did not laugh. Officers rarely do; it's in their contract.

* * *

Let me tell you how a print shop dies, since no one has written the elegy yet and I am, after all, the only man qualified.

It does not die all at once. First they take the press apart with pry bars — a Columbian hand-press, forty years old, that has outlived two emperors' worth of censors — and the sound of its frame cracking is like a joint giving way, an old man's hip on ice. Then they overturn the type cases. Forty drawers of type, every letter I have ever loved, spilling across the floorboards in a metal rain. All those words suddenly homeless. All those sentences that might have been, scattered now into alphabet soup that no compositor alive could sort before morning.

Then the officer looks around, dissatisfied, because destruction of property is not destruction of evidence, and evidence is what Reiner sent them for. He wants papers. He wants the archives. He wants seven years of my careful, cowardly bookkeeping.

And here is where I confess something, to whoever reads this — and someone will, because ash preserves nothing but curiosity. Seven years ago, when Tomas Veyra stood where you're imagining me standing now, young and furious and holding a copy of the forged ledger in his shaking hands, I told him to burn it. I told him silence was survival. I told him a man with ink on his fingers had no business playing with blood.

He went anyway, my brilliant boy. And three years later the official record said he was dead, and I let the record stand, because the record kept me safe, and I had a daughter, and safety was the whole of my religion.

Dominic. My Dominic, who learned her letters from the wrong side of a composing stick, who can set a page faster than any journeyman in this city and read a censor's face faster than she reads type. She is not here tonight because I sent her away yesterday with a lie about visiting Marta Veyra, and God forgive me if that was the last lie I ever told her, though I suspect — I hope — it was also the truest thing I ever arranged. She thinks she left against my wishes. Let her think so. A daughter should have something to argue about at a funeral. It keeps grief honest.

The officer's men found the archive room behind the paper store. Of course they did; Reiner would have told them where to look. He'd been in my shop twice, drinking my tea, complimenting my loyalty, running his eyes along my shelves the way a thief cases a house. What did he find there? Ledgers going back thirty years. Orders. Correspondence. Receipts. The sediment of a lifetime spent printing other people's lies, filed neatly because a tidy lie is harder to disprove.

And somewhere in that sediment — I could not swear where, I had not looked in years — things that would point downriver. Toward Velmora. Toward the guild. Toward a girl disguised as a widow with a shroud full of truth sewn against her ribs.

They dragged the boxes out into the main room. The officer opened one, leafed through, and smiled the smile of a man who has found what he was sent to find and is already composing his report.

"You'll come with us, Master Hartwell."

"I'm afraid not," I said. "My knees."

"Your knees—"

"Thirty years of standing at a case. You'd understand if you'd ever done honest work." I moved toward the hearth as I spoke, casual as a man going to warm his hands. Beside the hearth sits the lamp-oil. Beside the lamp-oil sits a printer's oldest companion: turpentine, spirits, solvent — we keep them for cleaning ink off everything including our sins. "But by all means, take the boxes. I'll just—"

I upended four gallons of spirits across the floorboards, across the scattered type, across the archive boxes themselves, and put a match to it before the officer finished drawing breath to shout.

* * *

Now, a word on fire, from a man who has spent his life near flammable things and ought to have known better.

Fire is honest. That is its great virtue and its horror. Everything I ever printed was a negotiation — with censors, with fear, with the small daily corruptions that keep a shop open and a family fed. But fire doesn't negotiate. It takes the sentence you wrote and the sentence you suppressed and burns them identically, without prejudice, without reading. It is the only censor I have ever respected.

The first minute was magnificent, I'll admit it. The spirits caught with a soft whump, and flame ran across the floor like water finding its level, and the type cases went up in a bloom of heat that made the spilled letters gleam like embers themselves — ten thousand little suns, each one a character finally free of meaning. I thought: *there is my life's work, and isn't it lovely.* Not the books. The burning of them. The refusal.

The officer shouted something about saving the documents and two soldiers actually lunged into the smoke, which I regret, because they were conscripts more than likely, boys fed the same lies I'd been paid to print. One got an armful of flaming ledger and dropped it, screaming oaths. The other simply gave up, the way men give up when the fire is bigger than the order.

Then the officer remembered me. He came through the smoke with his sword drawn, which is the answer officers give to most questions, and he took hold of my collar and said, "You've destroyed imperial evidence, old man. That is a hanging offense."

"Yes," I agreed. "Along with everything else. You may as well leave me to the fire; it's tidier, and your report will read better. 'The traitor perished in the blaze he set' — very clean. Whereas 'the traitor hanged while his shop burned anyway' sounds like incompetence."

I don't know why I baited him. Habit, perhaps. A lifetime of setting dangerous words and sending them out to do their work while I stayed safely behind — and here at the end, the last typeset line of my life, and I wanted to *set* it myself, choose the words, kern them properly. He struck me across the face with the flat of his scabbard, which hurt considerably less than my pride suggested, and then — and I will be fair to him forever for this, wherever he ends up — he left. Called his men off, said the fire was evidence enough, said something about reporting to the captain. Self-preservation, probably; if I burned in custody there'd be questions. Or maybe even officers tire of watching old men burn.

The door slammed. The fire climbed.

* * *

Here is the part I did not expect: the quiet.

A fire this size makes a great deal of noise at first — the roar, the crack of the shelves, the groan of beams — but underneath the noise there is a silence, the same silence I lived inside for seven years. The silence of things unsaid. Tomas's face when I told him to burn the ledger. Marta Veyra's eyes at the funeral-that-wasn't. Every night I set official proclamations and knew them false, and said nothing, and kissed my daughter's head, and slept. Silence is not empty. Anyone who tells you that has never carried it. It is the heaviest freight there is, and tonight I am loading all of it onto the flames, every box of it, and the fire takes it gladly because fire is the only creditor who never asks how you came by your debts.

I fed it. That was the work, and I did it properly, because I am a craftsman if nothing else. When the flames reached the archive room door I pushed the rest of the boxes through — every receipt, every order, every private note in Reiner's own hand thanking me for my cooperation over the years, oh yes, the captain wrote letters, and I kept them, because a careful coward keeps receipts, and those receipts would hang him if anyone ever read them. No one will read them now. That is the point. Nothing in this fire must survive to point at Dominic. Let them dig in the ashes and find a loyal printer who snapped at last, alone, mad with guilt or whatever story suits them. Let the story be mine. I have spent my life printing other people's stories; it seems only right to end as the author of one.

The heat drove me back toward the front of the shop. My eyes stung shut. Somewhere above me the sign creaked — HARTWELL & SON, which had been a lie for twenty years since there was no son, and which I never corrected because correcting it would have meant explaining, and explanation was the one thing I never printed.

I thought of Dominic's hands. Ink-stained like a man's, Reiner said, lingering, and I had watched my daughter absorb that insult the way type absorbs ink — completely, silently, keeping its shape. She gets her silences from me. God knows she got nothing else worth having. But she gets her courage from somewhere else entirely — her mother, who died too soon to teach her anything except how to leave a room with dignity — and tonight that borrowed courage is carrying the truest document this city has produced in thirty years downriver under a shroud, past checkpoints, past the ice, past everything I failed to protect her from.

She will be angry. That is my one regret, and it is a large one — large enough to warm me better than the fire. She will be angry that I stayed. She will say I chose the archives over coming home to her, and she will be right in the way that daughters are always right, which is the deepest way. But she will also understand, eventually, in some cold hour on some boat, looking back at the glow on the horizon: there was no version of tonight where both of us walked away, and between the two of us, the ledger needed the living courier more than the shop needed its old keeper.

Besides. I taught her disappearing, but she taught herself appearing. She'll be fine. She'll be more than fine. She will be the voice, and I was only ever the silence, and silences exist so that voices have something to break.

The beam overhead cracked. I sat down in the doorway of the burning archive room, on the step where Tomas once sat sharpening his knives and telling me about the massacre he'd witnessed, and I listened to the fire finish my sentence.

It is odd — I expected fear. There was a little, at the edges, animal and embarrassing. But mostly what I felt was the feeling I get when a forme is locked up perfectly: quoins tight, furniture square, every element of the page held fast and ready for the press. Completion. Whatever happens next happens to the page, not to the compositor. The compositor's work is done.

Through the window — glass long gone, a pleasant orange draft now — I could see the river, black between the banks, wearing its ice like a seal on a letter. Under that ice, somewhere south, a boat. On that boat, my daughter, carrying names that will not stay buried the way I am about to. Break, I thought at the ice. Break early. Be rude about it. Give them hell.

The roof came down, and the last thing I saw was the type melting — all those letters, every word I ever withheld, running together at last into one bright, honest, unreadable stream, silver in the fire, flowing toward the door like a river finding its level, going where it always should have gone.

Out.

Downstream.

Toward Velmora.
