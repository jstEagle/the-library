## 19. Checkpoint Three: The Governor's Wharf

My father's coat smelled of him. That was the first mistake I made, putting it on — not a tactical error, nothing so clever as that, just the simple cruelty of pulling his sleeves over my arms and finding that grief had been waiting there all along, folded into the wool like a pressed flower. He had worn it every winter for twenty years. The elbows were shiny. The right cuff was stained with ink where he used to drag his wrist across it while setting type, and when I raised my arm to check the fit, I could smell lamp oil and lead and the particular sourness of old solder, and for one full minute in the alley behind the burned shop I stood very still and let a dead man's coat hold me up.

Then I stopped, because the smoke was still rising behind me and standing still in this city gets you arrested.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew at dawn on the fourth day after the fire, crouched in Ilan's borrowed room above a chandlery with my hands still blistered from trying to dig through embers that no longer contained anything worth digging for.

I knew the river was locked. Reiner had done his work well; every public landing had soldiers on it, every boatmaster was answering questions, and the ice — which had been groaning all week like an old man climbing stairs — would break within days and take my window with it. I knew Ilan sat in a garrison cell being asked, in the manner of garrisons, where the courier had gone. And I knew that every honest way onto the water had been nailed shut, which meant I would have to go through a dishonest door.

There was exactly one boat leaving before the ice broke. A grain barge under contract to the governor's own household, loading at his private wharf below the citadel wall — the one stretch of bank in the whole city where Reiner's checkpoints couldn't reach without the governor's permission, because the governor did not extend permission to men who embarrassed him. Private property, imperial seal, guards paid from a different purse than the garrison's. Men who guard a different master ask different questions, and the question they ask is never *are you loyal to the state* but rather *do you belong here*.

Which meant I needed to belong there. And belonging, in this city, was a matter of paperwork and costume — two crafts I had been apprenticed to since birth.

The governor's household office kept its records at the customs annex, and the customs annex kept its forms printed by — you will forgive the irony — my father's shop. I had set that consignment manifest myself, eleven days ago, back when my largest worry was whether the proclamation paper would curl in the damp. I remembered every field of it: the barge *Marta's Grace* — no, I'm lying, it was called the *Imperial Provision*, because the governor's imagination extended precisely as far as his title — loading forty sacks of milled grain, two crates of lamp oil, and one last consignment of printed matter from Hartwell & Son, Printers, for the governor's winter correspondence stock.

Hartwell & Son. There had never been a son. My father had registered the name thirty years ago because *Hartwell & Daughter* would have invited questions, and Aldous Hartwell had built a life on not inviting questions. Now there was no son and no father either, only a daughter wearing his coat, and if the universe was keeping score of jokes, it was winning handily.

The consignment was real. That was the beautiful, terrible part of it. The governor's steward had ordered the paper stock weeks ago, and the order would be sitting in the wharf-master's book waiting to be fulfilled by a delivery that would now never come, because deliverymen whose shops burn down tend to lose their enthusiasm for the trade. All I had to do was become the man who came anyway.

Ilan would have told me it was suicide. Ilan was currently unavailable for pessimism, so I made do with my own supply.

* * *

Petrik found me at noon, or perhaps I found him — we had arranged the meeting badly, both of us too watched to stand anywhere long, and we ended up circling each other through the fish market like two suspicious cats until he muttered "the churchyard, follow slowly" and vanished among the stalls.

In the shadow of St. Aldric's bell tower, with dead leaves skidding around our boots, he looked at my father's coat and then at my face and said, "You can't pass as a man."

"Thank you. I'd wondered."

"I mean it. Your walk is wrong. You walk like someone carrying something fragile." His jaw worked. He was nineteen and had the eyes of a much older man, eyes that had seen his brother's name in a ledger of the dead and understood what his own treachery had been purchased with. "And your hands. You'll have to keep them in your pockets."

"They're ink-stained," I said. "A clerk's hands are supposed to be ink-stained."

"A clerk's hands are ink-stained on the *fingertips*. Yours are stained into the knuckles, the way they get when you wash type in cold water for years." He shook his head. "Reiner noticed that about you. First time he came to the shop. He held your hands a beat too long, remember? If he sees those knuckles—"

"He won't see them. He'll see a courier with a sealed consignment and a bad cough, and he'll want me off his wharf as quickly as possible, because the governor's men despise the garrison and the feeling is entirely mutual."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I'll improvise." I heard my father in it — *improvise, Dominic, the press jams, the type spills, the customer changes his mind at the last hour; the trade is ninety percent improvisation* — and something in my chest turned over like wet paper.

Petrik stared at the ground. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Ilan isn't talking. They took him down to the cells under the bridge and he isn't giving them anything. A fisherman's boy told me he heard one of the guards complain about it — said the man laughed at them. Laughed."

Of course he laughed. Ilan had crossed half an empire with a massacre sewn into his luggage; the garrison's questions were a formality he intended to survive out of spite. But hearing it still loosened something in my throat that I pretended was a cough.

"The ledger," Petrik said. "It's on you now."

"It's been on me since Ilan walked through the loading door." I adjusted my father's collar. Under my shirt, flat against my ribs, sewn into the lining of a waistcoat that had belonged to Marta's late brother-in-law, the true ledger of Veyra's Hollow rode against my heartbeat — forty pages recording who died and who was paid for saying they hadn't. "The question is whether it stays on me past sundown."

"There's a guard rotation change at four," Petrik said. "New shift comes on. The old shift stops checking papers carefully around three, because they want their pay and their supper, and the new shift doesn't start caring for another hour. There's a gap. It's not wide."

"Gaps are all I've ever worked with." I gave him the false travel passes we'd cut two nights before the fire, my father's finest work, done by lamplight with his hands shaking and his voice steady. "If I don't come back—"

"You'll come back."

"—then get the second set of passes to Marta Veyra, and tell her she was right to hate us, and wrong about how long." I turned up the collar. Somewhere beyond the churchyard walls, the river groaned against the ice, patient as a creditor. "And Petrik — thank you for choosing the harder thing twice."

He didn't answer. Nineteen-year-olds rarely do when the compliment lands where the wound is.

* * *

The governor's wharf sat below the citadel wall like a private mouth in a stone face, all timber and iron ring-bolts and a low customs gate guarded by two men in the household's livery — dark blue, silver trim, the posture of men paid enough to care and not so much as to be enthusiastic. Beyond the gate, the *Imperial Provision* lay against the pilings with steam rising from her deck stove, taking on the last of her cargo. The river ice pressed against her hull and retreated, pressed and retreated, like a crowd deciding whether to surge.

I had spent the walk rehearsing a limp. Not much of one — a clerk who limps invites sympathy, sympathy invites conversation — but enough to account for the walk Petrik had called wrong. I carried a document case under one arm and a sealed letter of instruction in my breast pocket, forged in Aldous Hartwell's hand, which was now the easiest forgery in the empire because I had inherited it along with his knuckles. The case held reams of good rag paper and a dozen quires of the governor's correspondence stock. The ledger rode against my ribs, going nowhere.

At the gate, the older guard — gray-whiskered, bored, with the flattened accent of a man born upriver — put out one hand without looking at me.

"Papers."

I produced them with the weary competence of a man who has shown these exact papers to eleven idiots already today. "Consignment from Hartwell and Son, printers. Final fulfillment on the steward's order. Paper stock for the household correspondence, per the attached instruction."

He read slowly. I let him. Impatience is a luxury for people with nothing to hide; I had learned that at nine years old, watching my father hand a censor a page of official proclamation with a smile that apologized for existing. Behind the guard, a stevedore swore at a sling of grain sacks. The ice boomed somewhere out midstream, a deep report like a cannon under the world.

"Hartwell and Son." He squinted at the letterhead. "You're not the one who usually comes."

"No one usually comes. This is the last order the firm is filling." I let my voice flatten appropriately. "We're closing."

That landed. Even a household guard has heard of the fire — smoke columns are civic news — and boredom flickered into something almost human. "That was your shop, then? Down on Tanner's Row?"

"It was."

He grunted, stamped the manifest, and handed it back. "Gate's the far arch. Wharf-master's at the barge ramp. Don't touch anything with the seal on it and don't talk to the captain, he's in a mood."

I thanked him and walked through, and it wasn't until I was ten steps inside that I realized my heart had been holding its breath the entire time and now presented the bill all at once, hammering against the ledger as if it wanted to read it.

* * *

The wharf-master was a different animal. Where the gate guard was bored, this man was busy, and busy men are more dangerous because they notice things. He stood at the barge ramp with a wax pencil behind his ear and a book under his arm, ticking off cargo, and when I approached he looked at me the way a compositor looks at a line of type with one character out of place.

"Print stock?" he said, before I'd spoken. "Steward's order. You're late."

"Three days late. The shop burned." I said it plainly, because plainness is armor. "This is everything that survived. The steward's instruction is sealed — I'm to deliver it to whoever signs for the consignment, and I'm told that's you."

"Everything that survived." His eyes went from my face to the case to my coat and back. "And the firm's clerk walks through a burned city to deliver paper. Loyal sort, are you?"

"My employer is dead," I said. "Loyalty is mostly habit at this point."

Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, of a tone he'd heard from other people standing in ruins. He took the case, weighed it in one hand — heavier than paper alone should feel, but I had packed the bottom with the oil-crate manifest copies to give it ballast, a trick from chapter six of my education — and flipped it open. Good rag stock, the governor's watermark, all correct. He ticked it into his book.

"Sign," he said, and held out the pencil.

I signed. *D. Hartwell.* Let him make of the initial what he liked; initials are the great neutral territory of bureaucracy. He barely glanced at it.

"That's the lot, then. You'll want the ferry landing, not here — no passenger traffic off this wharf, household rule." He was already turning away, pencil moving, mind elsewhere.

And that should have been the end of it. I should have signed, bowed, and walked back out the gate to find some other impossible route aboard. But the ice chose that moment to give a long grinding groan against the hull, loud enough that everyone on the wharf paused, and the wharf-master glanced upriver with an expression of professional anxiety, and I saw — clear as a corrected proof — that the *Imperial Provision* was short-handed. Two crew on the deck where four should be, and the mate shouting down into the hold for hands that weren't coming, because every able body in this city with reason to flee had fled, and the ones remaining were the ones nobody would hire.

A ship short-handed on the eve of the ice-break takes on anyone who can pull a rope. Especially anyone the wharf-master vouches for. Especially anyone already standing on his wharf holding a signed manifest.

I had planned to sneak aboard. Standing there in my dead father's coat, listening to the mate curse the labor market, I understood that sneaking was the amateur's route. You don't slip onto a ship through a crack. You walk on through the front door, with papers, in daylight, and you make yourself useful so fast that by the time anyone thinks to question you, you've already become part of the cargo.

"Wharf-master." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Grief, it turns out, burns hot enough to forge things. "Your barge is two hands light and the ice won't wait for your hiring problems. I grew up on this river. I'm going downriver anyway. Put me on as deckhand for the passage and dock my pay against the firm's outstanding account with the household."

He turned back and studied me with the thoroughness of a man doing sums. "Deck work's not gentle."

"Neither is this week."

"The captain will want to look at you."

"Then let's not waste his mood," I said, and picked up one end of the nearest crate of my own paper stock, because the fastest way to belong somewhere is to be caught mid-labor.

He hesitated one breath longer — one breath in which the whole plan hung like a sheet of wet paper over the press rollers — and then the ice boomed again, closer now, urgent, and practicality won whatever argument ambition was losing inside him. "Ramp. Aboard. Stay out of the cabin, stay off the stern, touch nothing with the seal." He scribbled my name — my false name, *Daniel Hartwell*, the son who had never existed finally getting his papers — into his book, and waved me toward the gangplank.

* * *

I carried my own crate up the ramp. It seemed fitting. Forty pages of the murdered truth riding against my ribs, and my arms full of the governor's blank paper, and the whole city's silence stacked in boxes around me, waiting to be filled with better words.

Halfway up the plank, the coat shifted on my shoulders and the smell of my father rose up again — lamp oil, lead, solder — and for a moment I was eight years old standing on a stool beside the case, learning which letters were quiet letters, the ones that kept people safe. He had taught me silence my whole life because silence was survival. He had died feeding a fire to keep that silence from killing me. And here I was walking onto the governor's own boat with the loudest document in the empire hidden against my heart, about to spend his last lesson like a counterfeit coin.

*I'm sorry,* I thought, to the coat, to the man, to the ash on Tanner's Row. *And thank you. And watch this.*

The mate took my crate, grunted, pointed me to the bow lines. The ice groaned and split a little further from the bank. And across the water, up the hill, at the edge of the citadel parade ground, a figure in a garrison greatcoat had stopped walking — a figure with a captain's bearing and a way of tilting his head when something interested him, the way he'd tilted it once over my ink-stained hands in a print shop that no longer existed.

Captain Reiner stood looking down at the wharf. At the barge. At, I would have sworn, me.

I bent to the bow line and coiled rope with hands that had learned steadiness from type metal, and waited to learn whether audacity was a craft or merely a slower way to die.
