## 20. Before the Ice Breaks

The river has a voice in late winter that no one who hasn't lived beside it can properly imagine. It is not the voice of water — water is polite, water murmurs — but of something enormous turning over in its sleep, groaning at the joints, muttering threats it half-intends to keep. I had heard it my whole life from behind the shop's shutters and thought I understood it. I had not understood it. There is a difference between hearing a beast through a wall and standing on its back.

That was where I stood on the morning everything ended and began: on the back of the river, aboard the *Marta's Hope*, with a dead man's ledger sewn into the lining of a funeral shroud, wearing my father's coat, which still smelled of him — lamp oil, lead dust, and the particular sweetness of ink gone soft with age.

I should say something about grief here, because everyone assumes grief is quiet. Grief is not quiet. Grief is the river. It groans under everything you do, and you learn to walk across it anyway, because the alternative is to stand still until it takes you.

* * *

We had made the governor's wharf by the grace of audacity, which is a currency I never knew I possessed until I was spending it in denominations that could get me hanged. A clerk delivering a last consignment for Aldous Hartwell, print-shop master, deceased as of two nights past — though the wharf guards did not know the second part, and I had not wept in front of them, so they assumed the first part was also invention. My father's coat did most of the work. Authority, I have learned, is largely a question of tailoring.

The manifest was forged in my own hand, set from type I had cut myself, which felt like a private joke between me and every letterform: *consignment of mourning goods and household effects, one lot, bound downriver.* The consignment was a coffin, empty, riding in the hold like an argument waiting to be won or lost. The shroud was folded inside it. The ledger was inside the shroud. And inside me, folded smaller than any of these, was the knowledge that Ilan Veyra had been taken off the garrison bridge two days ago buying me the seconds I needed to walk away, and that I did not know — could not know — what lamplight and iron were doing to him while I stood on a boat smelling of pitch and pretending to be bored.

Boredom, incidentally, is an excellent disguise. Fear reads on a face like wet ink; boredom dries matte. I practiced it against the rail while the deckhands loaded the last crates, letting my eyes go soft and slightly resentful, the expression of a clerk who would rather be anywhere else and intended to say so to no one.

Petrik found me there. He came along the deck with his collar up and his hands jammed in his pockets, and he did not look at me directly, which was itself a kind of message. Two days ago he had been the informer — the boy whose brother died in the massacre, who had sold us out for the promise of being left alone. Two days ago I had put a page in front of him with his brother's name among the dead, written in the governor's own accounting hand, priced like cargo. Petrik had looked at that page for a long time. Then he had vomited over the side of a fence, and when he straightened up his face had been scrubbed clean of everything except one thing, and the one thing was: *what do you need.*

"I heard them at the garrison this morning," he said quietly, facing the water, addressing the river rather than me. "Reiner's ordered inspections. Every boat, before lines are cast off. He's coming himself."

"Of course he is," I said. "Why delegate what you can do personally and ruinously."

"He knows the ledger's moving. The prisoner didn't tell him—" Petrik's jaw worked. "Whatever they did to him, he didn't tell them. But Reiner's not stupid. He counted the hours since the bridge and he's guessing."

Ilan hadn't told them. I let that settle into me like warmth into cold hands, and then I put it away, because warmth makes you slow and slowness on this boat today would mean the bottom of the river or the top of a scaffold, and I had promises to keep to both living people and dead ones.

"How many boats?"

"All of them going downriver. Ours is third in line." Petrik finally glanced at me. "There's no way off before inspection. The wharf gates are shut. I checked."

"You checked," I repeated. "The informer checked our escape routes for us. Forgive me if I find the symmetry unsettling."

Something flickered across his face — hurt, maybe, or the recognition that hurt was what he deserved and would be receiving in installments for years. Good, some small cruel part of me said. Let it come in installments. With interest.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"I know," I said. "It changes nothing and I accept it anyway. Go stand somewhere visible. When Reiner boards, be the first face he sees. A man hunting a traitor trusts the informer he already owns."

"And what will you be doing?"

I looked down at the coffin manifest in my hand. "Being bored," I said, "professionally."

* * *

The river groaned again as we waited, a long tectonic complaint from under the ice shelf downstream, where the frozen surface met the open current and argued about jurisdiction. The icebreaker pilots say you can hear the break coming hours before it comes — a change in the sound, from groaning to something almost like speech, almost like counting. I listened and tried to hear the counting.

Aldous Hartwell had taught me to count. Type cases, em quads, spaces — everything in the shop had a number, and everything that mattered was measured twice. He'd stood behind me at the stone with his failing eyes an inch from the forme and said, *Dominic, the difference between a printer and a scribbler is that a printer knows exactly how much room the truth requires.* I had thought it was a lesson about margins. Standing on the *Marta's Hope* with the ice counting down beneath my boots, I understood it had been a lesson about everything.

They took the second boat first. We heard it — boots on planks, a hatch thrown back, a voice with the flat patience of a man who has already decided the outcome and is merely gathering paperwork. Then silence. Then nothing. The second boat stayed tied.

"They're holding them," Petrik breathed. "He's detaining the—"

"Then ours isn't an inspection," I said. "Ours is a hunt with a customs form attached."

My hands wanted to shake. I let them want it, and declined. Somewhere below my feet, in the hold, a coffin rode the swell containing a shroud containing the true account of the Orchard Street massacre — the real numbers, the real names, the payments signed by every official who had laundered three hundred deaths into a footnote, including men who wore the garrison uniform and would be inspecting my boat within the hour. Tomas Veyra had carried that ledger out of the governor's counting-house at the cost of his name, his life as recorded, seven years of exile. Ilan had carried it to my door at the cost of his blood. My father had died feeding a fire with papers so that nothing in the ashes could point downriver to me. That ledger had cost more than its weight in gold; it had cost its weight in people. It was not going to stop forty feet short of Velmora because a captain with good posture liked to conduct his searches personally.

Though God help me, when I saw him come down the wharf, my first thought was not heroic. My first thought was: *he has my father's coat problem.* Reiner wore authority the way Aldous had worn his shop coat — like a skin grown rather than a garment chosen — and for one obscene moment I grieved for the man even as I hated him, because I recognized the fit.

* * *

He boarded without ceremony. No gangway flourish, no announcement; he simply stepped across the gap between wharf and deck as if the gap were a formality he'd personally abolished. Behind him came two soldiers and the wharfmaster, trailing like punctuation.

"Mourning goods," Reiner said, reading my manifest upside down from four feet away, which told me he'd done it often enough to have learned the trick. His eyes came up. "You're the Hartwell clerk."

"I'm the Hartwell clerk," I agreed. Bored. Matte. Slightly resentful of the weather, the delay, and the existence of captains generally.

"Aldous Hartwell." He said the name slowly, tasting it. "Loyal printer. Thirty years of proclamations, notices, tax schedules. A man who understood," and here the pause arrived, punctual as ever, "the value of printing only what is true."

"The shop prints what is commissioned," I said. "Truth is the customer's affair. We supply the letters."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not suspicion — worse. Recognition. He had heard that sentence before, or its twin, and it had come from a younger mouth in a different decade, and he had spent three years believing that mouth was closed forever.

"Tomas Veyra used to say nearly the same thing," Reiner remarked, conversational, terrible. "When he worked the garrison accounts. Before he drowned."

"The river takes its tithe," I said, and gestured vaguely at all that frozen water, bored to the point of theology.

He stepped closer. Close enough that I could see the lamplight burn had left him tired too — the hunt had cost him sleep, and sleeplessness had cost him precision, and I filed both facts where I filed everything now, in the case marked *use later*. His gaze dropped to my hands. Ink-stained, cracked at the knuckles, the right forefinger calloused in the compositor's crescent that no honest clerk develops and no dishonest one can hide.

"Ink," he said softly.

"We ship mourning goods, Captain. Someone has to address the envelopes."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the river filled the silence, groaning, counting. I watched him decide things. I watched him decide that the hands were wrong, that the manifest was too clean, that the empty coffin in the hold was either exactly what a grieving household shipped downriver or precisely where a clever woman would hide a book — and I watched him arrive, at last, at the place where suspicion meets its burden of proof and finds it heavy.

"Open the coffin," he said.

* * *

There is a moment — every printer knows it — when the forme is locked and lifted and you carry it to the stone, and if your grip slips the whole day's work spreads itself across the floor in a scatter of letters that spell nothing and cost everything. You do not slip. Your hands simply refuse, because refusal has been trained into them at the price of a hundred ruined formes. I felt that moment now, whole-body: the forme lifted, the stone approaching, the long walk during which everything depends on nothing but grip.

Petrik moved first.

"Captain." He came forward from the rail where I'd posted him, hat off, the very picture of a young man eager to be useful. "If I might — the Hartwell shipment. I processed the manifest myself at the gate. Consignment verified, seals intact, contents declared. The old man's death was registered Tuesday; the widow's household is clearing the estate. It's all in order." He smiled the small apologetic smile of clerks everywhere, the one that says *procedures exist and I am their humble servant.* "Unless the Captain wishes to open every coffin on every boat on the river, in which case the ice will break with us all still standing on the wharf, and the Governor will want to know why the spring freight is delayed over a box of mourning linen."

The silence stretched. The river counted. *Three,* I thought wildly. *Four. Break, damn you, break.*

Reiner looked at Petrik for a long time. An informer's word is a strange coin — the man spending it knows its false weight better than anyone, and hates it accordingly. But it was the coin he had, and we both knew it, and Petrik stood there letting him spend it with the serenity of a man who had already lost everything worth stealing.

"Very well," Reiner said at last. "Seals intact. The manifest stands." He turned to go, and then — because he was Reiner, and thoroughness was his religion — paused with one boot on the rail. "Clerk. When you reach Velmora, give the guild my regards. Tell them the Governor's office watches the downriver trade closely this season."

"I'll write it down," I said, "if you spell your name for me."

One of the soldiers made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh suppressed at great personal cost. Reiner's eyes rested on mine a heartbeat longer — and in that heartbeat I saw him see me, truly, the way he had perhaps seen the truth of the ledger once and burned it — and then he stepped onto the wharf and was walking away, and the lines began to cast off, and the *Marta's Hope* swung her bow toward the channel with all the urgency of a barge carrying furniture, which is to say none, and all the urgency of a revolution, which is to say everything.

* * *

The ice broke twenty minutes downstream, where the river narrows past the old quarry and the current runs fast and black beneath the sheet.

I heard it before I saw it — the counting becoming speech, the speech becoming a shout — and then the whole world ahead of us stood up. The ice didn't crack so much as surrender, mile-long panes rearing and shearing apart with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, a sound like a door opening in a house the size of a country. Spray went up silver in the morning light. The boat lurched, took the swell, and ran.

Behind us — I turned, I couldn't not turn — Reiner stood at the end of the governor's wharf, small in the distance, motionless. Watching the channel fill with moving water. Watching the season's first free current carry his quarry out beyond the reach of anything but weather. Even at that distance I could read his stillness. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he cannot make come out, subtracting a bridge, a fire, a coffin, an informer's smile, and arriving at a total he should have reached two days ago, on a frozen bridge, when a courier let himself be taken so that a woman in a dead man's coat could walk past him carrying the truth sewn into a shroud.

I raised my hand. Not a wave. Something older than a wave — the gesture a compositor makes when a page comes off the press perfect, held up to the light, shown to the shop. *Read it when it reaches you, Captain,* I thought. *Ten thousand copies. Your name is in it. Spelled correctly.*

Then the river took us around the bend, and the city was gone, and I went below to sit with my father's coat and the coffin and the shaking I had refused to do for three days, which arrived now all at once, generously, like a debt collector.

Petrik found me there after a while. He sat down on a crate opposite, said nothing for a long time, and then asked the only question that mattered:

"Velmora by tomorrow night. Will the guild be ready?"

I wiped my face with my sleeve, and underneath the grief I felt it — the thing that had been growing in me all winter under the weight of silences, breaking its ice now with the river, groaning, cracking, standing up at last.

"They'll be ready," I said. "I'll set the forme myself. And Petrik — fetch me ink. I've ten thousand names to print, and I intend to start with the ones nobody wanted read aloud."

The *Marta's Hope* ran south with the broken river, and the truth rode in an empty coffin toward morning.
