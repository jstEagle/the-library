## 21. The Reading in Velmora

The guild presses of Velmora did not sleep. Neither, by that hour, did I.

I had imagined this night a hundred times on the river — the boat groaning through the breaking ice, Reiner's face going slack as the water carried us past him, the ledger sewn into the shroud lining against my ribs like a second heart. In my imaginings there was always a moment of arrival: bells, perhaps, or an embrace, some ceremony to mark the crossing from hunted to delivered. What actually happened was a small man with ink to his elbows named Ferenc looked at me, looked at the bundle I unwrapped from its funeral linens, and said, "Well. About time someone brought us something worth printing."

Then he spat into his palms and called for compositors.

That was it. That was the whole ceremony. Seven years of silence, three checkpoints, one burned shop, one dead father, one captured friend — and the printers' guild received the truth of the Orchard massacre the way a hungry man receives bread. Not with awe. With appetite.

I loved them for it immediately.

* * *

The guildhall was a cathedral of iron and paper, all of it humming. There were six presses, four of them running already on guild business — tax notices, a funeral announcement for a councilman nobody would mourn — and Ferenc cleared them without ceremony. "The governor's proclamation can wait," he said, pulling a form off the bed like a surgeon abandoning a patient he'd never liked anyway. "It's been lying for weeks. It can lie a little longer."

By midnight, every compositor in Velmora who could be trusted — and a few, Ferenc admitted, who couldn't be trusted but were too stubborn to inform on anyone — was standing at cases under lamplight, setting the ledger page by page. The names came first. That had been Tomas's instruction in his letters, the ones Ilan had carried across half an empire in his boot: *Print the names before anything else. A man can argue with an argument. He cannot argue with his neighbor's name.*

So the names went first. Forty-one of them. Men, women, two children. The orchard hands and their families who had been shot at the governor's order in the autumn of 1843, whose deaths the official ledger recorded as "fever, seasonal" — signed, countersigned, and paid for, every signature listed alongside the sum it had cost to buy it.

I stood at a case myself. My hands knew the work better than they knew prayer; my father had made sure of that. Setting type for the truth felt no different from setting type for the governor's lies, which is either a condemnation of the craft or a testament to it, and I have gone back and forth on the question for years now and expect to die undecided. The type doesn't care what it says. It only cares that you set it true — level, tight, justified. The caring has to come from somewhere else.

Somewhere else, in my case, was a great deal of caring indeed.

"You're bleeding your ink," Ferenc said at my shoulder around three in the morning. "Too much pressure. You're angry."

"I'm not angry."

He looked at me over his spectacles the way a man looks at a child insisting the broken vase walked off the shelf on its own. "Girl, I've been pulling proofs for forty years. I know angry when I see it coming off a press." He adjusted nothing, offered nothing else, and moved on down the line. But I eased my hand after that, and the impressions came out clean, and maybe that was the whole lesson: anger isn't the enemy of good work. Unadmitted anger is.

* * *

They printed ten thousand copies before dawn.

I want that number to sit properly in your mind, because it sat improperly in mine. Ten thousand. In our shop in the river city, my father and I might pull five hundred sheets in a long day and consider ourselves blessed. Six presses running flat-out through the dark hours, apprentices ferrying reams up from the cellar, the smell of ink so thick you could chew it — by the time the windows went grey, copies were stacked in bricks of fifty, wrapped in waste paper, waiting for runners.

Ferenc counted the stacks twice, licked his thumb, turned a page of the proof, and read the last name on the list aloud just to hear it in the room: "Anka Veyra."

Marta's sister-in-law. Dead seven years, officially of fever. Actually shot in the orchard while reaching for a child who was also shot.

"The widow gets the first copy," I said.

"The widow gets the first copy," Ferenc agreed, and set it aside himself, which in a printer is roughly equivalent to knighting it.

* * *

The reading happened because of a boy.

I never learned his full name — everyone in the square called him Pip, which suggests either a nickname or parents with a sense of humor — but he was one of the runner apprentices, fourteen or so, freckled, missing a tooth, with the particular fearlessness of boys who have been beaten once too often for crimes they didn't commit. He took a stack of copies for distribution and then, instead of distributing them, climbed the fountain in the center of the square, held a single copy over his head, and began to read.

Not the preamble. Not the argument. The names.

"Jozef Marchetti. Anka Veyra. Stefan Brandt. Lise Brandt—"

A woman near the fountain dropped her market basket. I watched it happen from the arcade across the square, where I had stationed myself with the specific intention of watching everything and being seen by no one — old habits, and my father's lessons dying hard even now. The woman picked up her basket. Set it down again. Walked toward the fountain with the gait of someone walking in a dream she'd been having for years.

"Lise Brandt," Pip read again, because the crowd had grown and he was learning quickly how crowds worked. "Daughter of Stefan. Age nine. Recorded cause of death: fever, seasonal."

"It wasn't fever," the woman said. Her voice carried further than a girl of nine should have been able to make a voice carry. I understood then that she was Lise's mother, or aunt, or someone who had spent seven years carrying the word *fever* like a stone in her shoe. "It was not fever. I told them it was not fever. They wrote fever anyway."

"They wrote fever," Pip said, "because the governor paid them to write fever. Here are the payments." And he read those too — the sums, the signatures, Captain Reiner's name among them, the customs magistrate's, two men from the city council. He read the numbers plainly, without theater, the way you read a shopping list, and somehow that was worse and better than any speech could have been. It sounded true. Truth usually does sound like a shopping list. It's the lies that need music.

By the time the church bells rang eight, there were four hundred people in the square. By nine, a thousand. Someone had brought a second copy and people were passing pages hand to hand, each man or woman reading a name aloud and holding it up, and the sound of it — forty-one names, over and over, in different voices, some weeping, some furious, one old man saying each name like a toast — filled the square the way floodwater fills a cellar: quietly, completely, and then all at once everywhere.

I stood in the arcade with my back against a pillar and let it happen to me.

Here is what I had expected to feel: vindication, probably. Grief, certainly. Some clean catharsis, the kind the ballads promise, where the truth comes out and the air clears and you can finally breathe. What I actually felt was stranger and more ordinary. I felt like a compositor looking at a finished forme. All these months — the letters, the checkpoints, the shroud, the ice — I had been assembling piece by piece, locking each element tight, and now here was the pull of the lever, the sheet coming off the press, and the impression was good. Clean margins. No broken type. Every name legible.

And underneath that craftsman's satisfaction, quiet as a watermark: *Papa, look.*

He'd stayed behind in the burning shop so that nothing implicating me would survive the soldiers' search. I knew what he'd done with his last hour — I knew my father, and I knew that he would have fed the fire methodically, archive by archive, the way he did everything, checking his work. Somewhere in the smoke was every record of every seditious thing the Hartwell shop had ever set. And somewhere above the smoke, I chose to believe, was my father, doing the last job of his life well, and knowing — he must have known, he always knew things about me before I knew them myself — that the ledger would get through.

*Look, Papa,* I thought, at the thousand voices saying *Anka Veyra* like a psalm. *We set it true.*

* * *

The city did not become revolution all at once. Cities never do; that's another thing the ballads get wrong. It became revolution the way a river becomes flood — imperceptibly, then undeniably. By noon, the guildhall's doors were shut and barred, not to keep people out but to keep the presses safe, because Ferenc had ordered a second printing of twenty thousand and the runners couldn't carry stacks fast enough to satisfy demand. People came to the doors themselves. They stood in line in the cold to buy, at a penny apiece, their own griefs set in type.

Some of them, I noticed, were buying multiple copies. To send to relatives. To nail to church doors. One man bought ten and said, "For kindling," and then winked at me, and I decided some jokes are best left unexamined.

By mid-afternoon, the garrison gates had closed. By evening, someone had torn down the governor's proclamation from the customs house wall and pasted page eleven of the ledger in its place — the page with the payments. I went to look at it. The paste was crooked. The type was beautiful.

And in the middle of all of it, in the narrow street behind the guildhall where I was hauling wastepaper for pulping because work is what I do with my hands when my heart is too loud, I heard my name.

Not *Dominic*. My other name — the one Ilan used, the false one we'd built between checkpoints, the widow's alias that had gotten me past Reiner's own inspection table on the governor's wharf. Nobody in Velmora should have known it. I turned with a bale of spoil raised in both hands, ready to swing it like my father had taught me to swing anything heavy, and there he was.

Ilan. Alive. Walking with a limp that hadn't been there before the garrison bridge, thinner than the river ice had left him, wearing a coat that fit him the way a sack fits a sack. His wrists, above the too-long sleeves, bore marks I did not let myself study closely, because studying them would have meant stopping, and I was afraid that if I stopped moving I would come apart in the street.

"You're supposed to be in prison," I said.

"I was supposed to be in prison. Then your friend Petrik organized a distraction involving the guardhouse cook, a barrel of brandy, and a fire that was, I am assured, mostly accidental." He leaned against the doorframe and grinned, and the grin cost him something — I saw it cost him — and he paid it anyway. "Reiner locked down the river a day too late and interrogated me for two days and got, if I may boast, absolutely nothing worth the lamp oil. Then Velmora happened. The garrison here opened the cells when the crowd got to ten thousand, on the theory that prisoners in the courtyard would be more trouble than prisoners indoors. They weren't wrong."

"Petrik," I said. The boy whose brother's name was on the list. Who had vouched for me on the boat with Reiner standing right there, gambling his life on a forged ledger and a stranger's nerve. "He's here?"

"He's distributing. He has a talent for crowds." Ilan's grin faded into something quieter. He looked past me into the guildhall, at the presses still thundering, at the apprentices elbow-deep in type. "Your father," he said. "I heard. I'm sorry. He was — from everything you've told me —"

"A compositor," I said. "The best I ever met."

Ilan nodded, accepting the shape of the eulogy, because he understood — he of all people understood — that some men are best described by their work. Then he pushed off the doorframe, limped past me into the ink-smelling dark, picked up a composing stick from the bench, weighed it in his hand like a man testing a sword, and said:

"So. Twenty thousand more. Where do you need me?"

* * *

We worked until the lamps burned out. I set names; Ilan locked formes; Petrik ferried bundles to the runners and came back each time with news of the squares — the plaza by the cathedral, the market district, the docks, where the boatmen had reportedly stopped working entirely and were reading the ledger aloud from gangplank to gangplank. The governor's residence went dark early. Whether that was prudence or flight, no one yet knew.

Near dawn, Ferenc pulled the first proof sheet of the second edition, held it to the light, and handed it to me without a word. I read it standing in the doorway where the cold came in and the noise of the presses went out, and I found my father's craft in every line — no, not his craft alone. Mine. Ours. The trade he had given me in silence, in hidden compartments and pi fonts and water-soluble inks, the inheritance disguised as chores, the love disguised as correction. *Level, tight, justified.* Everything he knew, pressed into my hands like flowers in a book.

Somewhere across the mountains, the shop was ash. Somewhere in that ash was the case where I'd learned my letters. But type, Papa used to say, is the one thing that burns without dying. Melt it down and it's lead; cast it again and it says whatever you dare to say next.

I folded the proof and put it inside my coat, over my ribs, where the ledger had ridden. It fit there. It had learned the spot.

Outside, the sun was coming up over Velmora, and in the square below, a new sound had started — not names this time, but a single word, hundreds of voices strong, growing like the crack in the ice before the whole river lets go. I couldn't make out what they were saying yet.

But I knew we would print it by noon.
