## 22. A New Face for Old Words

There is a particular sound a graver makes when it bites into steel that wants to hold its shape — a thin, stubborn resistance, like a word someone refuses to say. I have spent the better part of six months listening to it. My father spent forty years listening to it. I used to think that made us the same kind of person. Now I know better: he listened to it the way a man listens to a debt collector at the door, and I listen to it the way a woman listens to her own pulse after she has finally stopped running.

The typeface has no name yet. That is a lie; it has had four names, and I have burned three of them in the stove.

The first I called *Veyra*, because grief is sentimental and so was I, in those first weeks in Velmora when everything smelled of other people's smoke. Ilan looked at the proof sheet for a long time and said, kindly, "It doesn't look like him." It didn't. Tomas's hand had been all flourishes and confidence, letters that leaned forward as if hurrying somewhere important. My letters lean back. They take their time. They were cut by a woman who learned that haste is what gets you searched at checkpoints.

The second name I never told anyone, which is perhaps the truest way to name something.

The third was my father's full name, Aldous Hartwell, set proud across the specimen page — and then Petrik read it aloud in the shop one evening, saying it the way you'd say the name of a street where something terrible happened, and I scraped it off the steel before the wax had cooled. Not because it wasn't his. Because it wasn't only his. A name is a kind of border, and borders are what got everyone killed.

So the fourth attempt sits half-finished on my bench this morning, and I have decided — in the way you decide things at dawn, when the stove has just caught and the light through the frosted window is the color of weak tea — that it will be called simply *Aldous*. One name. The way you'd call across a workshop to a man bending over his case. The way he'd answer without looking up: *What.*

I put the graver to the crossbar of an A and let the steel resist me.

* * *

The shop is not the shop. I want to be clear about that, because people in Velmora keep calling it "the rebuilt Hartwell printery" as if buildings could be resurrected like saints. What we have is a rented ground floor on Tanner's Row with a sagging lintel, a press bought secondhand from a widow who swore it had printed nothing but wedding invitations for twenty years (a lie, I'm fairly sure, but a forgivable genre), and a type case inventory that would make my father weep — half pi, half worn Garamond, and one drawer of sorts so badly kerned I suspect the previous owner set type drunk or furious, possibly both.

But the floorboards are ours until Ladyday, and the stove draws well, and there is a loading door at the back that opens onto the canal, and when I stand at that door in the early morning I can see ice going gray and rotten at the edges, and I think: *this time last year I was setting lies for a man who is now in prison, and the river was about to break, and I did not yet know what my hands could do.*

My hands know now. That is the whole inheritance, really. Not the tricks — though I kept the tricks, the hidden compartments in the type cases and the water-soluble ink recipe, written out fair and sealed, because some knowledge should survive even if it's never needed again. No. The inheritance is simpler and heavier: my hands know their own weight. When I set a line now, I feel every letter as a decision. My father set type the way penitents pray, each character an apology. I set it the way witnesses testify. Same craft. Different covenant.

The bell over the front door rattled — it rattles rather than rings, another bargain-rate feature — and I knew who it was by the rhythm. Two quick steps, a pause, one more step. Petrik still enters rooms like a man checking whether the room will forgive him.

"You're early," I said, without turning from the bench.

"You're late," he said. "Guild meeting's at nine."

"The guild can meet without me. The guild met without me for years and seemed perfectly happy."

"The guild," Petrik said, coming to stand beside me at the window, "is arguing about whether to admit women to full membership or create some insulting auxiliary category, and Marta said if you're not there to say the thing you say, she'll say it worse, and then there'll be a riot, and she's too old to run from soldiers now."

"I'm twenty-four."

"She said that too. She said, *tell her being twenty-four didn't stop her from toppling a government.*" He shrugged. "Her words. I'm only the messenger. Don't shoot me."

"I'd need a pistol, and the guild dues don't cover pistols." I wiped the graver, set down the A — good crossbar, honest serif, the steel had given in — and reached for my coat. Then stopped. "Petrik."

He knows that tone. Everyone who works with me longer than a week knows that tone. It's the tone that means *before we go out into the world where we perform our competence, here in this doorway, the truth.* He waited.

"The new face. When it's done. I want the first proof pulled on the old press paper — the stock from Father's shop. Marta kept some. I asked her last week and she said yes without asking why."

He considered this with the seriousness he gives everything now, as if caution were a discipline he practices daily, like scales on a fiddle. "She'll want to pull it herself."

"She'll want to watch me pull it. There's a difference, and I've learned to tell the difference."

* * *

The guild hall smelled of tallow and argument. Forty printers crammed into a room built for thirty, breath fogging, voices climbing — the revolution's aftertaste, everywhere in Velmora these past months: committees, factions, a man selling pamphlets outside every public building, three of them mine. We had won, in the way that rivers win. Not cleanly, not completely. The governor was gone east under guard; Reiner's trial had been proceeding all winter, slow as frost forming on glass, and every week the papers carried another name from the ledger read into the record. Ten thousand copies had become fifty thousand, then uncountable — passed hand to hand, read aloud in taverns, copied in schoolrooms. You cannot unring a bell. My father taught me that phrase as a warning. It turns out it was instructions.

"—and furthermore," Councillor Brandt was saying, a heavy man with a heavy voice, "the question of *associate* membership for—"

"The question," I said, standing, and the room did that thing rooms do now when I stand, which I have never gotten used to and privately suspect I never will, "is whether a woman who sets type is a printer or a curiosity. I set the proclamation that emptied the square in November. I set the ledger itself. If that work makes me a curiosity, then I'd like to meet the member whose work makes him anything else."

Silence. Then Marta, in the third row, said clearly, "Vote," and they voted, and it wasn't even close, and afterward Brandt shook my hand with the expression of a man swallowing a lemon whole and finding, to his surprise, that it was nourishing.

Walking out, Marta took my arm. She does that more than she used to. Age has bent her toward leaning on things, and she has decided I am a thing worth leaning on, and I have decided to be load-bearing.

"You'll come Sunday," she said. Not a question.

"For the proof-pulling, yes."

"Not just that." She glanced at me sideways, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen since the wharf — that ferocious, unsentimental tenderness she wields like a boat hook. "It's the anniversary. Of the burning. Ilan's coming up from the river district. Petrik's bringing his mother." A pause. "His mother, Dominic. Six months ago that woman wouldn't say my name in the street. Now she brings*kolaches*to meetings of the families of the dead. Things mend. Slowly, ugly, but they mend. You should be there for the mending."

"I'll be there," I said, and meant it, and felt the small familiar ache underneath the meaning — the ache that is grief's permanent lodger, paying no rent, asking nothing, simply *there*, the way a scar is there. My father's coat still hangs by my door. I wear it on Sundays.

* * *

Sunday came gray and cold, with a wind off the river that smelled of open water. The ice was breaking early this year. You could hear it at night from Tanner's Row — long groans, sharp cracks, the river clearing its throat after a winter of holding its peace. Ilan says he sleeps better to the sound. He spent three years sleeping to silence, and silence, for people like us, has never once meant safety.

They gathered in the shop at dusk: Marta in her good black, Petrik and his mother, Ilan damp-smelling of river fog, and a dozen others from the families' circle, filling the little shop with breath and warmth until the windows ran with condensation. On the press bed lay the day's work: the first complete proof of the Aldous face — a specimen sheet, alphabet and numerals, and beneath them, in the new-cut letters, a single line I had chosen weeks ago and rehearsed setting until my hands could do it blind:

*What is set in truth cannot be unset.*

Father's phrasing. His, from the old days, from the lessons in disappearing — except he had always said the opposite, in effect: *what is set can be unset, struck, redistributed, denied.* He believed in erasure as survival. I had taken his sentence and turned it inside out, the way you turn a glove right-side out, and it fit a different hand entirely. I hoped he'd have minded. I hoped he'd have minded terribly, and then, after shouting about it over dinner, quietly approved. That was how he loved things: first as obstacles, then as givens.

Marta stood at the press while I inked the forme. Her hands — Tomas's hands, the same long fingers, though hers had gone knuckled with age and rope-work — hovered near the tympan, not touching.

"You pull it," she said suddenly. "No. Wait." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Both of us. Your hands on the bar, mine over yours. Like teaching a child to row."

"That's exactly what it is," I said. "Only the child is the typeface."

She laughed — short, cracked, real — and put her hands over mine, and together we pulled the bar, and the press spoke its one word, and the impression was good. Deep enough to bite, clean enough to read. Honest pressure. My father would have checked it against the light, tilted it, squinted. I did the same, and handed it to her wet, and she held it the way you hold a letter you've waited years to receive.

"He'd have hated the serifs," she said, voice unsteady. "Too much shoulder. He liked them mean and narrow, Tomas did."

"It's not named for Tomas."

"No." She touched the word *Aldous* at the foot of the sheet, careful, as if the ink might still smudge — it wouldn't; oil ink sets fast; but grief makes you gentle with objects. "For the man who kept my husband's letters seven years and never found the courage to send them." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and fierce. "And for the girl who found the courage instead. Don't argue with me. I'm older than you and I buried more."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to argue. You get a line right here—" she tapped the space between her own eyebrows "—that means *preparing a rebuttal*. Your father had the identical line. It's uncanny. It's like being haunted by someone polite."

Around us the shop had gone quiet, the visitors watching with the particular reverence people give to presses and altars — both, in my experience, being machines for making words flesh. Petrik's mother stepped forward and, to my astonishment, kissed my cheek, smelling of cinnamon and woodsmoke, and said in her river-district accent, "My boy came home different after your bridge. Quieter. But a better quiet." And I understood — I hope I understood — that she meant it as the highest compliment our trade allows: that some silences are chosen, and choosing changes everything.

Ilan stayed after the others drifted out into the cold. He stood at the loading door, looking at the canal, hands in his pockets, and I joined him because that is where conversations with Ilan happen — side by side, facing water, both of us having learned that eye contact is for interrogators.

"The route's reopening," he said. "Spring traffic. The guild wants couriers again — legitimate ones, manifest papers, the whole honest machinery. They asked if I'd organize it." A pause. "They asked if *we'd* organize it."

"We?"

"You're the best forger on the river, Dominic. Don't look offended; it's a compliment in this economy. And I'm the best liar. Between us we could move anything — truth, mostly, but occasionally a fugitive, if the times require."

The river groaned downstream, long and low, a sound like the world shifting in its sleep.

"My father organized routes," I said. "Quietly. For years. It nearly killed him, and then it did."

"Yes," Ilan said. "And it also saved a ledger, and a city, and several hundred people I could name. The arithmetic doesn't balance. It never balances. You just choose which column you're willing to add to." He turned his head, and in the dark his eyes had the patience of a man who has been tortured and survived it and now finds ordinary life mildly boring. "You don't have to answer tonight."

"I know." I watched the last white plates of ice go grinding past the loading door, riding the current, breaking apart as they went, each one carrying a little piece of winter off toward the sea. "That's the difference, Ilan. He taught me to wait. He just never got to teach himself to stop waiting. I answer tonight."

I held out my hand, ink-stained, scarred at the thumb where the graver slipped in February — my blood is in that typeface now, literally, worked into the steel along with everything else — and he shook it, firm, and that was the whole contract. Some agreements need no paper. Though naturally we'd draw up papers too; I'd cut the form myself, in the Aldous face, and every clause would be legible.

Inside, the stove ticked as it cooled. On the press bed lay the proof, drying toward permanence. Tomorrow I would hang it on the wall above my father's portrait — the sketch Petrik made from my description, since no photograph survived the fire — and tomorrow after that, the first job would come in, and then the next, and the apprentice I'd promised the guild I'd take on in spring would arrive, some nervous boy or girl with ink-hungry hands and a head full of questions, and I would teach them what my father taught me and what he never quite believed himself:

That a letter is a small machine for refusing to disappear. That silence is a material, like lead, and you can cast it into anything. And that the first lesson of the trade is not how to hide the words —

It is how to make them too heavy, ever, to burn.

Outside, the river broke another mile of ice, all the way to Velmora, all the way to spring.
