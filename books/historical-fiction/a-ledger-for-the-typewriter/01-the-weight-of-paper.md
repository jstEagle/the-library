## 1. The Weight of Paper

There were three hundred and eleven letters in the cedar box under Marguerite Delacroix's bed, and she had read every one of them twice.

She knew this the way other women knew their prayers—by heart, by number, by the small rituals of repetition. The box had come to her honestly, or as honestly as anything came to a person who had reinvented herself out of thin air and a stolen birth certificate. It belonged to Madame Aubert's household, technically. Everything in the house on the Rue Sainte-Victoire belonged to Madame Aubert, including, in some sense that Marguerite preferred not to examine too closely, Marguerite herself. But the letters were hers to sort. That was the arrangement. Madame Aubert received an enormous correspondence—petitions, appeals, angry clergymen, hopeful schoolmistresses, one persistent gentleman in Lyon who wrote weekly proposals of marriage and was answered weekly with a form letter—and someone had to open it all, sort it, summarize it, and decide what was worth the mistress's morning.

Marguerite was that someone.

She worked at night, after the house settled, with the gas turned low and her door locked, though locking it was theater more than precaution. No one in the household would dream of disturbing her. She was the quiet one, the driver who kept the automobile immaculate and her opinions to herself, the woman who had arrived four years ago with one suitcase and no history worth asking about. In Marseille, a city built entirely of arrivals, nobody asked. That was the city's great mercy: it assumed everyone was fleeing something, and it extended the courtesy of not naming what.

She opened the first letter of the evening—a bill from a stationer, unpaid since March—and set it in the discard pile. Then a petition from a seamstress in Toulon regarding wages, which went to the keep pile. Then, wedged between them, a letter from a woman in Aix describing her husband's temper in language so frank that Marguerite found herself smiling at the ceiling like a girl.

"You would have liked her," she said aloud, to no one.

That was another ritual. She spoke to the empty room because it was the only conversation she could afford. Four years of practice had made the habit almost comfortable—the way a limp becomes part of your walk if you carry it long enough. There were things you did when you lived alone inside a constructed person. You narrated. You kept ledgers. You counted.

* * *

The counting was the oldest discipline, older than the letters. It began in 1909, in a barracks near Lyon, on the night Henri Delacroix decided he could not be a soldier anymore—or rather, on the night he understood that the army had already decided what he was, and that the decision was wrong in a way that would eventually kill him.

He had not been brave about it. He wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in his own head: he had not been brave. He had been frightened and methodical, which is a different animal altogether. He waited for a night exercise in the hills, walked into the dark during a halt, and kept walking until the dark became permanent. He took nothing but the clothes he wore, a razor, and forty francs. He left behind a uniform, a mother who had died believing him steady, a father who had died long enough before that to be spared everything, and a name that fit like a boot two sizes too small.

In Avignon, three days later, he bought a dress from a secondhand shop run by a widow who asked no questions because widows who ask questions sell fewer dresses. He paid for it with money he had saved for a bicycle. The dress was grey wool, serviceable, slightly too large. He remembered standing in the fitting area behind the curtain—not a fitting room, just a curtain hung from a nail—looking down at the hem and feeling something he had no word for yet. Not joy. Relief, maybe, but relief is a poor cousin of joy; relief is just the absence of dread, and dread always comes back.

The word came later, in bits and pieces, over years. *Comfortable*, perhaps. *Correct*. Or simply *mine*.

He learned to walk differently, to pitch his voice into its upper register where it sat naturally anyway—he had never been a baritone even as Henri—and to let silence do the work that performance couldn't. Women who were quiet were called reserved. Men who were quiet were called suspicious. This asymmetry struck him early as the single most useful fact in the world, and he built his entire new life on top of it like a foundation.

The name Marguerite came from a daisy growing through a crack in the courtyard of the pension in Avignon where he spent his first week as himself, sleeping badly and eating less, waiting for the panic either to pass or to finish him off. It passed. The daisy stayed. He took the name partly for the flower and partly because it sounded like a woman who paid her bills on time, which was exactly the reputation he needed.

The birth certificate was a different matter. That required forgery, and forgery required learning, and learning required a month in Marseille watching the clerks at the civil registry the way a painter watches light. He discovered that official documents had rhythms—that a real certificate was never quite symmetrical, that ink varied, that the truth was always slightly crooked in ways a forger must learn to imitate. Perfection gave you away. He practiced on grocery lists. By autumn he had a document saying Marguerite Delacroix, born 1888 in Nîmes, parents deceased, occupation: none stated. The Nîmes registry had burned in a fire years back—this was true, and therefore uncheckable, which is the best kind of true.

Five years of being Marguerite had taught him one governing principle, which he thought of as the suitcase rule: *own nothing that cannot be carried out of a room in ninety seconds.*

Everything he possessed fit one suitcase. Two dresses, one good coat, gloves, a hat with a modest brim, a brush, a razor hidden in a false bottom beneath the linings along with Henri's dog tags, sewing supplies, a photograph of no one, and a Bible he never read but which lent respectability to the contents should a landlady ever peek. The suitcase lived under the bed, packed, next to the cedar box of letters. He rehearsed the packing sometimes, on insomniac nights, timing himself against the church bells. Best time: seventy-four seconds. He was quietly proud of this and told absolutely no one.

* * *

Mornings belonged to the automobile.

Madame Aubert kept a 1911 De Dion-Bouton, black, well-maintained, and Marguerite drove it the way some men rode horses—with a competence so complete it looked like affection. She had learned mechanics from a chauffeur named Baptiste in her first year, trading kitchen shifts for lessons, and now she could strip the carburetor by feel. On the mornings when the engine sulked, she fixed it before anyone else was awake, sleeves rolled, hands black to the wrist, humming. These were the hours when she was happiest, though she would have denied it under oath. Happiness was a liability. You didn't put happiness in the suitcase.

At eight sharp she would present herself at the kitchen door in driving goggles and gloves, and Madame Aubert would descend the front steps with the unhurried majesty of a cathedral.

"Marguerite."

"Madame."

"The meeting ran late again. I have told Camille we are not a printing concern." They were, in fact, becoming a printing concern, but that was said in the house, in low voices, behind doors Marguerite was trusted enough to stand outside of. "Aix by ten. Take the Corniche road; the sea road settles my thoughts."

"Yes, madame."

This exchange happened every morning in some variation, and Marguerite treasured its monotony the way sailors treasure routine. Repetition meant safety. Safety meant survival. And survival, she had decided sometime around year three, was its own kind of success, even if it looked from the outside like nothing at all.

Madame Aubert talked while they drove. She talked the way rain falls—continuously, generously, requiring no response. Politics, mostly, and the movement, and the various disappointments of the Chamber of Deputies, and the moral failings of specific deputies whom she skewered with a precision Marguerite privately admired. Sometimes she asked Marguerite's opinion, and Marguerite gave careful, moderate answers, having learned that the safest opinions are the ones a lady might plausibly have absorbed from a newspaper.

"You're thinking about something," Madame Aubert said one morning, apropos of nothing, eyes on the sea.

"The road, madame."

"You've been thinking about the road for four years." A pause. "It's a fine road. But it isn't a thought."

Marguerite kept her hands easy on the wheel and her face arranged into pleasant blankness, and understood, with a small cold shock, that she had been seen—not caught, nothing so crude as caught, merely *noticed*—and that this had apparently been happening for some time without incident. Madame Aubert hired people she noticed. It was, perhaps, the whole secret of her.

* * *

Evenings, the letters. Nights, the counting. And in between, the loneliness, which she managed the way one manages a chronic ailment: with schedule and denial.

Here is what four years of constructed living actually felt like, though Marguerite would never have described it this way to anyone, there being no one: it felt like reading a very good book about another woman's life. The prose was excellent. The details rang true. Marguerite Delacroix drove well, sewed competently, held her tea cup correctly, laughed at the right moments in the right register. But every few days, usually at dusk, Marguerite would look up from the page of her own life and feel the strange vertigo of remembering that she was also the author, alone at a desk, with no one in the world who knew both halves.

She had remedies. Work, principally. The letters helped more than she admitted, because other people's correspondence is a kind of company—you sit inside their worries and joys, you learn how marriages fray and how sisters reconcile, you become fluent in humanity without once having to risk your own. It was intimacy at one remove, like warming yourself at a fire in a window across the street.

She knew things about Marseille that no census would ever record. She knew that the florist on the Rue Paradis wrote love letters for illiterate dockworkers and charged by the adjective. She knew that a judge's wife was paying off a debt to a man in Toulon and that the payments had stopped in May. She knew that half the petitions to Madame Aubert contained private sorrows folded inside public causes like notes passed in class. She carried all this silently, a confessor without a booth, and told herself it was a substitute for a life.

Some nights she almost believed it.

* * *

On the last evening of July 1914—it was a Friday; she would remember the day forever afterward, the way you remember the last ordinary day of anything—Marguerite sat cross-legged on her bed with the cedar box open and the gas lamp turned low, working through the afternoon's post.

Mostly it was the usual cargo: subscriptions, appeals, a furious letter from a bishop. Near the bottom of the stack she found an envelope she hadn't seen before, addressed in a hand she didn't recognize, and she opened it with the small mechanical pleasure she took in the work—the letter knife, the crisp tear, the unfolding.

It was from a typist. She could tell immediately, because the handwriting had that curious hybrid quality of women trained on machines: print-clear letters, machine-even spacing, as though the pen were slightly embarrassed not to be a keyboard. The letter concerned employment—an inquiry whether Madame Aubert's circle needed additional copyists for the autumn petition drive, signed *Liesl Brandt*, with an address in the Rue des Repentirs, up in the Panier.

Marguerite read it twice, and on the second reading noticed what she had missed the first time: the paper was French, the ink was French, the phrasing was nearly perfect—but the name. Liesl. Not Élise, not Lieselotte rendered properly for French postmasters. Liesl, plain and unconverted, sitting at the bottom of the page like a stone in a shoe.

German.

Marguerite set the letter down and looked at it for a long moment, feeling something move in the room that had nothing to do with the summer air. She thought: *that is a person who has done the same arithmetic I do every day.* Weighed her name. Decided what to keep, what to trim, what to carry.

She put the letter in the keep pile. Then, because the impulse was irrational and she was a disciplined woman, she moved it to the top of the pile, where Madame Aubert would be sure to see it first in the morning.

Then she sat in the lamplight with her hands in her lap and listened to the bells of Notre-Dame de la Garde strike ten, and told herself, as she told herself every night, that she was safe, that the system worked, that the suitcase rule had kept her alive five years and would keep her alive fifty more.

Outside, far away in another country whose newspapers she would begin reading obsessively within the week, something was already moving toward her like weather on the horizon. But that night Marseille was warm and smelled of salt and petrol, and Marguerite Delacroix blew out the lamp, lay down beside her packed suitcase, and slept the sleep of a woman who believes she has accounted for everything.

She had forgotten, of course, to account for one thing. She had forgotten that a letter can be a door, and that she had just, quite deliberately, opened one.
