## 2. Madame Aubert's Salon

The Delaunay-Dubois taxicab had been built for four passengers and a driver's optimism, and it carried both badly. Marguerite drove it the way he did everything—carefully, quietly, with an eye always on the exits—and on Thursday evenings he drove it down the Canebière with Madame Aubert in the back seat like a cannonball wrapped in grey silk.

"You're brooding, Marguerite," she said, not looking up from the pamphlet she was annotating in pencil.

"I'm driving, madame."

"You can do both. Most people manage neither." She made a small, decisive mark. "You brood the way other women knit. It keeps your hands busy while your mind does the real work."

He let that pass, as he let most things pass, because passing was the whole art of his life. Five years of passing. He had learned to laugh at a pitch slightly higher than felt natural, to walk with his hips a little looser than felt natural, to accept the small indignities of corsetry as taxes levied on freedom. He had learned, above all, to be unremarkable—a woman of no particular beauty and no particular oddity, whose face a gendarme would forget before she reached the corner.

It was remarkable how much skill went into being nobody.

"Madame Vidal will speak tonight," Madame Aubert continued. "On property rights for married women. Half the room will agree with her and the other half will call her a Bolshevik, which in Marseille is roughly the same thing as calling her Tuesday."

"And you?"

"I shall call her ambitious, which is worse."

The taxi turned off the boulevard into the narrower streets behind the Préfecture, where the light came down in slices between tall shuttered houses. The salon met in the back room of a dressmaker's shop, which was perfect: a room full of women with needles was invisible, and a room full of women with typewriters required only slightly more imagination.

Marguerite parked in the alley, checked the street both ways out of habit rather than fear, and carried Madame Aubert's document case inside through the shop, past bolts of fabric and a mannequin wearing half a bodice like a woman caught mid-argument.

The back room smelled of carbon paper and cold coffee. There were six typewriters under sheets, and the sheets were already coming off, and the women were already arriving—the way they did every Thursday, two or three at a time, never together, never in a group large enough to look like anything. A petition to the Chamber of Deputies. Two hundred copies by midnight. The words were mild; the act of typing them was not, quite yet, but everyone in the room understood they were living on the forgiving side of a line.

Madame Aubert shed her coat and became, instantly, a general. "Renée, you take the first section. Camille, the French in your last batch looked like it had been written during an earthquake. And someone find me more ribbon—we are not going to run out of ink before we run out of courage."

There was laughter, the low warm kind, the kind Marguerite stood just outside of. He set the document case on the sideboard and took up his usual post by the window, where a chair had been left for him without anyone ever discussing it. His job was to watch the alley, mind the door, and drive people home afterward one at a time so that no two departures could be connected. He was furniture with a license.

He was good at it. He had been good at it for five years.

Which is why it unsettled him, that evening, to realize he was watching someone else who was also watching.

* * *

Liesl Brandt had come to Marseille three years ago with a trunk, a typewriter, and a French vocabulary assembled from newspapers and a governess with Provençal vowels. She had learned quickly that the city rewarded speed—of hand, of tongue, of departure—and she had made herself quick at all three.

She typed beautifully. This was her one open secret, the thing she displayed precisely because it explained her presence everywhere else. A woman who types that well must be useful; a useful foreigner is tolerated; tolerance, carefully maintained, looks almost like belonging. She typed letters for the notary on rue Paradis. She typed addresses for the charity committee. On Thursdays she typed petitions in a back room behind a dressmaker's shop, because Madame Aubert had once heard her machine and said, "That girl has the fastest hands in Marseille," and Liesl had decided that being flattered into conspiracy was safer than being questioned into exile.

Her fingers were always stained. She had tried pumice, lemon, a patent soap from Paris advertised with a drawing of a triumphant housewife. Nothing touched the ink that worked its way into the whorls of her fingertips, and eventually she stopped trying. It was, she thought, a fair trade: her hands bore the marks of what they did, even if nothing else about her was allowed to.

She noticed him the third week she came to the salon—the driver. Tall for a woman, quiet, with hands too careful for the work and eyes that moved over a room the way a customs officer moves over luggage. Everyone else treated him as part of the building. Liesl, who had spent three years studying how invisibility was manufactured, recognized another artisan at work.

She noticed, first, that "Marguerite" sat like a coat rather than a name—not ill-fitting exactly, but worn in the wrong places, the way clothes wear when a body underneath keeps trying to stand differently.

She noticed, second, that when Renée dropped a page, he caught it before it hit the floor, and his reflexes belonged to someone who had once been taught to catch things falling from greater heights than paper.

She noticed, third—and this was the thing that kept her awake that night—that when he thought no one was looking, he read. Not the petitions; those were handed to him freely enough when a signature needed checking. Letters. Anything left near the sideboard. A note from Camille to her sister, left folded but not sealed. He read it in four seconds and put it back exactly as it had been, crease aligned, corner squared, and then he looked out the window with the expression of a man listening to distant weather.

Most people would have called it snooping and thought no more about it.

Liesl lay in her rented room on the rue Sainte and thought about it a great deal. Because there was reading and there was reading, and she knew the difference. Her father had been a postal clerk in Strasbourg, and she had grown up understanding that a letter is a locked room and that some people carry keys they don't advertise. The driver read the way her father described certain colleagues reading—not idly, not greedily, but *completely*, the way you'd read something you might someday need to have memorized.

And she had letters of her own. Letters she wrote to her sister Greta in Strasbourg and did not send, because sending them would be madness—what would she say? *Dear Greta, I am typing sedition in a dressmaker's back room and my hands smell of ink and I am, against all sense, happy.* The unsent letters lived in her trunk, addressed, stamped, ready, a correspondence with a country that had stopped answering.

She watched the driver watch the room, and she thought: *there is a person in there doing the same arithmetic I do.* Counting the exits. Weighing each acquaintance. Asking, constantly and silently, *what does this person see, and what do they do with what they see.*

* * *

Thursday after Thursday, the observation became mutual and unspoken, a duel conducted entirely in glances across a room of humming typewriters.

Marguerite told himself he was simply doing his job—watching everyone, cataloguing everyone, the discipline that kept him alive. But he found his eye returning to the German girl with unusual frequency, and he began to construct a file on her the way he constructed files on all threats: *Brandt, Liesl. Born Strasbourg, presumably. Typist. Ink-stained fingers. Watches me.*

*Watches me* was the entry that bothered him. Nobody watched him. That was the entire architecture of his existence—he had built himself out of materials specifically designed not to hold the eye. Grey dresses. A walk studied in mirrors until it passed. A voice kept soft and level. And here was this girl, hunched over her Underwood, who looked up at him perhaps twice an hour with an attention so direct it felt physical, like a hand laid flat against his shoulder blade.

He tested her, once, deliberately. He left a sealed letter of Madame Aubert's on the sideboard while he went to fetch coffee, timing himself—forty seconds, maybe fifty. When he returned, the letter was where he'd left it, angle unchanged, wax unbroken.

But the seal had been lifted. He was nearly sure. There was a faintness to it, a whisper of steam and patience, done by someone who knew the trick of a kettle and a knife. And the girl was typing with her head down, industrious, her ink-stained fingers moving like small black-tipped birds.

*Well,* he thought, somewhere between alarm and a strange, unwelcome delight. *Well, well.*

Two artisans, recognizing tool marks.

* * *

In June the heat came early, and the salon's windows stayed shut against the street noise, and the room grew close with the smell of hot metal and women's sweat and the particular sweetness of ribbon ink baking in the sun. The petition that night was a longer one—an appeal regarding conditions in the textile mills, three pages, four hundred copies—and the mood was tired and a little sharp-edged. Camille snapped at Renée over a misplaced comma. Madame Aubert, who governed her little republic with benevolent tyranny, restored order by producing a bottle of cherry cordial from her document case, because she believed, correctly, that no committee survives on conviction alone.

It was near eleven when the last copies came off the machines, and the women began their careful staggered departures, two here, three there, Marguerite ferrying them into the night like a man bailing out a boat with a teaspoon.

Liesl was last but one. She packed her typewriter into its wooden case—she carried it herself always, refusing help, because the case held papers of hers that were no one's business—and stood a moment in the doorway while Marguerite held the outer door for her.

The alley was dark and smelled of dust and cut lavender from the dressmaker's bins. Somewhere above, a cat was making its case to the moon.

"Your seal," Marguerite said quietly.

Liesl went still.

"The letter in May. You lifted it and put it back. Clean work—I'll grant you that. The kettle trick?"

For a long moment she said nothing. He could feel her deciding, the way he could feel weather deciding. Then she turned and looked at him fully, in the dark, where faces matter less and eyes matter more, and her gaze did the thing it always did—it *saw* him, straight through five years of careful construction, and he felt naked in a way that had nothing to do with corsetry.

"I could ask about your hands," she said.

"My hands?"

"They're a soldier's hands." She said it without accusation, almost gently, the way you'd note that the sky was blue. "I've shaken a hundred of them at charity functions. Yours are the first that ever made me want to check for calluses." She shifted the typewriter case in her grip. "But I won't ask. That's the arrangement I'm offering. I don't ask about your hands, you don't ask about my seal, and neither of us asks why the other needs this room."

"That's not an arrangement," Marguerite said. "That's a hostage exchange."

"It's Marseille," she said, and something in her mouth almost became a smile. "Same thing."

She walked to the corner and turned toward the rue Sainte, and he watched her go, and the strange thing—the thing he examined later, lying awake in his narrow bed, listening to the city breathe—was that he didn't feel exposed. He felt, for the first time in five years, *witnessed*. There was a difference. Exposure was danger. Witnessing was—

He couldn't find the word. He fell asleep without finding it, which hadn't happened since 1909.

* * *

Liesl, walking home through the warm dark, found her own word almost immediately, and it frightened her.

*Ally,* she thought. Or possibly *accomplice.* Possibly, if she was very careless, *friend.*

She climbed the four flights to her room, lit the lamp, and took the unsent letters from her trunk—the fat bundle of them, tied in string, addressed to Greta in a handwriting that grew more confident year by year. She untied them, as she did most nights, and reread the earliest one, from three years ago, when she still believed France would feel temporary:

*Dear Greta — The light here is yellow. Not gold, yellow, like old paper. Everything here seems written down already, waiting for someone to read it properly. I have taken a room with a view of a courtyard where a fig tree grows against the wall, which I am assured is impossible this far north, and yet.*

She touched the page and thought about the driver's face when she'd named his hands. Not fear—she had expected fear, had braced for it, had rehearsed retreats. Something closer to relief. As though she had spoken aloud a thing he'd been carrying alone for years and the carrying had gotten heavy.

*People are not what the paperwork says,* she wrote sometimes, in these letters no one would read. *Paperwork says I am German, therefore suspect. Paperwork says Madame Aubert is a wealthy eccentric, therefore harmless. Paperwork says the driver is a woman, and paperwork, Greta, is lying through its teeth.*

She smiled at that line, then stopped smiling, because it occurred to her—as it occurred to her every few weeks and was becoming more frequent—that someday the paperwork might win. That there existed, in some prefecture drawer, a card with her name on it, and that cards could be pulled, and drawers opened, and that the whole fragile arrangement of her life rested on no one having a reason to look.

She retied the string and put the letters away and blew out the lamp.

Below her window, in the street, a late tram sang past on its rails, heading for the terminus and sleep. In the back room of a dressmaker's shop, six typewriters sat cooling under their sheets. And somewhere across the sleeping city, a man who was not entirely a woman lay in the dark turning over a single sentence—*I could ask about your hands*—the way one turns over a key found on a beach, wondering what it opens, and whether one wants the door opened at all.

Neither of them knew that the answer was already coming, at the pace of chancelleries and mobilization timetables, gathering itself beyond the mountains like weather.

Neither of them knew that within two years there would be a promise sworn over a kettle and a lifted seal, and that the promise would cost more than either of them owned.

On that June night there was only the alley, and the lavender, and the first crack in two careful invisibilities—the small, terrifying, wonderful fact of being seen, and seeing, and neither of them running.
