## 3. A Promise in Peacetime

The trouble with reading other people's letters, Marguerite Delacroix had learned over five years of practice, was that it made you responsible for people you had never met.

He sat at the little table in the back room of Madame Aubert's house on the rue Sainte-Victoire, a lamp turned low, a letter from the archive open before him. It was one of the hundreds that passed through the salon's hands — petitions to deputies, appeals to prefects, the small desperate correspondence of women who wanted the vote and men who wanted them to stop asking. Madame Aubert's rule was simple: everything that entered the house was read by someone before it was copied, filed, or sent. *You cannot defend a letter you have not read,* she liked to say. *And you cannot trust a man who defends letters he has.*

Marguerite did most of the reading. He was good at it. He was good at quiet things — driving without being noticed, pouring coffee without being thanked, existing in a room like furniture that had learned manners. Five years ago he had been a soldier named something else entirely, and then he had deserted that name along with the uniform, and Marguerite Delacroix had stepped out of a Marseille pawnshop wearing a second-hand dress and carrying everything she owned in one suitcase. He still measured his life by what fit in that suitcase. A woman who could be packed in ten minutes, he told himself, was a woman who could never be caught.

He believed this the way one believes in fair weather: sincerely, and only until it changes.

The letter before him was dull — a deputy's secretary apologizing for a delay in a way that managed to be both florid and insulting — and Marguerite's mind drifted, as it did on these nights, down the familiar corridors of controlled loneliness. There was a comfort to it, he supposed. No one looked too closely at a person who asked for nothing. No one discovered what you were if you gave them nothing to discover. It was a small life, but it was *his*, and its walls were load-bearing.

He was thinking this, feeling quite settled about it, when he heard the floorboard creak at the top of the stairs.

Two years earlier, the floorboard had creaked too.

* * *

It was spring, 1912. The salon met on Thursday evenings, and afterward there were always letters left to sort — the ones too delicate to entrust to the post, the ones whose authors hadn't yet decided whether courage was worth the postage. Marguerite had stayed late, as he often did, and had spread the evening's correspondence across the back-room table, and had begun, out of habit and a certain hunger, to read.

He read a widow's letter to her son at the naval academy. He read an angry note from a shopkeeper threatening to withdraw her subscription unless Madame Aubert stopped "embarrassing decent women." He read, with particular care, a letter written in a small, precise hand on paper watermarked in Strasbourg — a letter addressed to no one, unsent, folded twice, sitting slightly apart from the others as though it had wandered away from its herd.

He should have put it down. He knew even then that he should have put it down. But the handwriting was beautiful in the way that careful things are beautiful, and the first line said, *I have now been in France for four years, and I am beginning to suspect I will die here without ever having told anyone the truth about anything,* and Marguerite — who had not told anyone the truth about anything in three years — read all six pages standing up, the way one reads a telegram.

It was signed *Liesl Brandt*. It described, in language of devastating mildness, how it felt to be German in a French city that had decided, politely and in advance, never to fully forgive her for it. How she had learned to laugh one half-beat late at jokes so no one would notice she hadn't understood them. How she typed ninety words a minute because speed meant usefulness and usefulness meant belonging, and belonging was a country she could visit but never hold citizenship in. And near the end, in a line that seemed almost accidental, almost thrown away:

*I sometimes think the only person in Marseille who has ever really looked at me is the driver. Which is absurd, because I am fairly sure the driver does not exist. She has the quality of a chair that has learned to walk.*

Marguerite was still holding the letter when the door opened.

Liesl Brandt stood in the doorway with her coat half-buttoned wrong and her ink-stained fingers wrapped around a book she had evidently come back for, and she looked at him looking at her letter, and the silence that followed had weight and texture and a temperature.

"I can explain," Marguerite said, which was the first lie he'd ever told that he didn't bother to make convincing.

"Can you?" She set the book down. She did not raise her voice. She crossed the room, took the letter gently from his hands — not snatching; taking, the way one takes back something borrowed — and refolded it along its original creases. Then she pulled out the opposite chair and sat down across from him, which was somehow more frightening than screaming would have been.

"You're going to tell Madame Aubert," Marguerite said. His heart was doing something undignified against his ribs. In five seconds she could end him — a foreign typist exposing a deserter living as a woman; the scandal would travel up the chain faster than gossip travels through a laundromat. "You should, probably. It would be the sensible—"

"Your seams are crooked," Liesl said.

Marguerite stopped breathing.

"The ones at your collar," she went on, in the tone of a woman discussing the weather. "A woman who has dressed herself since girlhood doesn't sew her own collars crooked. And you sit like my father used to sit, knees apart, like the chair owes you money. And yesterday, when the boy fell off the curb on the rue Paradis, you caught him by the strap of his trousers the way soldiers catch things — fast, and from above." She folded her ink-stained hands. "So either you are the strangest woman in Provence, or you are someone else entirely, and I find I don't much care which."

"You—" Marguerite's voice came out as a squeak, which did nothing for anyone. He tried again. "You don't care?"

"I care very much about several things," Liesl said. "Whether you are a man is not currently among them." She tilted her head, and for the first time he saw that behind her composure she was frightened too — frightened and curious in equal measure, two currents meeting. "But I will make you a trade."

"A trade."

"You read my mail," she said. "All of it. Every letter I receive, every letter I think about sending and don't. From tonight forward, I want you to read mine *on purpose*." She leaned forward. "Because here is what I have learned about secrets, Monsieur-or-Madame Delacroix: a secret kept alone becomes a stone you carry. A secret shared becomes a rope. I have been carrying stones for four years and my arms are tired."

"That is," Marguerite said slowly, "either the maddest or the kindest thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It's usually both," Liesl said. "With me, it's usually both."

* * *

In the present, Marguerite smiled at the memory despite himself, alone in the lamplight with the deputy's boring letter. That was the thing about Liesl Brandt: she made smiling ambush you.

She had kept her word, that night two years ago. She had never once threatened, never once hinted, never once let the knowledge sit between them like a debt. Instead she had simply begun bringing him her letters — sliding them across the sorting table with two fingers, deadpan, saying things like *this one is from my sister and I need you to tell me what she isn't saying,* and *read this and tell me honestly whether I sound pathetic, because I cannot tell anymore.* And Marguerite, who had spent five years perfecting the art of being unread, discovered what it was to be read instead. It was terrifying. It was, he came to understand, the thing he had been starving for without knowing its name.

They built a ritual around it. Thursday nights, after the salon emptied, the lamp in the back room, the pot of coffee that Marguerite made too strong and Liesl drank anyway while making faces. Her letters from her sister Ottilie in Strasbourg arrived perhaps once a month, and each one required what Liesl called *the full translation*: not just the words, but the silences. Ottilie wrote cheerfully about weather and cousins and the price of butter while omitting, with increasing thoroughness, any mention of their father, and Liesl needed Marguerite to sit with her and map the omissions the way other people mapped coastlines.

"She's protecting me," Liesl said one night, tapping a cheerful paragraph about a church renovation. "Or she's protecting herself. Or Papa has done something again and they've agreed together that I am safer not knowing. Read it again. You're better at this than I am, and it's *my family*, which is humiliating."

"You're better at it than you think," Marguerite said. "You just can't bear the conclusions."

"No one can bear the conclusions. That's what conclusions are." She took a sip of the terrible coffee and grimaced with genuine artistry. "This coffee is a war crime, incidentally."

"There's no war yet."

"There will be," Liesl said lightly, "if you keep making coffee like this."

They laughed. It was, later, the sentence he would return to — the last joke made in peacetime, made about coffee — and he would hate himself for laughing at the time, and treasure having laughed, both at once, forever.

* * *

The pact itself came later. It came in autumn, 1912, on a night when the mistral had blown down from the north and rattled the shutters of the rue Sainte-Victoire like an impatient creditor.

Marguerite remembered it in fragments, the way one remembers the moments that reorganize a life. Liesl arriving soaked and silent, uncharacteristically late. Liesl setting down not her usual letters but a single newspaper, folded to a page of dispatches from the Balkans, where the whole continent seemed to be rehearsing. Liesl saying, without preamble:

"If it comes here — if France and Germany — do you know what happens to me?"

Marguerite did know. He had thought about it more than he admitted. An enemy national in a port city, in wartime: internment, expulsion, worse. Papers checked, doors knocked upon at dawn. "Nothing good," he said.

"Nothing good," Liesl agreed. "Ottilie writes that the papers in Alsace already talk about it like weather. Something coming, everyone buying umbrellas." She looked at him across the table, rain still dripping from her hem onto his clean floor, and her voice did something he had never heard it do: it dropped all its armor. "I have no one here, Marguerite. Do you understand? Four years, and every friendship I have is a friendship with a version of me that I invented for their comfort. Except you. You know the actual me, the whole inventory, because you've read my mail like a customs officer." A ghost of a smile. "So I am asking the customs officer for a favor."

"Name it."

"If war comes — truly comes, not newspaper-comes, but *comes*-comes — you carry me out. Out of France. Across a border, aboard a boat, inside a barrel of herring if that's what it takes. You have the skills. God knows what else you've been besides a driver, but nobody reads a city the way you do, nobody watches exits the way you do. I've seen you watch exits." She held up one ink-stained finger before he could speak. "And before you say yes too quickly, hear the price. If you do this, you risk everything. Your name, your peace, this whole careful life you've built inside a suitcase. I am asking you to unpack yourself for my sake, and I want you to take a week and decide properly, because a promise given in one evening is worth less than the paper it isn't written on."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Rain on the glass. Lamp low. The suitcase upstairs under his bed, packed, faithful, absurd.

"Liesl," he said, "you are assuming I need a week. I have spent five years building a life designed so that no one would ever need me and I would never need anyone. It is extremely safe and it is extremely empty and some nights I eat dinner standing at the counter like a man waiting for a train that isn't scheduled." He heard his own voice steady in a way that surprised him. "You are the first person who has ever asked me for something dangerous. Do you know what a gift that is? Everyone else accepts my uselessness as if I'd earned it."

"So that's a yes."

"That's a yes," Marguerite said, "with conditions."

Liesl laughed outright — a real laugh, unguarded, the rare one. "Conditions! Listen to him. Go on."

"One: we plan it now, in peacetime, while planning is legal. Routes, names, papers — we build it all before we need it, because the worst time to forge a document is the day you need it forged. Two: you keep living exactly as you are until the day itself. No sudden departures, no nervous glances at border towns. Suspicion is a fire and attention is its oxygen. And three—" he hesitated, because the third condition was the true one and true things embarrassed him, "—three, you tell me if it stops being worth it. If the danger grows past what the promise can pay for. You get to release me. That's part of the deal. A promise with no exit clause is just a trap with better manners."

Liesl was quiet for a moment. Outside, the mistral worried the shutters, and somewhere down the street a dog complained about the wind on behalf of the entire neighborhood.

"All right," she said finally. "But I get a condition too. If the day comes, you don't decide for me what I'm allowed to risk. We decide together. I've spent four years being decided about — by the French, by the Germans, by well-meaning people who think a German girl should simply be grateful and invisible. Not by you. Never by you."

"Agreed," Marguerite said. "Never by me."

They shook on it, formally, ridiculously, her small ink-stained hand disappearing into his, and then Liesl — because she was Liesl — insisted they write it down. She fetched paper and typed it herself on the salon's machine, ninety words a minute, tongue between her teeth: *Memorandum of Understanding between M. Delacroix (driver) and L. Brandt (typist), concerning extraction in the event of war.* Two copies. They each signed both, and she kept hers folded inside the Strasbourg-watermarked letters, and he kept his in the suitcase, under the spare shirt, flat against the false bottom where his old life slept.

"It's not legally binding," Liesl observed, capping the ribbon.

"Neither is friendship," Marguerite said, "and yet."

* * *

After that night, everything changed and nothing changed, which is how the best friendships operate. They still met Thursdays. He still drove Madame Aubert through the traffic of a city that believed itself permanent. Liesl still typed petitions and drank bad coffee and made faces, and Marguerite still read her sister's letters and mapped their silences, and neither of them spoke of the memorandum again — it lived in its folds like a seed lives in soil, doing its work invisibly.

But Marguerite noticed things now. He noticed that he had begun, without deciding to, keeping a mental list of border routes and train timetables, updating it seasonally the way farmers update almanacs. He noticed that he'd bought a second suitcase — smaller, discreet, unremarkable — and told himself it was for market days. He noticed that Liesl had started teaching him things in passing, casually, the way one leaves tools where a person might need them: *this is how a Swiss transit stamp differs from a French one; this is the rhythm of an official document versus a personal letter; type slower when you lie, liars always rush.* He noticed that she noticed him noticing, and that neither of them said a word about it, because the whole architecture of their intimacy was built on the principle that some things are stronger for remaining unsigned.

Once — only once — she came close to naming it. It was a summer evening, 1913, the light going amber over the rooftops, and they were walking back from the post office along the Vieux-Port, and the fishing boats were coming in, and Liesl said, apropos of absolutely nothing, "Do you ever think about what we're doing? Building an escape for a war that may never happen. It's a bit mad."

"All the best infrastructure is," Marguerite said. "Nobody praises a lighthouse for the ships that don't wreck."

"Mm." Liesl walked a few steps, her shadow long beside his. "My mother used to say that a promise is a house you build for a person before they ask to live in it. She said it about marriage, mostly, usually bitterly." She glanced sideways at him, quick and bright. "But I think it applies."

"And is ours a good house?" Marguerite asked.

"It has excellent exits," Liesl said. "That's all I've ever wanted in a house."

He had laughed then. He thought of it now, in the present, two years downstream, alone with the lamp and the letters, and the laugh caught slightly in his chest on the way out, the way a key catches in a lock that has begun, very subtly, to change shape.

Because lately — this past month — the letters from Strasbourg had changed. Ottilie's cheerful omissions had developed a new texture. Where she used to skip over their father entirely, she now skipped over entire *weeks*, and her weather reports had grown oddly specific: *the reservists drilled in the square again on Sunday, such a spectacle, Ottilie says hello to Marseille.* Twice she had written sentences that read, to Marguerite's practiced eye, like messages passed through a window in code. Once she had used a phrase Liesl's father had supposedly hated — *the old lion sleeps badly these days* — in a letter claiming all was calm.

Liesl saw it too. He watched her see it, Thursday after Thursday, watched the translation sessions grow quieter, the silences between lines grow longer and more heavily populated. Neither of them said the word *war*. Saying it felt like inviting it, the way saying *cancer* in front of a healthy man feels like handing him the diagnosis.

Instead they worked. In May, Liesl taught him to type. "If you're going to forge documents," she said briskly, positioning his hands on the keys as though defusing something, "you must learn the machine's soul. Every typewriter has a personality — a lazy e, a drunken t. Officials match documents to machines the way priests match confessions to sinners. If your papers don't sing in one voice, you're dead." Her fingers over his on the keyboard, brief and businesslike and warm. "Again. Slower. Liars rush."

In June, he bought a third set of identity papers from a man behind the fish market who owed Madame Aubert a favor, and Liesl examined them under the lamp and pronounced them *almost* good enough, and spent an evening showing him why with the patience of a surgeon and the mercilessness of a friend.

In July, Ottilie's letter mentioned, buried in a paragraph about jam, that the borders of Alsace had grown *crowded with men in new uniforms who smile too easily.*

And on the last Thursday of July, 1914 — this present Thursday, this very hour — Liesl arrived at the back room early, before the salon, before anyone, and stood in the doorway with her hat still on, which she never did, holding a letter with a Strasbourg postmark dated nine days prior, and her face was the face of someone who has finished translating a message and wishes she hadn't.

"Marguerite," she said. Her voice was level. Her ink-stained hands were not. "Read this one. All of it. And then—" she swallowed, and the armor came back up over her voice, polished and thin, "—and then I think we should discuss the weather."

Outside, far to the north, past the rooftops and the harbor cranes and the fishing boats coming home, something was already moving — armies of paper first, then armies of men — and the lighthouse they had built was about, at last, to be tested.

Marguerite took the letter. The seal was broken. The handwriting, usually so precise, had gone hurried at the margins, as if written by candlelight by someone listening for footsteps on a stair.

He began to read.
