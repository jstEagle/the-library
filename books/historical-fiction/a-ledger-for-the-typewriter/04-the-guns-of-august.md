## 4. The Guns of August

The news came to Marseille the way bad weather comes off the sea: not all at once, but in a darkening that everyone pretended not to notice until the first drops fell.

Marguerite heard it first from a newspaper boy on the Canebière, who was shouting so hoarsely by nine o'clock that his words had worn down to pure sound—*Mobilisation! Mobilisation!*—as if the word itself were the army, and repetition its drill sergeant. The boy couldn't have been more than ten. He had a gap where a front tooth should have been, and when he grinned at the crowds, he looked less like a herald of catastrophe than a child who had found a coin on the pavement.

That was the thing about the declaration, Marguerite thought, watching faces stream past the windscreen. Nobody looked frightened. They looked *lit up*, the way people do at fireworks or weddings, and he supposed that for many of them it was both at once—a spectacle and a joining. Men in their Sunday coats stood outside café terraces repeating phrases they had read an hour ago as though they had composed them themselves. *The Germans crossed into Luxembourg. The ultimatum expired at noon. We shall be home before the leaves fall.*

Home before the leaves fall. He had heard a variation of that in 1909, before he'd walked away from a barracks in Toulon with nothing but a civilian suit bought secondhand and a name he was still learning to wear like borrowed clothing.

He drove carefully through the crowd. Madame Aubert sat beside him with her hands folded on her handbag, her face set in the particular expression she reserved for public displays of enthusiasm, which was to say, the expression of a woman watching someone else's dog relieve itself on her lawn.

"Slow down here," she said, though they were already going no faster than a funeral procession. "I want to see this properly."

What there was to see properly was a column of reservists coming down the boulevard toward the station, dusty blue greatcoats over civilian shoulders, flowers stuck in rifle barrels because somebody had decided that was how this ought to look. A woman ran alongside the column holding up a baby, presenting it like evidence, like a reason, and a soldier leaned out and kissed the child's fist and the crowd cheered and Marguerite's hands went very tight on the steering wheel and very loose again, deliberately, one finger at a time.

"You're white," Madame Aubert observed, without turning her head.

"It's the sun."

"It's August. Everyone else is pink."

"I'm told I'm exceptional."

She made a small sound that might have been a laugh in a better season. Then, quieter, in the voice she used only when the car doors were shut and the windows raised and even then not often: "You knew this was coming. You've been reading the papers longer than any of them."

"The papers say what they sell."

"And your letters?"

Marguerite kept his eyes on a dray horse ahead, on the slow swing of its tail. "My letters said it too. For three weeks they've been saying it. Fathers telling sons to come home while trains are cheap. Mothers asking daughters whether Swiss banks would take French money. And since Tuesday, everyone writing to everyone else: *are you safe, are you safe, are you safe.*"

"And Liesl?"

The name landed in the car like a dropped glass. He let a moment pass, long enough to seem casual, short enough not to seem studied.

"She wasn't at the salon yesterday."

"No," said Madame Aubert. "She wasn't."

* * *

By evening the city had begun the work of turning on itself.

It happened politely at first. A grocer took down a tin of imported coffee from his window and put up a card reading *PRODUIT FRANÇAIS*. A restaurant renamed its sauerkraut. These were the small accommodations, the rearranging of shelves. But by the third day Marguerite was driving Madame Aubert past a crowd gathered around a photograph shop on the rue Paradis, and the crowd was not polite. Someone had smashed the window with a cobblestone and someone else was feeding the owner's photographs—portraits, wedding pictures, studio studies of strangers—into the gutter, stamping on the glass negatives until they glittered like spilled sugar.

"A German photographer," Madame Aubert said. "Thirty years in Marseille. His daughter married a Provençal fisherman." She did not ask the driver to stop. Her voice had gone flat and cold in a way Marguerite had learned to respect; it was how she sounded when she was adding a name to some private ledger of debts. "Drive on, Marguerite."

He drove on. In the rearview mirror the glitter shrank to a bright point and disappeared.

That night, in the room above the garage where he lived among his one suitcase's worth of possessions, he took out the letters. Not his own—he had almost none, having spent five years ensuring that no paper existed anywhere bearing his true name—but other people's, or rather, his copies and notes of them, the archive he maintained in his head more than on paper, though certain things he wrote down in a cipher of his own devising that would read, if discovered, as laundry lists. *Two collars, starched. One hatband, black.*

There had been a letter from Liesl in June. He remembered it precisely because it had been addressed to nobody—to the salon, care of Madame Aubert—and because it had contained a sentence he had turned over so many times the words had gone smooth as river stones: *"If it starts, I will become very good at being invisible. I have had practice."*

At the time he had read it as a joke between two people who understood each other's disguises. Now it read like a will.

* * *

The salon met anyway. That surprised him. He had expected Madame Aubert to shutter the whole operation—the typists, the petition copies, the quiet machinery of women demanding the vote while their nation demanded sons—but she simply moved the meetings up an hour, closed the shutters, and informed everyone that patriotism and citizenship were not the same animal and never had been.

"Half of Europe believes that God is on its side," she told the room on Thursday evening, standing beside the cold fireplace with the lamps turned low. "Which proves only that God has poor correspondence habits. We will continue to correspond."

A few women laughed, thinly. Most were silent. Two did not come at all, and their chairs stood empty with the accusatory clarity of empty chairs.

Liesl's chair was empty too.

Marguerite noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—not continuously, but every time your tongue goes looking. She had always arrived early, ink-stained fingers already smudged, taking the seat nearest the window where the light was honestest. She typed the way some people pray: fast, absorbed, unselfconscious. And she watched him. That had been their strange etiquette since the pact—she watched him across rooms with an expression that said *I know what you are*, and he watched back with an expression that said *and I know what you know*, and between those two sentences a friendship had been built that neither of them ever spoke aloud in daylight.

Now the window seat held only lamplight.

"Has anyone heard from Marie?" asked one of the typists—Madame Féraud, a widow with a voice like a latch. "The German girl," she added, and then winced at her own phrasing, as if the war had gotten into her mouth overnight.

"Marie Lambert has concluded her employment," said Madame Aubert smoothly. "Her services are no longer required."

The lie was so clean that Marguerite nearly applauded. *Concluded her employment.* As if Liesl had been let go rather than vanished—as if there were a record, somewhere, of a Marie Lambert whose employment could be concluded. He understood then that Madame Aubert had been lying about Liesl for years, building her a paper existence the way one builds a bird a nest, twig by twig, and that the nest had just been kicked out of the tree by history.

After the meeting, while the women collected their coats and murmured about sons and husbands and train schedules, Madame Aubert caught Marguerite's sleeve.

"You've stopped eating," she said.

"I eat constantly. You've seen me."

"You push food around a plate like a man negotiating with it." She released his sleeve and straightened her cuffs, looking at the middle distance where Liesl's chair was being folded away by the caretaker. "I don't ask my drivers questions. It's why I have such excellent drivers. But I will say one thing, and then we shall never speak of it: a person who intends to disappear does not vanish without preparation. She has been preparing for years. Longer than you've known her, I'd wager. So whatever you're imagining—arrests, mobs, the gutter—it is very likely worse in your imagination than in fact."

"And if it isn't?"

Madame Aubert put on her gloves, finger by finger, unhurried. "Then we shall require your imagination for practical purposes. Good night, Marguerite."

* * *

Saturday, the crowd outside the prefecture sang the Marseillaise eleven times, and Marguerite counted, because counting kept his hands steady on the wheel.

Madame Aubert had business at the bank, which meant crossing the whole singing length of the Canebière at the hour of maximum fervor. Reservists marched. Flags hung from balconies like laundry. A man on a café chair gave an impromptu speech about cleaning the vermin out of Europe, and the crowd roared at *vermin*, and Marguerite felt the word go through him like a bayonet, because he had read enough letters that week to know what happened to vermin: they were photographed, catalogued, registered, expelled. Registered. The word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because registration was coming. He had seen the notices posted at the mairie that morning, pasted one over another like shingles: all foreign nationals of enemy powers must present themselves for census and control. Names taken. Addresses taken. Papers examined. And behind every name, a clerk with a rule and a red pencil, and behind every clerk, a filing cabinet, and inside every cabinet, a country slowly learning who lived where.

Liesl Brandt was German-born. If she presented herself, she would be interned or expelled—expelled into the path of the German army, or onto a ship through waters that already had mines in them. If she did not present herself, she became a fugitive, hunted by paperwork, and paperwork, Marguerite knew better than anyone alive, was patient and thorough and never slept.

Either way, the machine wanted her name.

And he had promised. Two years ago, over a stolen letter and a cup of terrible coffee, he had promised to carry her out of France if it ever came to war. He had imagined, in the abstract way one imagines such things, a train, a border, a suitcase. He had not imagined that the enemy would be a ledger.

"Marguerite." Madame Aubert's voice, sharp. "The corner. The corner, please."

He braked. A column of cavalry had swung out of the rue Noailles, horses gleaming, swords wrapped, and a boy darted under the car's nose chasing a drum, and for one absurd moment everything hung—horseflesh, cheering, the smell of dung and gun oil and someone's frying sardines—and Marguerite sat at the controls with his heart going like a telegraph key and his face arranged into pleasant, competent blankness, the face he wore over the panic the way a glove fits over a hand.

Outwardly calm. He was very good at outwardly calm. Five years of practice. He could drive through the end of the world with both hands at ten and two.

But inwardly, inwardly he was already running down stairways in the Panier district, already checking shuttered windows, already composing the letter he could not send and the search he could not admit to and the question that beat in him like the drums:

*Where are you, Liesl? What name are you wearing? Are you safe, are you safe, are you safe?*

That night, after he parked the car and checked the garage doors twice and stood a long time in the dark listening to the city sing itself hoarse, he made a decision that felt less like courage than like gravity—something he had been falling toward since a girl with ink-stained fingers caught him reading her mail and, instead of crying thief, handed him another letter.

Tomorrow he would begin looking for her. Properly. Through the salon's papers, through the letters, through whatever trail a careful woman leaves when she vanishes.

He did not know yet that the search would bring the census taker Armand Vasseur to Madame Aubert's door within the week, ledger open, pencil sharpened, asking after household members past and present—including, in his dry and courteous voice, *the German girl.*

Dread, Marguerite would learn, always arrives faster than the news that justifies it.

He locked his door, lay down on his narrow bed, and listened to Marseille cheer itself toward the guns.
