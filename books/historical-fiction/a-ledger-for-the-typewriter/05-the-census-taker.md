## 5. The Census Taker

The morning the census taker came to the rue Sainte-Victoire, Marguerite Delacroix was polishing the Berliet's headlamps with a chamois cloth and thinking about bread.

This was not avoidance, whatever Madame Aubert might have said. It was prioritization. The bakery on the corner sold its last decent loaves by nine o'clock, and if he did not get there before the queue of anxious housewives swelled into a mob — war had made everyone hungry and territorial in equal measure — then lunch would be the ends of loaves again, which were honest food but demoralizing in quantity. He had been eating bread ends for five years. He knew their moods.

He was rubbing the second headlamp in slow circles when the man came down the street.

Marguerite noticed him the way he noticed everything now: sideways, without appearing to. A tall man in a good grey suit gone slightly soft at the elbows, carrying a leather portfolio the way a priest carries a breviary — not as luggage but as office. He walked as though the pavement had been personally arranged for his convenience. He stopped at the gate, consulted a small notebook, looked up at the house with an expression of mild professional appetite, and rang the bell.

Census takers did not usually ring bells. They knocked, or they shouted up at windows, or they sent a boy ahead with a printed notice that everyone used to light the stove. This one rang the bell like a guest.

Marguerite set down the chamois cloth and felt the day rearrange itself around a new center of gravity.

* * *

"Madame Aubert is expecting no one," he said through the gate, in the tone he used for salesmen and debt collectors, both of whom had learned over the years that the driver was a wall with opinions.

"I am not expected," the man agreed pleasantly. "I am Armand Vasseur, of the Bureau de Recensement." He produced a card and held it up as though presenting credentials at a border crossing. "Fourth arrondissement. I am conducting the supplementary enumeration."

There was no such thing as a supplementary enumeration. There had been a census in 1911 like every civilized nation, and there would be another in 1921, and in between there was nothing but ordinary paperwork. But it was August of 1914, and the word *supplementary* had begun to grow little hooks.

"The household has already been enumerated," Marguerite said. "In June. I signed the form myself."

"Yes," said Vasseur, "I know. I've read it." He smiled. It was a small smile, tidy as a ledger line. "May I come in? It concerns corrections."

Corrections. Marguerite opened the gate.

* * *

Madame Aubert received him in the salon, which was the best room for receiving anyone because it contained nothing incriminating. The petitions lived upstairs behind the linen; the typewriters lived under dust covers that made them look like furniture for very small ghosts; the only literature in evidence was Zola and a book on beekeeping that Madame Aubert had never read but found useful as a prop. Marguerite stationed himself by the window with a watering can, on the theory that no one suspects a person holding a watering can, and prepared to listen.

Vasseur sat on the edge of his chair as though chairs were instruments he was merely authorized to use. He opened the portfolio. Inside were forms, squared and aligned, and beneath them a notebook filled with handwriting so regular it might have been typed.

"Madame Aubert. Thank you for your time. I will be brief; I know these are busy days." He said this as though busy days were a weather system he deplored but could not control. "Your household, as enumerated in June, comprised four persons. Yourself. Your niece, Céline Aubert. Your cook, Madame Roussel. And a domestic employee listed as — " he turned a page with one finger, delicately, "— Marguerite Delacroix. That would be you, mademoiselle?"

"The driver," Marguerite said from the window, and watered a fern that had been watered that morning.

"The driver. Yes." Vasseur did not look at him yet. He was still looking at Madame Aubert, which Marguerite understood to be deliberate. "You will forgive the questions. Wartime requires... thoroughness."

"Wartime requires a great many things," Madame Aubert said. She had arranged herself in her chair like a monument to patience. "Most of them unnecessary. But ask what you like, monsieur."

Vasseur asked about the cook first — where born, how long employed, whether she had family in the disputed territories — questions so dull that they lulled the room, and Marguerite recognized the technique with something like admiration. It was the technique of the good interrogator: begin with the bread, arrive at the knife. Then the niece, who was in fact no more a niece than Marguerite was a woman named Delacroix, being rather the daughter of a printer in Lyon whose pamphlets Madame Aubert distributed and whose lodging she provided under a familial fiction that had held for three years.

"And Mademoiselle Aubert," Vasseur said. "She is currently resident?"

"She is visiting friends in Aix," Madame Aubert said, without a flicker. "Since July."

"Aix." He wrote it down. Marguerite watched the pen move — small strokes, economical, a hand that wasted nothing — and thought: he writes faster than he speaks. Everything we say is already half in his book.

Then Vasseur closed the portfolio halfway, which somehow made it worse than open, and asked the question.

"There was also, I believe, a fifth member of the household earlier this year. Or perhaps not a member — a frequent presence. A typist." He paused, precisely, the way a surgeon pauses. "German-born. The records list a Lieselotte Brandt, resident in Marseille since 1909, employed variously, most recently at an address on the rue Paradis that has since — " the smallest shrug — "ceased to correspond with reality. She does not appear on your June enumeration. I wondered if you might account for her absence."

The fern got a great deal of water.

"I employ typists by the job, monsieur, not by the year," Madame Aubert said. "If a German girl copied things for me in the spring, she was paid and dismissed like any other. France is full of people who copy things. Half of them are foreign. That is what comes of having the finest port on the Mediterranean — everyone lands here eventually, including the people who type."

"Quite so." Vasseur's pen hovered. "And you would have no idea where she went?"

"Ideas are not my department. Payroll is my department. Her account was settled on the twelfth of June." A pause, masterly. "You may verify it. I keep receipts."

Something moved at the corner of Vasseur's mouth that was almost humor and decided against it. "I intend to verify everything, madame. It is my profession." He turned then, at last, fully toward the window, and Marguerite met his eyes across the ferns. They were pale eyes, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent his life finding the one figure in the column that didn't add, and who took no joy in it — or rather, who took exactly as much joy as the arithmetic deserved, and not a sou more.

"You are Mademoiselle Delacroix," he said. Not a question.

"For my sins."

"Born where?"

"Béziers." Smooth. Effortless. The date even matched the papers, because Marguerite had made the papers himself, twice, in two different hands, and had slept well afterward both times.

"And before that?"

The watering can tilted. One drop too many hit the saucer and ran over the edge onto the floorboards, a small bright sound in the quiet room.

"Before Béziers," Marguerite said, straightening, "there was childhood, monsieur, and I don't recommend it. Is there anything else about me in your book that needs correcting?"

Vasseur regarded him for a moment. Then he inclined his head, almost courteously, and wrote something — brief, unhurried, three words at the outside — and Marguerite felt those three words settle somewhere behind his ribs like a swallowed coin.

"No corrections yet," Vasseur said. "That is rather the point of a correction. One must first establish what is true." He rose, shook Madame Aubert's hand with the solemnity of a notary, gathered his portfolio, and paused at the door.

"One small thing," he said. "The Brandt woman. If she should contact you — for wages owed, sentimental reasons, anything at all — the Bureau would be grateful to be informed. There is no accusation attached. She is simply..." He considered. "An entry that must be reconciled. In wartime, unreconciled entries make people nervous. And nervous people do foolish things to other people. You understand."

"Perfectly," said Madame Aubert.

He tipped his hat to them both, and the door clicked shut behind him with the sound of a file closing.

* * *

They stood in the salon and listened to his footsteps go down the front path, measured as a metronome, and out into the street, and fade.

"Well," said Madame Aubert.

"He knows," Marguerite said. "About Liesl. Not where — but that she existed here, that she's gone, and that the going was recent enough to interest him."

"He knows something. Whether it's what you think—" Madame Aubert stopped, pressed her lips together, and Marguerite saw, with a small shock, that her hands were not entirely steady as she refolded the tea towel she'd been holding. He had never seen her hands unsteady. It was like watching a lighthouse blink. "Céline is at her sister's in Aix, thank God, that much is true. The rest of it — receipts, payroll — I can produce all of it, it will take an hour. But you." She looked at him properly. "You lied to that man's face as though you'd rehearsed it."

"I have been rehearsing it for five years," Marguerite said. "Every time someone asks my name."

"That isn't what I mean." She crossed the room and put the tea towel down and took his wrist, firmly, the way she took the wrists of young typists who were crying over rejected petitions. "You lied *well*. Quickly. With detail. With a joke at the end, even. Do you know how long it took me to learn to lie like that? Two years of meetings, raids, arrests. And you did it over a fern on your first afternoon." Her grip tightened. "It frightened me, Marguerite. Not for me. For you. Because lying like that is a door, and once it swings easily you stop noticing you're walking through it."

Marguerite looked at the wet ring the watering can had left on the floorboards, and thought about how easy it had been. How the answers had simply been *there*, stacked and labeled, like the archive drawers downstairs. Born Béziers, employed June, wages settled, no forwarding address. Five years of building a false woman out of true details, and apparently the whole structure could be deployed in ninety seconds by a man holding a watering can.

"It came too easily," he admitted. "That's what frightens me."

"Good," said Madame Aubert. "Fear keeps better accounts than courage does." She released his wrist and became brisk again, briskness descending on her like armor. "Now. He'll be back, or he'll send someone, or he'll pull the thread at the rue Paradis until it unravels to wherever she actually is. Which means we have perhaps days, possibly hours, in which to decide what we tell each other." She fixed him with a look that had cowed prefects. "Where is she, Marguerite?"

The question hung there. And Marguerite discovered, standing in the safe room of the safest house he knew, that the answer — the true one, the *Le Panier* one, the one he'd carried alone for two weeks like a coal in his chest — stuck in his throat. Not because he distrusted her. Because saying it aloud made it a fact, and facts were exactly what Vasseur collected.

"Not yet," he said. "Give me tonight. Let me make sure she's still there, still safe. Tomorrow I'll tell you everything, I swear it."

Madame Aubert studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she evidently decided it would keep, because she nodded once, sharply.

"Tonight, then. And Marguerite — drive carefully. Take the long way. Men who walk like that gentleman always leave someone behind them, watching the obvious routes." She swept toward the kitchen to manufacture receipts, then paused at the doorway without turning around. "For what it's worth, I think you lied badly."

"Badly? You said yourself—"

"Too smoothly. Nobody remembers their own birthplace that readily unless they're reciting it." The ghost of a smile. "Go on. Go and be careful."

* * *

He drove the long way, as instructed, past the Vieux-Port where the troopships sat fat and grey against the afternoon, past boys selling ribbons in red and blue, past a café radio crackling bulletins that everyone pretended were news rather than weather reports from hell. He drove with his hands easy on the wheel and his mind running like a clerk's pen.

*An entry that must be reconciled.*

Liesl was not an entry. She was ink-stained fingers and a laugh she tried to swallow and a promise made in peacetime by a person Marguerite had been, back when the suitcase rule was a philosophy instead of a survival strategy. And somewhere in the city, a man with a tidy notebook was drawing lines between addresses, patient as arithmetic, certain that one of them would fail to add up.

Marguerite parked the Berliet in its usual place, locked it, and stood a moment in the courtyard shadow with his heart going hard and quick.

Tomorrow he would tell Madame Aubert. Tonight he would go down into the Panier while the light lasted, and see with his own eyes that Marie Lambert of the print shop still drew breath behind her borrowed name. And after that —

After that, he understood, with the clarity that arrives only after a door has swung open too easily, the lies would need to become architecture. Load-bearing. Built fast, built well, built to hold the weight of a hunted woman and a hidden man and a promise that had finally, after two years of peace, come due.

Somewhere across the city, Armand Vasseur sharpened a pencil.

Marguerite went to find a tram that would take him close enough to the old quarter to walk the rest, and began, in his head, drafting the first forgery of the war.
