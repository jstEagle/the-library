## 6. Letters Undelivered

The archive lived in the cellar of Madame Aubert's house on the rue Saint-Suffren, and like all archives it pretended to be less than it was. Officially it was a filing room: four wooden cabinets, a deal table, a lamp with a green glass shade that made everything look faintly ill. Unofficially it was five years of France's private heart, sorted by neighborhood and alphabetized by Madame Aubert herself with the tenderness of a woman who believed no letter should ever be thrown away until its writer was safely dead or famous.

Marguerite went down after midnight, carrying the lamp from the hallway because the cellar's own bulb had died in June and nobody had replaced it. The stairs complained under his feet in their usual order—third step, seventh, ninth—a litany he knew better than any prayer. He had told himself he would not come down here. He had told himself that for eleven days now, since the guns of August had opened and Liesl Brandt had stopped coming to the salon, and every night he had lost the argument with himself at roughly this hour.

The truth was simple and ugly: he had stopped looking too soon.

He had told himself she'd gone to her sister. That was the story everyone would believe—the German-born typist, hearing the drums of mobilization, doing the sensible thing and going home. Home, for Liesl, meant Strasbourg, which meant Germany now, which meant safe in the way that occupied places are safe. He had let himself believe it for exactly as long as it took to notice that she had left her good shoes behind.

Liesl did not leave her good shoes anywhere. She had once walked two kilometers through a rainstorm rather than abandon a pair of buttoned boots to the care of the salon's cloakroom. A woman like that did not flee a country barefoot by choice.

So: the archive.

He set the lamp on the deal table and pulled the first drawer of the cabinet marked P–R. The letters inside were copies—not originals, never originals; Madame Aubert kept carbons the way other women kept pressed flowers—and they smelled of ink and dust and the particular sweetness of old paper that has been handled often. Petitions, mostly. Signatures gathered door to door. In peacetime this work had felt like planting a garden you might not live to sit in. Now it felt like evidence, though of what crime even the Ministry of War could not yet say. Asking for peace, perhaps. Asking loudly, while wearing sensible skirts.

Marguerite had read most of these already. Reading was his quiet sin, older than his name. He had been reading other people's mail since 1909, when he first understood that a letter is the only place where people write what they actually mean, and that a man who reads enough letters learns how to forge the ones he needs. His own papers were a masterpiece of borrowed sincerity. He knew how a widow apologized for asking twice about a pension. He knew how a soldier lied to his mother in the third paragraph and told her the truth in the fifth, when he thought she'd stopped reading carefully.

He had learned Liesl's handwriting the same way—slowly, then completely. Her capitals leaned forward as if hurrying somewhere important. Her lowercase e's were closed tight, like doors she hadn't decided to open yet. He could pick a page of hers out of a stack of fifty without looking at the signature.

Which is why, an hour into the search, when his fingers found a folder thicker than its neighbors, his heart recognized it before his eyes did.

*BRANDT — HOLD*, said the pencil notation on the tab, in Madame Aubert's hand. And beneath it, smaller: *no address confirmed. do not post.*

He carried the folder to the table as if it were a sleeping child.

There were nine letters. All addressed to *Fräulein Greta Brandt, 14 rue des Tonneliers, Strasbourg.* None of them stamped. None of them sent. The earliest was dated the eighth of August—six days ago. The latest was dated yesterday.

Yesterday.

Marguerite sat down so fast the chair skidded.

She was writing them still. Which meant she was still in Marseille. Which meant she had been in Marseille this whole time, eleven days, while he had mourned her efficiently and moved on with his life like a man closing a ledger entry marked *lost*. He put his hand flat on the table and breathed until the room steadied.

Then he read.

*My dear Greta,* the first letter began. *You will have heard before this reaches you that the trains are full of men singing. They sang past my window until three this morning and then, strangely, they sang again at dawn, worse than before. I am well. Do not worry about me, and above all do not write to me at the old address.*

The second letter explained why. *I have taken a French name. Marie Lambert. It was the name on a baptismal certificate I bought from a man in the Panier who sells such things the way other men sell chestnuts, warm and cheap. I am sorry to laugh. If I stop laughing I will have to think, and thinking is not currently useful.*

The third through sixth letters were short, daily reports disguised as small talk—the price of bread, a cat she had named Bismarck out of spite, a landlady who snored "with the authority of a general." She never gave a street name. But she mentioned the stairs. Twice. *These stairs are older than the republic and steeper than the church's opinions,* she wrote, and later, *I count ninety-one steps to my door, which is either exercise or penance, depending on the day.*

Marguerite smiled, and then the smile hurt him, because there were only a few dozen streets in Marseille where stairs like that came standard, and all of them were in the Panier—the old quarter below the Hôtel-Dieu, where buildings leaned on each other like tired sisters and a stranger could vanish between one laundry line and the next.

She had been telling him where she was. Not him specifically. Telling Greta. But letters written into silence are written to whoever loves you enough to read closely, and he had been reading closely since 1912, since the night—

No. He shut that memory's door gently. Later. First the last letters.

The seventh changed key. *Greta, I must tell you something I have told no one. There was a man at Madame Aubert's—I have spoken of him, I think, badly, the way one speaks of weather one cannot predict. He drove the car. He watched me the way I watch him, which is to say pretending not to. Before the war we made each other a promise. Foolish, sentimental, the sort of promise peace permits and war makes priceless. If he comes looking for me, I don't know whether I hope he does or hope he doesn't. Both hopes are exhausting.*

Marguerite read that sentence four times. *Both hopes are exhausting.* He wanted to write back across five days and tell her: come exhaust yourself at my door instead. Instead he turned the page.

The eighth letter was dated the day before yesterday. *A gendarme asked the concierge today whether any foreigners lodged on the street. She said no, bless her snoring heart. But the question itself is a bell that has been rung, and bells cannot be unrung, only waited out. I am moving rooms tomorrow. Same street, different door. If these letters ever reach you, look for me under Lambert. Tell no one else. Not even the kind ones. Especially not the kind ones.*

And the ninth—yesterday's letter, ink barely dry, the paper still holding the warmth of urgency:

*Greta. They took the Duval boy today, sixteen years old, waving from the train like it was a holiday. I stood in the crowd and applauded because everyone was applauding and my hands are traitors to me now along with everything else. I keep thinking of a promise someone made me in peacetime, that if war came he would carry me out of France. I laughed then. I am not laughing now, but I am also not asking. One does not ask a man to burn himself as fuel for your escape. Still. If you pray—and you do, you liar—pray that promises survive the people who make them.*

Marguerite folded the letter along its existing creases, very carefully, as if it were alive.

Then he sat in the green lamplight and let the guilt arrive, because it had earned its ticket.

Eleven days. He had given her eleven days of casual inquiry—a word to the concierge here, a question dressed as small talk there—and when the answers came back empty he had filed her under *gone* with the neatness he applied to everything, the neatness that kept him alive. Meanwhile she had been ninety-one steps above the harbor, buying false names and counting stairs, applauding with traitor hands, hoping against hoping and refusing to ask.

Because he had taught her not to ask. That was the wound under the wound. He had promised to carry her out of France if war ever came, and when war came he had assumed she'd gone home, because assuming was easier than searching, and searching risked questions, and questions risked him. His safety had masqueraded as prudence. His loneliness had masqueraded as patience.

"Well," he said aloud to the cellar, and the cellar declined to disagree.

* * *

He climbed the stairs past midnight, past one o'clock, past the hour when even the insomniacs of the rue Saint-Suffren surrender. The house slept around him in the particular way of houses full of purposeful women—doors closed, minds running.

He was halfway to his own narrow room when light stopped him. It leaked under the door of Madame Aubert's study, gold and steady, accompanied by the arrhythmic clatter he knew better than his own heartbeat: a typewriter being used by someone who thought best at speed.

He knocked.

"Enter, or don't," said Madame Aubert, "but decide quickly, the ribbon is dying."

He entered. She sat at her Remington in a dressing gown the color of strong tea, hair pinned for battle rather than sleep, a cigarette burning unattended in a saucer. At sixty-one, Céleste Aubert had the posture of a woman who had spent forty years refusing chairs offered to her by men. She typed without looking up.

"You smell of the cellar," she observed. "And of something else. Guilt has a smell, did you know? Like hot iron."

"Does it."

"It does. I raised three brothers; I know the smells." She finished her line, struck the carriage, and finally looked at him over her spectacles. "Sit down, Marguerite. You're swaying like a lamp in a doorway and it's making me seasick."

He sat. He held the ninth letter in his jacket pocket, against his ribs, where he could feel it like a coal.

"I need to ask you something," he said, "and I need you not to ask me anything back. Yet."

Her eyebrows rose a polite millimeter. "Those terms favor you enormously."

"Yes."

"Continue."

"The letters in the Brandt folder. The ones marked hold. When did you last try to find her?"

Madame Aubert removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which was what she did instead of sighing. "The third of August. I sent a girl from the typists' pool to the last known lodging. Empty room, rent paid through the month, shoes gone." She paused. "Not the good shoes. I remember noticing that. I thought it odd."

"She left them," Marguerite said. "Deliberately. To make it look like flight."

"Or to mark the trail for someone clever enough to follow it." Madame Aubert put her spectacles back on and regarded him with the expression she usually reserved for petitions that needed stronger verbs. "You've read the letters."

It wasn't a question. He nodded anyway.

"All nine?"

"All nine."

"And?" She spread her hands. "Tell me what the letters say, Marguerite, because I will be honest with you: I marked that folder *hold* in July and forgot it existed. The war arrived and ate my attention whole. If our Liesl is sitting in some stairwell writing daily reports to a sister in Alsace while the rest of us assume she's gone home—" she stopped, and for the first time that night she looked genuinely afraid, which frightened him more than anything Vasseur could have done. "Say it plainly. Where is she?"

"The Panier. Somewhere off the Montée des Accoules, I'd guess, from the stairs. Under the name Marie Lambert."

Madame Aubert repeated the name silently, lips moving. Then she stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at the dark harbor as though the answer might be moored out there among the freighters.

"Lambert," she said at last. "A good solid French name. The kind of name that gets waved through checkpoints by bored men." She turned back. "And the police are asking after foreigners. You know this."

"I know this."

"Vasseur's office sent a circular to every concierge in the third arrondissement. I saw it this afternoon. *Persons of German origin whose whereabouts are uncertain.* Uncertain." She tasted the word with distaste. "As though she were a parcel."

"He'll get to the Panier eventually," Marguerite said. "He's thorough. Thoroughness is his whole personality."

Something flickered across Madame Aubert's face—amusement, quickly suppressed. "You've met him."

"He interviewed me Tuesday. About the household. I lied to him."

"How did it go?"

"Smoothly." He heard the shame in his own voice and hated it. "That's what frightens me. It came easily. Five years of practice, apparently, and the first time I aim it at something that matters, it works like a machine."

Madame Aubert came back from the window and stood over him. She was not a tall woman, but standing over people was a thing she managed through sheer conviction.

"Listen to me," she said. "Tomorrow morning I will send word through the pool—to Colette, who has a cousin married to a fishmonger on the rue de l'Évêché, which is ten steps from where you say those stairs begin. By tomorrow evening we will know which door is Lambert's door. And then, Marguerite Delacroix, you and I are going to have a longer conversation about what exactly you intend to do, and about the promise you clearly made somebody in peacetime, because you wear it around your neck like a millstone and it shows."

He stared at her. "How—"

"I read people the way you read letters," she said mildly. "It's the family business. Sit there. Smoke if you want; I don't approve but tonight seems like a poor night for approval." She returned to the Remington, fed in a fresh sheet, and began to type—fast, certain, the keys striking like a loom.

"What are you writing?" he asked.

"A petition," she said, without pausing. "To the Ministry of the Interior, requesting clarification of the legal status of foreign women married to or employed by French citizens during a state of war. It will be ignored, of course. But it will take some clerk three weeks to ignore it properly, and in those three weeks the file on one Marie Lambert will remain pleasantly, bureaucratically thin." She glanced up, eyes bright behind the spectacles. "Paper is a weapon, Marguerite. Most people only ever learn to duck."

He almost smiled. Then he touched the coal of the letter through his jacket, and the smile went out.

"There's one more thing," he said. "In the ninth letter. She wrote that she isn't asking. For help. Because—one doesn't ask a man to burn himself as fuel."

The typing stopped.

Madame Aubert looked at him for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost all its command-room briskness and gone quiet and level, like water finding its true depth.

"Then she is braver than either of us," she said, "and it is our job to be brave enough to disobey her."

* * *

He did not sleep. He tried—he lay down, blew out the candle, performed the ritual of stillness—but the letters kept unfolding behind his eyelids like a fan, and somewhere around three in the morning he got up, relit the candle, and did the only useful thing an insomniac forger can do: he worked.

On the back of a laundry list, in pencil, he drew the Panier as he knew it. The Montée des Accoules climbing from the Hôtel-Dieu like a spine. The stairways branching off it—rue du Lacydon, the passage behind the church of Saint-Laurent, the covered steps near the Place des Moulins. Ninety-one steps. He counted possibilities the way other men counted sheep, crossing out streets whose stairways ran long or short, until three remained, clustered within a hundred meters of each other, all of them the kind of vertical labyrinth where a concierge sees nothing and a stranger sees everything.

Three doors. Somewhere behind one of them, a woman with ink-stained fingers was keeping a cat named Bismarck and applauding with traitor hands.

He thought about the seventh letter. *I don't know whether I hope he comes or hopes he doesn't.* He thought about how he had answered that hope with eleven days of efficient mourning. He thought about the word *whenever*—no. Not that word. That word belonged to a future he hadn't earned yet. Earn it first. Find the door.

At dawn the harbor fog lifted and the city began its new wartime noise: boots, bugles, the groan of troop transports backing down to the quays. Marguerite stood at his window in his nightclothes, watching reservists march along the boulevard with flowers in their rifle barrels, and he made himself a promise shaped exactly like a debt.

Today he would drive Madame Aubert where she needed driving. Today he would smile at clerks and salute at corners and be, to every eye, the most unremarkable woman in Marseille.

And tonight, when the lamps came on in the old quarter and the stairways filled with shadow, he would go down into the Panier and start knocking on doors—one, two, three—until the false name opened onto the true face, and the promise he had let gather dust for eleven days could finally be kept, at whatever price the keeping cost.

Somewhere below him, the third step of the cellar stairs creaked under Madame Aubert's early feet. He heard her pause there, as she always did, listening to the house wake up.

"Hold on, Liesl," he said to the glass, to the fog, to the ninety-one steps waiting in the old town. "I'm done being careful."

Down in the cellar, in a folder marked HOLD, nine unsent letters lay in their drawer like seeds—and one of them had already begun to grow.
