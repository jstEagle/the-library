## 7. Vasseur's Ledger

Armand Vasseur believed that God was in the details, and that He had, in His wisdom, delegated most of the work.

He arrived at the Bureau of Population and Census at seven minutes past eight each morning, because arriving at eight exactly would suggest eagerness, and arriving late would suggest a life outside the office, and Armand had long ago made peace with the fact that his life *was* the office. He hung his hat on the second peg from the left — the first peg belonged to old Marchetti, who came in later but whose rights of seniority were not to be trampled — and he unlocked the drawer that held his instruments: three pens of varying nib widths, a ruler of boxwood worn smooth as river stone, and an eraser so ancient it had been retired from actual use and now served only as a paperweight for things that must never be erased.

The war had come in August like a drunk uncle through a door, and the city beyond his window had gone mad in the ordinary way cities went mad: flags, bands, young men weeping with joy on their mothers' shoulders. Vasseur understood none of it and distrusted all of it. Grief and glory were both, in his experience, failures of bookkeeping. A man's life ought to balance at the end of every column, and war was simply a system under which no column ever balanced again.

But his work, thank God, continued. War made more work, if anything. Every reservist called up, every household emptied, every foreigner suddenly suspect — all of it generated paper, and paper, unlike men, could be brought to order.

"Vasseur." Marchetti appeared at his elbow holding a folder the way one holds a dead cat. "Prefecture wants the alien residents cross-check finished by Friday."

"The prefecture wanted it finished last month," Vasseur said, "and the month before that they wanted the sewer assessment tables, which I delivered, and received no acknowledgment of receipt. I have begun to suspect the prefecture is a rumor maintained by its own correspondence."

"You should write that down. You'd be shot."

"I would be promoted," said Vasseur, "in any properly administered country."

Marchetti laughed and left, and Vasseur opened the folder, and this is how it began — as everything in Armand Vasseur's life began: with a form.

* * *

The cross-check was simple in principle. Marseille held perhaps eighty thousand souls born outside France, give or take the ones the sea kept delivering. Each had registered under the wartime decree. Each registration listed an address. The census rolls — Vasseur's own beloved census rolls, updated annually with a thoroughness that other bureaus found faintly alarming — also recorded addresses. Where the two documents agreed, nothing needed doing. Where they disagreed, someone had lied, or moved, or died, or been born in the wrong country at the wrong time, and in each case it was Armand Vasseur's quiet vocation to find out which.

He worked by lamplight even in August mornings, because the office windows faced east and by nine o'clock the sun sat directly on his ledger like a cat that refused to move. He worked with two fingers moving down columns in parallel, left page and right, name against name, address against address, and he worked fast, because after twenty-two years he no longer read entries so much as *weighed* them. A true record had a certain weight to it. Dates matched occupations matched signatures matched neighbors' names. Falsehood, when it existed, was almost always lighter — thinner ink, thinner thought, a man writing quickly what he hoped no one would ever read closely.

By eleven he had cleared four hundred names. At twenty past eleven he set down his coffee, which had gone cold without his noticing, and read an entry a third time.

*Delacroix, Marguerite.* Rue Sainte, apartment over the glove shop. Occupation: chauffeur. Born: Lyon, 1888.

That was the census roll. Now the residency register, compiled in July, before the guns: *Delacroix, Marguerite.* Rue Sainte. Chauffeur. Fine. Consistent. Except —

Except that Vasseur remembered the name. This was not a gift he spoke of at dinner parties, partly because he attended none, and partly because people tended to look at him strangely when he mentioned that names arranged themselves in his memory the way cutlery arranges itself in a drawer, each in its proper place, so that when one was missing he felt it against his palm like a gap in the silver. *Marguerite Delacroix* sat in a drawer somewhere in his head, and the drawer was open, and something about it was wrong.

He pulled the 1912 roll. There: *Delacroix, Marguerite*, lodging on the Rue Paradis. Occupation then: seamstress. Seamstress to chauffeur in two years was unusual but not impossible; the motorcar was changing what work women could claim, though Vasseur privately doubted the streets of Marseille were yet ready for lady drivers, and suspected the entry reflected aspiration more than fact. Noted. Filed mentally.

Then the 1910 roll, because once a drawer opens, Armand Vasseur does not close it until he has inventoried it completely.

And there she was again. Or there *someone* was again. *Delacroix, Marguerite*, Rue des Convalescents. And here his pen stopped above the paper, hovering, because the handwriting was different.

Not dramatically different. Not the difference between a monk's hand and a schoolboy's scrawl. But different the way a voice is different on the second day of a cold — same person, altered instrument. The 1910 entry was written with a heavier pressure, the downstrokes biting into the paper. The 1912 entry floated. And the date of birth, Lyon 1888, appeared in both, but in the earlier roll the final digit had been corrected — corrected neatly, competently, with a fine pen and steady hand, but corrected nonetheless. People did not correct their own birth year twice in five years unless one of the years was a lie, and Vasseur had spent two decades learning that the lie was nearly always the *second* version, because the truth was what one wrote first, carelessly, believing no one would ever check.

He sat back. Outside, a parade was passing — he could hear the band playing something martial and slightly out of tune, and a woman's voice shouting encouragement to some boy marching below, and the whole city seemed to have decided that feeling loudly was the same as thinking clearly. In here it was cool and smelled of ink and dust, and three entries lay side by side like suspects in a lineup.

Three addresses. Three occupations. One name.

"Marchetti," he called. "Have we any correspondence file on a Delacroix? Marguerite Delacroix."

Marchetti rummaged. "Nothing. Why?"

"An inconsistency."

"Inconsistencies are everywhere these days. Half the city has moved addresses since mobilization; you'll drown in them."

"This isn't since mobilization," Vasseur said. "This goes back years."

Marchetti peered over his shoulder at the three rolls, and Vasseur watched him do what everyone did: see three lines of ink and feel nothing. It was not stupidity. Marchetti had raised five daughters on a clerk's salary, which required a genius Vasseur did not pretend to possess. But faced with paper, Marchetti saw paper. Vasseur saw — he hunted briefly for the word and settled on it reluctantly, because it sounded vain — *a person*. Someone had lived these three lives. Someone had written themselves into the city three times, each time slightly differently, the way a liar rehearses a story and improves the details with practice.

Or the way a frightened person builds a house with three doors.

"Huh," said Marchetti finally. "Probably clerical error. The 1910 roll was done by Duval's office, and Duval's people couldn't spell *Marseille*."

"Duval's errors are legible in their incompetence. These corrections are competent. That's what troubles me."

"You're troubled by competence now?"

"I'm troubled by competence in the wrong place," said Vasseur, "the way one is troubled by a locked door in a house where all the others stand open." He aligned the three pages precisely, edge to edge, a small ritual that calmed him the way prayer calmed other men. "A seamstress becomes a chauffeur. A lodger on the Rue Paradis vanishes and reappears over a glove shop. A birthdate is written, regretted, and rewritten. None of this is criminal. All of it is *careful*. And carelessness explains ninety percent of my anomalies, Marchetti, but carefulness explains none of them."

"So what does the other ten percent explain?"

"People with something to hide," said Vasseur, and heard how it sounded, and added, "or something to protect. The two are frequently identical."

* * *

He took his lunch at his desk, as always: bread, an apple, and half a wedge of cheese wrapped in waxed paper by Madame Vasseur, who had been dead six years and whom he still addressed in his thoughts in the present tense, because grief was also a kind of bookkeeping and he saw no reason to close her account. He ate and thought.

The war complicated everything, and he resented it for that. Two months ago, a triple-addressed seamstress-chauffeur would have been a Tuesday's curiosity, resolved with two visits and a polite conversation. Now every anomaly wore a uniform. The prefecture had issued directives about enemy aliens, about suspicious persons, about the duties of citizens to report — directives written by men who believed suspicion was a science, and who would take a woman with three addresses and see a spy before they saw, say, a landlady's mistake, or a widow dodging a landlord, or simply a person who had moved house the way poor people move house, often and quietly.

Vasseur did not believe in spies. That is, he believed they existed, as he believed in typhus and eclipses — real, occasional, and no reason to alter one's daily habits. What he believed in was order, and what he had learned across twenty-two years was that disorder was almost never malice. Disorder was poverty, illness, bad luck, bad clerks, and love — love above all, which caused more falsified documents than greed ever had. Men lied about ages to marry younger women. Women lied about marriages to escape worse ones. Whole families lied on forms to keep children off trains. If you read enough ledgers, you stopped seeing enemies and started seeing emergencies.

So he would not assume. He would *verify*. That was the whole of his method, and it had never once failed him, if one defined failure narrowly.

In the afternoon he requested the property records. The glove shop on the Rue Sainte belonged to one Madame Aubert — and here Vasseur paused, because the name arrived in his head with its own little card attached, filed and cross-referenced: *Aubert, Solange. Widow. Proprietress. Contributor of letters to newspapers regarding the vote for women, 1911–1914, eleven letters, increasingly firm in tone.*

He had read those letters. He read everything; it was practically his religion. Madame Aubert wrote the way she presumably ran a household — lucidly, and with an underlying suggestion that anyone disagreeing with her was welcome to try harder next time. He did not agree with her about the vote, particularly, but he had admired her punctuation, which was flawless, and he trusted flawless punctuation the way other men trusted a firm handshake.

A woman of that quality employing a driver with three pasts was probably nothing. Probably the driver drove well and asked few questions, which was the entire desired profile of a driver.

But *probably* was not a word Vasseur permitted himself. He wrote it down anyway — *probably employment, verify* — and beneath it, after some thought, he added the note that would govern the rest of this affair, though he did not yet know it:

*Find the original. Errors propagate forward; the first document tells the truth.*

Because that was the thing about the birthdate, the thing that had lodged in him like a splinter. The correction in the 1910 roll was good — good ink, good hand, good spacing. But it was a correction made *on* the roll, in the moment, by whoever filled it in. And the corrected digit matched the later rolls. Which meant the lie, if lie it was, had been invented early and then maintained faithfully for years, through three households and two professions, with the discipline of a soldier keeping a watch rotation.

People lied casually and then forgot. Nobody maintained a small lie with such consistency unless the small lie was load-bearing.

Unless the whole building stood on it.

* * *

At six o'clock he closed the office, declined Marchetti's invitation to the Café de la Paix on the grounds that the café had changed its name to something patriotic and the beer was now poured with more enthusiasm than accuracy, and walked home along the Old Port. The evening light did its usual shameless work on the water. Fishing boats came in; soldiers on leave crowded the quay wearing their new uniforms like costumes they hadn't grown into; a newsboy shouted headlines with the joy of a man paid by volume rather than accuracy. The city was beautiful and frightened and pretending hard, and Vasseur looked at it with the affectionate exasperation of a teacher watching a clever pupil cheat on an exam.

At home — two rooms above a chandler's shop, neat as a specimen case — he cooked rice, ate it, washed the pot, dried it, put it away, and then did the thing he did every night and told no one about: he took out his private notebook and wrote the day into it. Not a diary. Diaries were feelings wearing dates. His notebook was the reverse: dates wearing feelings, thinly disguised.

*11 August. Cross-check proceeding. Anomaly noted: Delacroix, M., x3 addresses, x2 occupations, corrected DOB. No criminal indication. Recommend interview, routine. Note: employé of Aubert, S., Rue Sainte. Verify employment, residence, references. Routine,* he wrote again, and underlined it, which was a habit he had developed whenever he caught himself protesting too much to his own notebook.

Because here was the honest entry, the one he did not write: the splinter in his mind was not the three addresses. Three addresses was arithmetic, and arithmetic solved itself. The splinter was the handwriting. He had turned it over all afternoon — the heavy 1910 hand, the floating 1912 hand — and there was something else in it, something below technique, and he had finally identified it while washing the rice pot, standing at his sink with his sleeves rolled up like any other bachelor of the quarter.

The hands were the same age. That is: the 1910 entry and the 1912 entry had been written by a person of the same apparent years, neither maturing nor declining between them, the way handwriting normally did. As though the writer had chosen both hands deliberately, the way one chose a coat — this one for winter, that one for summer. As though *Marguerite Delacroix* were a garment, and the garments varied, and underneath them —

Underneath them, thought Vasseur, drying the pot, was simply another resident of Marseille trying to live, and it was not his business what was underneath anyone's clothing or anyone's name. His business was the record. The record had an error in it. Errors offended him professionally the way false notes offend a tuner — not morally, just intolerably.

Tomorrow he would visit the Rue Sainte. Routine verification, nothing more. He would ask his questions in the gentle order he always used, the ones that gave a person three chances to tell the truth before he had to catch them in the fourth answer. Most people took the third chance. The interesting ones never did.

He wound his clock, set out tomorrow's pens in order of nib, and got into bed beneath the portrait of Madame Vasseur, who watched him with the expression of a woman who had married a man who alphabetized the spice rack and had made her peace with it.

Sleep took him quickly. Somewhere across the dark city, in a room over a glove shop, a suitcase stood packed by a rule he knew nothing about, and in the Panier district a typist named Marie Lambert was lying awake listening to footsteps on the stairs, and in a drawer in Armand Vasseur's orderly mind a single name sat with its drawer open, patient as a trap.

He dreamed, mildly, of columns that balanced. It was the last peaceful night either of them would have for some time.
