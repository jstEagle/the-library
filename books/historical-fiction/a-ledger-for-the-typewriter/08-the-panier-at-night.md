## 8. The Panier at Night

The Panier did not believe in straight lines.

Marguerite had learned this years ago, delivering Madame Aubert to a printer who claimed the quarter's alleys were designed by a drunkard or a saint, depending on whether you were trying to leave or hide. The streets folded over one another like linen in a laundress's arms; staircases appeared where logic said there should be walls, and walls where logic promised the sea. A stranger could wander it for a decade and never find the same door twice. Which was, Marguerite supposed, precisely why Liesl Brandt had chosen to disappear into it.

He came on foot after dark, having told Madame Aubert that the Delacroix car required an inspection of its brakes — a lie so small it hardly registered on the conscience anymore. He wore his oldest coat and no hat, because a hat made a man look like he was going somewhere official, and in the third week of August 1914, somewhere official was the last thing anyone in Marseille wanted to be near.

The city above him was still celebrating. He could hear it even here, bleeding down through the stone: the bands, the singing, the boys on the Cannebière selling little paper flags at three sous apiece. *À Berlin!* they shouted. As though Berlin were a town you could reach by evening and be home for supper. Marguerite had been a soldier once. He knew what the distance actually cost, and what the marching actually weighed, and he kept his head down and let the cheering wash over him like rain off a roof.

*She stopped coming to the salon.* That sentence had been sitting in his chest for eleven days now, growing heavier by the gram. *She stopped coming, and you told yourself it was nothing, and then Vasseur stood in Madame Aubert's parlor asking about "the German girl," and you understood that nothing had become everything while you weren't paying attention.*

He had spent two evenings with the letter archive before he found her trail. Two evenings of other people's correspondence — love letters, bills, a furious exchange between two women arguing over a hat — and then, tucked behind a bundle of petition copies in Liesl's own careful hand, six unsent letters addressed to *Fräulein Anneliese Brandt, Strasbourg*. Unsent, because since the first of August there had been no sending anything across that border, and Liesl must have known it, and written anyway, because that was what people did with their hearts when the post office failed them: they kept writing.

The last letter was dated July 30th. In it, almost as an aside, as though she were mentioning the weather: *I have taken work with M. Perrin, the printer, under the name Marie Lambert. The shop is in the rue du Refuge. You would laugh at how French I have become — I argue about pastis prices with the fishwives and they argue back.*

Rue du Refuge. Marguerite had read the name four times before his hands stopped shaking enough to fold the letter back along its creases.

*Refuge.* Someone, somewhere, had a sense of humor.

* * *

The stairway he needed began behind a shrine to the Virgin that some anonymous hand had decorated with a sprig of dried lavender, and it climbed in a switchback fashion that suggested the architect had been fleeing from something himself. Marguerite took the steps slowly, both for stealth and because the Panier's stairs had opinions about hurry. At the second landing a cat regarded him from atop a barrel with the flat judgment only cats and customs officials can manage.

"Not tonight," Marguerite told it.

The cat did not accept this.

At the top, the passage narrowed to a corridor roofed in laundry — shirts, sheets, a pair of enormous drawers snapping in the sea wind like signal flags. Somewhere below, a woman was scolding a child in Provençal, and somewhere else a radio — no, a phonograph, he corrected himself, the war hadn't managed to invent new miracles yet — scratched out a waltz. Life going on. It always astonished him, the sheer stubbornness of life going on, as if the century had not just decided to set itself on fire.

The print shop occupied the ground floor of a building whose sign had lost most of its paint but retained, in stubborn gold leaf, the word *PERRIN*, and beneath it, smaller, *Travaux en tous genres* — work of all kinds. Through the window, lamplit, Marguerite could see the press: a flatbed machine older than the Republic, squatting in the middle of the room like a sleeping animal. And at the composing table beside it, bent over a case of type, her sleeves rolled to the elbow —

Ink-stained fingers. Even at this distance, even upside down through glass, he knew those fingers. You spend enough hours reading a person's handwriting and you begin to know their hands.

Marguerite stood in the dark of the alley for a full minute, doing something he could not remember doing in five years of careful living: hesitating.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not make come out clean. Every step he took toward her made them both heavier with danger. A man seen visiting a hidden German woman became evidence. She became evidence of him. If anyone followed him down these stairs — and someone might; Vasseur struck Marguerite as a man who followed people the way water follows cracks — then the whole delicate architecture of his life and hers and Madame Aubert's would come down in an afternoon.

And yet.

*You promised,* said a voice in him that sounded suspiciously like his mother, dead these twelve years and still keeping the books. *Two years ago, in a salon smelling of ink and camellia tea, you put your hand on a letter and swore. And she asked you for exactly one thing, which was not to forget she existed.*

He knocked.

* * *

Through the frosted pane of the shop door he saw her go still — that particular stillness of prey animals and typists, the whole body listening. Then the lamp inside moved as it was carried toward him, and her shadow swelled up the door, and the bolt drew back with a sound like a held breath released.

Liesl Brandt looked at him through the gap of the chained door the way one looks at a ghost one has specifically requested not to see.

"No," she said.

"Hello to you too."

"You cannot be here." Her whisper had an edge of panic in it that cut through the charm he'd been planning to deploy. "Do you know what it means, a man knocking on this door at night? Do you know what Perrin will assume, what the neighbors will—"

"Liesl."

"—what *I* will assume," she finished, and stopped, and looked at him properly for the first time. The chain stayed on. Her eyes went over his face like a proofreader hunting errors. "You've been crying," she said finally. "Or it's windy. Which is it?"

"It's been eleven days since you came to the salon."

"Has it." Flat. Guarded. But her hand rose without her permission and touched the chain, not unlocking it, just holding it, the way one touches the rail of a ship.

"The census taker came to Madame Aubert's house. Asking about you. By name."

Something went out of her posture then — not collapse, nothing so dramatic, but a settling, the way a house settles when it accepts that the storm has arrived. She unhooked the chain. "Get inside before God or the gendarmes notice either of us," she said, "and mind the type case, it bites."

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled of ink and solvent and cold metal, a smell that hit Marguerite with such force of memory — the salon, the copying nights, Liesl's fingers black to the knuckle — that he had to stand still a moment. Liesl relit the lamp low, checked the window, checked it again, and then turned to face him with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman conducting an audit.

"Talk," she said. "Quickly."

So he talked. Vasseur — the name meant nothing to her, which somehow made it worse — the census, the questions about household members present and *past*, the smooth lie Marguerite had told and how much he had hated the ease of it. The letters. He watched her face when he mentioned the archive, saw her flinch at the violation of it and then, visibly, decide not to object, because she had long ago surrendered any claim to privacy where he was concerned. That had been their whole arrangement, after all. Their strange, crooked sacrament.

"And so you came," she said, when he finished. "Down into the one quarter of Marseille where nobody asks questions, to ask questions."

"To tell you that you're being hunted. Gently. By a man with excellent penmanship."

"I know I'm being hunted." She said it without drama, the way one reports rain. "Everyone German is being hunted. The concierge downstairs reported a family on Tuesday. They'd lived here nineteen years. Nineteen years, Marguerite, and a neighbor's memory proved shorter than that." She pulled a stool from under the composing table and sat, suddenly looking very tired. "That's why I stopped coming to the salon. Not fear for myself. Fear that I'd lead them to Madame Aubert. To the petitions. To—" she gestured at him, all of him, the whole improbable architecture, "—to everyone."

"You couldn't have known we'd worry."

"I hoped you wouldn't." A ghost of her old dry humor surfaced. "Hoping people don't worry about me is practically my profession. I was quite good at it until you started reading my mail."

"I didn't read it. I found it. There's a distinction."

"There is not, and you know it, and I've missed you unbearably." The words came out fast and fierce, as though she'd been holding them behind her teeth for eleven days and they had gotten sharp in there. Then she seemed startled by her own honesty and busied herself with the lamp.

Marguerite let the silence sit for a moment, warm as fresh bread. "I've missed you too," he said. "Unbearably. Possibly more, since I've had practice missing people."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you turn yourself into a sad joke so nobody looks too closely. I invented that trick. I hold the patent."

Despite everything — despite the war starting without permission, despite the census man with his neat questions — Marguerite laughed. It escaped him before he could inspect it, short and surprised, and Liesl smiled in response, and for perhaps thirty seconds the room was simply a room with two friends in it, and the press slept, and the sea did its distant work against the harbor stones.

Then she said, quietly: "You shouldn't have come."

"I know."

"No, listen to me. You don't know, you can't. You live a careful life, Marguerite. You have a whole system — the suitcase rule, the one exit, the name that fits on every form. I've admired it. Envied it, frankly. But I am not a careful life. I'm a hole in the records. And holes in the records attract men like your census taker the way spilled sugar attracts ants." She leaned forward. "Every minute you're in this room, you're becoming part of my error. Do you understand? I can survive being an error. I've practiced. You can't afford to be one."

"Then it's fortunate," Marguerite said, "that I've been an error for five years. Conflicting addresses. A name that doesn't quite add up. I'm apparently already on somebody's books." He watched her absorb that. "Vasseur found three listings for Marguerite Delacroix with details that disagree. I know because Madame Aubert heard him muttering about professional standards. So you see, Liesl — I'm not risking a clean life for a dirty one. I'm lending a dirty one its experience."

She stared at him. Outside, the phonograph had given up; the quarter settled into its nighttime murmur, pipes and gulls and a baby complaining somewhere above.

"That's the stupidest, bravest thing," she said at last, "and I want you to know that I hate it, and that it worked, and I hate that it worked."

"Welcome to friendship with me. Everyone says that eventually."

But the humor drained out of her face again, quickly, like water through sand, and when she spoke next her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "There's more. Isn't there. You didn't climb half the stairs in Marseille to deliver news I could've guessed."

Marguerite looked at the press. At the type cases, the little metal letters sleeping in their rows like a city at night. At the stack of blank paper waiting beside the roller with a patience that suddenly seemed sinister.

"Perrin prints pamphlets," he said. "Doesn't he."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't insult him by pretending otherwise. She rose instead, crossed to a cabinet, and unlocked it with a key she wore on a string around her neck — and brought out a single sheet, setting it face-down on the table between them like a card player laying down the ace she'd hoped not to use.

"Turn it over if you want. You'll wish you hadn't."

He turned it over. The pamphlet was titled, plainly, without decoration: *To the Women of France and Germany*. No sedition in it, no treason — only sentences about sons and harvests and the stupidity of solving arguments between governments with the bodies of the young. It was the gentlest document Marguerite had ever been afraid of.

"We printed four hundred before the borders closed," Liesl said. "Madame Aubert's network has been carrying them out with the Red Cross supplies. And the plates are still here. All of them. In that cabinet." She rested her hand on the wood. "If the soldiers find them, they won't need to prove anything about me. The plates will testify. And they'll say the names of every woman whose hands touched that press."

"So we take them and go."

"Go *where*?" For the first time her voice cracked, just slightly, the steel showing its seam. "On foot? With four hundred pounds of type and a suitcase rule? Marguerite, I've thought about nothing else for eleven days. There is no version of leaving that includes the plates, and there is no version of leaving without them that leaves Madame Aubert safe. I've done the sums. The sums are impossible." She laughed, a small broken sound. "I used to be good at sums. My father taught mathematics. He'd be so disappointed."

Marguerite stood there with the pamphlet in his hands and felt the promise he'd made two years ago shift its weight, the way cargo shifts in a truck taking a corner too fast. *I'll carry you out of France.* He had said it lightly then, sealing it with her ink-stained handshake, certain that peacetime would keep the debt forever unpaid.

Outside, far off toward the port, a whistle blew — a train, or a patrol, impossible to say. Liesl's head came up sharply at the sound, and in the lamplight Marguerite saw her eyes fix on something past his shoulder, through the window, out into the dark street.

"What," he whispered.

"A light," she breathed. "Across the way. Second floor. It's been dark all week."

A candle flickered behind a shuttered window opposite the shop. Steady. Patient. The kind of light a man keeps burning while he writes things down.

Liesl's hand found Marguerite's sleeve in the dark. "Someone's watching the shop," she said.
