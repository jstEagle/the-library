## 9. What Can Be Carried

The print shop smelled of ink and cold iron and, faintly, of the sardine stalls on the Rue de la République, because everything in the Panier eventually smelled of fish no matter how hard it tried otherwise.

Marguerite stood in the doorway with his one suitcase in hand—the good one, the leather one with the brass clasps he had polished every Sunday for five years—and waited for Liesl to look up from the composing stick she was holding.

She did not look up immediately. That was Liesl. She finished setting the line of type first, because a half-set line of type was, as far as Marguerite could tell, a moral offense in her private religion, and then she set the stick down on the stone bench beside the press and looked at him with the expression of a woman who had been expecting him since Tuesday.

"You brought the case," she said.

"I told you I would."

"You also told me you'd be here before dark, and the church bells have been arguing about the hour for some time." She came around the bench, wiping her hands on her apron out of habit rather than necessity—there was no clean spot left on that apron that could be ruined—and stopped an arm's length away, studying him the way she studied proofs. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. I practiced."

"You've been driving Madame Aubert through those crowds all week. Through the flags and the drums." Her voice softened by a degree, which for Liesl was practically a lullaby. "And you came anyway."

"The bells were arguing," Marguerite said. "I couldn't sleep through it."

It was not the whole truth and they both knew it, and the fact that they both knew it—that there existed a person alive to whom he did not have to explain himself completely, exhaustively, in triplicate—was such a luxury that Marguerite had to look down at his suitcase to keep his composure.

The shop was small even by Panier standards: a single room wedged between a chandler's and a man who claimed to repair clocks but seemed mostly to collect them. The press occupied most of the floor like a sleeping animal. Above it, on shelves, sat the reason Marguerite had climbed two hundred stairs in the dark with a bad knee and a worse conscience.

The plates. Eleven of them, wrapped in oilcloth, each one etched with the text of the pamphlet Madame Aubert's typists had produced in the spring—a quiet, reasonable, four-page argument that France and Germany shared more grandmothers than grievances, and that no border was worth a son. The kind of document that, in August of 1914, read less like pacifism than treason.

"We're leaving tonight," Marguerite said. "There's a fishing boat out of the Joliette. A friend of Madame Aubert's. He owes her more than he admits and drinks less than he claims. He'll take us along the coast as far as Sète, and from there—" He heard himself planning aloud, the sentences stacking up neatly, and stopped. "From there we'll manage. We always manage."

"Us," Liesl said.

"If you'll come."

"And the press?"

Marguerite set the suitcase down on the floor with the deliberate care of a man placing a chess piece. "Liesl."

"It weighs nothing, the press. It's cast iron, but spiritually it weighs nothing."

"You cannot bring a printing press onto a fishing boat."

"Then we take the plates and buy another press in Spain. Or Italy. Or wherever boats go. Boats are famously mobile."

"The plates weigh forty kilos."

"They weigh the truth," Liesl said, "which is heavier but better distributed."

* * *

They had this argument, in one form or another, for the better part of two hours, and Marguerite would remember afterward that neither of them ever raised their voice. It was conducted the way their entire friendship had been conducted—in low tones, over objects, with occasional recourse to tea that had gone lukewarm because neither of them trusted the shop's stove.

He laid out the rule. His rule. The one he had lived by since 1909, when he had walked out of the barracks at Toulon carrying one suitcase and wearing, under his uniform coat, the first dress he had ever owned, bought secondhand from a woman who asked no questions because he paid in advance.

"One suitcase," he said. "That's the whole philosophy. You decide what fits, and everything that doesn't fit is already lost, so you grieve it quickly and walk. If you start making exceptions—if you say, well, this is important, this matters, this is irreplaceable—then the suitcase grows. And then it's two cases, then a trunk, then a cart, and then you're the poor fool at the station in 1870 with a piano strapped to a cart while the Prussians come down the road. You know what happens to people with pianos, Liesl. They become stories other people tell."

"That's very moving."

"I workshopped it."

"But your rule has a flaw."

"It has survived five years. It has an excellent record."

"Your rule," she said, "assumes the only thing worth saving is yourself." She turned and unwrapped the top plate from its oilcloth, and held it up to the lamp so he could see the reversed letters shining backward like a message written in a mirror: *LET THE WOMEN OF EUROPE REFUSE.*

"This is not mine," she said. "I didn't write it. Madame Aubert wrote it. Odette typed it. The little one—Camille?—she carried it here in her hatbox, past two gendarmes who complimented her hat. Eleven people made this thing exist, Marguerite. And if Vasseur finds it—if the army finds it—it won't just be evidence against me. It will name the salon. It will name Madame Aubert. It will name every woman whose hands touched it, and they will hang 'sedition' around their necks while their sons are dying at the Marne." She set the plate down gently, the way one sets down a sleeping child. "So yes. Your suitcase rule keeps me alive. But these plates keep *them* alive too, and I am not in the business of choosing which women survive."

"The boat leaves at three."

"Then we have time to solve it properly."

"There is no properly. There's only what fits."

"Marguerite." She crossed the small room and took both his hands, ink-stained fingers against his gloved ones—he never went anywhere without gloves now, even at night, even for her, and she had never once remarked upon it, which was its own form of love—and said, "Five years ago I caught a woman reading my mail in a hallway, and instead of screaming, I asked her if she'd found anything interesting. Do you remember what you answered?"

"'The postage rates,'" Marguerite said. "You'd written to your sister about postage rates."

"Three pages about postage rates. Because I was lonely and my sister was the only person who knew me, and you stood there holding my loneliness in your hands and lied to protect it." Her grip tightened. "I have spent two years trusting you with things that could destroy us both. Trust me now. There is a way to do this that doesn't leave eleven women to the mercy of a census taker. We haven't found it yet. That's different from it not existing."

Marguerite looked at the plates. Forty kilos of truth, give or take. Enough weight to slow a boat, sink a plan, drown them all in the harbor if the wrong gendarme glanced into the hold.

Enough weight, also, to prove that he was not the same man who had deserted in 1909. That man had saved exactly one life, and it was the smallest possible unit of rescue: his own.

* * *

They solved nothing, of course. They only rearranged the problem, which is what intelligent people do when they are in love with an unsolvable thing and cannot admit it.

By midnight they had moved the plates twice—to the loft above the clock-mender's, back down again after Liesl decided the loft boards wouldn't hold the combined weight of iron and conscience—and drunk the tea, and argued the geography of Provence as though a coastline might yield a loophole. Marguerite proposed burying the plates. Liesl observed that buried evidence, once discovered, looked exactly like guilt. He proposed sinking them in the harbor. She looked at him with such quiet horror that he apologized to the plates directly, which made her laugh despite everything—a short, sharp sound, quickly muffled, because the walls in the Panier were made of gossip.

It was during the second pot of tea that she told him the thing she had been circling all evening.

"My sister wrote," Liesl said. "Before the borders closed. Strasbourg is German again, officially, so the mail goes through, slowly, like everything German. She says the city is full of reservists and everyone pretends it's normal. She says our mother asks why I don't come home." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "And I sit here and I think: I could go home. Walk across the border, present my papers, be German again, safe again, my mother's daughter again. No one in Strasbourg would ask me a single question."

"But?"

"But the pamphlets would still be here. In French. Asking for peace." She smiled without much humor. "You see the flaw in me, Marguerite. You want to save my life. I want to save my argument."

"They're the same shape," Marguerite said. "Roughly."

"No. Yours fits in a suitcase." She reached across the table and tapped the leather case, still sitting where he'd put it hours ago, patient as a dog. "Mine needs a country."

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when the compromise arrived, the way compromises do—not as a solution but as exhaustion wearing a solution's clothes.

Marguerite was lying on the shop's narrow daybed, staring at the ceiling, cataloguing everything that could go wrong in the next six hours, when Liesl spoke from her chair by the dead stove.

"The fishing boat takes the plates."

"It can't. Weight."

"The fishing boat takes *me* and the plates," she said, "and you stay."

He sat up so fast the daybed protested.

"You drive Madame Aubert," Liesl went on, in the reasonable tone of someone proposing a change of menu. "No one questions you. No one has ever questioned you—you're furniture, darling, beautiful furniture, invisible as a hat stand. You stay in Marseille, you keep the salon running, you keep watch. If Vasseur comes looking for Marie Lambert, Marie Lambert has sailed for Algiers with a fisherman and no forwarding address. And if the plates surface somewhere, someday—printed in Switzerland, printed in Barcelona—they point to a ghost. Not to eleven women on the Rue Sainte."

"No," Marguerite said.

"You haven't thought about it."

"I don't need to. No."

"You promised me safe passage. You promised. Two years ago, in a hallway, over postage rates."

"I promised to carry you out of France," Marguerite said. "I did not promise to be sensible about it, and I certainly didn't promise to stay behind. What would I even be staying for? To answer Vasseur's questions?" He heard his own voice climb and dragged it back down. "Liesl. If I'm questioned—about you, about the salon, about anything—I can lie. I've been lying professionally since 1909. I'm very good. But I'm only good at it when there's nothing at stake. Put something I love on the other side of the question and my hands shake. You saw them shake tonight, over tea."

"I saw your hands hold a teacup."

"They shook."

"They were cold."

"They *shook*, Liesl." He got up, crossed the room, and knelt by her chair so that they were eye to eye, and this close he could see the ink under her nails had faded to gray, and that she was frightened, and that she would die before showing it. "Here is what I know. If you sail alone, I will spend every day until the war ends imagining the boat sinking, the boat being boarded, the boat arriving and you being arrested on the dock in Sète because some clerk spelled Lambert with a *t*. I cannot drive a car with that in my head. I will kill us both on the Corniche within the week. So either we go together, tonight, with whatever fits—or we find another way entirely. Those are the options. The suitcase rule stands."

For a long moment she said nothing. The clock-mender's collection ticked discordantly through the wall, thirty clocks disagreeing about the hour, which struck Marguerite as the most accurate portrait of his own mind he had ever encountered.

"Another way," Liesl repeated. "Meaning?"

"Meaning the plates stay hidden here—properly hidden, not buried in a garden—until the war ends or the world regains its senses, whichever comes first. Meaning you and I disappear cleanly, lightly, one suitcase each. Meaning nobody sails alone and nobody stays behind and nobody's mother's daughter drowns in the harbor for forty kilos of lead."

"And if Vasseur searches this building?"

"He won't. He doesn't know it exists."

"You're certain?"

"I'm—" Marguerite began, and then stopped, because certainty, in the last week of August 1914, was a currency that had collapsed overnight, and he was not rich enough to spend it.

Because here was the thing he had not told her. Here was the splinter working deeper under his skin every day since the census taker had stood in Madame Aubert's parlor asking his soft, meticulous questions: Armand Vasseur did not make accusations. He made *lists*. He wrote down names and dates and discrepancies in a ledger of his own, neat as scripture, and Marguerite Delacroix was in that ledger now—he was sure of it, sure the way one is sure of weather coming—three addresses' worth of discrepancies deep.

And a man like Vasseur did not stop at the third address. He followed the thread to the fourth, and the fifth, and eventually the thread led to the Rue Sainte, and from the Rue Sainte to the salon's typists, and from the typists to anyone who had ever set foot in that print shop smelling of ink and sardines.

"Liesl," Marguerite said carefully, "when you came to the salon. Did you ever correspond with anyone else there? Letters, notes, anything in your hand?"

"Odette. About typefaces. Why?"

"And did Odette file her correspondence?"

"She filed everything. Odette alphabetizes her regrets." Liesl frowned, catching his tone. "Marguerite. What aren't you telling me?"

He opened his mouth to answer—*nothing, nothing, only that a careful man with a ledger is hunting a girl named Brandt and I have been feeding him discrepancies like a man feeding pigeons*—and at that moment, from the street below, came a sound that stopped both their breaths.

Footsteps. Measured, unhurried, climbing the Rue du Panier past the shuttered chandler's, past the clock-mender's door, pausing—

—only briefly, only long enough for a match to flare and die behind the shutters, a man lighting a cigarette at two in the morning in a quarter where honest men slept—

—and moving on, fading down toward the harbor, until the last thing audible was the sea itself, slapping at the stones as though applauding its own patience.

Liesl exhaled. "A sailor."

"A sailor," Marguerite agreed.

Neither of them believed it.

He picked up the suitcase. She picked up the oilcloth. And somewhere across the dark city, in a tidy office lit by a single lamp, a meticulous man dipped his pen and wrote a date at the top of a fresh page—the date of a formal interview, seven days hence—with a name beneath it that Marguerite did not yet know had been copied out so carefully, in such beautiful handwriting:

*Mlle Marguerite Delacroix.*
