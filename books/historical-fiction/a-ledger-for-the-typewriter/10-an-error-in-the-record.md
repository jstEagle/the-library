## 10. An Error in the Record

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Marguerite would later consider a small mercy—had it come on a Sunday he would have had nothing to do but sit with his fear, and sitting with one's fear is how men talk themselves into running.

It was not a summons, strictly speaking. It was worse. It was an invitation.

*Madame Delacroix — The Bureau of Census and Verification requests your attendance at your earliest convenience regarding certain routine matters of record-keeping.*

Routine matters of record-keeping. Marguerite read it four times at the kitchen table while the coffee went cold, looking for the trap in the phrasing the way he'd once looked for tripwires in the dark outside Belfort. There were two ways to read *certain routine matters*: either they truly were routine, or the word *routine* was doing the work that a blindfold does at an execution.

He showed the letter to Madame Aubert over lunch. She held it at arm's length as though it smelled, then set it down beside her plate and cut her fish with great deliberation.

"He's invited you in," she said. "That's interesting."

"Interesting how?"

"A man who wanted to arrest you would come to you. A man who isn't sure yet asks you to come to him. He wants to watch you walk through the door." She speared a potato. "So don't walk like a woman who has something to hide. Walk like a woman who thinks this whole business is tedious beyond endurance."

"And if I can't manage tedious?"

"Marguerite." She looked up then, and her eyes had the flat gray kindness of the sea in February. "You have spent five years being someone you are not, in a city where one wrong glance could ruin you. You are the best actress I employ, and I employ women who lie to their husbands for a living. Manage tedious."

* * *

The Bureau of Census and Verification occupied three floors of a former insurance office on the Rue Paradis, a building that had clearly never recovered from its previous profession. Everything about it was beige and apologetic: beige walls, beige filing cabinets, a beige clerk who took Marguerite's name without looking up and told him—he was fairly sure it was *him*, though the clerk gave no sign—to wait.

He waited forty minutes, which he understood was also part of the examination. They want to see what you do with unstructured time. Do you fidget? Do you leave? Do you read every notice on the walls the way a guilty person reads the list of fines?

He read the notices. He was guilty; there seemed little point pretending otherwise with himself. But he read them the way a bored woman reads notices, lips moving slightly, sighing at intervals, and when the inner door opened at last he rose with the weary grace of someone whose afternoon has been thoroughly ruined by bureaucracy.

"Monsieur—pardon—" The clerk consulted his card again, frowning at it as though it had misspelled itself. "*Madame* Delacroix?"

"Madame Delacroix," Marguerite said. "Yes. It's on my card too. Everyone finds it very amusing the first time."

"I wasn't—"

"They always were." He swept past before the man could arrange his face.

Vasseur's office was at the end of a corridor lined with the census ledgers themselves—the actual books, thousands of them, spines labeled by arrondissement and year—and Marguerite made himself look at them the way one looks at scenery, idly, incuriously, while inside his chest something cold turned over. Somewhere along that wall was the book with his name in it. Three times now, thanks to Liesl's letters and Madame Aubert's old addresses and his own carelessness with the truth. Three entries like three footprints in wet cement.

Armand Vasseur stood when Marguerite entered, and Marguerite revised several assumptions on the spot.

He had imagined the census taker as a bureaucrat of the usual species: soft, spectacled, ink-stained to the elbow. Instead the man who shook his hand was tall and spare, perhaps fifty, with the upright carriage of a career soldier who has traded the uniform for a desk and never quite forgiven the desk. His hands were dry and precise. His office contained exactly one personal object—a framed photograph of a young man in infantry blue, positioned not sentimentally but squarely, facing the visitor's chair, as if the dead boy were also conducting the interview.

"Madame Delacroix. Thank you for coming." Vasseur gestured to the chair. "I know these inquiries feel intrusive. In wartime they are necessary. I will be as brief as accuracy permits."

"Accuracy is a virtue I admire," Marguerite said, settling in and crossing his ankles with a rustle of skirt. "Though I'm told it makes one unpopular at dinner parties."

Something flickered at the corner of Vasseur's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one had been demolished. "Then you and I are both lonely at parties, madame. May I begin?"

"Please."

Vasseur opened a folder. Not a ledger—the ledger stayed shut on the shelf behind him, deliberately, Marguerite noticed—but a working file, thin, annotated in a hand so neat it looked printed.

"You have been employed by Madame Aubert of the Rue Sainte since March of 1912. As driver."

"As driver, yes. And general dogsbody. She pays me to drive and keeps me because I can also fix a radiator and remember where she puts things."

"You reside at the same address. You resided previously at the Rue d'Endoume, from 1909 to 1912. Before that—" Vasseur turned a page with two fingers, delicately, as though the paper might bruise, "—the records become less conversational. You arrived in Marseille in 1909."

"That sounds right. I don't celebrate the anniversary."

"Where did you arrive from?"

"The north." Marguerite let a small pause fall, the kind of pause a woman gives who is deciding how much of her sad history to spend. "Lille. After my husband died. There was nothing in Lille after he died except his family, and his family and I had reached a civilized disagreement about the disposition of his furniture."

"My condolences."

"He'd have hated the furniture going to them more than dying, so perhaps save them."

Again that flicker. Vasseur wrote something down—Marguerite could not see what—and continued in the same mild, unhurried voice. "And your household duties at the Rue Sainte. You receive the post for the house?"

"I sign for parcels, sometimes. Madame Aubert trusts me with the mail, yes. If you're wondering whether I've been reading sedition in other people's letters, monsieur, I can tell you that half of what comes to that house is complaints about corsets and the other half is petitions, and neither keeps me awake."

"I wasn't wondering anything yet, madame. I am merely establishing the ordinary facts." He turned another page. "Which brings us to dates."

"Dates," Marguerite repeated pleasantly, and felt beneath his ribs the first cold thread of attention, like ice water finding a seam.

"Your residency at the Rue d'Endoume. The census entry of 1911 lists a Mademoiselle Marguerite Delacroix, born 1888, occupation—let me see—'modiste.' But the entry of 1913, at the Rue Sainte, gives the same name with a birth year of 1890, and no occupation listed until the following spring, when 'chauffeur' appears in the margin in a different hand. Small discrepancies. The sort that accumulate in any city." Vasseur looked up. His eyes were pale brown and entirely without malice, which made them much worse. "I am not accusing anyone of anything, madame. I am simply a man who dislikes errors. Errors are how the wrong people disappear into a city, or the right ones vanish from it. So I correct them. Tell me—which birth year is true?"

*Say 1888,* some cold competent voice in him instructed. *Say 1888, laugh at clerical sloppiness, offer to swear an affidavit.*

What came out instead, in the mild tone of a woman discussing hat ribbons, was: "Oh, whichever one is in the older book. I've been twenty-nine for so long I've grown attached to it."

The sentence crossed the room and lay there between them.

Vasseur did not move. He regarded Marguerite with the expression of a man examining a clock that has chimed thirteen—nothing alarming in itself, clocks chime, but *thirteen*.

"Attached to it," he said at last.

"It's a joke, monsieur. Women of a certain age make them. It's why we're unpopular at dinner parties too."

"Mm." He wrote. The pen moved in small exact strokes. And Marguerite understood, with the particular horror of the soldier who hears the shell coming and knows he cannot move fast enough, that the joke had cost him something. That a careful man had just been handed one small true splinter—that Marguerite Delacroix's age was negotiable—and that a man like this would worry that splinter the way a surgeon worries shrapnel, patiently, under good light, for as long as it pleased him.

He changed the subject to give his hands something lawful to do. He asked about the neighbors at Endoume. About the concierge. About whether Madame Aubert entertained German visitors before the war, and Marguerite said that Madame Aubert entertained everyone, it was cheaper than choosing, and Vasseur noted even the joke down, damn him, noting jokes.

It was during this—somewhere around the question of whether the house employed any domestic staff of foreign origin—that Marguerite became aware of his hands.

They were in his lap. That was correct; he'd put them there deliberately. But they had begun, very quietly, to tremble—a fine vibration, barely visible, the hands of a tuning fork. He pressed them together. The trembling moved to his fingers. He laced them. It traveled up into his wrists.

Vasseur saw.

There was no way in the world he didn't see; the man noticed the difference of two years in a birthdate across five years of ledgers. He saw, and he said nothing about it, and that courtesy was somehow the most frightening thing in the room. He simply glanced down at his own notes and remarked, gently, "These interviews run long. Would you like some water?"

"No, thank you." Marguerite's voice came out steady, which astonished him. "My nerves are my own scandal, monsieur, and I hoard them."

"Of course." Vasseur closed the file. Then, as Marguerite rose with relief flooding through him like warm water through a broken dike, the census taker added, in the same mild tone:

"One more thing, and then I release you to your afternoon. Your predecessor in Madame Aubert's employment—or rather, the person who drove for her before you, according to the household schedule of 1911. There is a gap. Six months, roughly, autumn 1911 to spring 1912, where the driving seems to have been done by... no one in particular. Entries in different hands. A name scratched out here and there." He tilted his head. "Do you know who drove for Madame Aubert before you?"

Marguerite stood in the beige office with the photograph of the dead boy watching him and felt the entire architecture of his life balance on the answer.

"No idea," he said. "She goes through drivers like gloves. Ask the garage."

"Ah." Vasseur smiled—it was a real smile, tired and almost kind, and it frightened Marguerite more than a shout would have. "I intend to."

* * *

The neighbor's name was Madame Roussel, and she lived on the ground floor of the building on the Rue d'Endoume where Marguerite had kept rooms from 1909 to 1912, and she was, by her own cheerful admission, seventy-one years old with nothing left to lose and a talent for conversation that her late husband had once compared favorably to artillery.

Vasseur found her watering geraniums on Thursday morning.

"Census follow-up," he explained, notebook ready. "Former resident. Marguerite Delacroix. I understand she lodged upstairs from you."

"Mademoiselle Delacroix!" Madame Roussel lit up like a gas lamp. "Lovely girl. Quiet. Kept herself to herself, which in my experience means either grief or secrets, and she had the shoulders for both."

"The shoulders."

"Grief carries them high, monsieur. Secrets carry them stiff." She snipped a dead bloom with terrible authority. "Poor thing, her husband dying and all. Though between us—and you may write this down—I never thought the mourning quite fit her."

"How do you mean?"

"Oh, I don't know. Grief sits differently on different women, doesn't it. Some weep in the stairwell, some take to their beds. She—" Madame Roussel searched for it, squinting at her geraniums. "She *practiced*. That's the word. Like a woman learning a piece of music. Every gesture a little too correct, every sad smile arriving right on time. And she was stronger than she pretended—one day the coal delivery came and she carried the sack up two flights rather than wait for the porter, and I'll tell you what struck me, monsieur: she carried it *correctly*. On the shoulder. The way a man learns to carry weight. My husband hauled stone thirty years; I know a man's back when I see one working."

Vasseur wrote none of this down, which was how she knew he already had it memorized.

"There was a period," he said carefully, "in late 1911, when the household records suggest she may have been away. Or that someone else—"

"Oh, the disappearing act." Madame Roussel waved her scissors. "Everyone remembers that. Six months, gone. Came back thinner and quieter, said she'd been convalescing in the country. But my cousin Odette works at the laundry on the Rue Sainte—you know how it is, linen tells everything—and Odette swears the sheets from that room never stopped coming all winter. Someone was sleeping in that bed every night. Just never the same someone twice." She leaned over her railing and dropped her voice to the register reserved for the genuinely delicious. "And the porter's boy used to say—and children see *everything*, monsieur—that our Mademoiselle Delacroix went out some mornings as one person and came home as another. Different walk. Different hat. The boy called her *the driver who used to be someone else*. Children are wicked, of course. But they are rarely wrong."

Vasseur closed his notebook slowly.

"Madame Roussel. You have been extremely helpful."

"I haven't even started on the cat," she said. "Come back Tuesday."

He walked back toward the Rue Paradis through streets loud with recruiting drums, and in his breast pocket the thin file seemed heavier than its paper. He did not yet believe what the neighbor believed—belief required proof, and proof required order, and order was his religion. But he had spent thirty years learning that a record does not lie outright; it *stumbles*. It hesitates in the wrong places. And the file on Marguerite Delacroix stumbled like a drunk leaving church.

Back in his office, he unlocked the drawer where he kept the anomalies—the errors that offended him most—and laid out what he had gathered. Three addresses. Two birth years. A six-month silence in the household books. Hands that shook at questions about dates. A woman who carried coal like a mason.

And underneath it all, the newest page, delivered that morning by a clerk from the gendarmerie liaison: a report on a print shop in the Panier raided for seditious plates, and among the seized pamphlets, a distribution list with names half-blotted—one of them, in a hand the examiner was still comparing, that appeared to read *Delacroix*.

Vasseur sat for a long time in the beige quiet, looking at the photograph of his son.

"Errors," he said finally, to the empty room, "are how the wrong people disappear."

Then he picked up his pen, and began—at last—to write down everything.
