## 11. Madame Aubert's Choice

Marguerite knew he had been caught before Madame Aubert said a single word. He knew it the way a driver knows a tire has gone soft—not by any dramatic failure, but by a small change in the sound of things, a slight pull to one side of an ordinary afternoon.

She was waiting in the garage when he returned from the Panier.

Not standing. Not pacing. *Sitting*, on the wooden crate beside his toolbox, with her hands folded in her lap and her hat still on, which was worse than anything. Madame Aubert without her hat was a woman caught off guard; Madame Aubert with her hat on at seven in the evening, indoors, in a garage that smelled of petrol and horse blankets left over from some previous decade, was a woman who had dressed for an occasion. Marguerite could not decide which occasion he would have preferred: a dismissal or a funeral.

"You're back late," she said.

"The tram on the Rue de la République was stopped. A parade."

"There is no parade."

"A procession, then. Reservists marching to the station. There's one every hour now. The city has become a clock made of men." He hung his cap on its peg and busied himself with the motorcar's bonnet, though there was nothing wrong with the motorcar and both of them knew it. "I walked the last stretch. It seemed faster than waiting."

"Mm," said Madame Aubert.

He let the silence run for exactly as long as dignity allowed, then said, "You wanted something, Madame?"

"I wanted to know where my driver goes three evenings a week when he tells me he is checking on his aunt in Endoume."

The bonnet latch clicked under his fingers, louder than he intended. "My aunt—"

"Has no telephone, I understand. Very convenient. My own aunt in Aix was the same way. One could tell her anything and she could never contradict it, being fictional." Madame Aubert rose from the crate, unhurried, brushing off her skirt as though she had been sitting on dust all day and had only just now decided to mind. "I had you followed, Marguerite. Not out of suspicion. Out of arithmetic. You were spending money we did not pay you, and buying bread for two, and coming back with ink on your cuff that is not the ink I use. Someone who reads letters learns to notice ink. I assume you've noticed mine."

He turned around slowly. There was no point in anything else. Five years of practice told him exactly how much fear was permitted to show on a face—very little, and none of it in the eyes—and he deployed all of that practice now, and watched it fail completely against a woman who had spent thirty years making prefects blink first.

"Madame," he began, "what I do in my own hours—"

"Is your own affair, yes. Except that it isn't, because what you do in your own hours involves a German-born typist who vanished from my salon in August, whose name appeared on a census list I was asked to account for, and whose continued existence in this city—if she does continue to exist—is currently the single most dangerous fact in Marseille." She took a step closer. In the dim light of the garage her face was unreadable, which was itself a kind of reading. "So. Is she alive?"

And here was the moment, Marguerite thought later, when he understood that he had already made his choice, weeks ago, on a stairway in the Panier, and that everything since had merely been paperwork. He could lie. He was good at lying now; he'd had lessons from necessity, the strictest schoolmistress in France. But Liesl had once asked him to read her letters because he had been honest about the worst thing he did, and honesty between people with secrets was the only currency that held its value.

"She's alive," he said. "She's working in a print shop under the name Marie Lambert. She's frightened, and thin, and stubborn as a mule about a set of printing plates she refuses to leave behind, and every day Vasseur's clerks come one street closer to finding her. That's where I go. Three evenings a week. To bring bread, and argue with her about the plates, and sit with her while she types letters she cannot send, so that she remembers someone knows she exists."

The words came out faster than he meant them to, and rougher, and by the end his voice had done something he disliked intensely—it had cracked, slightly, on *exists*.

Madame Aubert looked at him for a long moment.

"Well," she said at last. "That's more sentences strung together than you've produced since 1912. I begin to see why you drive instead of talk."

* * *

She did not dismiss him. She did the opposite, which was more frightening: she poured him a glass of wine in her study—the study where the petitions were copied, where the typewriters lived under dust covers like sleeping animals—and sat him down across from her desk, and said, "Tell me everything, in order, omitting nothing, and do not perform for me. I am too old and too tired to be performed at."

So he told her. Not everything—he kept the suitcase rule and the desertion folded away where they had lived for five years—but everything about Liesl: the mail, the pact, the promise, the search abandoned too soon, the guilt that had rekindled like a lamp turned up. He told her about the print shop, the false name, the plates. He told her about Vasseur's visits and the smooth lies he had told, and how each lie felt less like armor and more like debt.

Madame Aubert listened the way she did everything—completely, without interruption, as if listening were a form of labor she took pride in doing well. When he finished, she swirled her wine and looked at the window, where the evening was going blue over the rooftops.

"Do you know why I employ women typists?" she said. "Really employ them, pay them properly, teach them, defend them?"

"Because no one else will."

"Because no one else will," she agreed, "but that is the reason everyone can see. The reason underneath is that in 1871, I had a brother." She said it plainly, the way one states a date or a price. "Étienne. Two years older than me. Conscripted in the war against Prussia, the one we lost, the one that made all of this—" she gestured at the window, at the flags, at the century—"inevitable. He deserted in March of '71. Walked away from his regiment outside Dijon and came home, and hid in our cellar for eleven days, and I brought him bread, and on the twelfth day they found him."

Marguerite did not move. The wine sat untouched in his hand.

"They shot him against the wall of the citadel," Madame Aubert went on, in the tone of a woman reading aloud from a ledger she had memorized long ago. "He was nineteen. My mother never spoke his name again. My father tore him out of the family Bible—cut the page, actually, with a razor, so that God Himself would have trouble finding the record. And I learned at thirteen years old that a family will erase its own rather than share its shame, and that the state will shoot a boy for deciding, quite sanely, that he did not wish to die in a field." She set down her glass. "So when I see a young person hiding inside a name that doesn't belong to them, protecting themselves with paper, I recognize the work. I recognize the cellar."

"Madame—"

"I am not finished." But her voice gentled. "I am not telling you this to purchase your confidence with mine, although I suppose that is what I'm doing, and I find I don't mind. I am telling you because you have been carrying this alone for five years, and carrying alone is how people get shot against walls. Alone is the condition that kills. Do you understand me? Not the papers. Not Vasseur. *Alone.*"

He understood. He understood so well that for a moment he could not speak, because the alternative was to say something true about 1909, about a train south and a uniform buried in a riverbank and the long apprenticeship of becoming Marguerite Delacroix, and he was not certain the evening could hold that much truth along with everything else.

Madame Aubert, watching his face, inclined her head very slightly—as if he had spoken after all.

"So," she said briskly, and the briskness came down over her like a shutter, practical and protective. "Here is what happens now. First: you will stop going to the Panier alone. You are a known face attached to my household, and my household is on a list somewhere—I guarantee it, I've been on lists since before you were born. Second: the girl needs documents, real ones, coherent ones, ones that will survive a man like Vasseur, who I am told cross-references the way monks used to copy scripture. Third—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been humor, arriving late to a grim occasion, as humor does—"third, I appear to be running a conspiracy now. At my age. I had planned to spend the war writing angry letters to newspapers. Life is what happens while you're drafting."

"The plates," Marguerite said. "She won't leave without them. They're evidence of the pamphlets—if they're seized, they implicate everyone. Your typists. You."

"Yes, I gathered that from your report. A sensible woman would insist they be destroyed." Madame Aubert considered. "Fortunately, I have never once been mistaken for a sensible woman. We'll deal with the plates when we deal with the plates. Étienne taught me one thing worth keeping: you do not abandon the heavy luggage of the people you love. You carry it together, or you admit you never loved them." She stood, and straightened her skirt, and became once more entirely the mistress of the house. "Now go home and sleep. Tomorrow you'll drive me to the mairie, where I intend to make a nuisance of myself about a census correction, and I want you rested enough to look innocent."

"I've had practice," Marguerite said.

"I know," said Madame Aubert. "It shows. That's the problem."

* * *

He slept badly, which was usual, but woke strangely light, which was not. It took him until he was buttoning his collar to identify the feeling, and when he identified it he had to sit down on the edge of his narrow bed and let it pass through him like weather.

It was relief. Five years of relief, arriving all at once, because one old woman had cut a page out of a Bible and never forgotten what was printed on it.

At the house, the morning had the bright, brittle quality of autumn days that pretend to be kind. The typists were already at their machines, and the sound of them—keys striking in overlapping rhythms, like rain on different roofs—filled the hallway. Odette, the youngest, waved at him with a pencil. Little Colette, who was not little and not called Colette either, according to a joke Marguerite had never been told the end of, asked if he'd heard the rumor that sugar was disappearing from the shops, and he said rumors of sugar were the only reliable news left in France, and she laughed, and he thought: *this is what she means. This room. This is the thing that gets people shot against walls, if you count it wrong.*

Madame Aubert emerged from her study wearing her second-best hat, which meant business, and handed him her gloves.

"The mairie," she said. "And Marguerite—on the way, you will tell me precisely what a census correction requires. Forms, signatures, dates. Every detail. Do not spare me the tedium. Tedium is where governments keep their secrets."

"It's where they keep everything," he said, taking the wheel. "Governments are mostly tedium with flags on top."

"Then we shall fight them with their own ammunition." She settled into the seat beside him and pulled on her gloves finger by finger, a small ceremony of intent. "I have spent forty years annoying officials into behaving like human beings. One more file will hardly kill me. Though it may kill you, if you drive us into a canal while explaining form forty-four."

"I would never."

"You nearly did in June, over a horse."

"That horse came out of nowhere."

"All horses come out of nowhere. It's their defining feature." She looked straight ahead through the windscreen, at the streets full of reservists and bunting and boys selling newspapers shouting numbers that were really names of the dead, and her voice dropped half a register, into the register she saved for things she meant. "We will need more than forms, in the end. You know that. A corrected entry buys her weeks. Getting her out of France buys her a life. When the time comes—and it is coming, don't make that face, I can smell a deadline the way horses smell weather—we will need a route, papers that travel, and someone at the border willing to look at the wrong thing. I know people. Some of them owe me. One of them is a deputy with a weakness for petition signatures and flattery."

"And the other?"

"The other," said Madame Aubert, "is a railway inspector in Geneva who has been in love with me since 1888 and has never once had the courage to mention it. Which makes him, after forty years of practice, the most reliable man in Europe." She allowed herself, briefly, the smile of a woman opening a drawer she hadn't opened in decades and finding the contents exactly where she'd left them. "But that is next week's conspiracy. Today: the mairie, form forty-four, and a census clerk named Pujol who owes me a favor involving a dog."

Marguerite put the motorcar in gear. As they pulled out into the street, past the kiosk where the newspaper headlines marched in ever-larger type, he realized that his hands, for the first time in weeks, were steady on the wheel.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a clerk from the census office noted the address of Madame Aubert's house in a small notebook, and wrote, in a careful hand: *frequent visitors — inquire further.*
