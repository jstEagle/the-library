## 12. Forged Entries

The back room of the print shop smelled of ink and cold tea, and by the light of a single lamp shaded with newspaper, Liesl Brandt was teaching Marguerite Delacroix how to be a typewriter.

"No," she said, for the fourth time. "You are typing *at* the machine. You must type *with* it. Listen."

She pulled the carriage back with a small bell-like chime, set her fingers on the keys, and typed a single line:

*Marie Lambert, née at Lyon, 1889, occupation: compositor.*

Then she turned the paper toward him. "Now you."

He sat down. The Underwood was older than the one at Madame Aubert's salon, its E worn nearly smooth, its ribbon faded to a gray that was neither black nor quite purple. He typed the same line.

Liesl read it and winced as though he'd stepped on her foot.

"The M is too heavy," she said. "And your commas — look." She bent close, her shoulder against his, and pointed with an ink-stained finger. "A machine has moods. This one hesitates before the comma because the key sticks, so every comma on a genuine document from this machine sits a hair too far right. If we produce a paper where the commas sit perfectly, Vasseur will know in ten seconds it's false. Not because of what it says. Because of how it breathes."

"How it breathes," Marguerite repeated. Five years ago, in a barracks near Toulon, he would have laughed at such talk. Now it sounded like the most serious thing in Marseille.

"Everything breathes," Liesl said. "Paper breathes. Ink breathes. You taught me that yourself, do you remember? The night you confessed you'd been reading my letters. You said a letter is never only words — it's the pressure of a hand, the hurry or the patience. You said I wrote like someone afraid of being interrupted."

"I said that?"

"You did. It was very rude." But she was almost smiling, and in the lamplight he saw again the girl from the salon, the one who watched him watching her, who had caught him red-handed over her correspondence and instead of crying theft had said: *then read this one properly.*

He flexed his fingers and began again. Slower. Listening for the sticking comma, the tired E, the way the shift key needed a second press if you wanted a capital after a parenthesis. It was, he realized, not so different from driving Madame Aubert's motorcar — learning a machine's temper until it stopped being a machine and became a collaborator.

* * *

He had learned forgery the way most people learn grief: suddenly, out of necessity, in a single bad year.

In 1909, running from the army with nothing but a stolen civilian coat and a name borrowed from a gravestone in a Provençal churchyard, he had discovered that documents were not facts. They were performances. A birth certificate did not prove you were born; it proved that some clerk, somewhere, had once believed a story well enough to write it down. And clerks, he learned, believed stories the way juries believed lawyers — according to the confidence of the telling.

So he had built Marguerite Delacroix the way a carpenter builds a table. A baptismal record aged with weak tea and pressed under a mattress. A reference letter from an employment agency that existed only as a letterhead he had drawn himself, practicing the flourish on the D for two weeks until his hand did it in its sleep. A lodging book from a pension in Aix where the proprietor owed him a favor and asked no questions, because he had paid six months in advance and fixed her roof.

Five years of small careful lies, each one load-bearing, each one holding up the next. He had thought, until now, that the work was behind him — that Marguerite Delacroix was finished, complete, a house with all its papers in order.

But a house built for one person to hide in is a different thing from a corridor built to lead another person to safety. The first only needs to withstand a glance. The second must withstand a man like Armand Vasseur, who read census rolls the way monks read scripture, looking not for sins but for inconsistencies.

"He'll check three things," Marguerite said, spreading his notes across the workbench. "First, when Marie Lambert entered Marseille. Second, where she lived before. Third—" he tapped the page "—who can swear to her. Papers open doors, but people close them. Every forged document needs one true witness, or it dies."

"Then we need a true witness who will lie," Liesl said.

"We need something better. We need a witness who doesn't know she's lying."

* * *

Madame Aubert's cook, Bernadette, had worked in the household for eleven years and remembered everything except what she chose to forget. She was sixty, half-blind in one eye, devoted to Madame Aubert with the fierce unreasoning loyalty of a woman who had once been rescued from something no one spoke about. She also, crucially, had spent three weeks in the autumn of 1912 with her sister in Lyon — and while there, had shared a boarding house with a quiet young woman who helped at a print shop and who had once fixed the sister's sewing machine for free.

"You remember Marie?" Marguerite asked, setting a photograph on the kitchen table. It showed a woman in a print apron, face half-turned, unmistakably Liesl and yet not quite — the photograph itself was a forgery, composed from two exposures in the Panier shop's darkroom, but the woman in it looked real because she was.

Bernadette squinted. Her good eye softened. "Marie. Of course. Quiet girl. Good hands." She looked up sharply. "Why? Has something happened to her?"

"That's what we're trying to prevent," Marguerite said, which was true, and therefore easy to say. "The census office is making errors. Names misplaced. They may come asking whether Marie lodged with you in Lyon. We want to make sure the record is corrected before they confuse her with someone else."

"With a German, you mean." Bernadette spat into the fire with tremendous dignity. "Half this city would denounce their own mothers for a franc and a flag ribbon. You tell those census men that Marie Lambert ate Sunday dinner at my sister's table and complained about the gravy, same as any Frenchwoman." She paused. "The gravy was terrible. That part's true, so they can hang me for it if they like."

Marguerite walked back up the stairs feeling like a counterfeiter who has just been handed genuine gold. The best forgery, he understood now, was not a perfect lie. It was a true memory, gently redirected. Bernadette would testify with her whole honest heart to a past that had acquired, in the retelling, a slightly different shape — and no examiner on earth could make her doubt herself, because she didn't.

* * *

For six nights, the print shop after closing became a schoolroom, a workshop, and a confessional.

They worked by lamplight among the trays of type, Liesl correcting his rhythm, Marguerite teaching her the clerk's eye. He showed her how official forms were filled top-left first, always, so that a forger must begin the same way and let the hand warm gradually; how ink from a fresh nib darkened over the first minute, so corrections made later betrayed themselves by shade; how a lie kept simple survived, and a lie kept elaborate died of its own architecture.

"One address change, believable," he said. "Two, suspicious. Three, and you're writing a novel, and novels get read."

"And which of us knows anything about novels," Liesl murmured, and he threw an eraser at her, and she caught it without looking up from the machine.

She, in turn, taught him what the lamp could not: that Vasseur's office used a particular violet stamp pad, obtainable from exactly two stationers in the city, and that she knew which one because she had bought ink there for the salon. That correction slips were filed separately from the main register and cross-checked quarterly — "quarterly means we have until January, no longer." That clerks trusted handwriting more than typescript, because handwriting felt human, and so the final signature on each slip should be pen, not type — but a pen signature imitated too well was worse than one imitated imperfectly, because perfection was itself a signature of fraud.

"You've thought about this a great deal," Marguerite said, "for a typist."

"I have thought about disappearing a great deal," she said, "for a German. Since 1912, when I understood what my name would cost me. You were not the only one building an escape route, Marguerite. Yours just happened to involve becoming someone else entirely. Mine only involved becoming someone else slightly." She smiled, thin and tired. "We are both forgers. I simply started smaller."

There it was again — the thing between them that had no better name than the pact, the intimacy of two people who each held the other's life in a drawer. He found, working beside her night after night, that he did not want the work to end. Each corrected entry was a sentence they composed together; each matching rhythm of keys was a conversation conducted in a language only the two of them and one stubborn Underwood could speak. When her fingers brushed his reaching for the same ribbon spool, neither of them moved away, and the machine waited, patient, its little typebars standing at attention like soldiers who had decided to desert.

On the fifth night she fell asleep in the chair, mid-sentence, her head tipping against the frame of the press, ink still on her fingers, and Marguerite sat without moving for an hour rather than wake her, memorizing the sound of her breathing the way he had once memorized the weight of a rifle. He thought: *this is what I am good at. Making records say true things falsely. And I am doing it for her.* The thought frightened him more than Vasseur ever could, because it meant he finally had something whose loss would matter.

* * *

By the seventh night, the ledger was ready.

Not the real census ledger — that lived under lock in the prefecture, and stealing it would be suicide. Instead, the corrections themselves, prepared as official-looking amendment slips, each one destined to arrive at the census office through channels so ordinary they would raise no alarm: one delivered by a law student who ran errands for the salon, one slipped into the municipal post, one carried by Bernadette's nephew, a gendarme's clerk who asked nothing because family asked nothing.

Each slip bore the violet-adjacent stamp, the correct hesitation of the comma, the breathing rhythm of machines that genuinely existed in offices across the arrondissement. Each told the same clean story: *Marie Lambert, French-born, Lyon, resident of Marseille since 1911, employed as compositor, verified witness: B. Reynaud (housekeeper), lodging history confirmed.*

A corridor of paper, leading from nowhere to nowhere, wide enough for one woman to walk through.

Liesl read the last slip twice, holding it up to the lamp as though she could see through it into January.

"It's good," she said finally. "It's better than good. It's the kind of boring that saves lives." She set it down and looked at him, and her voice went quieter. "You understand what you've done. These aren't just papers anymore. If anyone traces them — the ink, the stamps, the errand boys — every thread leads back to the salon. To Madame Aubert. To you. You haven't forged a census entry, Marguerite. You've forged yourself into a crime."

"I've done that before," he said. "1909. The crime was existing." He gathered the slips into their oilcloth wrapping, squared the corners precisely — Vasseur would notice sloppy corners even through sealed paper, he was sure of it — and tied the string with the double knot postal clerks favored. "Besides. You taught me the machine breathes. Well. So does a lie. And these breathe."

Outside, the bells of the Panier rang eleven, and somewhere below, boots on stone — a patrol, unhurried, passing. Liesl blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening, her hand finding his sleeve in the blackness and holding on.

"They'll come here eventually," she whispered. "Vasseur. Sooner or later he walks into every room in this city. What do we do when he opens that door?"

Marguerite was saved from answering by the sound that came instead: footsteps on the shop's outer stair, quick and light and utterly wrong for midnight — and then a knock, three sharp raps, followed by a voice he knew, low and furious through the shuttered window:

"Marguerite Delacroix. Open this door. We need to talk about what my clerk saw written in your notebook — and we need to talk about it *now*."

It was Madame Aubert.
