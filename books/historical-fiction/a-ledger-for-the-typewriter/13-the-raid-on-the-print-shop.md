## 13. The Raid on the Print Shop

The morning of the raid began, as so many catastrophes do, with a delivery.

Marguerite was loading crates of Red Cross bandages into the Delaunay-Belleville when Madame Aubert came down the steps holding a letter at arm's length, the way one holds a dead mouse found in the pantry.

"From the prefecture," she said. "A form. They want a complete inventory of household members, occupations, and—get this—" she peered over her spectacles, "—*national origins*. As though my cook were going to declare herself Prussian for the fun of it."

"Colette would sooner declare herself Empress of Brazil," Marguerite said, cinching a strap. "She has opinions about the Kaiser's moustache."

"She has opinions about everyone's moustache." But Madame Aubert's humor had a thin place in it this morning; he could hear it, the way one hears a crack in a teacup before it gives way. "Marguerite. You've been to the Panier twice this week."

"Three times," he said. Honesty had become, lately, a strange new luxury he permitted himself only with her.

"And?"

"And nothing. She won't leave. She says the plates stay or she stays."

Madame Aubert sighed and folded the prefecture's letter into quarters, then eighths, then something approaching a paper dart. "I spent thirty years arguing that women should be trusted with the vote because we are reasonable creatures. Then I meet your Liesl, who wants to defend six kilograms of lead alloy against the German army, and I begin to understand why they resisted us so long."

"They're not her plates."

"No. They're worse. They're *evidence*." She handed him the letter. "Take this to the office. And take her a message from me: tell her that a person is not a printing press. Tell her the press can be replaced and she cannot, and if she argues, tell her I said that a woman who hides under a false name in a city full of soldiers does not get to lecture anyone about risk."

"What if she asks what you did during the Commune?"

"Then she knows perfectly well why I'm not lecturing either." Madame Aubert patted his arm, once, briskly—the whole of her tenderness compressed into a single gesture, like a telegram. "Go. Before noon, please. The city gets stupid after noon."

---

It took him forty minutes to cross the Vieux-Port, because Marseille had decided that August required every able-bodied man to stand in queues for reasons no one could articulate, and the queue for the ferry overlapped the queue for tobacco, which overlapped the queue simply called *the queue*, which existed apparently out of civic pride. Marguerite waited with the patience he'd learned in barracks—the patience of a man who had discovered that the army's true purpose was teaching you to hate waiting—and watched gulls argue over fish guts while reservists sang something about Alsace with more enthusiasm than accuracy.

He thought about Liesl's hands on the type case, quick and certain, letters lifted like small black birds. He thought about the suitcase rule, which he had invented at nineteen and clung to ever since: everything you love must fit in one case, because the day always comes when you carry it out the door. He had never managed to follow it himself. His entire life was a suitcase he kept repacking.

At the foot of the rue du Panier he left the car wedged between a hay cart and a café awning, tipped the boy who guarded it, and started up through the old quarter's vertical maze of stairs and hanging laundry, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the shadows smelled of salt, soap, and frying sardines.

He was two streets away when he heard it.

Not shouting—not yet. The Panier communicated in a language older than words: shutters closing in sequence, like dominoes falling. A woman sweeping her step with sudden, furious diligence. A child yanked indoors mid-sentence. And underneath it all, boots. Not the loose slap of fishermen's clogs. Boots, in formation, on stone.

Marguerite ran.

---

He came around the corner onto the print shop's narrow street in time to see the door standing open and four soldiers in horizon blue filling it like furniture delivered to the wrong house. A sergeant stood in the street consulting a paper. Two others were already inside; from within came the crash of a cabinet, the musical ruin of glass.

And across the street, half-hidden behind a doorway, a fifth man. Civilian clothes. Grey hat held at an angle of perfect, unhurried attention. Armand Vasseur did not watch raids the way other men watched fires—with hunger or excitement. He watched them the way an accountant watches a column of figures being summed. Checking the arithmetic.

Marguerite pressed himself into an alcove between a shrine to the Virgin and a stack of empty olive crates, and did the mathematics of his own position. Twenty meters to the shop door. Four soldiers. One sergeant. One census official whose profession, Marguerite had learned, was noticing things. If he crossed that street now, he became a name in Vasseur's ledger—a name already, he suspected, written there in pencil, waiting for ink.

Inside, a voice he knew rose above the noise of destruction. Not Liesl. The printer, old Ferrand, indignant in Provençal: "You have no warrant for the back room! The back room is my *home*, monsieur, my home—"

Something heavy hit the floor.

Marguerite closed his eyes for one second—one second, no more—and made the calculation that would keep him awake for years afterward: that the plates were already gone, that the woman might not be, that twenty meters of open street was a distance he could cross exactly once in his life and only if what waited on the far side justified the price.

He turned and went up instead. Up the stairway beside the shrine, up through the warren where the Panier stacked its poor like cordwood, along a ledge barely wider than his shoulders where someone's washing dripped on his neck, to the flat roof of the building behind the print shop.

Because he knew her now. Knew that Liesl Brandt, who had crossed a border once already with forged papers and a borrowed name, who had taught him the rhythm of a typewriter so his documents would sing in tune—who had argued for truth over safety with ink still on her fingers—would not be waiting politely by the front door to be arrested.

He was right.

---

She came over the rooftop gap like something the wind had misdelivered: a blur of grey skirt and flying dark hair, landing badly, catching herself on the parapet with a sound Marguerite felt in his own wrists. Behind her, below, the shop door spilled light and shouting. A soldier leaned out, craning upward, and someone yelled about the roofs.

"Liesl." He grabbed her arm. Her sleeve tore under his grip and neither of them apologized, because there was no time for the ceremony of apology.

"You're supposed to be at the salon," she gasped. There was soot on her cheekbone and blood at her palm where the parapet had bitten it, and her eyes were doing something he had never seen them do: darting. Calculating exits. Living like an animal.

"Ferrand—"

"They took him. They were polite about it. That's the worst part, they were *polite*, they thanked him." She laughed—a short, awful sound with no floor under it. "Vasseur was here. He stood in the street and read the seizure inventory aloud to the sergeant like a bedtime story. Every plate. Every pamphlet. *For the pacifist series, twelve plates; for the suffragist series, nine.*"

"He'll trace the typography to the salon."

"I know."

"He'll trace the *paper*—"

"*I know*, Marguerite." She said his name the way she said it only when they were alone—carefully, like a word in a language the rest of the world wasn't meant to speak. Then the shouting below swelled, boots on stairs somewhere beneath them, and her face changed, and the woman he loved disappeared into the fugitive again, quick as a lamp blown out.

"The roofs go all the way to the Place des Moulins," he said. "Then down through the tannery lanes. Nobody official goes into the tannery lanes; they can't afford the shoes."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was geography." He pulled off his jacket, wrapped her bleeding hand in the lining, tied it with a violence that surprised them both. "Listen to me. The salon is finished as a refuge—you know it and I know it. Madame Aubert has friends in Geneva who owe her favors from the congress days. There's a convoy leaving for the border within the week, Red Cross supplies. Women ride in those trucks. Nobody searches women carrying bandages; it would be *unseemly*."

Liesl stared at him. Below, a soldier's helmet appeared briefly over a chimney pot, absurd as a periscope, and sank away.

"You've been planning this," she said slowly. "Since the day I refused to leave."

"I've been planning it since 1909. I just didn't know for whom." He almost smiled. It hurt to do it. "One suitcase, Liesl. That's the rule. Everything you love fits in one case, and right now the case contains you, and the plates are somebody else's problem."

"The plates contain the names."

"The plates contain *lead*."

"The plates contain every woman who copied those petitions thinking her handwriting was anonymous!" Her voice cracked and she mastered it, jaw tight, tears standing but refusing orders to fall. "You read letters for five years and never once gave a name away. Don't ask me to do less."

Boots again, closer, on the stairway of the building they stood on. Someone had finally thought of the interior stairs, which told Marguerite the sergeant had a map and Vasseur had drawn on it.

"Later," he breathed. "Argue later. Move now."

They moved.

---

What followed was forty minutes that Marguerite would remember afterward not as fear but as a kind of terrible grace: the two of them running the spine of the old quarter hand in hand, over tiled slopes hot as griddles, dropping through a lightwell into a courtyard where a washerwoman looked up, took in their faces, and pointed silently toward the tannery lane without missing a beat of her scrubbing. The Panier hid its people the way a body hides its blood—every household a vessel, every doorway a valve.

In the shadow of the tannery walls, stinking of lime and hides, they stopped at last to breathe. Liesl leaned against the wall and laughed until it nearly became the other thing, and Marguerite held her shoulders and let her, because some storms you don't argue with, you just stand in.

"They saw me," she said finally. "The sergeant saw my face. Vasseur saw my face."

"Yes."

"Not a false name in a file anymore. A face. A torn grey skirt. A woman running over rooftops." She looked at her bandaged hand as if it belonged to a stranger. "Do you know how many times I've been invisible since Strasbourg? Five years of being nobody. I used to hate it. This morning I'd have given anything for it."

"Invisibility is a skill," Marguerite said. "Like typing. You're out of practice, that's all. We'll get you a different skirt, a different walk. You'll be somebody else by Thursday."

"And then somebody else after that? Until when?"

He had no answer that wasn't a lie, and lying to her had been forbidden by treaty since 1912. So he said nothing, and she nodded as though he'd answered, which perhaps he had.

Somewhere beyond the tannery wall, a church bell rang three o'clock. Madame Aubert had asked for noon.

* * *

They reached the salon at dusk, entering through the kitchen like thieves in their own sanctuary, and found Madame Aubert waiting with Colette's stew untouched and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair—an omen, in her private liturgy, of the gravest emergencies.

"Well?" she said.

"They have the plates. All of them. And Ferrand."

"Ferrand will say nothing. Ferrand has been saying nothing since 1871; it's his principal talent." But her eyes went to Liesl's bandaged hand, then to Marguerite's missing jacket, then to both their faces, and whatever inventory she took there, she kept to herself. "And you? Are you seen?"

"A face," Liesl said quietly. "They have my face now."

Madame Aubert removed her glasses entirely, which Marguerite had witnessed exactly twice in five years, both times involving the police. She looked at Liesl for a long moment—not with pity, which she considered an insult, but with the appraising respect of one professional recognizing another in a bad season.

"Then we stop being careful," she said. "Geneva. Within the week. Marguerite—"

But the knock came before he could answer. Three knocks, spaced with bureaucratic precision, on the front door of a house that had received no visitors all day except, at noon, a letter from the prefecture requesting a full inventory of its household.

Madame Aubert put her glasses back on. Through the window, in the last violet light, Marguerite could see the figure on the step: a grey hat, held at an angle of perfect patience.

Armand Vasseur had come to finish his census.
