## 14. Interrogation by Candlelight

The pamphlets sat on Armand Vasseur's desk like small animals that had wandered in from the cold and did not yet know they were unwelcome.

He had arranged them in a fan—seven of them, seized from the print shop in the Panier along with two trays of type, a composing stick, and one extremely indignant apprentice who had wept so thoroughly that Vasseur had sent him home with a caution and a recommendation to drink something sweet. The soldiers had wanted to burn the things in the courtyard. Vasseur had said no. Evidence, he'd explained, was meant to be *read*, and burning a document before reading it was not investigation but arson with paperwork attached.

So now it was nearly eleven o'clock, and his office smelled of tallow and ink, and Armand Vasseur, senior clerk of the census bureau of Marseille, second class, was reading seditious literature by candlelight like a man committing a sin he intended to enjoy.

*To the mothers of Provence,* the first pamphlet began. *Your sons were given rifles. You were given nothing but the waiting.*

He read it twice. Then a third time, because on the third reading he noticed what he should have noticed immediately, and noticing it made him sit very still, the way he sat still when a column of figures refused to balance.

The typeface. The em-dashes—long ones, slightly high, set with an idiosyncratic hand that left a hair of extra space after semicolons. He had seen this before. Not in a pamphlet. In petitions. In 1912, in 1913, in the neat carbon copies that arrived at his office from the suffrage society on the rue Sainte, the ones Madame Aubert signed with a flourish like a bird taking off, the ones his own clerks filed without comment because the demands were legal even if the tone was impertinent.

Someone at that salon had set this type. Someone with careful hands and strong opinions about punctuation.

Vasseur took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which he did when thinking, and put them back on, which he did when he had finished thinking and arrived somewhere he didn't like.

* * *

His office at night was the truest version of himself. By day it belonged to the Republic: ledgers squared, stamps aligned, visitors received at the appointed hour with the appointed courtesy. But after ten, when the clerks went home to their wives and their wine, the room became a private country with a population of one, governed by lamplight and method.

There was a photograph on the credenza behind him. He did not turn to look at it. He knew exactly what it showed—a young man in uniform, squinting slightly against the sun, with his mother's crooked smile and his father's stubborn chin. Étienne. Taken three weeks before mobilization, though of course no one knew that then; the photograph had believed itself to be an ordinary picture of an ordinary summer.

Étienne had been dead for five months now. A hill in the Vosges whose name Vasseur had learned only so that he could hate it precisely.

He had not cried at the funeral. He had stood at the graveside with his hands folded like a man waiting for a train, and he had thought, with terrible clarity: *the paperwork for this will be enormous.* And it had been—the notifications, the pension adjustment, the amendment of the family census card from four persons to three, which he had filled out himself because he could not bear to let a stranger do it. He remembered the pen hesitating over the word that explained the change. There was no abbreviation for *killed*. The form offered only *deceased*, as if Étienne had died of old age in a chair, as if death could be that tidy.

He had written *deceased* anyway. Method carried you through. That was the whole secret of his life: if you kept the columns straight, the world could do anything it liked to the figures and you would still be standing.

And here was the trouble, then, laid out on his desk in seven pamphlets: the columns were straight, and the figures were obscene.

*Your sons were given rifles. You were given nothing but the waiting.*

There was nothing in these pages about overthrowing the government. Nothing about German gold—he had checked, twice, with the particular suspicion of a man who had spent thirty years detecting forged sums. No calls to desert, no maps, no codes. It was, he thought with something close to despair, *clean*. It asked for an end to the war. It asked for bread for the women queueing outside bakeries. It asked whether the ministers' sons were in the trenches, and if not, why not.

Treason wore many costumes. This one wore an apron and asked polite questions in excellent French.

Vasseur poured himself the last of the coffee, found it cold, drank it anyway. His wife would have scolded him. His wife had gone to live with her sister in Avignon in October—"for the air," she said, though they both knew it was for the grief, which filled their apartment like smoke and neither of them could open a window wide enough—and so there was no one to scold him, and he missed even that.

* * *

He worked the way he always worked: outward in, from margins to center.

First, the paper. Good stock, French-made, purchased in quantity—which meant money or a sympathetic supplier. Second, the ink, standard commercial black. Third—and here he leaned close to the candle—the setting itself.

Whoever had composed these pages knew their trade. The justification was even, the spacing generous enough to be read by tired eyes by bad light, which suggested the author had thought about *who* would read it: women, mostly, in kitchens, in queues, at midnight after a telegram came. The person who set this type had imagined the reader. That was not sedition. That was—Vasseur searched for the word and disliked the one that arrived—that was *care*.

And the em-dashes. High, long, confident. He pulled the file of the Aubert society's petitions from his cabinet—he kept everything, filing was his religion—and laid a carbon beside a pamphlet. There it was. Same dash. Same breath of space after the semicolon. Same habit, visible in three documents across two years, of setting the numeral *1* with a small serif foot like a little man standing firmly on his line.

The salon on the rue Sainte had printed these. Or someone very near it.

Which brought him, inevitably, around the long curve of the evening, to Marguerite Delacroix.

He opened the folder he had built on her—not officially, officially she appeared in nothing but census cards, but every official kept a private drawer, and his was immaculate. Three addresses across six years. A woman who moved often but never far. Occupation listed variously as *seamstress*, *clerk*, and once, memorably, *none*, which no honest census-taker ever wrote down, because everyone was something. Her handwriting changed subtly between forms—not dramatically, nothing a casual eye would catch, but the slant varied by a degree or two, as if the same hand were being performed rather than merely used.

And the neighbor on the rue des Chartreux—young Madame Roux, who gossiped the way other people breathed—had said, in her artless way, the sentence that had been sitting in Vasseur's chest all week like a swallowed coin:

"Oh, the driver? Lovely creature. Though my aunt swore up and down she'd known her years ago under another name entirely. Said she used to be *someone else*. Well, you know how aunties are."

He did know how aunties were. Aunties were more accurate than archives.

But here is what Armand Vasseur did not do that night, though he drafted the memorandum three times: he did not write down what he suspected about Marguerite Delacroix's identity. Because a suspicion, once written, became a fact that lived in a drawer forever, and facts in drawers had a way of growing teeth.

* * *

What he wrote instead, on the third draft, was this: *Materials seized in the Panier raid appear connected to the Aubert petition society. Further inquiry warranted.*

Eleven words. He looked at them for a long time. They were true, and they were small, and between those two qualities lay the whole width of his conscience, which had been giving him considerable trouble since August.

Because here was the thing he could not file anywhere: he agreed with the pamphlets.

Not the printing of them—printing such things in wartime was dangerous, and dangerous things had consequences that landed on other people, usually the printers, usually at dawn. But the substance. *Your sons were given rifles.* Yes. His son had been given a rifle and a hill and a date, and the Republic had sent back a form with no proper word on it. If some anonymous compositor in the Panier wished to ask why, Vasseur privately, shamefully, at eleven o'clock at night, in a country populated by one—wished to ask it too.

He blew out one of the two candles and watched the shadows rearrange themselves.

His son had believed in order. Étienne had been the sort of boy who alphabetized his books unprompted, who turned in lost purses unopened, who enlisted in the first week because a country at war was a country with rules and he trusted rules the way other boys trusted luck. And the rules had taken him up a hill and not brought him back down, and now his father enforced those same rules in a city full of frightened people, and the question that had been circling Vasseur all autumn finally landed and would not leave:

*Of what use is a perfect ledger if the sum is wrong?*

You could keep flawless accounts of a theft. It made the theft no less a theft.

He thought about Liesl Brandt—or Marie Lambert, or whatever name she wore this week—with a clerk's genuine curiosity. A German-born typist, vanished from the records in the first week of August, which meant either flight, arrest, or concealment, and since no arrest existed in any register he could find, and since Germany was not accepting return correspondence from French typists, concealment it was. Someone was hiding her. Someone with access to paper, patience, and a talent for clean entries.

Someone, possibly, who had once been *someone else* themselves.

Vasseur rubbed his eyes until he saw sparks. He was tired in the way that had stopped being physical months ago and become architectural—a fatigue built into the load-bearing walls of him.

And yet. And yet. A man who hid a fugitive broke the law. A man who printed pleas for peace endangered the state. These were facts, and facts were his trade, and he had never once in thirty years let a fact go soft out of sentiment.

But he had also never, before this year, held a folded flag while a bugle played, and he had discovered that some facts weigh differently after that.

* * *

It was past midnight when he heard the footsteps in the corridor—no, not footsteps; the building settling, its pipes ticking as the heat went out of them. Marseille at war sounded like that at night: creaks and drips and distant sirens, a city talking in its sleep.

He took out a fresh sheet of paper and, instead of the memorandum, wrote a note. Short. Formal. The kind of summons that gave nothing away:

*Monsieur—Madame Delacroix: The bureau requires your attendance regarding household records for the current quarter. Thursday, ten o'clock. Bring your identification papers. —A. Vasseur*

He read it over. *Bring your identification papers.* Four words that would land on that careful, changing handwriting like ice water, and both of them would know precisely why, and neither of them would say it aloud. It was, he realized with faint surprise, almost a kindness—an announcement, a courtesy of the hunt. He was giving her time. He examined that impulse suspiciously, the way he'd examine an entry that balanced too neatly. Sentiment in a ledger was worse than error; error could be corrected.

But he sealed the note anyway.

Tomorrow he would deliver it himself, or send a clerk. Tomorrow he would decide whether Thursday was an interrogation or a negotiation. Tonight he sat a while longer with his candles and his pamphlets and the photograph behind him that he still would not turn to look at, and he admitted the truth into the record of himself, where no one could audit it:

He did not want to catch them. He wanted, desperately, professionally, unforgivably, to understand them—to know how a woman drove a motorcar by day and erased a person by night, how a German girl set type asking France for peace, how a salon of typists had built a small country of mercy inside his meticulous, murderous paperwork. He wanted to know whether such a thing could survive contact with a man like him.

Thursday, then. Ten o'clock. He would bring the questions, and perhaps—if the courage held, if the candle burned long enough—he would bring something else too: an offer shaped like a trap and sounding like mercy, and he would see which of them it was.

Vasseur snuffed the last candle. In the dark, the seven pamphlets waited on the desk, patient as unanswered letters, asking the only question that mattered to anyone now:

*Why not peace?*

He had until Thursday to decide what he would answer.
