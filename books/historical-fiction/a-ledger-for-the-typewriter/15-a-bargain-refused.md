## 15. A Bargain Refused

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Marguerite would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Madame Aubert scheduled her correspondence hour, and everything in his life had begun to arrange itself around letters.

It was not a letter, of course. It was a boy in a post office cap who did not work for the post office at all but for the Bureau of Census Verification, and he handed Marguerite a card so stiff it could have stood upright on the table like a small, obedient soldier. *M. Armand Vasseur requests the pleasure—* no, not the pleasure. *Requests the attendance* of Mlle Delacroix at his office on the Rue Saint-Ferréol, Thursday, at four o'clock.

"He said to tell you," the boy added, with the relish of someone delivering someone else's doom, "that it concerns household records."

"Doesn't everything?" Marguerite said, and tipped him ten centimes for his trouble, because if one was going to be summoned like a criminal one might as well be generous about it.

Madame Aubert read the card twice, then held it up to the window as though the light might reveal a second, kinder message printed between the lines.

"Four o'clock," she said. "That's a civilized hour. Vasseur is a civilized man. That's what worries me. Barbarians I could buy off or bribe or run over with the Delaunay-Belleville. Civilized men want things."

"What sort of things?"

"The sort they ask for politely." She set the card down and looked at him over her spectacles with an expression he had learned to dread, because it meant she was about to say something true. "Marguerite. If he offers you a trade—and men like Vasseur always offer trades before they offer handcuffs—remember that whatever you give him, you cannot take back."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you have the look of a man—" she caught herself, smiled faintly, corrected, "—of a person who has spent five years practicing the art of giving nothing away, and I have never known whether that practice has made you strong or merely made you tired."

"Both," Marguerite admitted. "Mostly tired."

"Then sleep well Wednesday night. You'll want your wits." She returned to her petitions, then added, without looking up: "And Marguerite—if it comes to choosing, choose the living girl over the tidy secret. Secrets can be rebuilt. Girls, in my experience, are rather harder to replace."

He wanted to ask her, right then, about the brother she had told him of—the one shot as a deserter in 1871, whose photograph she kept in a drawer she opened only when she thought no one was watching. But some doors you did not knock on. You simply noted where they were, and were grateful they existed.

* * *

Vasseur's office smelled of ink and cold coffee and, unexpectedly, of lilies—a small vase of them sat on the windowsill, absurdly domestic among the filing cabinets, and Marguerite stared at it for a full three seconds before understanding that this was deliberate. The flowers were bait of a kind. An offering. *I am a man with a mother, a garden, a life,* they said. *Sit down.*

Marguerite sat down.

Vasseur was smaller than Marguerite remembered from the salon visit—or perhaps he had simply seemed larger then, filling a doorway with his ledger and his questions. Up close he was a narrow man, precise as a ruled line, with the kind of face that had been forty years old since roughly 1898 and would remain so until it abruptly became seventy. His hands, resting on the desk, were ink-stained at the second finger of the right hand—the hand of a man who wrote everything himself and trusted no typist.

"Mlle Delacroix." He did not offer to shake. "Thank you for coming. May I offer you coffee?"

"That depends entirely on how long you intend to keep me."

Something flickered across Vasseur's face that was almost, almost a smile. He poured two cups anyway, from a pot that had clearly been reheated more than once, and pushed one across the desk.

"You drive for Madame Aubert," he began. "You have done so for three years. Before that, you appear in Marseille in the spring of 1909, with references from a dressmaking house in Lyon that closed in 1907. Before that—" he turned a page with the delicacy of a priest handling relics, "—before that, Mlle Delacroix, there is a gap. Gaps interest me. Most people leave footprints. You appear to have arrived by parachute."

"The Lyon house burned down," Marguerite said. "With its records. There was a fire. In 1907."

"There was a fire," Vasseur agreed pleasantly, "in 1907. I have read the fire report. It is very thorough." He let that sit between them like a stone dropped into still water. "Which is why it surprises me that the dressmaker's owner, a Mme Rousseau, is alive and well and living in Vienne, and when my clerk wrote to her last month asking after a former employee named Delacroix, she replied—her letter is here, you may read it—that she never employed anyone by that name."

Outside, a tram bell clanged. Somewhere below, a fishmonger was shouting the price of sardines as though the war were a rumor he refused to dignify. Marguerite drank his terrible coffee and made his face into a pleasant, patient blankness, the face he wore for traffic police and customs men and drunk passengers who asked too many questions.

"That seems careless of her," he said. "Employers forget. I was only there a season."

"A season." Vasseur wrote something down. Marguerite noticed that he wrote very little; most of what he knew, apparently, he kept in his head, arranged in drawers. "Mlle Delacroix, let me tell you why you are here, and then we may both stop pretending to enjoy this coffee."

He rose, went to a cabinet, and withdrew a folder—not thick, but dense, the way dangerous folders are. From it he took a single sheet and laid it facing Marguerite.

It was a census entry. Marguerite recognized the form instantly—he had forged enough of them by now to know them the way a counterfeiter knows a banknote, better than the mint did. This one recorded the household of one Armand Vasseur, Rue Saint-Ferréol, as of the census of 1911: himself, his late wife (the entry amended in careful black), a son—

The son's line was struck through. Amended. The date of the amendment was August of this year.

"My son Paul," Vasseur said, in a voice so flat it had clearly been ironed many times, "was a corporal in the 15th Battalion. He was killed on the 22nd of August, near Charleroi, along with most of his company. I amended the record myself. It is the only entry in fifteen years of service that I have amended with regret." He sat back down. "I tell you this not to move you—I suspect you are difficult to move—but so that you understand I know what paper does to people. Paper says my son is dead. Paper also says you do not exist before 1909. One of these statements I am prepared to accept. I wonder about the other."

Marguerite set down his cup. His hands, he noted distantly, were perfectly steady. Five years of practice, Madame Aubert had said. Strong or tired. Today, apparently, strong.

"What is it you want, Monsieur Vasseur?"

* * *

What Vasseur wanted, it emerged, was simple, and this simplicity was the most frightening thing about him.

He wanted Liesl Brandt. Not arrested—not yet, not necessarily. Found. Registered. Made real again on paper, because a woman who vanished from the census was a woman who could vanish into anything, and anything, these days, meant espionage. Every week he left her unaccounted for was a week in which his superiors asked questions he could not answer, and Vasseur had built his entire life on answering questions.

"But here is my difficulty," he said, folding his hands. "I believe you know where she is. I have believed it since the day in Madame Aubert's parlor when I mentioned her name and your breathing changed. You are very good, Mlle Delacroix. Your face never moved. Your breath moved. Faces can lie all day; lungs are amateurs." He said it without triumph, almost with respect, the way one craftsman compliments another's joinery. "So. Here is what I am prepared to offer, and I offer it once, and I will deny offering it if you repeat it."

Marguerite waited.

"Tell me where Liesl Brandt is. Tell me, and I will file my anomaly report on the Delacroix entries as a clerical duplication—an honest error, common enough, regrettable, closed. No charges. No questions about Lyon. No questions about 1909. No questions at all." He paused, and for the first time the ruled line of his mouth bent slightly. "You walk out of here clean. She walks into the record. That is the trade."

"And if she's innocent?"

"Then the record will show it. Records protect the innocent. That is their entire purpose."

Marguerite looked at the man—at the lilies wilting slightly in their vase, at the struck-through name of the dead son, at the ink-stained fingers that had amended a census entry for a dead boy with regret—and felt something unexpected, which was pity, arriving at the worst possible moment.

"No," he said.

Vasseur blinked. He had, Marguerite realized, genuinely expected acceptance. Men who lived by ledgers expected other men to live by them too; a debt was a debt, a trade was a trade.

"No?"

"No."

"May I ask why? You gain everything. She loses—" he paused, searching for the word, "—anonymity. That is all. Anonymity is not liberty, Mlle Delacroix. Believe me. I have watched people hide inside anonymity for years and call it freedom. It is a room with no windows. I am offering to open a door."

"She's German-born," Marguerite said quietly. "In August of 1914. You would open a door onto a street where every neighbor is now legally entitled to denounce her. You know it. I know it. Your own bureau has processed eleven such denunciations this month alone—I've read the mail that thanks you for them." He leaned forward. "You want her in your ledger so that your ledger is complete. I understand that. It must feel like a splinter. But completeness is not justice, Monsieur Vasseur, and I will not sell her a room without windows to buy myself one with a view."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the lilies to wilt another degree.

"You understand," Vasseur said at last, and there was something raw under the precision now, something that sounded less like an official and more like a father, "that I lost a son six weeks ago, and that the newspapers tell me daily that Germans killed him, and that every rational instrument of my profession tells me that a hidden German woman in this city is exactly the sort of thread that, pulled, unravels into treason. And you sit in my office, drinking my bad coffee, and refuse me. On principle."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because I promised her, Marguerite thought. Because in peacetime, when promises cost nothing, I made one anyway, and a promise made cheaply is still a promise. Because you are not the first man to lose someone and demand the world flatten itself around the shape of the loss.

But he said none of this. Instead he said: "Because she is one person, and I know her. And the men who make these lists never do. They count us, Monsieur Vasseur. They don't know us. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and I would think a man who amended his own census entry with regret would understand that better than anyone in France."

* * *

Later—much later, in the small hours, when the Panier was quiet and Liesl slept in the room above the print shop with the plates wrapped in oilcloth beneath her bed like a sleeping animal—Marguerite would replay the end of that conversation and marvel at what Vasseur had done next. Because Vasseur did not shout. He did not threaten. He did a thing far more unsettling.

He put the folder away.

"Here is what will happen," he said, standing, straightening his jacket with two crisp tugs. "I am required to file my report on the Delacroix anomalies. Procedure demands it. I have until Friday." He walked Marguerite to the door, and in the doorway, with the afternoon light behind him making his face unreadable, he stopped. "I am a man of order, Mlle Delacroix. Order is the only prayer I know how to say. But I will confess something to you, since you have been honest with me in your way, which is more than most people manage."

He glanced back at his desk, at the folder, at the lilies.

"When I offered you the trade just now, I hoped you would refuse me. Isn't that a foolish thing? I constructed a bargain I did not want honored, purely to learn what sort of person you were. And you refused it for reasons I would have refused it myself, thirty years ago, before I learned that order keeps the peace better than mercy does. Or so I told myself." He opened the door. "Friday, Mlle Delacroix. At close of business Friday, I file what my conscience instructs me to file. Between now and then, my conscience and I will be having words. I suggest you do the same with yours."

Marguerite stepped into the corridor. Then Vasseur added, quietly, almost as an afterthought, the sentence that would follow Marguerite down every stairway in Marseille for the rest of the week:

"For what it is worth—and it is worth nothing, officially—I do not believe she is a spy. I believe she is a frightened typist who reads too much Schiller. Which makes my job neither easier nor harder. Only sadder."

* * *

He found Liesl at the print shop, sleeves rolled, setting type by lamplight, her ink-stained fingers moving with the blind certainty of long habit. When she saw his face she set down the composing stick at once, because she had learned to read him the way he had once learned to read sealed letters—by weight, by pressure, by what showed through from the other side.

"He offered you a trade," she said. Not a question.

"He did."

"And you refused."

"I refused."

Liesl exhaled, and for a moment she looked younger than her twenty-six years, and older than any century. Then she laughed—a short, startled, disbelieving laugh—and pressed both ink-dark hands to her mouth.

"They will arrest you," she said through her fingers. "They will dig, and they will find Lyon, and before Lyon they will find 1909, and they will court-martial you for a uniform you haven't worn in five years, for a name that isn't even the one you love, and all to keep a German typist off a list." She lowered her hands. "Marguerite. Tell me you have a plan beyond being noble."

"Noble is a plan," Marguerite said. "It's a terrible plan. It's the plan of last resort. But I've been thinking, these past weeks, about what Madame Aubert said—about choosing the living girl over the tidy secret. And I've been thinking about Vasseur's conscience, having its words with him. And I think—" he heard himself say it and was surprised at how steady it sounded, how much like a decision and not like a surrender, "—I think there may be a third thing. Something between his bargain and his handcuffs."

"Which is?"

"Confession," Marguerite said. "Mine. Freely given. All of it—the desertion, the name, the forgeries—handed to him before Friday, before he has to hunt for it. Because a man like Vasseur doesn't want prisoners, Liesl. He wants order. And maybe—maybe—a confession that explains everything, that closes every gap in his beautiful ledger, will buy the one thing money couldn't."

Liesl stared at him. Outside, somewhere above the rooftops, a searchlight swept the clouds, looking for Zeppelins that were four hundred kilometers away, and the whole city lay under its borrowed light like paper under a lamp.

"Buy what?" she whispered.

"Time," said Marguerite. "Enough time to get you on a train."
