## 16. The Suitcase Rule Broken

She knew before I said it. That was the worst part.

I had rehearsed the confession on the walk up from the Vieux-Port — the whole speech, word for word, the way I'd once rehearsed a woman's laugh in front of a cracked mirror until it stopped sounding borrowed. I had planned to tell her gently, over coffee I would pretend to drink, that there was another way out of this: my way. The ledger, the desertion, the five years of forged paper — all of it laid on Vasseur's desk like a hand of losing cards, played so that no one would ever need to look at her again.

I had it all arranged in my mouth like coins, and then Liesl looked up from the printing plate she was polishing with vinegar and a rag and said, "You're going to give yourself to him."

The rag went on moving. Her hands were always steady; it was one of the first things I'd loved about her, back when love was still a word I permitted myself at a polite distance. Ink-stained fingers, steady as a metronome.

"Who told you?" I asked, which was coward's French for *yes*.

"Nobody had to." She set down the plate — the third of nine, each one wrapped now in newspaper like a loaf of bread nobody would ever eat. "You have a face you make when you're about to do something noble and stupid. You wore it the day you offered to carry me out of France in a laundry basket. You're wearing it now."

"It was a *hamper*."

"Marguerite."

"Liesl."

"You are not going to that man's office."

"And you," I said, "are not going to Switzerland with a price on your head and nine plates of seditious tin in your suitcase."

We stood in the print shop's back room among the smell of ink and cold stove iron, and outside the shuttered window the Panier went on being the Panier — someone arguing about fish two streets over, a child practicing the same four bars of piano as though the war could be worn down by repetition. Somewhere below, old Madame Roux was singing to herself, off-key, defiantly, the way people sang when they'd decided the times did not get a vote.

"The plates stay or I stay," Liesl said. It was not the first time she'd said it. It would not be the last. We had been having this argument for three days in alternating languages, which gave each of us twice as many ways to be right.

"They're evidence," I said. "They're *weight*. You taught me the rule yourself, or did you forget? One suitcase. What fits is what you keep. Everything else is the past wearing a disguise as the future."

"I never taught you any such rule. That's *your* rule. You measure your whole life by what fits in a suitcase, and it has made you a very tidy ghost." She wiped her hands and finally turned to face me fully. "Those plates aren't the past. They're proof. Proof that we printed peace while France printed enlistment posters. If I run and leave them, then everything Madame Aubert built becomes just — rumor. And if Vasseur seizes them anyway, unclaimed, they become *his* story instead of ours."

"I know what they are."

"Do you?" She came closer, and her voice dropped to the register she used for dangerous things. "Because I think you've already decided what you are. I think you decided it before you walked in here tonight, and everything since has been you arranging your goodbye."

I want it recorded, in whatever ledger this account eventually becomes, that I did not lie to her. I had lied to census takers and landladies and a colonel's widow and God Himself on at least two occasions I could name, but I did not lie to Liesl Brandt.

"If you don't go," I said quietly, "he finds you. And when he finds you, he doesn't find a pacifist typist. He finds a German national hiding under a false name in a print shop full of pamphlets, three weeks after the murder at Sarajevo. There is no version of that where the paperwork goes well. Whereas me—" I spread my hands, palms up, an honest man's gesture from a professional liar. "Me, he already suspects. Me, half-suspecting is nearly caught anyway. I trade him something real, he stops digging. That's arithmetic, Liesl. Even I can do it."

"That isn't arithmetic. That's martyrdom, and martyrdom is just vanity that's given up mirrors." But her voice cracked on *vanity*, and I saw it then — not agreement, not yet, but the terrible arithmetic beginning behind her eyes, her doing sums of her own. Who was worth more to whom. Whose absence broke fewer people.

"I'm not being noble," I told her. "I'm being selfish. Do you know how exhausting it's been, being no one? Five years of being a careful handwriting instead of a person. If I hand him the ledger, then for one hour — one hour, Liesl — somebody official reads my name and knows exactly who I am. Every crime. Every year. All of it true and signed." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too light. "It sounds like a holiday."

"Don't." She grabbed my wrist, hard, ink fingers pressing into the pulse point like she could take my heart rate hostage. "Don't joke it small. Not this."

* * *

We didn't resolve it that night. We resolved it the way most things between us were resolved: sideways, through objects.

She made coffee on the cold stove using the last of the grounds, and we drank it standing up because there was only one chair and neither of us would take it. And somewhere between the first cup and the second, without either of us announcing anything, the plates began to be unwrapped.

I noticed it first as a sound — newspaper unfolding, that dry whisper. Then Liesl was holding plate number four in both hands, looking at it the way you look at a photograph of someone who has died.

"If they take them," she said, "they'll melt them down or lock them in a warehouse, and in twenty years some clerk will sell them for scrap. No one will ever know what they said."

"No."

"But if they're *never found* — if the shop is empty when they come, empty of press, empty of type, empty of me — then the pamphlets become ghosts too. Unprovable. A rumor with no body." She looked up. "You'd like that. You're very good at making things unprovable."

"It's my one marketable skill."

And that was how it ended — not with surrender, not with a grand speech, but with Liesl Brandt setting down a printing plate she had defended for three days, and saying, in German, very softly, something I only understood afterward: *then carry me out, and carry what I couldn't.* She was leaving the plates not because she believed the suitcase rule. She was leaving them because she had learned, from watching me read other people's letters, exactly what I intended to do in Vasseur's office — and she had chosen to let me do it rather than lose me to a worse arithmetic.

Neither of us said this aloud. Some bargains are too heavy to sign.

* * *

We packed together the next evening, in the back room, by one lamp, in near-silence.

There is a particular silence that falls between two people who are memorizing each other. It isn't comfortable and it isn't sad, exactly. It's the silence of a painter working fast before the light goes. Liesl folded a blouse and held it against her cheek for a moment before it went into the suitcase. I watched her do it and pretended I hadn't, and then, because pretending had gotten expensive lately, I said, "Do that again."

"Do what?"

"The thing with the blouse."

She looked at me. The lamp hissed. And then, deliberately, wickedly, she picked up a plain grey stocking, pressed it to her cheek, closed her eyes, and moaned with theatrical pleasure until I laughed — really laughed, the ugly snorting kind I usually kept locked away with everything else — and she grinned at me across the suitcase like a girl who had never seen a border in her life.

"There," she said. "Now you'll remember me ridiculous. That's better than tragic. Tragic gets forgotten. Ridiculous gets retold."

"What I'll remember," I said, "is your hands. Always ink. You could wash them in the sea and they'd still print."

"Yours shake."

"Not tonight."

"They shook Tuesday. In front of the lamp. You thought I wasn't watching." She took my hands then, turned them over, examined the palms like a fortune-teller who already knew the ending and was stalling for time. "When you see him — when you give him whatever you're going to give him — hold them flat on the desk first. Before you speak. Flat and still. He's a man who reads hands the way you read letters. Give him steadiness to read."

"When did you become the expert on Armand Vasseur?"

"The night you described him. You talked about him for forty minutes and called him 'the enemy' eleven times. People don't count repetitions about men who don't matter." She folded my fingers closed over my own palms, keeping the advice inside like a coin in a fist. "He lost someone. You said his file was thin but his face wasn't. Men whose faces are heavier than their files have buried someone."

The suitcase filled slowly, the way a life does when you're paying attention. Two dresses, chosen for dullness. Papers — the Marie Lambert papers, clean as new snow, the finest forgery of my career, though she'd helped tune the typewriter's rhythm until even I couldn't tell which lines were hers and which were mine. A photograph of her sister in Strasbourg, taken before anyone had heard of Sarajevo. The unsent letters, all of them — she wouldn't leave those behind, and I wouldn't have let her; I had read every one, after all, and a reader keeps what he reads.

At the bottom, last, she placed a small tin box. Inside: a printer's thumb of ink, dried to a black jewel, and — I confess it — one of my own letters. From the early days. From the pact. *If war ever comes, I will carry you out of France,* in my careful, invented hand. She had kept my promise in a tin like a relic.

"That's cheating," I said. "That doesn't fit the rule."

"The rule is broken," she said simply. "You broke it the moment you decided to trade yourself for me. A woman may as well pack sentimentally after that."

* * *

Near midnight we sat on the floor, backs against the press, sharing the last cup of coffee and the last of a bag of candied chestnuts Madame Aubert had sent up with the driver's compliments, meaning mine, meaning *be careful*, meaning *come home*, all compressed into sugar.

"We should sleep," Liesl said.

"In a minute."

"In a minute," she agreed, and didn't move.

Her head found my shoulder. I sat extremely still, the way you do when a bird lands on you — not from fear it will fly off, but from the knowledge that it must. Outside, the Panier had gone quiet except for the sea doing its endless clerical work against the harbor stones. Somewhere across the city, Armand Vasseur was presumably asleep beside a neat stack of files, and somewhere beyond him the border lights were burning at Annemasse and Geneva, patient as stars.

"Tell me something true," Liesl said. "One thing. For the road."

I considered my inventory. Five years of true things, all contraband.

"My name isn't Marguerite," I began, "but it is now. That's the truest thing I own." I turned my head and felt her hair against my jaw. "And I read your sister's address off an envelope in 1912 and I have known it by heart ever since, and if you ever stop writing to me I will find her, and I will stand in her kitchen in Strasbourg and tell her embarrassing stories until she writes to you just to make me stop."

Liesl laughed into my shoulder, wet and quiet. "That's a threat."

"It's a promise. I keep those. Ask anyone whose mail I've intercepted."

She pulled back then and looked at me for a long moment, doing her memorizing openly now, no pretense of folding clothes — my face, my hands, the collar of the dress I'd wear tomorrow into an office I might not walk out of as a free woman. And I memorized her back: the ink ghost under her fingernails, the stubborn line of her mouth, the way lamplight pooled in the hollow of her throat.

"Thursday," she said finally. "The convoy leaves Thursday morning with the Red Cross supplies. Madame Aubert confirmed it tonight. I'll be under the bandages, and I'll be thinking of a man reading my letters in a salon in 1912, deciding to be kind instead of safe." Her voice held. It cost her; I watched it cost her. "So be steady in that office. Flat hands. And Marguerite—"

"Yes."

"Whatever you give him. Don't give him everything. Keep one page back. Everyone needs one page the record can't reach."

I promised. It was the second promise I made that week that I already knew I would break, because the ledger I meant to lay on Vasseur's desk was complete — every page, every crime, every name — and a complete ledger was the only currency he would accept for a life.

I walked her down the stairways of the Panier at dawn, one suitcase between us, carried alternately, neither of us willing to let go of the handle for long. At the corner where Rue Sainte split toward the port, she stopped.

"Thursday morning," she said.

"Thursday morning."

And it was only later — that afternoon, breathless, Madame Aubert's housekeeper waving a telegram in the doorway — that I learned the world had voted against our arithmetic: the convoy had been moved up. Twenty-four hours. It would roll at dawn Wednesday.

The same hour Vasseur expected me in his office.
