## 17. The Night Train Problem

The telegram arrived at eleven minutes past seven on a Tuesday morning, folded inside a delivery of bandages that had no business being delivered at all.

Marguerite knew the system. Madame Aubert's network ran on mislabeled parcels the way Marseille ran on pastis—quietly, habitually, and with everyone pretending not to notice. A crate marked *SURGICAL COTTON* might contain pamphlets. A box marked *PAMPHLETS*—never used, but kept in the ledger as a joke only three people understood—might contain actual surgical cotton, because even conspirators needed gauze. So when the porter from the Hôtel des Postes set down a parcel of bandages and said, "There's a paper tucked in with the compresses, madame, the clerk thought you'd want it separate," Marguerite's hands went still over the tea tray.

He waited until the porter's boots had reached the bottom of the stairs before he opened it.

The message was written in pencil on the back of a pharmacy receipt, in the cramped hand of Doctor Reynaud at the border depot:

*CONVOY ADVANCED. DEPARTURE WEDNESDAY 6 A.M. NOT THURSDAY. NO EXPLANATION GIVEN. R.*

Marguerite read it twice. Then a third time, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder if he gave them the chance.

Wednesday. Six in the morning.

And Vasseur's final interview—the interview Marguerite had been dreading for nine days, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone—was scheduled for Wednesday at six o'clock in the evening.

Twenty-four hours. The whole plan had been built like a bridge across a river, with his confession timed to fall exactly between Vasseur's suspicion and Liesl's escape, and someone upstream had just moved the river.

He set the receipt down on the tea tray, picked up the teapot, and poured two cups with perfectly steady hands. This was the trick he'd learned in the army and perfected in five years of living a lie: the hands could be trained to know nothing. It was the rest of him that trembled.

"Madame Aubert," he called toward the study, "I think you should come see what the post office has sent us."

* * *

She came in drying her hands on her apron, because Madame Aubert wore an apron even to receive deputies and bishops, and read the receipt without touching it, which was her way of refusing to acknowledge bad news until she'd decided what to do about it.

"Reynaud wouldn't move the date lightly," she said at last.

"No."

"Which means either the military timetable changed—and those change for reasons no one explains to doctors—or someone at the border is nervous." She looked up. Her eyes were pale gray and entirely unsentimental. "Or someone here has made them nervous. Has Vasseur been asking questions again?"

"He sent me a card yesterday confirming Wednesday evening. He was almost courteous." Marguerite turned the teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, then back. "Courteous from Vasseur is worse than rude. Rude means he thinks he knows everything already. Courteous means he wants to watch my face."

"Then we must make sure your face has nothing interesting to say." Madame Aubert pulled out a chair and sat with the decisive motion of a woman who had spent forty years chairing committees that men assured her were unnecessary. "Sit down, Marguerite. We're going to think. Thinking is free, and unlike everything else in this country just now, it still works."

So they thought.

The problem laid itself out on the table between the teacups with terrible clarity. Liesl had to be in the Red Cross convoy when it left the warehouse on the Canebière at six Wednesday morning—hidden among crates of dressings, in the false compartment they'd built into the ambulance body, with papers identifying her as Sister Marie Lambert, orderly second class. That part was settled; it had been settled for a week, along with the packing, the route, the bribed customs clerk at Modane who owed Madame Aubert a favor older than the war. But Marguerite's half of the bargain—the surrender of the forged ledger, the confession that would pull Vasseur's eyes away from the convoy and onto Marguerite himself—had been designed to happen Wednesday *evening*, hours after the border lights of Geneva had swallowed a certain German typist whole.

Now both things wanted the same morning.

"If I confess Tuesday night," Marguerite said slowly, "Vasseur sits on me through the night. He telephones the border by dawn out of sheer thoroughness—he's incapable of not checking something once it occurs to him. Every checkpoint from here to Switzerland gets a description."

"And if you wait until Wednesday evening as planned," Madame Aubert said, "and the convoy is stopped at six in the morning—which it will be, if he's moved his own schedule to match ours, and I'd bet my late husband's library that he has—then Liesl is taken, and your confession becomes a eulogy instead of a shield."

They looked at each other across the wreckage of their careful arithmetic.

"There's a third way," Madame Aubert said, and there was something in her voice Marguerite hadn't heard before—not the brisk command she wore like armor, nor the dry humor she used like a fan, but something quieter underneath. "It's ugly, and I don't like it, and I'm going to say it anyway. You go to the interview Wednesday morning. Not to confess. To hold him."

"Hold him how? With conversation?"

"With yourself." She said it without flinching, which was the only way she ever said anything. "You are the most interesting thing in that man's file, Marguerite Delacroix. Three addresses, conflicting records, a neighbor who talks too much, hands that shake. He doesn't care about Liesl Brandt—one German typist among ten thousand. What he cares about is *error*, and you are the most magnificent error he has ever found. If you sit in front of him Wednesday morning and let him interrogate you—really interrogate you, hours of it—you will be giving him exactly what he wants. And a man who is getting what he wants does not telephone the border."

"And while he's savoring me," Marguerite said, "the convoy rolls through."

"While he's savoring you," she agreed. "And then, when the wire comes from Modane that the ambulance passed clean—" she glanced at the clock on the mantel, "—you give him the ledger. All of it. Because he'll have earned it, in his strange way, and because by then it won't matter anymore."

It was a good plan. Marguerite recognized a good plan the way he recognized good weather—gratefully, and without quite trusting it. But something in him resisted, some old soldier's instinct that the moment a battle depends on one man keeping another man talking, the universe starts sharpening its pencil.

"What if he asks the wrong question?" Marguerite said quietly. "What if, halfway through, he stops being interested in errors and starts being interested in where Marie Lambert went?"

"Then you lie," Madame Aubert said. "You've had practice."

"I've had practice lying to people who weren't looking. He *looks*, Madame. He looks like a man reading a document."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down on the street below, a fishmonger was crying his wares in the rising heat, and somewhere a bugle band was rehearsing for the departure of yet another regiment, the notes thin and brave and slightly flat.

"Well," said Madame Aubert at last, standing and pulling off her apron with a snap. "Then we had better make sure you're worth looking at. Go and pack, Marguerite. And take this to Liesl tonight—she'll need to hear it from you, not from a pharmacy receipt." She pressed the telegram into his hand. "Tell her—" She paused, and for once the committee chairman fumbled for words. "Tell her that whatever happens Wednesday, she crosses that border owing nothing to anyone. That's the whole point of what we do. Tell her she goes free."

* * *

The Panier district at dusk was all shadow and stairway, a quarter built by people who had never trusted anyone enough to build on flat ground. Marguerite climbed the steps behind the print shop two at a time, the telegram in his breast pocket like a coal, and knocked the pattern on the door—two, pause, three—that meant *it's me and it's safe* and also, though neither of them had ever said so aloud, *it's me*.

Liesl opened the door with the lamp already lit behind her, which meant she'd heard his footsteps on the stairs and known them, which meant she'd been listening for them, which was a fact Marguerite stored away the way he stored everything about her: carefully, in the place where he kept things he couldn't afford to lose.

"You're early," she said. "You're never early. Something's happened."

"Can't a man simply visit?"

"A man can. You can't. You're the most punctual person I've ever met, and I worked for a railway office in Strasbourg." She stepped back to let him in, and he saw that she'd already packed. Two small cases stood by the door, squared and strapped, and the room behind her had been stripped down to bare walls and a swept floor, as if she'd been practicing disappearing for days.

"The departure's moved up," he said, because there was no gentle way to build to it. "Tomorrow morning. Six o'clock."

He watched her take it. She didn't gasp or clutch anything; she went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and then she crossed to the window and looked out at the rooftops for a long time.

"Six tomorrow," she said finally. "And your interview with the census man?"

"Six tomorrow evening."

"So the whole day." She laughed—a short, unamused sound. "Someone up there has a sense of humor. All right. All right. Tell me the plan. You have one, or Madame Aubert has one, which is better."

He told her. The ambulance, the false compartment, Sister Marie Lambert's papers, the customs man at Modane. And then the part he told last, watching her face: that he would sit in Vasseur's office from morning until the wire came through, being fascinating, being erroneous, being the single most suspicious individual in the Provence census.

When he finished, Liesl was quiet for so long that the lamp flame seemed loud.

"No," she said.

"Liesl—"

"No." She turned from the window. "That's not a plan, that's a sacrifice with extra steps. You'll hand him yourself on a plate and pray he chews slowly. What if the wire is delayed? What if the road washes out? What if he decides at noon, instead of evening, that he's heard enough?" She came toward him, ink-stained fingers catching the lamplight. "Give him the ledger now. Tonight. Let them arrest you tonight, if it comes to that, and I will walk out of this city tomorrow knowing exactly what it cost and exactly who paid."

"And every checkpoint between here and Geneva gets your description before sunrise. You know that. We've been over it."

"Then I'll walk!" Her voice cracked upward. "Over the mountains, like the smugglers do, like—"

"In shoes you don't have, in a language you barely speak, past guards who shoot first at anyone moving at night near a border." He took her hands. They were cold. "Liesl. Look at me. The plan works. It works because it's awful, and awful plans are the ones nobody prepares for. Madame Aubert thought of it herself."

That landed. He saw it land—the slight easing of her jaw, the reluctant respect that Madame Aubert commanded even in absentia, the way a storm commands sailors.

"She would enjoy hearing you admit that," Liesl said.

"She'd frame it."

Liesl laughed despite herself, a real laugh this time, brief and startled out of her, and then she pulled her hands free and did something she had never done in all the months of their wary, word-starved friendship. She put her arms around him and held on.

Marguerite stood rigid for a heartbeat—old habits, old fears, the whole architecture of a life spent making sure no one got close enough to notice anything—then closed his eyes and held her back. She was smaller than he'd always privately imagined, and she smelled of printer's ink and cheap soap, and her heart against his chest was going fast, faster than her composure ever allowed her face to show.

"If this works," she said into his shoulder, "if I'm in Switzerland by Thursday and you're—wherever you are—"

"Wherever I am."

"Then you'll write to me. Properly. None of this reading-my-mail-from-a-distance nonsense. Actual letters, with your actual handwriting, saying actual things."

"That sounds terrifying," Marguerite said.

"Good," said Liesl. "You've terrified me for years. Fair's fair."

They packed together after that, or rather she repacked and he handed her things, and the work took on that hush that falls over people who are each trying to memorize the other without being caught at it. He learned that she folded her clothes into squares, precisely, like documents. She learned that he could not pass the little tin of pencils on her shelf without straightening them. Neither of them mentioned Wednesday. Both of them glanced at the clock.

At the door, with the night warm and smelling of salt and stone, she stopped him with a hand on his sleeve.

"The letters," she said. "The ones you read, all those years ago, that started all this. The ones to my sister." She hesitated, and in the dark he could hear the smile rather than see it. "Bring them tomorrow. When you go to surrender your grand criminal ledger. I want you carrying something of mine into that office. For luck."

"It's evidence," Marguerite said. "Sentimental, incriminating evidence."

"Everything important is," said Liesl Brandt, and closed the door.

* * *

He walked home through streets gone quiet under curfew, and the city felt like a held breath. At the house, Madame Aubert was waiting up in the kitchen with the lamp turned low and two glasses and a bottle of the good brandy she claimed not to own.

"To tomorrow," she said, pouring.

"To tomorrow," Marguerite agreed.

They drank. She studied him over the rim of her glass with those unsentimental gray eyes, and then, instead of any of the speeches he braced for, she said: "Do you remember what you told me the first week you drove for me? About why you took the job?"

"I said I liked the routes."

"You said you liked the routes." She smiled faintly. "You were a terrible liar then, too. Go to bed, Marguerite. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow you have to be the most interesting man in France for eight hours, and it's hard work being interesting when you're tired."

He climbed the stairs to his narrow room, where everything he owned fit in one suitcase—it had always fit in one suitcase; that was the rule, that had been the rule since 1909—and lay down on the bed without undressing.

From the street below came the sound of the last tram, and then the bugle band again, far off, still rehearsing in the dark.

At four minutes past midnight, a knock at the front door. Soft. Deliberate. Two knocks, a pause, three—

—and Marguerite was on his feet before his mind caught up with his body, because that was *his* knock, *their* knock, the Panier knock, and Liesl had no reason to be here tonight. No reason except that the plan had changed. No reason except that something, somewhere, had moved twenty-four hours in the wrong direction.

Downstairs, Madame Aubert's lamp bloomed to life. Marguerite took the stairs two at a time.
