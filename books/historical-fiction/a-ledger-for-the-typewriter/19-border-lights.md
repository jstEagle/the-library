## 19. Border Lights

The ambulance smelled of carbolic and oranges.

Liesl Brandt—Marie Lambert for eleven days now, though the name still sat on her like a borrowed coat—lay wedged between crates of Red Cross bandages and a crate of oranges that nobody had thought to explain. The oranges, she had decided somewhere outside Aix, were either a stroke of genius or a catastrophe. Genius, because no customs officer in the world would suspect contraband human flesh hidden among something so cheerful. Catastrophe, because she had eaten four of them out of nerves, and if anyone counted the crate against its manifest, there would be questions.

She lay very still and listened to the convoy.

There were three vehicles: the ambulance she occupied, a supply truck behind it, and a small car at the rear where Madame Aubert sat upright with her hat pinned as though she were attending a funeral she intended to win. The engines droned. The road climbed. Somewhere ahead was the border, and somewhere behind her—hours back now, a whole world back—was Marseille, and the Panier, and Marguerite Delacroix, who had not come to say goodbye.

That was the part she kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He hadn't come. There had been only a note, left under the print shop's ruined door in handwriting she knew better than her own: *The truck leaves at five. Don't be late. Don't be brave about it.* No farewell. No promise. Just logistics, delivered flat as a railway timetable, from a man who had once read her mail and made it sound like poetry.

*Fine*, she told the darkness under the canvas. *Fine.* She was good at leaving without farewells. She'd been practicing since Strasbourg.

The ambulance jolted over a rut. An orange rolled against her hip, insistent, like a small round conscience.

*You are Marie Lambert,* she reminded herself, silently, in French, because she'd learned to think in French when afraid. It was one of the many small treacheries of survival: even her fear now spoke with a foreign accent. *Marie Lambert, typist, born Lyon, twenty-six years old, parents deceased, no siblings worth mentioning.*

No siblings worth mentioning. The lie was the cruelest of all of them, because it was true. Her sister Greta was in Strasbourg, on the other side of the war, and every letter Liesl had ever written her sat unsent in a drawer in Marseille until a man who shouldn't have existed found them and refused to look away.

She pressed her hand flat against the floor of the ambulance and felt the letters through the wrapping paper. That was the whole of her luggage—the entire inventory of Liesl Brandt, twenty-eight years of German girlhood and French womanhood reduced to a bundle of correspondence that had never crossed any border at all. Marguerite's rule had been one suitcase. She had beaten his record by an entire suitcase.

*Don't be brave about it.*

She wasn't going to be brave. She was going to be obedient and invisible and alive, which was harder.

* * *

They stopped twice before dark.

The first time was for petrol, and Liesl listened to the driver—a laconic Provençal named Roux who smelled permanently of tobacco and engine oil—argue amiably with a garage man about the price of everything in France since August. She had learned in eleven days of hiding that the war had made everyone an economist. Bread cost more. Petrol cost more. Sons cost more than anyone could pay, and everyone was keeping accounts.

The second time was worse. Boots on gravel, voices, a lantern held up to the ambulance's side. Roux said something bored and bureaucratic; another voice said something bored and more bureaucratic; and then the canvas flap lifted six inches and lamplight fell across her feet, missing her by the width of a prayer.

"Medical supplies," Roux said. "Geneva. You want to count the bandages, be my guest, but we're late and the Swiss are fussy."

A pause. Then, mercifully, the flap dropped, and the light went away, and Liesl discovered she had been holding an orange so tightly that juice had run down her wrist. She licked it off and felt briefly, absurdly, like a criminal of the first order—not for hiding, but for wasting citrus during wartime.

When they moved again, she allowed herself the luxury of one full minute of terror, spent it completely, and then put it away. Madame Aubert had taught her that trick, or rather had demonstrated it without teaching: the great suffragist could absorb a catastrophe over tea and be discussing timetables before the cups were cleared. *Fear is a servant,* she had said once, arranging Liesl's false papers on the table with two fingers, as though dealing cards. *Useful in the house. Fatal at the table.*

Liesl lay back among the bandages and tried to make fear useful. Fear said: *check the papers again.* She checked them. Marie Lambert, transit permit, medical auxiliary, stamped and countersigned with stamps Marguerite had taught himself to cut from potato halves and cork and, in one unforgettable afternoon of inspired madness, the heel of a boot. Fear said: *memorize your story.* She did. Fear said: *and what happens at the border, where they will actually look at you, actually speak to you, actually want you to be a person instead of a parcel?*

Fear had no answer for that one. So Liesl closed her eyes and did what she always did with insoluble problems: she thought about typography. The border would come or it wouldn't. Somewhere a typesetter was setting tomorrow's lies into tomorrow's newspapers, and every letter of it would sit exactly where it was meant to sit, and none of it would be true, and yet the page would hold together. That was all she asked of herself tonight. To hold together.

* * *

They drove through the night, and near dawn the mountains arrived.

She felt them before she saw them—the road tilting, the engine straining, the air coming through the canvas seams thinner and colder and cleaner than any air Marseille had ever offered. When Roux stopped to check a wheel, she risked lifting the flap an inch, and there they were: the Jura, blue-black and enormous, wearing their snow like officials wearing sashes. Between two foothills, far off, a village smoked gently into the morning, chimneys rising straight up in the windless cold.

It was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when you are about to gamble your life on whether someone reads a stamp correctly.

The convoy regrouped in a valley village whose name she never learned. Madame Aubert came to the ambulance's tailgate, looked in at Liesl lying among the oranges like a very tired saint in an icon of dubious theology, and adjusted her gloves.

"You look terrible," Madame Aubert said fondly.

"I've eaten nine oranges."

"A good omen. Citrus is the fruit of the bourgeois; no one suspects a refugee of liking it." She passed in a thermos of coffee, real coffee, hoarded from some prewar treasury. "The crossing is at Vallorbe. There will be a checkpoint. There will be a man from the census office attached to the inspection—I don't know why, the paperwork has been circulating through three ministries and each one has added its own signature, God help us all. His name is Delorme. If he asks you anything, answer slowly. Foreigners rush. Innocent people are bored."

"Innocent people are bored," Liesl repeated.

"And frightened people are polite. Boredom is the thing to aim for." Madame Aubert paused, and for a moment the general gave way to the grandmother, and her voice dropped. "Marguerite should have come to see you off."

"He sent a note."

"He sends notes." Something moved behind her eyes—not guilt exactly; more like the expression of a woman holding a door shut from the inside. "He is... attending to something else. He asked me to tell you—" She stopped, recalibrated, chose honesty over comfort, which was her most expensive habit. "He asked me to tell you nothing, actually. He said you'd know."

Liesl lay back against the bandages. *I'd know.* Typical. Five years of friendship conducted entirely in subtext, sealed pacts and unsent letters and a promise carried like a stone in the pocket, and at the end of it all, the man couldn't manage three words at a train station. She was furious at him. She catalogued the fury carefully, itemized it, wrapped it in brown paper and string, and put it with the letters, because fury at Marguerite Delacroix was also a form of luggage she intended to keep.

"When we cross," Madame Aubert said, "there is a woman in Lausanne. Her name is on the inside of your collar. She runs a boarding house and a very illegal newspaper, sometimes simultaneously. You will be safe, and then you will be useful, and then—one day, when the maps stop changing—you may even be happy. In that order. Do not attempt to reorder them."

"Yes, madame."

"Madame Aubert. We are past 'madame,' I should think. I've smuggled you across half of Provence; the least you can do is use my Christian name, which is Hélène, and which I haven't heard spoken kindly since 1908."

"Hélène."

"There." The old woman's mouth twitched. "Now you're one of my girls, and God help the Republic, because my girls are everywhere."

* * *

The checkpoint appeared around ten in the morning, materializing out of the mountain fog like a bad idea.

Liesl heard it before she saw it: a barrier arm, voices, the wet slap of a flag in cold air. The ambulance slowed, stopped. Through a gap in the canvas she could see a strip of the scene—a guardhouse, two gendarmes in capes, a stovepipe breathing smoke, and beyond the barrier, tantalizingly, a second flag. Swiss. Red with a white cross, hanging limp in the mist like a hand not yet extended.

Her heart performed maneuvers that would have failed any army medical examination.

Boots approached. The flap lifted. And this time the light fell fully into the ambulance, and a face came with it—and Liesl's blood, which had been merely panicking, now performed something structural.

She knew that face.

Not well. Twice, maybe three times, in the corridors of the census office in Marseille, carrying files for Armand Vasseur: a young man with a long serious face and ink on his fingers, the clerk who stamped while Vasseur considered. Delorme. The name arrived a half-second after the face, and with it came the full weight of what it meant: the office that had hunted her, the ledger that had condemned her, Vasseur's own right hand, standing at the border with her papers in his reach.

*He'll know the file. He'll know the name. He stamped the warrant, or he watched it being typed, he sat at that desk, he—*

Delorme looked at her. She looked at him. The fog stood around them like a jury.

"Passenger manifest?" he said to Roux, and his voice was exactly as she remembered it: dry, unhurried, the voice of a man who had written so many numbers that numbers had become his native language.

"Medical auxiliary, one. Papers in the folder."

Delorme took the folder. Liesl watched him open it and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was watching her life pass before someone else's eyes. Marie Lambert, born Lyon. Transit permit. Medical authorization. Every stamp Marguerite had cut from potatoes and cork and the heel of a boot, arranged in an order designed to satisfy the eye of a bureaucrat moving quickly.

But Delorme was not moving quickly.

He turned a page. Turned another. Held the transit permit up against the gray light, tilted it slightly—the way men do when checking watermarks, the way Marguerite had warned her real inspectors would—and Liesl's soul quietly left her body, walked a few steps toward Switzerland, realized it had forgotten its papers, and came back.

"The auxiliary," Delorme said. "Name?"

"Marie Lambert." Slowly. Bored. Innocent people are bored. She let her voice flatten into the tone of a woman who had been in an ambulance since yesterday and wanted, principally, coffee.

"Born?"

"Lyon. Twenty-six years ago. Is something wrong with the permit, monsieur? Only the convoy is late, and the matron will have my head."

Something flickered across Delorme's face. Not suspicion—that was the horrifying part. Something almost like recognition, but pointed elsewhere, aimed at some private calculation of his own. He looked at the papers. He looked at her. He looked, briefly, at the oranges, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he disliked.

And then Armand Vasseur's clerk did the last thing in the world Liesl expected.

He closed the folder.

"Convoy's in order," he said to Roux, and handed the folder back, and stamped the manifest against his knee with the rubber stamp he carried on a strap, one hard decisive blow, the way a man closes a door on a room he has decided not to enter. "Medical traffic goes through priority since November. Tell your matron she can thank the ministry for one thing."

Roux grunted thanks enough for both of them.

Delorme stepped back. And just before the barrier rose, just before the ambulance lurched forward into the no-man's-land of the crossing, he looked once more into the gap in the canvas—at Liesl, at Marie Lambert, at whoever he believed or declined to believe she was—and said, quietly, in a voice too low for the gendarmes:

"The census makes errors. It's known for it."

Then the flap fell, the barrier rose, the wheels rolled.

* * *

For a hundred meters, Liesl did not breathe.

Then the Swiss flag was beside her rather than ahead of her, then behind her, then gone, and the road ran white and clean between snowfields, and Roux began to whistle—an old Marseille tune, off-key, indecent in its cheerfulness—and the mountains opened like hands.

She laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, halfway to sobbing, and she pressed her fist against her mouth and laughed anyway, alone in the back of the ambulance among the bandages and the orange peels, shaking with a relief so total it felt indistinguishable from grief. They had waved her through. The hunting office's own clerk had looked at her forged life and set it down like something fragile, and said, in effect: *go.* Whether it was mercy, or indifference, or some order from above that she would never see, she didn't know and didn't care. She had been saved by paperwork, and she had been saved by the absence of it, and both times the saving had worn a bureaucratic face.

The ambulance descended toward the lake. By early afternoon the fog burned away and there was Lausanne below them, red roofs stacked above blue water, neutral and ordinary and impossibly, obscenely peaceful. Church bells were ringing for no reason at all except that it was noon.

At the boarding house, Madame Aubert embraced her once, briskly, like a general decorating a survivor, and then held on a moment longer than the decoration required.

"You'll write?" she said. "Through the channel. Nothing sentimental. Sentiment gets read."

"I'll write nothing sentimental," Liesl promised, and meant to break the promise immediately.

That evening, alone in a narrow clean room with a view of the lake going violet, she unwrapped the letters. Twenty-two of them, unsent, addressed to a sister in a city that now belonged to the war. She spread them on the bed and touched them one by one, and found, folded small at the bottom of the bundle, a page she had never seen before—in Marguerite's hand, undated, unsigned:

*If you are reading this, you are in Switzerland, which means the promise kept itself. I have read every letter you ever wrote and I am not sorry. Whoever you decide to be next, write to me as her. I will know the handwriting.*

No address. Of course not. No address, no farewell, no explanation of why he hadn't come, no word of what he was "attending to" while the convoy rolled east without him. Just that—arrogant, tender, infuriating, exactly like him.

Liesl sat by the window a long time, watching the lights come on along the far shore, another country's lights, harmless ones. Then she took out the boarding house's single sheet of paper, dipped the pen, and began.

*Dear Marguerite,* she wrote. *I am bored, therefore innocent. I have eaten eleven oranges and I intend to tell you about every one of them. But first you must tell me what you did—*

She stopped. Because she already knew. She knew the way she knew a misprinted line on a proof sheet—by the shape of the silence where the type should have been. He hadn't come to the departure because he had made himself into the thing that stayed behind. He had traded himself for her passage, the way you trade a plate for the meal, and somewhere back in Marseille a man with meticulous handwriting was accepting the payment.

Liesl put down the pen. Outside, the lake lay still, and the border she had crossed was a hundred kilometers and an entire act of mercy away.

She picked the pen back up, and wrote instead:

*Whatever you have done, undo half of it. Keep the rest. I am keeping count, and I intend to collect you in person when this war remembers how to end.*

She signed it with a name that existed on no census anywhere, sealed it, and gave it to Madame Aubert's channel the next morning.

Then she unpacked her one piece of luggage—twenty-three letters now, counting her own—and began, patiently, to wait.
