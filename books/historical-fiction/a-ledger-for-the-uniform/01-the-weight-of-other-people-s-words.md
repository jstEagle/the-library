## 1. The Weight of Other People's Words

There was a particular way Edith Marlowe sealed a letter, and Yara Nakamura had watched it so many times from the driver's seat of her own attention that she could have performed it blindfolded: the tongue along the gummed edge, the thumb pressed flat and slow from left to right as though smoothing down a small stubborn animal, the finished envelope set face-up on the desk with its corners squared to the wood. It was a ritual of finality. Once sealed, a letter stopped being a thought and became an event.

Yara had already read every word inside this one.

She stood now in the morning room of the house on Campden Hill with six envelopes fanned across her forearm like a card trick she intended never to reveal, and she waited for Mrs. Marlowe to finish at the writing desk, because a chauffeur did not hurry her employer and a chauffeur certainly did not look at what she was carrying—except, of course, that she had looked, thoroughly, twice, once by lamplight last night when the household slept and once again over breakfast tea while the letters lay unattended beside the marmalade.

The letters were going to three addresses. Two were safe houses in the East End—a printer's loft off Cable Street, a widow's rooms above a chandler's in Wapping—where women whose names could not appear on any march list would sleep tomorrow night after Hyde Park, when the police had finished their work and the work of the police was bruises. The third was to a Member of Parliament whose sympathies were real but whose courage required handling, like a bird with a broken wing, and the letter handled him accordingly, with a firmness that had made Yara smile into her tea.

*You will speak on the platform or you will explain to your constituents why you did not,* Mrs. Marlowe had written, in a hand that leaned forward like a runner at a starting line. *I am too old for flattery and you are too ambitious for cowardice. Both of us should mind our natures.*

Yara had memorized it anyway. She memorized everything. It was not spying, she told herself, in the way that people tell themselves things in the space between two o'clock and four in the morning, when the truth is asleep. It was only that words crossed through her hands the way water crosses a lock, and it seemed wasteful—not to mention dangerous—to let so much meaning pass without knowing its depth. A chauffeur who knew where the car was going and why was a better chauffeur. That was her creed, the practical one, the one she kept in daylight.

The other creed she kept in the dark, folded up small: *history happens elsewhere, to other people.* Wars happened in the Balkans, to Serbs. Revolutions happened in Russia, to students with bad coats. Movements happened in meeting halls, to women with loud voices and names printed in newspapers. Yara drove the car that carried one such woman to the edges of all these events, and then she parked the car and cleaned the windscreen and waited, and history went on happening just past the glass, like weather.

It was a comfortable creed. It had kept her safe for four years. She polished it every morning along with the Daimler's lamps.

"Miss Nakamura." Edith Marlowe did not turn from the desk. Fifty-three years old, upright as a mast, grey hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided long ago what her face would be used for. "Are those letters ready?"

"Yes, ma'am." Yara crossed the room and laid them down, squared to the desk's edge, exactly as her employer liked. "Cable Street, Wapping, and Mr. Ainsworth in Westminster."

Something flickered behind Edith's eyes—the briefest recalculation, gone before it could be named. "Mr. Ainsworth," she repeated mildly. "Yes."

Yara felt the flicker land somewhere beneath her ribs. Had she said too much? She knew the addresses because she had read the letters, but a chauffeur ought to know addresses anyway; they were written on her route sheets; there was nothing remarkable—except that she had said *Westminster* unprompted, with the ease of someone who had held the letter against the light and smiled at its contents, and for half a second the two women regarded each other across the desk like duelists who have both noticed the other's pistol is loaded.

Then Edith Marlowe said, "Good," and put on her gloves, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

The Daimler came out of the garage at nine-forty smelling of brass polish and petrol, and Yara drove her employer down into London the way a fish swims downstream, without effort, without comment, part of the current.

October had come in cold and clean, scrubbing the city's air until the towers of Westminster showed their full grey teeth above the Embankment. Chestnut leaves lay in the gutters in drifts the colour of old blood and new copper. Yara took Kensington Road eastward past the gardens, past the Royal Albert Hall with its great dome like an upturned bowl waiting to be filled, and the whole time Edith Marlowe sat beside her in the open tonneau—she preferred the open car even in October, wrapped in a rug, saying it was the only place in England where a woman got honest weather—and read a newspaper whose headlines Yara could feel rather than see.

*ULSTER IN FLAMES*, the newspaper probably said. Or *BALKAN POWDER TRAIN SLOWING*. The papers had been full of elsewhere all year. Elsewhere was very crowded lately.

"You'll take me by the office first," Edith said over the engine's clatter. "Then Clement's Inn. Then home by two. Tomorrow we leave at half-nine for the park, and I want the car spotless, Miss Nakamura, because photographs may be taken, and if I am to be photographed being arrested I intend to arrive looking better than the policemen."

"Yes, ma'am."

"They say there will be forty thousand of us." Edith said it the way other people reported rainfall. "Forty thousand women walking from the Embankment to Hyde Park. And perhaps six thousand police, and perhaps a hundred thousand spectators who have come to watch us be knocked about, because that is the national theatre now, isn't it? We are the play." She lowered the paper. "Do you know what the papers call it? Not a march. A *procession*. As though we were a funeral."

Yara watched the road. A dray horse was baulking outside the Prince of Wales's gates, and she eased the Daimler around it with two movements of her wrists, smooth as pouring. "Will it be a funeral, ma'am?"

Edith laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her. "With any luck, no. With bad luck—" She folded the paper and tapped it against her knee. "With bad luck, several funerals, and the government will express regret and the King will send flowers and nothing else will change, and we shall do it again in the spring, because that is what we do. We are very good at doing it again." She was quiet a moment, watching the shopfronts slide by, the hat shops and the chemists and the bank with its brass doors polished by a hundred years of hands. "You think I don't notice you, Miss Nakamura."

Yara's hands went still on the wheel for precisely the length of one cobblestone. "Ma'am?"

"I said, you think nobody notices you. It's a common affliction among competent people." Edith settled back into the leather. "The incompetent are noticed constantly. It's the chief advantage of incompetence. Whereas you—you drive this car as though the world ends at the windscreen. As though everything beyond the glass were a film at the picture palace. Entertaining. Remote. Someone else's story." She said it without malice, almost fondly, the way one describes a scar to a doctor. "One day something is going to come through that windscreen, my girl, and I hope for your sake you're holding the wheel properly when it does."

"I always hold the wheel properly, ma'am."

"I know," said Edith Marlowe. "That is what worries me."

* * *

They delivered Mr. Ainsworth's letter to his committee rooms in Tothill Street, where a young man with a nervous moustache received it as though it might detonate, and then Clement's Inn, where the building wore its windows in posters—*CONDEMNED TO VOICELESSNESS*, in letters like black flames—and women came out and went in carrying bundles and boxes and the particular set faces of people preparing for impact.

Yara waited with the car in the narrow street, engine idling, and let herself do the thing she always did, the thing that was not spying but was also not not-spying: she listened.

Through the office window, faintly, a typewriter. Through the door propped open for the carriers, voices in fragments—*the banners go to the front, the band forms behind, if the police take the platform we fall back on the second speakers*—and underneath it all the sound of a machine assembling itself out of paper and certainty. Tomorrow forty thousand women would walk into Hyde Park because these women in these rooms had decided it, had typed it, had posted it, and the deciding was happening here, in ink and string and the squeak of boot heels on linoleum.

History, being made, ten feet away. Yara sat with her gloved hands on the wheel and felt the familiar glass come down between herself and it, clear and cool and comforting.

Her father would have said it differently. Her father, three thousand miles away in Yokohama, in the house she had not written to in four years, would have said that a Japanese girl who chauffeured English radicals had made her bed in other people's history and should lie in it without complaint. He had said something of the kind the day he disowned her, standing in the hallway of the house in Kensington where he lodged during his trading years, his voice low so the neighbours wouldn't hear—her father was always careful of neighbours, careful of surfaces, careful of the ledger of appearances—and Yara had walked out of that hallway with one suitcase and thirty-one pounds and a conviction she could not have explained then and could barely explain now: that she would rather be a servant in a house where things *mattered* than a daughter in a house where nothing ever happened except propriety.

Four years. Four years of oiling chains and polishing lamps and driving a woman who was becoming, entry by entry, arrest by arrest, a name in the newspapers. Four years of reading the mail.

Because here was the thing Yara had told no one, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: she loved the letters. Not their secrets—secrets were merely facts wearing coats—but their weight. Each envelope that crossed the Marlowe hall table carried somebody's whole heart compressed into a page: mothers writing to daughters in prison, girls writing about hunger strikes with the breathless pride of the young describing their first dance, frightened husbands, furious sisters, strangers begging Mrs. Marlowe to intercede with judges and Home Secretaries and God. Yara read them and resealed them and carried them, and somewhere in four years of carrying, without ever marching, without ever speaking, without ever once putting her name to anything, she had become a kind of archivist of other people's courage.

It was not the same as having any. She knew that. Knowing it was the loneliest part of her, the part that rode in the car with her every day, quiet as a passenger.

*History happens elsewhere, to other people.* She said it to herself now, watching a newsboy sprint between carts crying something about the Continent, watching two girls in sashes pin a leaflet to the office door. But the creed had developed a hairline crack lately, the way good china develops cracks—from use, from heat, from being gripped too often by hands that were not quite steady. She examined the crack sometimes at night. She did not yet know what it meant. She suspected it meant that the glass was thinner than she'd believed, and that somewhere out there was the stone with her name on it.

* * *

At half-past one, driving home up Park Lane with the park lying green and enormous on their left—tomorrow's battlefield, today just grass and leaves and a man selling roasted chestnuts—Edith spoke again, unexpectedly.

"There's a parcel coming this evening. From a shipping agent. You'll sign for it and put it in my study, unopened." A pause. "It concerns some family business. Nothing that need concern you."

"Very good, ma'am."

"And Yara—" Edith almost never used her Christian name; hearing it now was like a key turning in a door Yara hadn't known was locked. "Tomorrow, whatever happens in the park—if it goes badly, if there are truncheons, if there are arrests—you will stay with the car."

Yara glanced in the mirror. Edith's face was turned toward the park, toward the trees going gold, and her profile was composed and distant and absolutely certain.

"With respect, ma'am," Yara said, "my instructions are to drive you. If you're arrested, I can't very well drive you away from the police station."

"That is precisely what I mean. You will drive yourself home, and you will wait, and you will not make yourself useful, because making yourself useful is how people like you get hurt, and I have buried enough of this movement's dead without adding my excellent chauffeur to the list." Now Edith did turn, and her eyes were grey and level and saw entirely too much. "Do you understand me?"

"Yes, ma'am," said Yara, which was not the same as agreeing, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said anything further, and the Daimler hummed northward through the cold bright afternoon with forty thousand tomorrows folded up in its boot.

* * *

The parcel arrived at six, as promised: brown paper, tied with string, stamped by a shipping agent whose mark Yara didn't recognize. She signed the chitty, carried it to the study, set it on the desk unopened, and then stood in the doorway a moment, looking back at it, feeling the old pull—the pull of sealed things.

She didn't touch it. That night, at least, she didn't touch it. Discipline was also a creed, and she kept hers the way sailors keep knots.

But later, much later—past midnight, when the house had settled into its creaking sleep and the gas was turned low—Yara sat in her narrow room above the garage with her one private possession, a cheap exercise book in which she recorded, in a neat hand learned from a convent school in Yokohama, the small true account of her days. Mileage. Petrol. Repairs. Weather. She had kept it since her first week, this ledger of an invisible life, and tonight she wrote the usual entries and then sat with the pen hovering, and finally added a line she did not fully understand:

*Mrs. M. expecting a parcel from a shipping agent. Family business. She looked frightened when she said it, and she has never once looked frightened at the police.*

She underlined *frightened* twice, then blotted it, then blew out the lamp and lay listening to October rattle the skylight, telling herself—as the glass came down, as the creed resumed—that whatever was coming belonged to the Marlowe family, and to the movement, and to history, and to everyone in the wide loud world except a chauffeur with clean nails and a full petrol tank.

Elsewhere, she reminded herself. Other people.

The garage clock ticked toward morning. Somewhere south of her, beyond the rooftops and the river and the darkening counties, a war was gathering itself out of treaties and timetables; somewhere nearer, a stranger was already walking toward this house through the rain with a bundle of impossible letters under his coat; and tomorrow forty thousand women would step off the Embankment into the swinging arms of the law.

But that was tomorrow, and that was elsewhere, and Yara Nakamura slept the sleep of the competent and the untouched—for one more night.
