## 2. Grease and Ink

The Daimler had a voice, and Yara Nakamura had learned to listen to it the way other people listened to weather.

This morning it spoke through the left front wheel—a thin complaint, metallic and patient, that began at twelve miles an hour and sharpened toward thirty. She had heard it for three days now, first as a whisper beneath Edith Marlowe's conversation about the march route, then as a certainty. Something in the hub. A bearing drying out, or worse, a cotter pin working loose where no cotter pin should work loose. If she let it go, the car would fail on some street corner at the worst possible hour, surrounded by policemen who would enjoy nothing more than an excuse to impound the Marlowe vehicle on grounds of public danger.

So at half past seven, while the house above her still smelled of toast and coal smoke, Yara knelt on the garage's cold flagstones in her oldest overalls and jacked up the nearside front wheel.

The garage was her cathedral. Nobody else wanted it—the space was too narrow for the gardener's cart, too dark for the maids to linger, too greasy for anyone with sense. But Yara loved its particular darkness: the way light came only through the high window over the doors, falling in one dusty column that moved across the floor as the morning aged; the smell of petrol and axle grease and old leather; the orderly chaos of her toolboard, each spanner hanging in its chalked silhouette like a saint in a niche. Here she was not Miss Nakamura, the curiosity, the Japanese girl in chauffeur's livery whose English was so correct people looked faintly disappointed when they met her. Here she was simply the person who knew things. Which bolt wanted oil. Which valve was temperamental. Which of Mr. Pardoe's instructions could be safely ignored because Pardoe had never actually driven the car he professed to maintain.

She spun the wheel free and set it aside, then worked the hub cap off with a soft-faced mallet. The brake drum resisted, as drums did, and she coaxed it with the heel of her hand and a word in Japanese her father would have boxed her ears for—though her father was four thousand miles away in Yokohama and had not written in eleven months, which meant his authority had thinned to something she could spend freely.

Inside, the assembly lay open to her like anatomy. There. The bearing race was scored, a bright spiral gouged into metal that should have been smooth as a spoon's bowl. Grit had gotten in—road dust from the Kent run last week, probably—and once grit entered, everything after was arithmetic. She would need to clean, repack, and if the race was bad beyond dressing, send to the agents for a replacement, which would take a day and cost money Mrs. Fairweather the housekeeper would sigh over.

Yara settled onto the low stool, pulled the lamp close, and began.

Her hands worked by themselves. That was the secret pleasure of mechanical labor, the thing she had never told anyone: that it freed the mind entirely. Hands turned the rag; the mind went where it pleased. And this morning, the mind went straight to the letter.

It had been in yesterday's post, third from the top of the stack Edith handed her each evening for sealing and addressing. Yara had carried them to the hall table, warmed the sealing wax over the spirit lamp, and read every word of every letter before the wax touched paper. She had been doing it for two years.

Not out of malice. She wanted to be clear with herself about that, and she was rigorous about being clear with herself—it was one of the few luxuries she allowed. She read because the letters passed through her hands, and because she had discovered early that knowledge was the only property a person like her could accumulate in this country without anyone noticing she owned it. Money, no. Land, no. A face that belonged, a name that opened doors—no, no, and no. But information. Information sat quietly in the skull and compounded like interest. No one could tax it or confiscate it or look through her at it across a drawing room.

Yesterday's letter had come from Manchester, from a Mrs. Osgood whose handwriting slanted hard to the right as though the words were trying to escape the page. It was, on its surface, an ordinary suffrage letter: reports of branch meetings, complaints about a hall that had double-booked, a request that Mrs. Marlowe speak in Salford if her schedule permitted.

And then, in the fourth paragraph, folded into talk of arrangements the way a blade is folded into a newspaper:

*Regarding the other matter we discussed—you may tell your friends that the house on Bexley Street remains open, though the landlady has grown nervous since the business in Nottingham and asks that arrivals come singly and after dark. The rooms above the bakery are better but cannot hold more than three at once. Do not write these addresses again. Burn this.*

Yara had held the envelope over the lamp flame a moment longer than necessary, watching the wax pool, thinking: *I am holding six addresses in my head now.* Bexley Street. The bakery rooms. Two houses in Nottingham whose names she'd gathered from letters in March. A flat behind a tailor's shop in Camden that Edith had visited twice in July. And a cottage in Hertfordshire mentioned only ever as "the aunt's," which was not an aunt at all, judging by the women who arrived there with bandaged wrists and left without their hatpins.

Safe houses. The movement's circulatory system, hidden inside the body of ordinary correspondence, invisible unless you knew how to read—which, apparently, nobody suspected the chauffeur of doing.

She memorized rather than wrote anything down. Writing was evidence; memory was deniable. Her father, before the estrangement, had kept merchant ledgers, and he had taught her a clerk's trick for committing figures to mind: attach each item to an image, absurd and vivid, so the list became a procession. So now Bexley Street walked through her imagination wearing a bexley of bluebells; the bakery rooms smelled permanently of burnt sugar; the Camden flat hid behind a giant pair of tailor's scissors. Absurd. Unforgettable. Six addresses filed away in a head that officially contained nothing but driving routes and the correct pressure for Daimler tires.

Was it theft? She turned the question over as her fingers cleaned the bearing race with paraffin, examining the metal under the lamp. She decided—not for the first time—that it was closer to inheritance. These women were building something that would matter, and they did not know they were building it; they thought they were writing letters. Someone ought to keep the record properly. Someone who understood that history was a ledger, and that most entries were lost because no one thought to copy them out.

History happened elsewhere, to other people—so Yara had long ago concluded. Wars happened to soldiers. Movements happened to orators. But a chauffeur? A chauffeur stood at the edge of the photograph, half-cropped by the frame, holding the door. All the same, she thought, tightening the last nut with a quarter-turn past firm, someone ought to keep the accounts. Even the ones nobody asked to be kept.

She repacked the bearing with fresh grease—cool, pale, smelling faintly of tallow—reseated the drum, remounted the wheel, and spun it. It turned in silence. The Daimler's voice would be clear tomorrow.

Up above, through the floorboards, she heard the front doorbell ring. Then voices. Then, distinctly, the sound of Edith Marlowe coming down the service stairs into the garage itself, which she almost never did.

Yara rose, wiped her hands on the rag, and pulled off her overalls just as the door opened.

* * *

Edith Marlowe descended into her own garage the way a queen descends into a kitchen: rarely, deliberately, and with the understanding that her presence altered the temperature of the room. She was fifty-one years old and built like the argument she was—square-shouldered, unbending, dressed today in the severe grey wool she reserved for days requiring gravitas. She carried a leather portfolio under one arm and an expression under the other that Yara had learned to parse the way she parsed engine noises: this was not anger. This was calculation wearing anger's coat.

"Miss Nakamura."

"Ma'am." Yara stood by the workbench, grease still shadowing the creases of her knuckles, spine straight, face arranged into its professional calm. "The car will be ready by nine. There was a fault in the nearside bearing—I've attended to it."

"I didn't come down about the car." Edith crossed the floor, her heels ringing on stone, and stopped near the workbench, looking—not at Yara, but at the letter rack mounted beside the bench, where outgoing correspondence waited for the afternoon collection. Three envelopes sat there. One of them was addressed to Manchester.

A silence stretched. Yara let it stretch. In her experience, whoever filled a silence first surrendered something, and she had learned economy early.

"You sealed last night's post," Edith said at last. It was not quite a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you sealed Tuesday's. And every evening's post since you took up the position." Edith turned then and looked at her directly, and Yara felt the full weight of those pale grey eyes, eyes that had stared down magistrates and hecklers and at least one member of the Cabinet. "You are aware, I imagine, what a great many of those letters contain."

Here it was, then. Two years of careful reading balanced on a knife's edge, and the blade was already tipping. Yara considered lying—*I seal them, ma'am, I don't read them*—and rejected it in the same instant. Edith Marlowe had spent twenty years interrogating hostile witnesses. A lie would insult her intelligence and end whatever this was before it began. Besides—and this was the stranger truth, the one Yara examined later, in the dark—she found she did not want to lie to this woman. She wanted, very badly, to be *seen* by her, and had wanted it for two years without admitting it even to herself.

"I'm aware," Yara said.

Something moved at the corner of Edith's mouth. Not a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, that the game had been played honestly on both sides. "How long?"

"Since my first week, ma'am. The letter about the Pankhurst meeting. You'd misaddressed it—written Islington where you meant Kensington—and I corrected the envelope before posting it. To do that, I had to read it." She paused. "After that, it seemed dishonest to pretend I hadn't read all the others."

"And you said nothing. For two years."

"You didn't ask, ma'am."

Edith set the portfolio down on the workbench with a soft thump. She was quiet a moment, studying the toolboard, the chalk silhouettes, the column of dusty light. When she spoke again her voice had shifted register—lower, less the platform orator, more something private. "Do you know what would happen to me if the wrong person learned what passes through this house? Not prison—I've made my peace with prison; Holloway and I are old acquaintances by reputation. I mean what would happen to the *women*. Girls of nineteen whose fathers would put them on a boat to Canada. Teachers who'd lose their posts. Women whose husbands would have them certified." She tapped the portfolio. "Names, addresses, aliases. A whole underground railway made of stationery."

"I know, ma'am."

"You know." Edith's gaze came back to her, sharp. "Because you've read it."

"Because I've read it," Yara agreed, and did not flinch, though flinching was available.

Another silence, but a different species of silence now. Edith picked up one of the outgoing envelopes—the Manchester one—and weighed it in her hand as though testing whether the secrets inside had grown heavier since sealing. "I have known for eighteen months," she said. "Since the misaddressed letter. I checked the postmark against my draft book. You'd changed Kensington back correctly, which meant you'd known which was intended, which meant you'd read enough to know. After that I watched. I left deliberate errors in drafts—wrong initials, a false date. You corrected every one. Quietly. Without ever once letting me see you do it."

Yara blinked. Eighteen months. All that time she had believed herself the only watcher in the house, the sole keeper of the hidden account, and meanwhile the principal herself had been running a parallel audit. She thought of the phrase her father used about merchants who cheated each other politely: *two honest thieves, keeping honest books.*

"Why didn't you dismiss me?" Yara asked. The question escaped before she could weigh it, and she was glad, afterward, that it had.

Edith replaced the envelope in the rack, squaring it with the others so precisely that Yara understood the gesture for what it was: this woman ordered small things because large things refused order. "Because in eighteen months," Edith said, "not one address, not one alias, not one name from those letters has appeared anywhere it shouldn't. Not in gossip, not in the newspapers, not in the mouths of the police—who, I assure you, pay informants handsomely and would find a Japanese chauffeur a very convenient story. Whatever you are doing with what you read, Miss Nakamura, you are not selling it. And in my experience, a person who can keep a secret for two years without being asked to is worth ten who swear oaths on request."

She opened the portfolio then and drew out a single sheet, holding it so Yara could see only the blank reverse. "Saturday's march. The route, the marshals, the contingency addresses if the police kettle us on the Embankment. I am going to hand it to you to type fair copies—three of them, for the marshals. And I am going to assume you will read it, because you read everything. And I am going to sleep soundly tonight anyway. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Yara understood several things, nested like boxes. She understood that she was being trusted. She understood that the trust had been extended eighteen months ago and she had never noticed, which stung her pride in a way she intended to examine privately and at length. And she understood, distantly, that something had just happened to her that she had no ledger entry for: she had been *counted*. Included in the machinery, not merely adjacent to it.

"I understand, ma'am," she said.

"Edith," said Edith Marlowe. "In this house, when we're conspiring, you may call me Edith. Everyone else calls me Mrs. Marlowe, and everyone else means it as armor." She handed over the sheet. "The typing can wait until the car is ready. I'm told the Daimler had a voice this week."

Yara took the paper, and for a moment neither woman moved, standing in the dust-bright column of garage light between the jack stands and the letter rack—two custodians of stolen archives, acknowledging each other across the grease and the ink without another word needing to be spent on it.

"Miss Nakamura," Edith added, turning toward the stairs, "the bearing. Was it bad?"

"Scored, ma'am. Another week and the wheel would have come off on Westminster Bridge."

"Hm." Edith paused on the bottom step, and now she did smile, briefly, crookedly, a expression few newspapers had ever captured. "Then I'm glad one of us listens."

Her heels rang up the stairs. The door closed.

Yara stood alone in the cathedral of grease and ink, the route sheet in her hand, and found that her heart was beating faster than any engine trouble could account for. Carefully, methodically, she committed the contingency addresses to memory—attaching each to an absurd image, a procession, a ledger of the mind—and then she sat down at the bench to make the fair copies, and outside, far off toward the river, a church bell struck nine, and somewhere east of the city, though none of them could know it yet, a man with three dead years in his pockets was stepping off a boat at Tilbury with wet boots and a bundle of letters that should have burned.
