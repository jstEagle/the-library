## 4. The Dead Man's Handwriting

The letters sat on the hall table like a patient no one wished to examine.

Yara had placed them there herself—had dried them first, carefully, with a linen cloth warmed before the kitchen range, because the paper had gone soft as skin from the rain and she feared that a careless hand would tear through three years of the impossible. Tobias had watched her do it from the doorway of the scullery, dripping, saying nothing, his hands opening and closing at his sides as though he could still feel the bundle he had carried against his chest for God knew how many miles. When she finished, he had looked at her with an expression she could not name and had said only, "They're real," in the voice of a man defending himself before a court that had not yet convened.

Now it was past eleven, and the house was quiet in the particular way of a house waiting.

Edith Marlowe was still upstairs. She had been dressing when Tobias arrived—or so her maid claimed—and the interval since had stretched long enough that Yara had begun to count seconds without meaning to, the way one counts in a dentist's anteroom or on a platform before a train that is late. She stood in the hall with her cap freshly straightened and her gloves back on, because uniforms were armour, and armour was what she had. The Daimler waited outside under the wet plane trees, ticking as it cooled. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked twice and went silent.

*History happens elsewhere,* she told herself, and the creed rang hollow as a cracked bell. It had happened here, tonight, on the service steps, in the handwriting of a dead man.

She heard Edith before she saw her—the quick, unhesitating tread on the stairs, the rustle of wool, the small metallic sound of a watch chain settling into a waistcoat pocket. Edith Marlowe dressed for the evening even when the evening held nothing but correspondence; she held that a woman who asked to be taken seriously by the Empire ought not to give the Empire the courtesy of looking apologetic. Tonight she wore dark green, high-collared, her grey hair pinned with mathematical precision. She came down the stairs like weather.

She stopped at the bottom step.

For a moment she did not move at all. Her eyes went to the table—to the bundle, tied now with the same string it had arrived with, string Yara had not dared replace because replacing it felt like tampering with evidence, with scripture—and Yara, watching from the shadows beside the coat stand, saw something pass over her employer's face that frightened her more than any of the night's strangeness. It was recognition. Not of the letters themselves. Of their shape. Of the way they were bundled, corner to corner, the way soldiers were taught to tie things so they could be stuffed into a tunic and run with.

Edith had grown up with a brother in uniform. Some knowledge lives in the hands longer than the head permits it.

"Miss Nakamura." Her voice was level. Too level. "You admitted him yourself?"

"Yes, madam."

"Through the service door."

"It was raining, madam. And he was—" Yara paused, selecting. "Unwell."

"Unwell." Edith crossed the hall. Up close there was a fine tremor in her jaw, the kind that comes from clenching. "And you did not send for me at once."

"He asked how long you'd been awake. I said you kept late hours. He said—" Yara stopped. She had sworn she would deliver this exactly, word for word, the way she delivered everything: accurately, without addition. "He said he would wait until morning if he must, but that the rain was doing his thinking no good."

Something moved behind Edith's eyes. "His thinking," she repeated softly. "Yes. He always said that. Rain made the thoughts pool."

She reached the table. She stood over the letters without touching them, the way one stands over a grave one has not visited in years, and Yara understood suddenly that Edith Marlowe—who had faced mounted police at Westminster, who had been spat upon in Guildford and had answered with a speech, who treated His Majesty's Government as a moderately stubborn pupil—was afraid.

It was the most human thing Yara had ever seen her do.

"Light the lamp properly," Edith said. "And then you may go."

"I'd rather stay, madam."

Edith turned. In the lamplight her face was all planes and decision, and for an instant Yara expected the dismissal, crisp as a slammed ledger. Instead her employer studied her—a long, appraising look that began at her cap and ended somewhere deeper than her face.

"You read my letters," Edith said.

It was not a question. It was not even an accusation. It landed in the hall like a coin spun on marble, turning and turning.

"Some of them, madam. Before sealing."

"And why?"

"Because someone should know what passes through this house besides you. Because you are one woman, and the world you've chosen is full of men paid to be careful." Yara heard her own voice and marvelled at it—steady, unhurried, as though she had rehearsed this confession for years instead of never. "I have never taken one out of the house. I have never told anyone. But I know what's in your post, Mrs. Marlowe, and tonight I think that may matter."

The silence held for four heartbeats. Yara counted them; counting was what she did instead of praying.

"You are either the most loyal servant I possess," Edith said at last, "or the most dangerous." She untied the string. "We shall find out which presently."

* * *

She opened the top letter with her thumb alone, sliding it free the way a surgeon draws a blade, and Yara—standing two paces behind and slightly to the left, where servants stand—saw the handwriting upside down and knew it instantly, because she had known it for years.

Alistair Marlowe had written to his sister perhaps six times in the three years Yara had been in the household, before the war took him, and his hand was unmistakable: a slanted, impatient script that leaned forward as though the words themselves were in a hurry to arrive, the crossbars of the *t*s struck hard enough to tear thin paper, the descenders of the *g*s looping extravagantly, a schoolboy's flourish surviving inside an officer's discipline. Yara had sorted those letters, sealed those letters, once carried one down to the Daimler through sleet because Edith wanted it posted within the hour. A person does not forget a hand like that. A person might spend a lifetime trying.

The letter Edith held was dated the fourteenth of March.

Nineteen thirteen.

Yara watched the date land. It went into Edith Marlowe like a bullet goes into water—no sound, but everything after it displaced. Her shoulders did not change. Her chin did not drop. But her free hand found the edge of the table and gripped it, and the knuckles went white in a slow wave, finger by finger, like frost crossing a windowpane.

"Madam," Yara said, and had nothing to follow it with.

Edith read. She read the way she read everything, fast and complete, lips barely moving, and Yara watched her face do something she had never seen a face do: grieve and hope at once, openly, neither winning. It was terrible to witness. It was like watching a fire try to burn underwater. Grief said: *he is dead, we buried the news in April of '11, we wore black, you gave the eulogy at the memorial meeting and your voice never shook.* Hope said: *the ink is not faded, the paper smells of carbolic, the date is March.* Between the two, Edith Marlowe's composure tore along its seams, quietly, stitch by stitch, while her spine remained perfectly straight.

"The hospital," she said. Her voice came out sanded down to bare wood. "Imtarfa. That's—Malta. The military hospital above Rabat." She knew it. Of course she knew it; the Marlowes had cousins in the Mediterranean fleet, dinner-table geography absorbed over decades. "He wrote to me from Malta once. Nineteen-oh-nine. He said the light there made even the fortifications look forgiving."

She drew the second letter from beneath the first. Also dated March. Then a third, April. A fourth, undated but bearing a censor's stamp—a triangular mark, passed and initialled—that Yara memorised on sight, because memorising was the one muscle she trusted completely.

"There are eleven," Edith said. She counted them without touching, her forefinger circling above the table like a dowser's rod. "Eleven letters. Written over—" she checked, swiftly, "—over five months. To me. None delivered." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and blazing and wet all at once, which should not have been possible and was. "Not lost, Miss Nakamura. *Withheld.* Someone read these. Someone stamped them. Someone put them in a file and closed the drawer."

"The young man," Yara said carefully. "Tobias. He said he was ordered to burn them."

"Ordered to burn them." Edith set the first letter down with exaggerated gentleness, the gentleness of fury. "And kept them instead. For three years' worth of courage, or however long he's carried them." She pressed both palms flat against the table. "Do you understand what this means? Do you begin to understand?"

"No, madam."

"Neither do I. That is what frightens me." She laughed—one short, cracked syllable, entirely unlike her—and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. When she lowered it, the mask had been hastily reassembled, but Yara had seen behind it now, and seeing is a thing that cannot be undone. "My brother died at Gallipoli. That is what the telegram said, that is what the regimental record says, that is what I told four hundred women from a platform in Manchester with his photograph propped against the water jug. Dead men do not write from Malta in March. Dead men do not complain about the nursing, and ask whether I am still starving myself for the cause, and sign off *your affectionate brother, who owes you better than this*—"

Her voice went. Not broke—went, simply, like a fuse.

Yara stepped forward without deciding to. She did not touch her employer; one did not. She pulled the chair from the writing desk and set it behind Edith, and after a moment Edith sat, abruptly, like a puppet whose strings had been cut one at a time, and put her face in her hands, and the great suffragist Edith Marlowe wept in her own hallway with her chauffeur standing guard at two paces, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

Later—much later, when the hall clock had given up its second midnight chime and the rain had returned to worry the windows—Edith sat at the desk with every letter arranged before her in chronological order, and the lamplight lay across the pages like a held breath.

Yara had brought tea neither of them touched. She stood by the window, ostensibly watching the street, actually reading Edith's reflection in the glass, which was easier to look at than the woman herself. In the window, London went about its business below: a cab horse clopping home, a lamplighter working his way down Cadogan Gardens with his pole rising and falling like a man planting flags in a country he owned nothing of.

"He hid the censorship," Edith murmured. She had been silent for twenty minutes, moving between letters. "Look. The early ones carry the stamp openly—here, and here. Passed. Initialled—some captain's initials, R-something. But the last three—" she lifted a page to the lamp, angling it, and Yara crossed the room despite herself and saw it: fainter marks, half-struck, as though someone had begun to stamp and thought better. "These were meant to pass without record. Or someone was growing careless. Or someone wanted them found eventually." She set it down. "Bureaucracies never destroy cleanly, whatever novels say. They misfile. They defer. They wait for a clerk with a conscience."

"Tobias," Yara said.

"Tobias," Edith agreed, tasting the name. "Who is sitting in my kitchen drinking tea my cook made him, and who has not yet told us the tenth part of what he knows. Men like that don't survive field hospitals and desert crossings by accident. He chose what to keep." She looked up sharply. "You searched him, I hope."

"He had nothing else on him, madam. I checked his coat while he dried off. Letters, a ration tin, a photograph of a group of orderlies outside a marquee, and a railway ticket to Paddington, third class, bought yesterday at—" she closed her eyes, retrieving, "—Avonmouth."

"Avonmouth. He came in by ship." Edith nodded slowly. "From where, I wonder. And carrying whose permission?" She gathered the letters together—not retying them, but squaring the edges, an unconscious gesture, a woman restoring order wherever order could be had. "Tomorrow we will question him properly. Tonight—" her voice caught again, and she mastered it with visible effort, "tonight I intend to read every word my brother wrote me, including the words someone decided I should not receive, and I will do it alone."

"Of course, madam."

"Miss Nakamura." Yara paused at the door. Edith had not turned around; she spoke to the letters, to the lamp, perhaps to the brother folded inside them. "When you sealed my correspondence all these years—you were right. I shan't pretend I didn't suspect. A woman in my position cannot afford innocence, and apparently I acquired a driver instead." A pause. "But understand what you witnessed tonight. If Alistair lives—if some part of this is true—then someone with rank and reach went to considerable trouble to make a living man into a dead one. That is not grief I'm holding. That is a weapon someone built, and I mean to take it apart screw by screw."

"Yes, madam," Yara said, and meant: *so do I.*

She let herself out into the back passage, and the cold of the flagstones came up through her boots, and she realised her hands were shaking for the first time since the soldier had collapsed against the service door.

In the garage the Daimler waited, smelling of oil and rain. Yara did not get into it. She sat on the mechanic's stool beneath the hanging lamp, took the stub of pencil she kept for mileage figures and the back of an old service schedule, and wrote, because writing was the only prayer she had:

*14 March 1913 — Imtarfa Hospital, Malta — his hand, no doubt possible.*
*Eleven letters. Five months. None delivered.*
*A censor's initials: begins with R.*

She stared at the last line. *R.* It was nothing. An initial was less than nothing; half the Empire answered to an R. And yet it lodged in her mind like a splinter, because the world she had believed in—one in which history happened elsewhere, to other people, in languages she read about rather than lived—had died on the service steps tonight, and something new was forming in its place, itemised and numbered and awake.

Above her, through the floorboards, she could hear the faint creak of Edith pacing her study, and the scratch of a pen answering pen across the years.

Then, from the direction of the kitchens, a floorboard shifted—the deliberate weight of someone who had been waiting for the household to sleep.

Tobias was not in the spare room.

Yara put down the pencil and reached for the lamp.
