## 6. The Ledger

The kitchen at Belgrave Row had never been meant for three people's grief and one person's thinking, but by half past ten that night it held all four anyway. The range had been banked for hours; the coals still threw a dull orange light across the scrubbed table where Mrs. Petch, the cook, had left her bread cloth folded into a shape like a sleeping animal. Yara had sent her to bed with the lie that all was well, which was becoming a professional skill.

Tobias Finch sat where she had put him, in the chair nearest the stove, wrapped in the spare driver's coat because his own had not yet stopped being wet through. He was twenty-four or twenty-five, she judged, though the tremor in his hands made him seem older and younger at once, the way illness does. His bundle of letters lay on the table between them, squared now, no longer the sodden wad he had carried against his chest through the rain. She had laid them out on the drying rack earlier, weighted with the flat iron, and they had come back to something like paper—warped, faintly mildewed at the edges, but legible. Legible was everything. Yara had built a life on legible things.

Edith Marlowe had gone up an hour ago, after standing over the letters for the length of a cigarette she did not smoke and then lighting one anyway. *Dated March,* she had said, in the voice she used for committee minutes, which was the voice she used when feeling would otherwise get loose. *March of this year.* Then she had gone upstairs, and Yara had heard the floorboards above her study creak in the rhythm that meant pacing, and had heard them stop, which meant Edith had sat down at her desk, which meant she was writing to someone, and Yara had found herself aching to know whom without any right at all.

So: the kitchen. The kettle. Three cups, one of them hers, though she had poured tea for employers and their guests for six years without ever quite believing the cup was hers too. Tonight she drank from it as if it were.

"You should sleep," she said to Tobias, in the tone she used for chauffeurs' small talk, neutral, practical, a voice with no hooks in it.

"I sleep fine," he said, which was a lie so worn it had gone smooth. "It's the waking that's the trouble."

She knew the shape of that sentence. She had thought versions of it herself in the years after Yokohama, after the ship, after the boarding house in Limehouse where she had learned that sleep is a country you can be exiled from. But sympathy was not what the moment wanted. What the moment wanted—the thing she felt pressing at her like weather—was method.

"Mr. Finch," she said. "Tobias." She set down her cup. "Tell me exactly what you were ordered to do. Not what you did. What you were ordered."

He looked at his hands. "Burn them. All correspondence from the Malta station relating to Captain A. Marlowe, deceased. That was the instruction. Verbal, nothing written—there's never anything written. I was to take the file from the outgoing tray, burn it in the incinerator behind the records hut, initial the destruction log, and enter the date. Standard procedure for closed files." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "There's a log for burning things. That's the army for you. Even erasure gets bookkeeping."

Yara felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like the Daimler's engine catching on a cold morning.

*Even erasure gets bookkeeping.*

She had spent six years reading other people's mail in this house—not all of it, not pruriently, but enough; enough to know which creditors frightened Edith, which nieces lied about where they'd been, which causes were losing money—and she had told herself it was a private vice, a small theft, the one dishonesty permitted to a woman whose whole employment was honesty. She had told herself history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals she would never see, and that her work was to keep the motorcar running and the letters sealed and her own name out of every column of every account.

But here was a man who had been ordered to make a dead man deader, and who had instead put the evidence in an oilskin packet and walked it across London through the rain to the one house where it would detonate. And here was she, sitting in a warm kitchen with the facts of an impossibility spread on a scrubbed table, and finding that her mind—her orderly, itemizing, account-book mind—wanted, more than it had ever wanted anything, to *enter them.*

Dates. Names. Sums. That was how her father had taught her to read a shipping manifest before she could read much else: find the dates, find the names, find the sums, and the truth of the voyage will show itself, because lies are expensive to maintain and arithmetic is cheap. He had said it about cargo. He had not meant it as a philosophy. But philosophies arrive where they're needed, uninvited, like soldiers at a service entrance.

"Tobias," she said again, and pulled the bread cloth aside, and cleared a space on the table as deliberately as a surgeon draping a field. "Do you have a pencil?"

He patted the coat, found one behind his ear where clerks keep pencils the way priests keep crosses, and handed it over without asking why. That, too, was a kind of recognition. Clerks and chauffeurs both lived by the implements of other people's business.

From the dresser drawer—the third one, behind the string and the brown paper, where she kept the household's spare keys and her own few secrets—she took a notebook. Not just any notebook. It was a ledger, a real one, quarter-bound in green cloth, eight columns ruled in pale blue, the kind used for petty cash. She had bought it eighteen months ago from a stationer on Chancery Lane with money from her own wages, and she had never written a word in it, and she had never been able to say why she'd bought it, only that standing in the shop she had picked it up and her hands had refused to put it down. Some purchases are prophecies you make about yourself.

Now she opened it flat. The pages smelled of nothing at all, which was the smell of a thing waiting.

"We are going to do accounts," she said.

Tobias blinked at her. "Accounts."

"On Alistair Marlowe." She wrote the name at the head of the first page, in her best hand, the copperplate her father had drilled into her with a bamboo ruler and no mercy: *MARLOWE, Capt. Alistair J.* "Every fact we have. Every date. Every place. Every name that touches him. We enter it the way a clerk enters a transaction—cold, exact, dated. No theories. Theories go in the margin, in pencil, and get erased when they fail." She tapped the ruled columns. "Date received. Date of document. Origin. Parties. Sum of knowledge gained. You were a records clerk; you know this grammar better than I do."

"I know the grammar," Tobias said quietly. "I watched men die of it." He didn't explain that, and she didn't ask, not yet. There would be time; the night was long and neither of them was going anywhere.

"Then you know," she said, "that a file can't argue. It can only sit there and be true. People argue. Files don't." She turned the pencil over. "Someone in the War Office believes a file can be made untrue by fire. I want to test that belief."

For a moment he simply looked at her, and Yara understood that she was being seen—really seen—for perhaps the fourth time since she had come to England, and that the seeing was mutual. Two people whose entire trades consisted of handling other people's truths without leaving fingerprints, recognizing the trade in each other across a kitchen table.

"All right," he said. "Where do we start?"

"Where all audits start," she said. "With the discrepancy."

* * *

The discrepancy was this: Alistair James Marlowe, captain, 1/4th Battalion, died at Gallipoli—or was reported to have—in August of 1910. Waited upon by the War Office telegram, published in *The Times* casualty list, mourned at a memorial service at St. George's, Hanover Square, at which Yara had stood at the back beside the hired cars in her chauffeur's uniform while three hundred people who had never liked him said generous things about him. Dead three years. Stone dead. The kind of dead that gets a line in the family Bible.

And yet.

She laid the letters out in order of their postmarks, and Tobias, drying his hands on the borrowed coat, leaned over them with the particular hunger of a man reading his own sentence. Nine letters. No—eight letters and one official-looking card. All in the same hand, a hand Yara knew intimately from six years of sealing Edith's correspondence, because handwriting runs in families like a crooked nose: the Marlowe hand, upright, slightly over-ornamented on the capital letters, the *g* descending in a straight spineless tail. Alistair's hand. Dated November last. January. February. March. April. May. The most recent less than four months old.

"Postmarks," Yara said, and entered them: *Valletta, Malta. Valletta. Valletta. Sliema.* One, oddly, bore a mark she couldn't parse—a double ring with something struck through the centre—and Tobias bent close and went grey around the mouth.

"That's a censor's stamp," he said. "Field service censorship. Everything going out of a garrison passes through it. They read it, they stamp it, it goes on." He touched the double ring with one finger, not quite touching the ink. "Which means these weren't smuggled out. He posted them *through channels.* Through the army's own post. Which means the army knew he was alive. Which means—" He stopped.

"Which means someone decided the family would go on believing he was dead," Yara finished, and wrote it, and hated the neatness of her own writing while doing it. *Army aware of survival. Censorship applied. Suppression at source or destination?* She underlined *destination* twice and then, remembering her own rule about theories, erased one underline.

They worked until two in the morning, and the work had a rhythm to it that Yara recognized from the garage: the pleasure of a mechanism coming apart cleanly, each part lifting free, each part having a reason. Names surfaced and were logged. *Sister V.C., Station Hospital, Imtarfa*—the initials alone, on the second letter, in a line thanking someone for "the kindness beyond what your position permits." *Lt.-Col. R., commanding*—mentioned once, obliquely, in a sentence that managed to be grateful and afraid in the same breath: *"I am more beholden to certain parties than my conscience entirely approves."* Dates cross-referenced against the memorial service, against the telegram, against the anniversary Edith observed every August by wearing black gloves to committee meetings and telling no one why.

And sums. Because there were sums. On the fourth letter, half a line, almost thrown away: *"the fifty pounds reached me and I have used it as instructed."* Fifty pounds. In 1910, fifty pounds was half a year of a chauffeur's wages. Someone had sent a dead man fifty pounds, and a dead man had instructions for its use, and none of it appeared in any account Yara had ever secretly read, and Edith Marlowe kept her private accounts locked in the green tin box in her study, and Yara had picked that lock exactly once, two years ago, out of boredom, and found nothing but insurance papers and a receipt for a hat.

Had she missed a page? Had there been a payment? Her memory—good, trained, a chauffeur's memory for routes and reg numbers—went back over the tin box and found nothing. So either the sum came from outside the household, or—

Or Edith had known.

The thought arrived and sat down at the table like a fourth guest, and Yara refused to look at it directly. She entered instead, in the ledger, in pencil, in the margin where theories belonged: *E.M.'s knowledge — unknown. Do not assume. Do not assume anything.*

"Do you think she knew?" Tobias asked, as if the thought had been audible. Perhaps grief makes people clairvoyant; perhaps it was simply written on her face the way everything was always written on her face, which was why she had learned to stand slightly behind people.

"I think," Yara said carefully, "that this house runs on things nobody says, and I have been its quietest employee for six years, and I have learned not to guess ahead of the evidence." She turned the pencil in her fingers. "But I think that tomorrow morning, when she comes down, we are going to find out."

* * *

She did not sleep either. She tried; she lay in her narrow room above the garage with the window cracked to the smell of wet London and petrol, and her mind ran the entries over and over like a motor idling. At some point she gave up, lit the little lamp, and brought the ledger onto the bed with her, and read her own entries as if they belonged to a stranger—which, in a way, they did. Six years of *history happens elsewhere* and here was a page of history in her own copperplate, itemized, ruled, balanced like a column of figures. *Capt. A.J. Marlowe: reported dead, Aug 1910. Correspondence extant, Nov 1912–May 1913. Discrepancy: 33 months.*

Thirty-three months. She did the division the way her father would have, automatically, horribly: thirty-three months of a sister setting a place in her heart for a corpse. Thirty-three months of mourning that had been, what—stolen? Administered? Prescribed? Somewhere in Malta a censor had read a brother's love letter to a sister and stamped it into silence, and somewhere in London a clerk had been ordered to burn the file, and the machinery had run smoothly, politely, on rails, right up until one small clerk's hands had shaken too badly to obey.

History, she wrote in the margin, and then crossed it out, because the margin was for theories and this was not a theory, it was a vertigo. History was not elsewhere. History was a filing system, and filing systems could be opened.

She got up and dressed and went down to the kitchen at five, because the household rose early and because her body had never learned any other hour, and she stood at the table looking at the nine documents lying in their careful row, and she understood that she had crossed some line from which there was no driving back. She had sealed Edith's letters for six years and called it loyalty. She had memorized safe-house addresses and called it caution. But entering facts into a ledger of her own—that was different. That was not witnessing history. That was *keeping* it. Custody. The word came to her with its full weight, legal and sacred at once: custody of the record.

Mrs. Petch would come down at six and want the range and the table, so Yara did what custodians do: she took the letters upstairs, past the landing where the house still smelled of Edith's cigarette, and stood a moment outside the study door. Light showed under it. Of course it did. Edith Marlowe slept the way her brother apparently had not slept at all—badly, briefly, and at her desk.

Yara knocked.

"Enter," said the voice, roughened, and she entered, and found Edith exactly as predicted: jacket off, hair half-down, the green tin box open before her, and—Yara's heart performed a small cold somersault—a bank passbook in her hand, open to a page dated November 1910.

Edith looked up. For a long moment neither woman spoke, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere below a cab horse shifted its weight on the stones. Then Edith turned the passbook around and slid it across the desk, and her face was the face of a woman presenting evidence against herself.

"Fifty pounds," she said. "Withdrawn the ninth of November, 1910. I have wondered for three years whether I dreamed it." Her voice did not break, but it came within a measurable distance. "You've been making a list, haven't you, Yara. I saw the light under the kitchen door. I saw you carrying them up just now. Give me the list."

It was not a request. It was also, somehow, not an order. It was the voice of a woman who had spent her life among petitions and deputations and had learned that the strongest thing you can say to another woman is *I need your competence*, and Yara took the green ledger from under her arm and laid it on the desk beside the passbook, and the two records lay together like two halves of a sentence finally joined.

"Page one," Yara said. "We start with the discrepancy."

Edith put on her spectacles, and began to read, and outside the window the sky over Belgrave Row went the colour of unprinted paper, waiting for whatever would be written on it next.
