## 7. A Brother, Properly Buried

The winter of 1910 came down on Belgrave Row like wet wool, and Edith Marlowe remembered it afterward as the season of two fires: the coal fire in the dining room, banked high for Alistair's farewell dinner, and the smaller, colder one that had been burning in her chest since October and would not be put out.

She had dressed for the evening in the grey silk with the jet buttons—the good dress, the one that made her look, her maid once said, like a cathedral—and then she had stood before the glass and taken it off again, because cathedrals were for funerals, and this was not to be a funeral. It was only her brother leaving. It was only her brother going eleven thousand miles away to an island whose name she had to look up twice in the atlas before she could find it, and it was not a funeral, and she would not dress it as one.

In the end she wore the blue wool, serviceable, unremarkable, and told herself the choice meant nothing.

* * *

Alistair arrived at seven o'clock precisely, which was his way; he had been precise since he was a boy folding his shirts into squares small enough to fit in a drawer's corner, precise as a man who believed the universe could be kept tidy if only one were sufficiently attentive. He brought flowers—white chrysanthemums, out of season and therefore ruinously expensive—and he brought a bottle of the claret their father had laid down the year Edith was born, and he brought, folded inside his breast pocket where she could not see it but somehow knew it was there, the thing they had not yet said to each other.

He had grown a moustache for Malta. It sat on his face like a piece of furniture belonging to another house.

"You look well, Edie," he said, and kissed her cheek, and she smelled bay rum and train smoke and something underneath both—nervousness, she thought. Her brother, thirty-one years old, decorated in nothing yet but ambition, nervous as a curate before his first sermon.

"I look tired," she corrected him. "I've been up since five writing to the Home Secretary about force-feeding."

"Then don't tell me about it at dinner," he said lightly, too lightly, "or I shall lose my appetite, and your cook will be wounded."

And there it was, the first small stone laid across the road between them, so smoothly done that a stranger would never have noticed it. But Edith had spent six years learning to notice things—learning to read a chairman's pause, a policeman's shift of weight, the exact temperature of a silence—and she noticed this, and let it pass, because it was his farewell dinner and she loved him, and love, she had learned, consisted largely of the things one declined to say on schedule.

* * *

Dinner was oxtail soup, turbot, a saddle of mutton, and the claret, which was very good and which they drank too fast. Mrs. Petch had made the treacle tart Alistair had asked for every birthday since he was nine, and when it came to the table he laughed—a real laugh, sudden and boyish—and for a moment Edith saw him at nine years old again, elbows on the schoolroom table, asking for treacle tart as though the empire might fall without it.

"Do you remember," he said, "when we buried the cat?"

"Under the yew tree, in the biscuit tin."

"With full honours. I read the burial service over it. I'd stolen it from Father's prayer book and crossed out 'our sister' and written 'Percival.'"

"You were very solemn about it."

"I was eight years old and taking my first funeral." He turned his glass by its stem. "I have always thought I did it rather well. Better than the vicar does it, anyway. He rushes the committal."

Edith smiled. The fire ticked. Outside, a cab went past on wet stones, its lamp swinging, and somewhere further off a barrel-organ played three bars of something cheerful and gave up.

"Alistair." She said his name the way one sets a cup down carefully, knowing it is already cracked. "We should talk about it. About what I'm doing. Before you go."

His hand stopped turning the glass.

"I know what you're doing, Edie."

"You know what the newspapers say I'm doing. You know what Aunt Constance says I'm doing. I'm not sure anyone in this family knows what I am actually doing, and I should like—you, particularly—to know. Before you're eleven thousand miles away and it can't be said face to face."

"Must we?" He said it quietly. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it; he was never cruel. "It's your last night at home for—I don't know how long. Three years, perhaps four. Must we spend it on the vote?"

"Yes," Edith said. "Because if we don't, we'll spend the next three years not saying it, and I have watched this family do that before. With Mother. With Uncle Henry. We are champions of the thing unsaid, Alistair. We could medal in it."

Her brother looked into the fire for a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had changed—it had gone careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he has already tested from the bank.

"I signed the counter-petition," he said.

"I know."

"You know?"

"Of course I know. I read everything that comes through this house, you know that. Your signature is the fourteenth on the list. Your handwriting is still the same as it was at Eton—your capital A looks like a ladder falling over."

He almost smiled at that, and then didn't. He took out his cigarette case, considered it, and put it away again without opening it, which was what he always did instead of losing his temper.

"The counter-petition says that votes for women would be, quote, injurious to the order and wellbeing of the nation, end quote. I signed it, Edith. I want you to hear it from me and not from someone at a garden party. I believe it. Or—" he pressed his knuckles briefly against his mouth— "I believe some of it, and the rest of it I believe because it's easier than believing you."

"There it is," Edith said softly.

"Don't."

"No, say it. Say the whole of it. You've started; you may as well finish. Injurious to the order and wellbeing of the nation. And what do you imagine happens to the order and wellbeing of women while the nation is being so carefully ordered? Do you know what they do in Holloway now? Do you know what the feeding is? Because I have seen the medical testimony, Alistair. I have held the hands of women who came out of there looking like survivors of a shipwreck, and they went in for asking to be counted."

"They went in for breaking windows."

"Some of them broke windows. Some of them stood on a pavement. Some of them were my friends and weighed eight stone before they began and you would not recognise them now." She heard her own voice rise and made herself lower it. This was the discipline she had learned: not calm, exactly—calm was a fiction—but control, the holding of the flame at the height you chose. "I'm not asking you to agree with me. I am asking you to stop pretending this is a matter of etiquette."

"I'm not pretending anything."

"You are. You have all evening. You've been pretending since you walked in—the flowers, the claret, the treacle tart, the story about the cat. You've arranged the whole evening so that we need never arrive anywhere. And tomorrow you sail for Malta, and I shall stand on the dock and wave my handkerchief like a woman in a music-hall song, and neither of us will ever have said the true thing, and it will calcify. That's what unsaid things do, Alistair. They set, like plaster. And then you are carrying them about inside you for years, and they ache in cold weather."

The clock on the mantel did its patient work. Alistair got up at last and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back in the stance he used for reviewing things—troops, accounts, arguments, his own conscience.

"You want to know the truth?" he said to the glass. "Here is the truth. When I was at Sandhurst, Father told me the family name was a kind of trust. That I was to be a steward of it. And I have tried to be. And now my only sister stands on platforms in Manchester with her name in every paper in England next to the word *unwomanly*, and I cannot make those two facts sit together in the same ledger, and I am ashamed—do you understand? Ashamed. Not of you. Of myself. Because half of me thinks you are the bravest person I have ever known, and the other half flinches every time a fellow officer makes a joke about harem-suffragettes and broken crockery, and I laugh, Edith. I laugh at the jokes. Every time. And then I hate myself for a week."

Edith sat very still. The fire had burned down to a red lacework, and the light moved on her brother's back like water.

"Why do you laugh?" she asked.

"Because I am a coward in company," he said simply. "I am brave in letters. I wrote you a letter last March saying half of this. You never answered it."

"I never received it."

"It was sent care of the office. I have the copy in my files; I keep copies of everything."

"I never received it," she said again, and felt, absurdly, the sting of tears, and blinked them down with fury at herself. "Someone in that office reads the post, or loses the post, or—never mind. Write to me from Malta, Alistair. Directly. To the house, marked private. Promise me."

"If I write to you directly," he said, turning round at last, and his face in the firelight was older than thirty-one, much older, "it will be because I have stopped laughing at the jokes. Are you prepared for that letter?"

"I have been preparing for it my whole life."

He came back to the table. He poured the last of their father's claret into both glasses, though hers was not empty, and raised his own.

"To Percival," he said. "Who was buried properly, with full honours, by an eight-year-old who understood the requirements better than the Church of England."

"To Percival," said Edith.

They drank. And it was almost enough—it was nearly, nearly enough—and then he ruined it, or saved it, or both, by saying, as he set down the glass:

"When I come home, we'll settle it. Whatever this is between us, we'll settle it properly, over dinner, with hours in front of us. Three years, perhaps four, and then I'll be back, and we'll have it out like grown people."

"Yes," said Edith Marlowe, who believed in schedules and ledgers and the keeping of promises. "When you come home."

* * *

She drove with him to the dock at Southampton the next morning, in the car—her first motorcar, purchased that spring, scandalous, useful—and the roads were rimed white and the engine complained, and neither of them talked about anything at all, which after the previous night was its own kind of eloquence.

On the quay the gulls screamed and the gangway smoked with steamer trunks and farewell. Alistair in his uniform looked suddenly very young and very far away already, as though the sea had begun removing him from the world in advance.

"Well," he said. "Goodbye, Edie."

"Write to me."

"Every month. Marked private, to the house." He hesitated, then gripped her shoulders and kissed her forehead, hard, the way he had when they were children and one of them had fallen off something. "Mind yourself. Don't get arrested."

"Don't get drowned."

He laughed, boarded, and was absorbed into the crowd at the rail, and when the ship moved she waved her handkerchief like a woman in a music-hall song after all, and hated herself for it, and kept waving until the funnel smoke swallowed the rail entirely and there was nothing on the grey water but weather.

* * *

Three years later—less, in fact, two years and eight months—she had stood in the drawing room while a young officer from the War Office, hat in his hands, informed her that Captain Alistair Marlowe had been killed at Gallipoli, and had watched her own hands lie perfectly still in her lap and thought, with a clarity that felt like insanity: *he promised me a letter.*

They gave her a flag and a form and a date, and the family plot at the church in Surrey took him under a stone that said what stones say, and she stood at the graveside in the grey silk with the jet buttons—the good dress, the funeral dress after all—and heard the vicar rush the committal, just as she had known he would, just as Alistair had predicted at a dinner table three winters dead.

*Properly buried.* That was the phrase that lived in her afterward, that woke her at four in the morning with its little cold hand around her heart. Buried properly, with full honours, by people who understood the requirements. A stone, a date, a folded flag. Everything in order. Everything itemized.

Except that her brother had promised her a letter, and Alistair Marlowe had never in his life broken a promise made across a dinner table—he had broken her heart a dozen times and never once broken his word—and so some stubborn clerk in the back office of her grief refused, year after year, to close the account.

She had told no one this. Not her solicitor, not her colleagues, not the girl who drove her car and sealed her letters with such quiet, infuriating competence. It was the one entry she kept off every ledger: *the dead man owes me a letter, and I do not believe he is dead.*

Then, on a wet Thursday in 1913, a stranger had come to the service entrance carrying a bundle tied in oilcloth, and Edith had unwrapped it, and there in her own drawing room, dated months ago, in handwriting she would have known blindfolded—the capital A like a ladder falling over—were eleven letters from her brother.

Alive. Alive, and writing, and silenced by someone, and she stood in her drawing room with the papers trembling in her hands and thought of a winter dinner long ago, and a glass of their father's claret, and a promise: *when I come home, we'll settle it properly.*

The account, she understood now, had never been closed. It had merely been hidden—by censors, by clerks, by men in good coats—and somewhere in London there were people who knew why, and Edith Marlowe had spent her whole life learning exactly how to find them.

She rang the bell for Yara.
