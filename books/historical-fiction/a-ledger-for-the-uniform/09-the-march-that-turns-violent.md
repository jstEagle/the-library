## 9. The March That Turns Violent

The morning of the march arrived wearing grey, like a uniform. Rain had come in the night and gone again, leaving the streets of Kensington shining as though the city had been polished for an occasion it did not yet understand. Yara stood at the garage door with a chamois cloth and watched the light climb the wet brick opposite, and she thought: today something will happen, but it will happen to other people.

She had held that belief the way some women held prayer beads—turning it over and over until it was smooth, until it meant nothing and everything at once. History, Yara Nakamura had decided at sixteen, on the deck of a ship crossing from Yokohama with her father's disowning letter folded against her heart, history was a weather that fell elsewhere. It fell on Russia, where they said the Tsar's people were restless. It fell in the Balkans, whose names she could not keep straight. It fell on soldiers in far garrisons and on dead brothers whose letters came back stamped from Malta. It did not fall on chauffeurs. Chauffeurs were the ones who drove through the rain while it landed on everyone else.

She polished the Daimler's bonnet until she could see her own face in it, faint and dark and composed.

Edith Marlowe emerged from the house at half past nine dressed not in her usual tailored severity but in something older—a heavy walking skirt, boots laced to the knee, a sash of green and purple folded over her arm but not yet worn. She looked, Yara thought, like a woman dressing for a battle she had been rehearsing all her life.

"You'll take me by the park," Edith said. It was not quite a request. "Not through Whitehall. The police have been stopping cars on Victoria Street since dawn. Taking names."

"Then we go by Chelsea Embankment," Yara said. "And I'll wear my uniform. No one questions a chauffeur. I'm furniture with an engine."

Edith paused at the car door, one hand on its cold silver handle, and looked at her driver with that particular attention she sometimes gave—the attention of a woman who read people the way she read correspondence, twice, and kept copies. "You could stay home, you know. The house won't burn down without either of us in it."

"I'm driving you," Yara said. "That's my work."

It was more than her work, and they both knew it, and neither said so, because the thing between them—the unspoken ledger of what each woman knew about the other—was maintained precisely by never balancing the accounts aloud. Yara opened the door. Edith got in. The Daimler started on the second turn of the crank, coughed, caught, and settled into its deep animal idle, and they went south toward the river through streets already filling.

* * *

London on a march morning was a different city than London on any other morning. Yara felt it as she drove: the pavements thickening, trams halted and hissing, knots of women in the colours gathering at corners like water finding its level. Shopgirls and schoolmistresses, milliners and widows, a phalanx of women in nurse's aprons who had walked up from the docks. Men too—some jeering, some merely curious, a few walking alongside in awkward solidarity, hats in hand. Banners furled and unfurled in the damp wind. Somewhere a band was playing something martial that kept dissolving into ragtime.

Edith watched it all through the window with an expression Yara could only describe as hunger restrained by dignity.

"Look at them," Edith murmured, mostly to herself. "They've come out of their houses. Do you understand what that costs? Every one of them left a kitchen this morning knowing she might not sleep in her own bed tonight, and she came anyway." Her gloved fingers tapped once against her knee. "That is the whole movement, Yara. Not the speeches. The leaving."

Yara said nothing. She was watching the road, and also watching the road's reflection in the windscreen, where the crowds moved like fish under ice. She was aware—as she had been increasingly aware since Tobias Finch had appeared dripping at the service entrance with a dead man's handwriting in his coat—that the glass between herself and events was thinner than she had always pretended. A crack ran through her old creed. She drove around it.

They crossed the river at Chelsea. On the Embankment the crowd became a current. Police stood at intervals with the patient faces of men waiting for a train that would be late. A boy sold newspapers shouting something about Ulster; nobody bought. Edith put on her sash inside the car, before they stopped, so that when the door opened she would already be herself.

"Wait for me near the Marble Arch entrance," she said as Yara drew up short of Hyde Park Corner, where the crowd was densest and the mounted police sat their horses in a long glittering line like a fence made of animals. "If there's trouble—and there may be, they've been provoking trouble for weeks—you're to drive home. Do you hear me? You are not to wait. You are not to be clever. You are to drive home."

"Yes, ma'am," said Yara, who had been told similar things before and had developed a private taxonomy of obedience: the things she obeyed, the things she delayed, and the things she filed away for later consideration. This instruction she placed, for the moment, in the middle drawer.

Edith stepped out into the noise, straightened her sash with both hands as though putting on armour, and was absorbed. One moment she was a tall grey-haired woman standing alone by a motorcar; the next she was part of a river, and the river carried her off toward the rostrums, and Yara was alone with the engine ticking and the smell of hot oil and ten thousand voices.

* * *

For two hours Yara kept the car moving, as instructed, circling the park's perimeter the way a shepherd circles a flock she does not trust the dogs to hold. She parked briefly behind a bakery on the Bayswater Road and bought a penny loaf and ate it leaning against the wing of the Daimler, listening.

From inside the park the sound rose and swelled in waves—cheering, then a speaker's voice flattened by distance into music without melody, then cheering again. There was a rhythm to it that Yara found herself counting unconsciously, the way she counted gear changes. She thought about the letters in the coal cellar of her mind—no, not the cellar yet, that came later—but in the locked drawer of her room: Alistair Marlowe's hand, slanting rightward, alive on paper dated months after his death. She thought about how Edith had gone white as milk when she saw those dates, and how her hands had trembled, and how she had then folded the letters away and said, *Later. We will deal with this later,* because there was a march to lead and grief is a luxury movements cannot afford on schedule.

Everyone in this house, Yara thought, is carrying a letter they haven't opened.

At half past two the rhythm changed.

She heard it before she understood it. The cheering broke—not faded, broke, like a plate dropped on stone—and underneath it came a new sound, lower and uglier: shouting that wasn't oratory, whistles, the flat concussion of something heavy meeting something soft. Then horses. She knew the sound of horses in a crowd now, God help her; she'd learned it during the window-smashing trials last spring, standing in the public gallery taking notes she never showed anyone. Horses in a crowd sound like the sea deciding to come inland.

Yara was around the corner and into the park before she had decided to be.

* * *

What she found at the Marble Arch end of the park was not a rally anymore. It was a tide going wrong. The platforms had been rushed—she could see one overturned, its boards scattered, a purple banner trampled into the mud with a bootprint across the suffrage colours like a signature. Mounted police had formed a line and were advancing it, slowly, with the terrible patience of men doing a job, and the crowd compressed ahead of them like wool in a press. Women were being pulled out of the crush by their arms, by their hair, by their sashes, and marched off with their hands bound in whatever cord the constables carried. Somewhere a woman was screaming a name over and over—not a slogan, a name, someone's daughter, someone's sister—and the name was being swallowed whole by the noise.

History, Yara thought absurdly, standing on the running board of the Daimler with the brake still set. So this is what it looks like from inside the weather.

She should have driven home. The instruction had been explicit. Later, in the ledger she would begin—dates, names, sums, times—she would write down honestly that she had known exactly what obedience cost in that moment and chosen the other price. But choice is a luxury of hindsight; in the moment there was no choosing at all, only the sight of a grey head and a green-and-purple sash at the edge of the press, going down.

Edith had not gone to speak. Yara would learn afterward that Edith had waded into the crush deliberately, to pull free a girl of perhaps nineteen whom a constable had by the collar, and the constable had objected, and the line of horses had shifted, and the world had tilted. Now Edith was on one knee in the mud, one hand pressed to her temple, the girl clinging to her shoulder, and the press was closing over them like water over a stone, and a mounted policeman was turning his horse toward the knot of them with the bland face of a man clearing a drain.

Yara abandoned the car.

She would remember, forever, the small indecencies of that abandonment: the door left open, the handbrake still on, the ignition key still in place, her cap tumbling off somewhere in the first ten yards so that her dark hair came loose and streamed behind her. She remembered the mud taking her boot to the ankle, the smell of horse and wet wool and something copper underneath. She remembered reaching the edge of the crush and doing the thing she had seen dockhands do at Wapping during the drivers' strike—dropping her shoulder and driving into the gap between bodies rather than pushing against them, letting the crowd's own panic open for her because she was small and fast and absolutely certain.

"Edith." She said it aloud though no one could hear. "Edith. Mrs. Marlowe."

The mounted policeman was three yards away. She could see the horse's eye, rolling white-rimmed, as frightened as anyone. She could see the constable raise his arm—not in fury; worse, in routine—and she got there between the arm and its falling arc, and took the blow instead, across the shoulders, a bright bar of pain that rang through her like a struck bell and taught her in one instant what batons weighed and what bones cost. She heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize as hers. She stayed up. She got her hands under Edith's arms.

"Up," she gasped. "Madam—up—now—"

Edith's face was grey, blood threading from her temple into her eyebrow, but her eyes were clear and furious and utterly present. She saw Yara—actually saw her, the way she had looked at her that morning by the car door—and for a fraction of a second something passed between them that needed no words at all. Then Edith grabbed the girl, Yara took Edith's weight across her bad shoulder, and the three of them moved together, bent low, through the gap Yara had made, away from the horses, away from the line, into the thinning edge of the crowd where the trees began and the noise fell off like a tide retreating.

Behind them the arrests continued, orderly, bureaucratic, a machine doing its afternoon's work. Constables with notebooks. Women marching to Black Maria vans with their heads high and their mouths still going, shouting words Yara could not hear and did not need to. Someone had chalked on the path, already half smeared by boots: *WE WILL BE BACK.* The chalk was already being washed out by the drizzle. It didn't matter. Chalk always comes back.

* * *

They got the girl onto a bus going west—her own idea; she refused anything else, pressing a crumpled address card into Edith's hand and promising, with the ferocious brightness of the very young, that she'd be at the next one, *ma'am, you'll see me at every single one.* Then it was just the two of them, in the lee of a shuttered bandstand, Yara kneeling in the wet grass with her handkerchief pressed to Edith's temple and her own shoulder screaming every time she moved her arm.

"You were told to drive home," Edith said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; Yara felt them trembling where they gripped her wrist.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You left a twelve-hundred-pound motorcar unlocked in a riot."

"The windows are closed, ma'am. Rioters prefer windows."

Edith laughed—one short, cracked syllable, more breath than sound—and then pressed her lips together hard, and Yara realized with a jolt that her employer was not far from tears, and that the tears were not for the blood or the bruises but for the girl on the bus, and the overturned platform, and the hundred women in the vans, and perhaps—for the brother whose letters lay unopened in a drawer upstairs, alive when the world had agreed he was dead. Grief keeps a calendar of its own. It waits for quiet and then presents its account.

"Your shoulder," Edith said.

"It's nothing."

"Yara."

"A bruise," Yara said. "I've had worse from the crank handle." This was true, which was convenient, because it let her say it firmly.

They sat for a moment in the dripping silence under the trees, the roar of the park muffled now to a distant sea-sound. Yara became aware, gradually, of her own heartbeat, and of the strange fact of the ground beneath her knees, and of the wet grass staining her uniform trousers, and of the thought arriving in her mind with the calm of a clerk entering a figure into a column:

*I touched it. Today I touched it.*

All her life she had believed history was a country she lacked papers to enter. She had watched it from service entrances and driver's seats, through windscreens, through the sealed envelopes of other people's letters. She had believed that if she was careful enough, quiet enough, competent enough, the century would pass over her like weather over a stone. But her shoulder burned where the constable's baton had landed, and the burn was a fact, itemizable, undeniable, and it belonged to the same history that had taken Alistair Marlowe's name off the casualty lists and put him secretly alive in a Maltese hospital bed; the same history that was even now filling the cells of Holloway with women who had done nothing but stand in a park and refuse to sit down. It was not elsewhere. It had never been elsewhere. It was here, in the mud of Hyde Park, under her fingernails, soaking through her uniform, and it knew her name.

"We have to move," Edith said at last, rising, testing her legs. "They'll sweep the perimeter. If they take me tonight it should be for something better than loitering." She managed the ghost of her usual dryness, then lost it again. "Yara. What you did—" She stopped. Started again, in the tone she used for dictation, precise and deliberate, as though the sentence had to be built correctly or not at all. "I have spent thirty years telling women that the personal is political, that private courage is the seed of public change. And I have never once, in all that time, had someone step between me and a raised baton. I don't know how to enter that in any of my ledgers."

"There's more than one kind of ledger," Yara said.

Edith looked at her for a long moment. Rain dripped from the bandstand's iron eaves between them, keeping time.

"Drive us home," Edith said finally. "By the back ways. And Yara—" she hesitated, and then, quietly, in a voice Yara had never heard her use, "—thank you."

* * *

The Daimler was untouched. Yara noted this with a professional satisfaction that felt almost obscene given the afternoon, started the engine, and turned for home through streets that were already pretending nothing had happened—shopkeepers sweeping broken glass from the suffrage windows of two hours ago, a constable idly directing traffic, London buttoning itself up and calling it order.

But halfway down the Edgware Road, stuck behind a brewer's dray, Yara glanced sideways and saw Edith sitting very upright with the bloodstained handkerchief still in her lap, staring straight ahead. And she saw what was clutched in her employer's free hand, half hidden by the folds of her sash: a piece of paper, torn hastily from a notebook, that had not been in Edith's possession that morning.

A police notice. An official summons, printed in black and red. Edith Marlowe was ordered to appear—or, reading the small type that Yara could decipher at a glance from the driver's seat, was *liable to arrest upon failure to appear*—under terms that had nothing to do with today's riot at all.

Edith caught her looking. For a moment neither spoke. The dray rattled on; the rain came harder against the windscreen.

"Well," Edith said at last, folding the paper away with terrible composure. "It seems, Yara, that they have been keeping a file on me longer than I have been keeping mine."
