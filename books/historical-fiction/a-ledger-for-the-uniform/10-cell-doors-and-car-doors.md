## 10. Cell Doors and Car Doors

The gates of Holloway opened at nine minutes past seven in the morning, and Edith Marlowe came through them like a woman stepping off a train she had not wanted to end.

Yara saw her from the Daimler, parked where the warders' shadows did not quite reach, and felt something in her chest turn over—not relief, exactly, though there was that too, a loosening she had carried all night in the muscles of her jaw—but recognition. This was not the woman who had been taken from the pavement outside King's Cross three days ago. That woman had been tired, bruised at the elbow, furious in the ordinary way of a person who has been handled roughly by strangers. The woman walking toward the car now was thinner, greyer about the mouth, and lit from somewhere behind the eyes with a flame that had nothing gentle in it.

Holloway had not broken her. Holloway had *edited* her—struck out everything soft and left only the argument.

Edith reached the running board before Yara could get out to open the door herself, which was unusual; Edith always waited for the ritual, liked the small theatre of being handed into her own motorcar. Today she hauled the door open with her own hand. Her knuckles were scabbed. She had been force-fed—that was what the whispers at the gate had said, one matron to another, careless of the chauffeur standing within earshot—and Yara's stomach clenched at the thought of it, the tube, the throat, the indignity dressed up as medical care.

"Good morning, Yara," Edith said, settling onto the seat. Her voice was hoarse, scraped down to gravel. "Home, please."

"The housekeeper's sent word you're to see Dr. Pelling the moment—"

"Home."

It was not a shout. It was worse than a shout. It was the voice of a woman who had spent thirty-six hours in a cell learning exactly how much breath she had to spend on any given word, and intended to spend none of it twice.

Yara closed the door. Walked around the bonnet. Climbed in. Took the wheel.

She did not drive home.

* * *

They went south along Camden Road because going north meant Holloway again and Yara would not put that building in the mirror so soon, even as a smear of brick behind them. The morning was cold enough that the windscreen kept fogging at its lower edge, and Yara wiped it with the back of her glove at every stop while pretending to check the magneto. Beside her Edith sat very straight, hands folded, watching the streets go by with the expression of an accountant reviewing figures she already knew were wrong.

London was awake around them: milk carts, a dray horse shying at a bicycle bell, shopboys rolling up awnings, the smell of coal smoke and bread from a bakery on Caledonian Road. Ordinary life, proceeding without reference to anything. Yara had loved this once—the sense that the city was a river and she a boat upon it, moving through other people's mornings untouched, history happening elsewhere, in newspapers, in Parliament, in places whose weather she never had to feel. Now the river felt like something else. It felt like water rising.

"You haven't turned for home," Edith said.

"No, ma'am."

"Where are we going?"

"Away," Yara said. "For twenty minutes. Then home."

Edith turned her head slowly. Yara kept her eyes on the road, but she felt the look land between her shoulder blades like a hand.

"I have a deputation at eleven," Edith said. "I have letters to dictate. I have—" a dry sound, almost a laugh, "—I have three days of the world to catch up on, and you are taking me *away*?"

"You've been force-fed." The words came out before Yara had decided to say them. "I heard the warders. Your throat will be torn. You need broth and rest and a doctor who isn't one of theirs, and if you go straight into that house you'll be dictating within the hour until your voice gives out entirely, and then they'll have won twice."

Silence. The engine ticked beneath them. A woman with a pram stepped off the kerb and Yara braked, gently, and let her cross, and the woman smiled her thanks at the chauffeur without ever seeing the passenger, and this was the whole of Yara's life in miniature and she knew it.

"You heard the warders," Edith repeated.

"They talk near me. They forget I'm here."

"Yes," Edith said softly. "People do that. It is one of the great uses of people like you."

The sentence went in under Yara's ribs before she understood why. *People like you.* She told herself it meant chauffeurs. She drove on.

* * *

She took them to the reservoir path behind Stoke Newington, where the Daimler could stand unremarked among tradesmen's carts, and where the water lay flat and pewter-coloured under a sky the colour of dishwater. She left the engine ticking and sat with her gloved hands in her lap, waiting for the storm she had invited.

It came, but not at once. First there was quiet, the particular quiet of Edith Marlowe deciding whether to be angry or merely disappointed, and Yara had learned over four years that the second was far more dangerous.

"Do you know what they did to me last night?" Edith said at last, conversationally, watching the ducks.

"No, ma'am."

"They held my head against a chair back while two women pinned my arms and a doctor—I use the word generously—inserted a tube down my throat. He missed the first time. And the second. On the third attempt I vomited blood, and he said, quite calmly, 'Hold her still,' as though I were furniture that had developed opinions." She said it without heat, the way she read out committee minutes. "And do you know what I thought? While they were doing it? I thought: somewhere in this city tonight there are girls reading my name in the papers, girls who have never dared say an unsanctioned word aloud, and they will read that Mrs. Marlowe was fed by force and did not cry out, and tomorrow they will be a little less afraid. That is worth a tube in the throat. That is the entire arithmetic of it."

"It isn't arithmetic," Yara said. "You can't itemize a body like a ledger."

Something flickered across Edith's face—surprise, perhaps, that Yara had spoken of ledgers at all. Yara had her private ledger hidden in the false bottom of her sewing box upstairs, dates and names and sums, Alistair's impossible handwriting itemized line by line, and she had never once mentioned it aloud. But the metaphor had come naturally, because everything in her life now came naturally in columns.

"No?" Edith said. "Then what would you call it?"

"Fear," Yara said. "Fear with good bookkeeping. You're afraid that stopping—even for a day—means admitting they can stop you. So you'd rather destroy yourself on schedule than give them the satisfaction. That's not strategy, ma'am. That's pride wearing strategy's coat."

The words hung in the cold air between them. Yara heard them as though someone else had spoken, and was appalled, and was not sorry. Both at once. It was a sensation she was having more often lately, since Tobias Finch had arrived dripping at the service door with a dead man's letters in his arms—this feeling of her own voice escaping ahead of her judgment, saying things she had spent years keeping folded away.

Edith looked at her for a long moment. The wind moved the surface of the reservoir in long slow wrinkles. Somewhere behind them a cart driver swore at his horse with real affection.

"And what would you have me do instead?" Edith asked. "Rest? Take the waters? Let Miss Pankhurst carry the standard while I convalesce like a dowager? You were not in the cell, Yara. You did not hear the key in the lock and think, *this is the sound of the century, and I am on the wrong side of the door.*"

"No," Yara said. "I heard the door of the garage close every night for four years, and I read your letters after you sealed them, and I memorized the names of your safe houses, and I never once wrote to my father to tell him what I believed in, because believing things gets people locked up and I had decided—very sensibly, very early—that history happens to other people." She stopped. The confession had run further than she'd meant it to, and she reined it in too late. "So perhaps I'm not entitled to advise you."

"No," Edith said quietly. "Perhaps you aren't."

And there it was. Not shouted. Not cruel, even—or rather, cruel precisely because it was not shouted, because it was delivered in the mild tone of a woman correcting a date in a manuscript. Yara felt the words settle over her like a coat that fit too well. *A spectator.* Edith hadn't used the word, but she had drawn its outline perfectly, and inside the outline was Yara Nakamura, aged twenty-three, sitting in the front seat of a car she maintained beautifully, holding a steering wheel instead of a banner, watching.

"You think I don't understand what you've given," Edith went on, and her voice softened, which somehow made it worse. "I do. Four years of loyalty, of discretion, of—for heaven's sake, Yara, you drive me to prison gates and wait for me in the rain. I am not blind. But there is a difference between serving a cause and belonging to one, and I have never been able to tell which side of that difference you wished to stand on. You keep yourself—" she searched for the word, found it, and used it with terrible gentleness— "*available*. Available is not the same as committed."

"I committed," Yara said. Her hands were tight on her knees inside the gloves. "You just didn't notice, because my commitment looks like work."

"Work is what servants do," Edith said. "Commitment is what comrades do. Which are you?"

The question dropped into the space between them and neither of them picked it up.

* * *

Afterwards Yara could not have said how long they sat at the reservoir. Five minutes. Ten. The engine cooled and began to tick irregularly, and a light rain started, spotting the windscreen, and still neither woman moved. It was Edith who finally sighed—a deep, scraped, exhausted sound, the sound of a body reminding its owner it existed—and said, "Take me home, then. And send for broth, since you insist on playing nursemaid."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Yara." A pause. "I spoke harshly."

"No, ma'am. You spoke clearly. There's a difference."

"Don't be clever with me. I'm too tired to enjoy it." But there was the ghost of the old warmth in it, the warmth Yara had basked in for four years like a cat in a window, and she clutched at it even as she understood that something had cracked this morning and would not set the same way. Some quarrels, once begun, simply wait. They sit in the house like unopened post.

She drove them home through thinning rain. At the corner of the mews she saw, before she allowed herself to properly see it, a man standing by the lamppost opposite the service entrance—a solid, respectable man in a good coat and a bowler, holding a newspaper he was not reading. He had the stillness of a man who has been posted somewhere. She had grown up in a port city; she knew watchers the way sailors know weather.

She pulled the car past the entrance and round to the mews behind, as though by habit, and watched the man in the wing mirror. He turned a page. He did not follow. But when she brought the Daimler in through the rear gate ten minutes later, he had shifted position—twenty yards east, still unreadable, still patient.

Tobias was waiting in the garage, sleeves rolled, greasing the axle stands she'd left out. He took one look at her face and set down the grease gun.

"Well?" he said.

"She called me available," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "As opposed to committed. And there's a man across the street who reads his newspaper upside down."

Tobias went pale in the way he did—all at once, like a lamp guttering. "Renwick?"

"Or Renwick's kind. Bureaucracy doesn't do its own watching; it hires it." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, buying a moment, and found that her heart was not hammering with fear but with something adjacent to it, hotter and more useful. All her life she had stood at windows watching events occur to other people. This morning a woman she loved had called her a spectator, and she had been unable to argue, and now there was a watcher at the gate, and the ledger in her sewing box had eleven entries in it, and every one of them ended with a name that was not hers.

"Tobias," she said. "How quickly could we move the letters from the house if we had to?"

He blinked. "Tonight, if we had to. Why?"

"Because I've decided something," Yara said, and heard, to her astonishment, that it was true. "I'm done being available."

Above them, through the garage roof, they both heard the front bell of the house ring—one sharp, official double-peal, the ring of a man who expects to be answered—and Tobias's eyes went to hers in the dimness, and neither of them moved toward the door.
