## 12. A Name in the Margin

The rain came in off the river that Tuesday like a debt collector, patient and thorough, and Yara Nakamura was glad of it. Rain kept callers away. Rain gave the house on Cheyne Walk an excuse to draw its curtains and go dim and inward, and it gave her an excuse to sit at the kitchen table with the good lamp lit and the letters of a dead man spread before her like cards from a hand she was learning to read.

She had been at it since nine o'clock. It was now past three.

The ledger lay open beside her, its columns ruled in her own careful hand — *date, place, mark, note* — because that was how she had been taught to make sense of anything: reduce it to entries, weigh it, carry the remainder forward. Her father had kept ledgers for his shipping concern in Yokohama, and even disowned and four thousand miles distant, she could not shake the belief that anything true could be written in columns. Anything false, too. That was the trouble with ledgers. They were only as honest as the hand that held the pen.

Seventeen letters. She had catalogued them all twice over now, once by postmark and once by content, and the picture they built was maddeningly incomplete — a hospital ward in Malta rendered in Alistair Marlowe's looping, impatient script; references to doctors whose names she had traced to the garrison records Tobias had copied out; long silences explained only by the censor's blue pencil, which had struck through whole sentences with the tidy brutality of a man weeding a garden.

The censorship itself told her things. She had learned to read the omissions. Where the pencil fell thickest, Alistair had written most urgently. And there was one passage — letter eleven, dated the fourth of March — where the censor had cut so deep the paper had nearly torn, leaving only the ends of lines hanging like roots pulled from soil:

*…cannot remain under his eye another fortnight. She has arranged—* [struck] *—the boat leaves from the harbour on the night of the—* [struck] *—I know what it costs her to do this and I will not repay her with—*

Yara read it again. She had read it perhaps forty times.

*A woman,* she wrote in the ledger's margin, and then sat back and looked at what she'd written, because writing it made it real in a way that thinking it never did.

Outside, the rain ticked against the garage roof where the Daimler slept, and the kitchen clock counted its small brass seconds, and somewhere above her Edith Marlowe was pacing her study with the uneven rhythm Yara had come to know as grief wearing its walking shoes. The household had settled into a strange new weather system since Tobias Finch's arrival. Edith moved through the halls like a woman listening for a knock. Tobias took his meals in the servants' hall without complaint but with the fixed, apologetic stillness of a man who has not yet been asked to leave and cannot quite believe it. And Yara herself — Yara had stopped sleeping properly, which she concealed with the same competence with which she concealed everything else.

She turned back to letter eleven, meaning to file it, and it was then — folding it along its old creases, the way one folds a thing a hundred times folded — that she saw the margin.

Not the margin she had written in. The other margin. The left-hand one, narrow, close to the binding fold, where the paper had yellowed differently because the ink had sat there longer.

There was a name.

It was not Alistair's handwriting. She was certain of this within a heartbeat, certain the way one is certain of a face across a crowded street — not by analysis but by recognition. His script leaned forward eagerly, all loops and ambition. This was upright, small, economical, each letter finished cleanly as if the writer could not afford waste. The ink was a different colour too, a violet gone brownish with age, the kind that came from the cheap fountain pens used in offices and hospitals and ticket booths — anywhere a person wrote many things quickly for little pay.

A single initial and a surname, half-finished, abandoned mid-stroke as though the pen had been snatched away or the courage run out:

*V. Callow—*

Nothing more. No date. No explanation. Just those seven letters sitting in the margin of a dead man's letter like a passenger left standing on a platform.

Yara did not move for a full minute. The clock ticked. The rain fell. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked twice and went quiet.

Then she reached for her ledger and, with a hand that was almost steady, entered the finding beneath her earlier note, striking out *a woman* and writing instead: *V. Callow—— (surname incomplete). Hand not A.M.'s. Violet ink. Present on letters 9, 11, 13; absent elsewhere.*

Because it was present on the others. Once she knew to look — once the pattern broke open the way a lock breaks when you finally find the right pressure — she found it again and again, fainter each time, as though whoever had written it had grown warier with every letter. On letter nine it was tucked into the corner beside the salutation, so small she might have taken it for a blot. On thirteen it rode along the bottom edge below the signature, upside down, as if written after the page had been turned. Three appearances. Then nothing. As though the hand had decided, somewhere around the spring of that year, to stop signing its work.

*She has arranged—* the censor had struck. *The boat leaves from the harbour.* *I know what it costs her.*

Yara laid her pen down and pressed both palms flat against the table, as if the wood might hold still long enough for her to think.

Here was the shape of it, and she did not like the shape. Alistair Marlowe, declared dead at Gallipoli three years ago, alive in a Maltese hospital, writing of boats and harbours and arrangements, writing of a *she* who paid costs on his behalf — and in the margins, in another hand entirely, the beginning of a name. If a woman had helped him, then he had not simply survived. He had *gone*. Deserted, hidden, smuggled — the words assembled themselves in her mind unbidden, each one heavier than the last. And if he had deserted, then the death notice, the mourning, the black-edged card Edith had kept framed in her study these three years like an icon — none of it was tragedy. It was construction. Someone had built a dead man out of paperwork, and someone else — this upright, careful, violet-inked someone — had held the pen.

History happened to other people. That had been her creed, her comfort, the wall she had built between herself and the century. But here was history lying flat on a kitchen table, and here was Yara Nakamura holding the page, and the page was asking her a question she did not want to answer: *what would you have done, in that hospital, with that pen?*

Would she have signed? Would she have risked whatever that woman had risked — her position, her name, very possibly her freedom — for a man who was not hers, whose war was not hers, whose country would have called her a criminal for kindness?

Yara thought of herself sealing Edith's letters unread. Except she had never sealed them unread. She read every one, had read them for two years, and told herself the reading cost nothing because no one ever knew.

But this woman's marks had been known to someone, hadn't they? Or feared. That was why they stopped. That was why the margins went silent after March.

She heard him on the stairs — Tobias's tread had become familiar now, uneven on the descent, favouring the leg the field hospital had not fully given back to him — and she had just enough time to cover the letters with the newspaper before he appeared in the doorway, hatless, drying his hair with a towel that had once belonged to better circumstances.

"Mrs. Hobbs said I'd find you here." He nodded at the newspaper. "Working?"

"Accounts," Yara said. It was not even a lie; it was simply a smaller truth inside the larger one, and she was growing expert at those.

He lingered. Rain still dripped from the hem of his coat onto the stone flags, and he seemed unaware of it, standing there with the particular hesitance of a guest who has stayed too long and knows it. "I've been to the Public Record Office," he said at last. "The clerk I knew — Halloran — he's transferred, but the man who took his desk remembers me. Remembers *Renwick*, rather. Renwick visited the Malta files in November. Twice."

"Twice," Yara repeated. "After the death notice was already filed?"

"After." Tobias pulled out the chair opposite her and sat, uninvited, which was unlike him, and she understood that he had come back frightened and had nowhere else to put it. "You don't visit closed files twice. You don't visit them at all, usually. Unless something's surfaced. Unless you're checking whether—" He stopped, rubbed his jaw. "Unless someone's started asking questions. Miss Nakamura, I've been turning this over for days. When I took those letters from the burn sack, I told myself it was sentiment. A clerk's weakness. But what if it wasn't mine alone? What if Alistair *wanted* them kept — wanted them found — and chose me because I was the one man in that office too broken to follow orders cleanly?"

The rain filled the pause. Yara looked at him across the covered letters — this shaking, stubborn, guilt-riddled man who had carried a dead man's correspondence through three years and two countries — and felt the strange vertiginous tenderness she had begun to feel whenever the household assembled itself in her mind. Edith at the window. Tobias dripping on the flags. Herself at the lamp. None of them related by blood. All of them bound by paper.

"Then we should ask what else he wanted found," she said quietly, and lifted the corner of the newspaper, and watched Tobias's face as she slid letter eleven across the table and turned it to show him the margin.

He bent close. His breath fogged slightly in the cold kitchen air. "*V. Callow—*," he read aloud, and frowned. "That's not his hand."

"No."

"You're sure?"

"I've spent a week with his hand. I'd know it asleep." She tapped the violet letters with one fingertip. "This is someone else entirely. Someone careful. Someone who wrote fast and small, the way clerks write, or nurses, or telegraphists. Someone who stopped signing after March."

Tobias sat back slowly. In the lamplight his face went through several expressions in succession — puzzlement, dawning comprehension, and then something darker that gathered behind his eyes like weather. "Callow," he said. "Callaghan. Calloway. Calloway — wait." He pressed the heel of his hand against his forehead, digging for it. "There was a nurse. At the field hospital outside Suvla — no, later, the convalescent depot on Malta, after they shipped me south. There was a matron's assistant, English, quiet, worked the night ward. The men called her—"

He stopped. His hand came away from his forehead and lay flat on the table, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone lower, gone careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing it.

"The men called her the Quill. Because she wrote letters home for anyone who couldn't write — and because she'd alter them if you asked her to. Make a coward brave, make a drunkard sober, make a deserter a hero, all in the space of a page. We thought it was a joke." His throat moved. "We thought it was a joke. Miss Nakamura — if she could alter a letter, she could alter a record. A death record. A service record. *Anything.*"

The clock knocked its way past the quarter hour. Neither of them moved.

"And Alistair trusted her," Yara said. Each word placed like a coin on a scale. "Or she volunteered. Either way — she forged his death, or helped forge it, and then she followed him to London, or she didn't, and either way she is the second entry in our ledger and we do not have her first name."

"*Vera,*" Tobias said suddenly. "Vera. That was it. Vera. I'm nearly sure — Vera, yes. The night sister used to shout for her down the corridor. *Vera! Ward four!* You don't forget a name shouted like that at three in the morning." He laughed, short and disbelieving, without any humour in it. "Three years I've carried these letters and never once opened my memory of her. Isn't that something. Isn't that exactly the sort of thing a man can do — lose a person entire, right in his own head, because remembering would cost too much."

"We are all of us keeping ledgers we don't read," Yara said, and was surprised by her own voice, how bare it sounded.

She drew the ledger toward her and wrote, in her best hand, the entry she had been circling all afternoon:

*Vera Calloway(?), nurse, Malta. Forger of records. Author of marginal notes, letters 9–13. Probable architect of A. Marlowe's death. Location unknown. Presumed London.*

Presumed London. She underlined it twice, and the underline was a prayer and a threat both, because if the woman was in London then she was findable, and if she was findable then she was in danger — from Renwick, from the War Office, from whatever machinery had ground a living soldier into a dead one and expected the paperwork to hold.

And if Yara found her first —

If Yara found her first, she would be looking into a mirror. Another woman who believed the truth was a document, and documents were instruments, and instruments could be played in either direction. Another keeper of accounts that no one was meant to audit.

"She'll be afraid," Yara said aloud, closing the ledger. "Whoever she is. She'll have been waiting three years for someone to come asking about Malta, and every knock will sound like Renwick's men."

"Then we'd best be gentle about the knocking," Tobias said.

"Yes." Yara stood, gathering the letters into their oilcloth wrapping with movements that were, as always, precise and unhurried, though her pulse was not. Above them, the floorboards announced Edith's pacing, round and round the study, a metronome for the household's held breath. "And we'd best be quick. Because if I were a woman who had erased a man from the British Army — and I had just learned that the man's sister employs a chauffeur who reads her mail —"

She paused at the door, the lamplight catching the edge of the ledger under her arm.

"— I should want very much to know who else was keeping books."

* * *

That evening, after supper, Yara climbed to the attic under pretence of fetching spare lamps and stood instead at the small round window that looked east over the roofs toward the river. The rain had cleared. Gaslights came up along the Embankment one by one, and beyond them the city went on with its enormous indifferent life, and she thought: *she is out there somewhere. A woman with careful hands and a violet pen, who has spent three years being no one, on purpose.*

Yara knew exactly what that was like. Which meant she also knew that such women could be found — if you knew where no ones went to hide.

Tomorrow she would start with the nursing registries. Tomorrow she would begin, item by item, to build a file on the forger.

Tonight she wrote one line in the ledger before blowing out the lamp, and the line was this:

*Entry the third: the dead man had help. Find the hand that held the pen.*
