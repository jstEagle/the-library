## 13. Vera

The ward smelled of carbolic and cut flowers, which was how Vera Calloway knew it was Sunday. On weekdays the flowers were stripped out by seven o'clock—Matron said they bred flies—but on Sundays, and only Sundays, somebody from the Ladies' Committee came with armfuls of chrysanthemums and arranged them in jam jars along the windowsills of the Royal Free, and for one day the hospital pretended to be a place where people recovered instead of a place where people were processed.

Vera had learned to live inside pretences. She had, in fact, become rather accomplished at them.

She moved down the aisle of iron beds with her trolley, changing dressings that did not need changing because the men liked the attention, and she let her mind do what it always did on Sundays when the work went soft: it went back to Malta.

Not to the whole of it. To a single afternoon.

* * *

St. Elmo's convalescent annex had been a barracks once, then a granary, then a hospital, which meant its walls were three feet thick and held heat like an oven. In August of 1911 the fans did nothing but stir the air around. Vera had been on night duty for eleven months by then and had stopped dreaming of England entirely; she dreamed in Maltese now, or in the flat grey language of charts and temperatures.

Alistair Marlowe had come to her ward in July, carried off a hospital ship from the Dardanelles station with a wound that should have killed him—a shell fragment through the shoulder, sepsis knocking at the door—and had refused, with a politeness that infuriated her, to die.

"You're not eating," she told him the third week.

"I'm eating exactly what's put in front of me."

"Which is half of what's put in front of you."

He smiled at that, the slow smile of a man who had learned that charm was cheaper than honesty. "You count everything, Sister Calloway."

"Somebody has to. The Army counts ammunition and forgets to count men." She took the bowl away. He watched her go with those eyes that seemed always to be reading something just over her shoulder—the way he'd looked at her chart, at the censor's stamp on his own outgoing letters, at everything—as though the world were a document he'd been asked to initial without reading first.

He was a captain in the Royal Engineers, seconded somewhere he would not name, and his file was thicker than most and thinner in information than any she'd seen. And he was afraid. Not of the wound. Vera had nursed four hundred men through wounds and knew the shape of fear, and Alistair Marlowe's fear had paperwork in it. It arrived with the morning post. It wore a major's insignia.

She found out in September, because she was the sort of woman who found things out, who read the returns before they were filed, who noticed that Captain Marlowe's outgoing correspondence went through a different censor's desk than every other officer on the ward, and that nothing he received ever bore a civilian postmark.

She found out because one evening she stayed late in the records room collating admission books, and Major Renwick came in without knocking—he never knocked, doors were things that happened to other people—and stood over the clerk's desk reviewing a folder, and she saw, upside down and across a crowded room, a sheet of paper in a hand she knew from a hundred sick-bed letters home. Alistair's hand. A confession, or something shaped like one, signed and dated, sitting in Renwick's folder like a loaded gun.

She could not tell you afterward what the words were. She remembered only the sensation: cold in the spine, in August, in Malta.

* * *

What she pieced together over the following fortnight was this: Alistair, in the course of whatever duties had taken him to places maps didn't label, had discovered something about his commanding officer. Something involving sums. Sums involving stores that were sold rather than issued, men who were listed as present who had died, money moving through Cairo banks under names that belonged to dead privates. Alistair, being Alistair—being a man who believed that accounts ought to balance—had begun keeping notes.

And Renwick, being Renwick, had found out. And Renwick had done the thing that clever cruel men do: he had taken Alistair's own indiscretions—an affair, a forged leave pass, small sins, human ones—and written them down beside the large ones, and made it clear that if Alistair ever opened his mouth about stores and ledgers, Alistair's family would learn what kind of man their son was, and a court-martial would learn it more officially still.

Blackmail is such an ugly word. In the files it was called *protective custody of sensitive correspondence*. Vera had come to understand that the Empire ran on euphemism the way the hospital ran on carbolic: you didn't notice it until you tried to scrub it off.

* * *

The idea came to her on a Tuesday, folding laundry, of all places.

It was not a dramatic moment. That was the thing no one understood about forgery—it wasn't drama, it was *laundry*. It was repetition. She had spent two years copying doctors' orders onto bed-cards in her neatest hand; she had filled out death notices by the dozen, because Malta in those years produced death the way Lancashire produced cotton. She knew the forms. She knew the forms better than the officers did, because the officers dictated and she wrote.

And she knew—this was the thought that stopped her with a pillowcase half-folded in her hands—that a death notice required exactly three signatures, only one of which anyone ever actually verified.

The medical officer signed cause of death. The sister of the ward countersigned. The adjutant filed it.

She was the sister of the ward.

The MO, Dr. Ferris, was a man who signed anything put in front of him after six o'clock. The adjutant was a boy of twenty-three whose entire ambition was to be transferred somewhere with trees.

Three signatures. One pen.

She stood in the linen room holding a pillowcase and felt the idea settle into her body with terrible calm, the way a diagnosis settles. Not *should I?* but *I am going to.* Some decisions are made years before you make them; you only find out later when they arrive.

Because here was the arithmetic as she saw it, and Vera Calloway trusted arithmetic above scripture: one man alive and ruined, blackmailed into silence forever, his notes burned, his life owned—or one man legally dead, free, his knowledge intact, waiting. Renwick wanted Alistair erased. Very well. She would erase him herself—on her terms, into freedom instead of into a grave. Let the Empire have its paperwork. Paperwork was a country, and she was a citizen of long standing.

There was a cost, and she counted it too, because she counted everything. A dead man cannot write to his mother. A dead man cannot come home. A dead man's sister campaigns for votes in London believing—no. Vera set that thought down carefully and did not pick it up again for two years. There are weights you can carry and weights you can't, and you learn which is which by trying.

* * *

She chose the date with a nurse's cunning: the worst week of the dysentery season, when the annex buried four or five men a day and the registers were copied in haste and the clerks were falling asleep standing up. She chose the name from a list of men who had genuinely died that week at another hospital up the coast—a private named Ellery whose notice had gone astray in the post, so that the number matched, the totals balanced, and no auditor counting bodies against beds would ever find a discrepancy. That was the whole art of it, she thought then and thought now: not to hide the lie, but to make the lie *balance*. Every entry has its double-entry. Every theft its receipt.

Dr. Ferris signed at 6:40 in the evening, in the corridor, holding her clipboard against the wall while a stretcher party jostled past. The adjutant filed it at nine the next morning between a requisition for bandages and a complaint about the ice supply.

And then she sat down at the ward table at midnight, with the register open and the lamp turned low, and committed the act itself: she wrote, in a fair imitation of Dr. Ferris's cramped hand, the words *died of cardiac failure following septicaemia*, and beneath them, *C. Marlowe, Capt., R.E.*, and she entered him into death the way she had entered four hundred others, with a steady line and even pressure, and she blew out the lamp, and she walked back down the ward past the sleeping men, and she stopped at Alistair's bed, and he was awake, of course he was awake, and neither of them said anything at all.

He understood. That was the horror and the mercy of it. She had told him three days earlier, in the linen room, in a voice pitched under the sound of the mangle: *"On Friday you will be dead. There is a fishing boat from Valetta that runs on Saturday nights. Be on it, or be buried in it, I don't care which, but don't be here."* And he had gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "Why?" and she had said, "Because you're the only man I've met in this war who keeps honest accounts, and the world needs one," and that was the nearest she ever came, in that whole affair, to saying what she meant.

* * *

London, October 1913.

"Mrs. Calloway? Bed six is asking for you."

Vera blinked the Mediterranean out of her eyes. The Royal Free swam back into focus—gas brackets, chrysanthemums, the smell of Sunday. Bed six was a boy of nineteen with a fractured femur who wanted someone to hold his hand while the bone was manipulated, and she went and held it, and told him about the cricket match at Lord's she had invented for the occasion, and he screamed into a leather strap and thanked her afterward as though she'd given him something.

Afterward she climbed the stairs to the sisters' room, took out the key she wore on a ribbon under her uniform, and unlocked the drawer of her own small desk—the one privilege of her rank—and lifted out the tin.

A tobacco tin, Player's, empty, lined now with oilcloth. Inside: a ledger. Not a real ledger, nothing so incriminating—a pocket diary, 1910, unused, its pages repurposed. But she kept it like a ledger, because that was what she was, underneath the starch and the cap: a keeper of accounts. Her creed, if she'd been asked to state it, would have run thus: *Everything true must be written down twice—once where they can find it, and once where they can't.*

In it she recorded, in a cipher of her own devising—dates shifted by three days, names rendered as drug dosages—every fact of the Alistair Marlowe affair she could bear to keep: the amounts she'd traced through the Cairo bank notices Renwick had carelessly left in the records room; the name of the fishing boat; the date she'd posted the false telegram; the sum she'd paid, in instalments across two years, to the widow of Private Ellery, whose husband's name she had borrowed for the grave—because a lie that spends someone else's grief owes interest, and Vera paid her interest.

That was the difference between her and Renwick, she told herself, and some nights she almost believed it: Renwick wrote things down in order to bury people. She wrote things down so that burial was never the last word.

Tonight there was a new entry to consider. The landlady's girl had brought up a card that afternoon, left at the boarding house in Camden where Vera lodged: *Miss Yara Nakamura, calling on behalf of Mrs. E. Marlowe.* No address beyond the house in Hampstead. No explanation. Just the name, sitting on her dressing table like a lit fuse.

They know. Or they suspect. Or they have the letters—which meant the letters she had posted, all those careful months of coded consolation in a dying man's handwriting, had reached Hampstead after all, which meant Alistair was writing again, which meant—

Which meant the record was moving. And a moving record is either being corrected or being hunted, and Vera had spent two years learning to tell the difference, and she could not yet tell this one.

She closed the tin. She locked the drawer. She pressed her palm flat against the desk's worn wood and allowed herself ten seconds—not more—of the fear she rationed out nightly, the fear that lived directly beneath the competence, the same address where it lived in every quiet woman she had ever known.

Then she took out her pen and wrote tomorrow into her duty book like any other day: wards, dressings, Matron's round at ten.

But below it, in pencil, lightly, so lightly it might have been a dosage: *Miss N. — afternoon. Bring the Ellery papers. If she is who she says she is, show her everything. If she isn't —*

She did not finish the sentence. Some entries you leave open, and trust the next hand to complete them.

Down in the ward, bed six slept, and the chrysanthemums drooped in their jam jars, and somewhere north of the river a motor car was carrying a chauffeur who read other people's letters toward a door Vera Calloway had been keeping locked for two years.

She turned down the lamp and sat in the dark a while longer, a woman alone with two sets of books—one for the world, and one for the truth—and waited, as all hidden historians wait, for the knock.
