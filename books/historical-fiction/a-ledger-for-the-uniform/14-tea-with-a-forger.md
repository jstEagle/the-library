## 14. Tea with a Forger

The address Yara had been given was a narrow house in Camden Town, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold surgical instruments, its brick face blackened by the trains that ran behind it. Three bells beside the door, each with a different tenant's name on a card gone soft with damp. The middle one said *Calloway* in a hand so small and precise it looked printed.

Yara had come alone. That had been the whole of the arrangement, delivered by note in an envelope with no return address: *Tuesday, four o'clock. Come without the motor. Come without her.* Vera Calloway did not write like a nurse; she wrote like someone who had learned to make handwriting do what she wanted, which was, Yara supposed, exactly what she was.

She climbed three flights of stairs that smelled of coal smoke and camphor, and knocked at a door with a brass number four polished to a dull gold.

It opened before her knuckles fell a second time.

"You're earlier than I expected," said the woman in the doorway. "Which means you took a hansom and told yourself you'd walk from the corner. You didn't walk. Your boots are clean."

Vera Calloway was smaller than Yara had imagined—she had built the woman up out of her function, the way one builds a villain or a saint—and she was neither pretty nor plain in any way that settled. Her hair was pinned with mathematical severity. Her hands, when she offered one, were cool and dry, and there was a faint stain of iodine at the cuticle of her left thumb that no amount of scrubbing had quite defeated.

"Miss Nakamura," Vera said. "Come in. Mind the trunk."

The trunk stood in the middle of the room like an animal that had not yet decided where to lie down. Beyond it, the lodgings were spare but fiercely ordered: a bed made with hospital corners, a washstand, two chairs, a table set for tea as though for a ceremony. A window looked out over a wilderness of slate roofs and chimney pots, and on its sill sat a row of potted herbs—thyme, mint, something woody Yara couldn't name—all of them alive, all of them trimmed.

"You keep plants," Yara said, because she could think of nothing else.

"I kill them slowly instead of quickly. It's the same discipline as nursing." Vera gestured to a chair. "Sit. The kettle's just off the boil. Milk's gone off, so it's lemon, and if that offends you I'm afraid the offence is included in the rent."

Yara sat. She had rehearsed this meeting on the ride across town—opening lines, deflections, the careful circling she'd learned from watching Edith handle donors and journalists—but every prepared sentence dissolved in the flat, unsentimental air of the room. Vera poured tea with the economy of someone who had never owned more cups than people, and handed one across, and waited.

"You forged a death certificate," Yara said.

"I altered a document that contained a falsehood," Vera corrected, settling into the other chair, "in order to make it contain a different falsehood that happened to be kinder. Whether that is forging depends entirely on whose ledger you keep." She sipped. "You've read his letters. All of them. Don't look surprised—I know how you read, Miss Nakamura. You read the way I file. Everything goes somewhere."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." But there was no cruelty in it; there was almost tenderness. "I mean that we are the same animal in different cages. Sit comfortably. This will take longer than your mistress would like, and shorter than you will."

* * *

Outside, a train went past behind the houses, and the tea trembled in its cups, and neither woman moved until it had finished.

Vera talked the way she wrote: precisely, without ornament, as though decoration were a form of lying. She had trained at Guy's, gone out to Malta in 1910 with a nursing order she no longer named without a certain dry contempt, and found herself in a ward where men arrived with numbers instead of names and left, sometimes, with numbers instead of graves.

"The paperwork was the war," she said. "Not the guns. The guns are loud, so everyone believes they're the war. But a man is wounded, and then he is a form; he is transported, and he is a stamp; he dies, and he is a line ruled through. Whoever holds the pen holds the man. I learned that in my first month, when a sergeant-major crossed out my dosage because the requisition wouldn't allow it, and a boy died of a dose that existed only in my memory." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the chip in the rim away from her. "After that I stopped believing the records were a picture of things. They are the thing. And once you understand that—" she shrugged, a small, terrible shrug— "you understand that anyone with steady hands can redraw the world."

"And Captain Marlowe asked you to."

"Captain Marlowe asked me for nothing. That's rather the point of him." For the first time, something moved under the composure—not softness, exactly; more like a door opening onto a lit room and being closed again. "He came into my ward with a wound that should have killed him and a silence that nearly did. He'd written reports. Reports about Major Renwick's superior—Colonel Ashworth, if you want the name; you'll find it eventually, you're that sort—about what Ashworth was doing with army stores and army pay, and about the men who'd signed things they shouldn't have signed, some of them boys who didn't know what they were signing. He gave the reports to Renwick to pass up the chain, because that was the proper channel, because Alistair Marlowe was raised to believe in channels." Her mouth curved without warmth. "And Renwick passed them down instead. To Ashworth. Who began, very politely, to ruin him."

"How?"

"The way ruin is always done. Slowly. An accusation of drunkenness entered in his file. A gambling debt manufactured and then forgiven, so that the forgiveness was itself a debt. A suggestion that his sister's politics made him a security risk—which, in 1912, was nearly true enough to be fatal. By spring, Alistair understood that he would not be ruined and restored; he would be ruined and buried. And buried meant Malta, meant a posting designed to kill, meant a line ruled through in someone else's hand." She drank. "So I ruled it first. My hand, my line. Death by fever, certified by me, witnessed by an orderly who owed me more than he could repay. The machinery that would have erased him erased nothing, because I had already done the erasing myself. It is very satisfying," she added, "to beat the empire at clerical work. It is the only contest it notices."

Yara set down her cup. "And the letters? If he was dead, why did letters come?"

"Because dead men may write. They simply cannot post." Vera's eyes flicked to the window, to the roofs, habitually, the way Edith checked a room for listeners. "There was a chaplain—a real one—who agreed to carry sealed packets in his effects when he rotated home on leave, and post them from England. It cost Alistair most of what he had. The censorship stamps you've been puzzling over were applied in Malta before the packets ever travelled; I arranged that too. A dead private's letters get opened. A dead captain's letters, marked examined and filed, sit in a drawer until someone retires and burns them." She paused. "They were never meant to reach your household yet. There was a schedule. Tobias Finch destroyed it when he destroyed nothing else, though I suspect he doesn't know that's what he did."

"He kept them," Yara said. "The letters. He was ordered to burn them and he kept them."

"Yes. I know." Something in Vera's voice changed—the flatness cracking along a seam. "I have spent three years building a dead man out of ink and stamps, Miss Nakamura, and one frightened clerk carried the whole architecture home in a satchel because he couldn't bear to feed a fire with another man's voice. I ought to be furious with him. I have tried. I sat in this chair and composed the fury, item by item, the way I compose everything." She looked at her own hands. "Instead I bought a second cup."

Yara followed her glance. There were indeed two cups on the shelf, mismatched, and the second one was new, its gilt still bright.

* * *

The light shifted while the pot refilled; the roofs outside went from grey to the colour of old pewter, and Vera lit a lamp rather than draw the curtain, as though curtains admitted something worse than darkness.

"Tell me what you thought I was," Vera said. "Before you came. Honestly. I collect honest answers the way other women collect fans."

Yara considered lying, found it would be like lying to a mirror, and didn't. "I thought you'd be harder. Or softer. One of the two. I thought you'd either justify it completely or be drowning in guilt, and I could tell which by how you held the teapot."

"And?"

"You hold it like a nurse holding a thermometer. It's an instrument. You've decided what it's for."

"Mm." Vera seemed pleased, in the way of a teacher whose pupil has answered wrongly in an interesting direction. "And you? What does history say about you, Miss Nakamura? Because I've read the margins of those letters too—the pencil marks, the little tally system. Dates cross-referenced against postal marks. Sums. You keep accounts." She leaned forward. "So answer me as an accountant: was I wrong?"

The question hung there, and Yara let it hang, because she had been turning it over since the night the letters arrived, turning it in the dark of the garage with grease on her hands, and she still hadn't solved it.

"You falsified a record," Yara said slowly. "A death record. Records are how anything survives. If a person can be unwritten by whoever holds the pen, then writing is the only weapon most of us will ever hold, and you—" she stopped, restarted. "You used the weapon. Which means anyone can. Which means the next person might unwrite someone I love."

"Or write them back," Vera said quietly. "That's the other edge. You've noticed it already—I can see it. You seal Edith Marlowe's correspondence every week knowing exactly what's inside. You have committed a hundred small violations of trust in the name of understanding, and you've never once called them violations. You call them reading." She smiled, and it was not unkind, and it was devastating anyway. "I am not your accuser, Miss Nakamura. I am your future, if you're careless. We alter records because we cannot bear the ones the world gives us. The question is only whether you'll do it for love or for fear, and whether you'll ever be able to tell the difference afterward."

Yara's tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

"Alistair stayed hidden," she said. "Three years. His sister mourned him. She built her whole grief into the ground floor of her life, and he let her. That wasn't your doing alone."

"No. That was his. He wanted to surface after eighteen months, when Ashworth was posted to India. I advised against it. Renwick had inherited the file by then, and Renwick's genius is patience—he doesn't destroy inconvenient men, he warehouses them. Alistair surfacing meant an inquiry, and an inquiry meant Vera Calloway of the Queen Alexandra's corps explaining her signature to a board of officers, and it meant thirty soldiers whose names were in those reports becoming witnesses, and witnesses become casualties. He did the sums himself. He was better at them than I was." She looked at the lamp flame. "Grief is a price. We priced it. We paid it out of other people's pockets. I've made my peace with the arithmetic, more or less, on the days when I sleep. What I have not made peace with—" and here her voice dropped, and the lamplight made her face suddenly older, "—is Major Renwick."

"You've met him."

"I've received him. In this room. Eighteen months ago, when he traced the chaplain's route and wanted to know how much I knew and whom I'd told. He sat in that chair—" she nodded at Yara's, and Yara felt the wood go cold beneath her— "and he was perfectly civil. He praised my penmanship. He said that women of my skills were wasted in wards. Then he explained, with great gentleness, that a nurse who falsifies a certificate can be struck off, prosecuted, ruined, at his discretion, forever, whenever it suits him. He didn't ask for anything that day. That's his method. He installs himself. Like a lease." She folded her hands. "Now his file has walked out of a burning cabinet and into a suffragette's house in Hampstead, and he knows it, because Tobias Finch is followed—has been followed since the day he left the War Office, though the poor man has no idea. So hear me, and hear me plainly, because I am going to say the thing your courage won't let you say to Mrs. Marlowe."

"Say it."

"Renwick will not let this stand. Not because Alistair matters—he's a rounding error, a single clerk's conscience in a decade of them—but because the ledger Ashworth kept, the one that proves everything, is the only copy in existence, and Renwick has spent six years convincing himself it doesn't exist. Proof is the one thing he fears, and proof is the one thing your household keeps collecting. He will not send thugs—he never sends thugs, thugs leave traces. He will send letters. Warrants. Inspectors. He will make the law arrive on your doorstep wearing its best coat, and it will be legal, every step of it, and by the time you understand what's happening you'll be answering questions in a room with no windows." She reached to the mantel and picked up a small object Yara had taken for a paperweight: a rubber stamp, worn smooth, its face illegible with age. "This is what I stole when I left Malta. Not jewellery. Not money. A stamp. Because I understood, finally, that the entire empire runs on these, and the difference between a heroine and a criminal is which side of the stamp you stand on." She set it down again, gently. "Renwick stands on the other side, and he has the whole weight of the machine behind his feet. Love each other quickly, Miss Nakamura. Decide quickly. Machines move faster than families."

* * *

Yara left as the church bells across the rooftops were striking seven, and Vera walked her down the three flights with the lamp, which struck Yara as a courtesy, until the last landing, when she understood it was reconnaissance—Vera checking the stairwell, the street door, the angle of the chandler's window—with the same unhurried thoroughness she brought to everything.

At the door, Vera held out her hand again, and this time the grip lingered half a second past convention.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your ledger idea—the dates, the sums—it's good. It's how I survived. Write everything down, in your own hand, in duplicate, and hide the copies apart. Paper is the only witness that doesn't flinch." A ghost of the dry smile. "And Miss Nakamura—one more thing. When Renwick comes, and he will come, he'll offer you something small. A kindness. A concession. He always begins with the least powerful person in the room, because the least powerful person can be bought for almost nothing, and their sale unravels everyone above them." Her eyes were steady and dark in the lamplight. "Whatever he offers you, remember that I sat in that chair, and I listened to his voice for forty minutes, and I still went home and slept. You'll want to believe you're stronger than I was. Don't. Just decide now, tonight, in advance, what you won't sell. Deciding later is how people disappear."

The street door closed behind her, and Yara stood a moment on the step, breathing air that tasted of coal and river.

Then she heard, above her, faint through the glass of the third-floor window, the sound of a drawer being unlocked—slowly, deliberately, the way one opens a drawer one expects someday to need—and she turned up her collar and went to find a cab, and the whole way home she composed the entry she would make that night in the ledger, and could not finish it, because every version ended with the same unfinished clause:

*If he offers me nothing, it means he thinks I've already been bought.*

She got out at the corner of the mews and saw, before she even reached the gate, that the Daimler had a visitor: a black Humber idling across the lane with its lamps unlit, and a man in a good coat leaning against the Marlowe garage wall as though waiting for a friend, smoking a cigarette down to the wire.

He tipped his hat to her as she passed.

He did not leave.
