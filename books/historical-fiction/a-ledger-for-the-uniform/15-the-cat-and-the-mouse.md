## 15. The Cat and the Mouse

The first time they released Edith Marlowe from Holloway, it was raining, and Yara learned what a woman looked like when the state had finished using her body as an argument.

She stood at the prison gates under a grey umbrella of sky, the Daimler idling behind her, and watched the door open on its hinge of iron and bureaucracy. Edith came out alone—no wardress to see her off, no escort, just the governor's clerk with his ledger of releases, ticking her name into freedom as though freedom were an item that had been out on loan. She wore the same clothes she'd been arrested in eleven days before, and they hung on her now like laundry left out in weather. Her collar sat wrong against her neck because there was less neck than there had been. Her hands, always so certain on paper, trembled around a small brown paper parcel containing everything she owned at present: a hairbrush, a prayer book she never prayed with, and the stub of a pencil the authorities had overlooked.

"Miss Marlowe," Yara said, and opened the car door, and did not trust herself to say anything else.

Edith paused on the step. Up close, in the flat light, Yara could see the work the hunger strike had done—the hollowing at the cheeks, the skin gone translucent over the cheekbones, the eyes too bright, burning on some fuel that was no longer food. Eleven days without eating. Four of them without water, until the force-feeding began, until the tube, until the screaming Yara had only ever heard described secondhand by women who spoke about it in the tone people used for weather in foreign countries: factual, distant, untranslatable.

"You've been waiting," Edith said. It was not gratitude. It was observation, delivered in the voice of a woman taking inventory of what still worked.

"Since six o'clock."

"It's half past nine."

"Yes, miss."

Edith looked at her for a moment longer, and something moved behind the brightness—not softness exactly, but recognition, the acknowledgment of one stubborn thing meeting another. Then she folded herself into the passenger seat with the economy of a woman who had learned, in eleven days, not to waste movement, and said, "Home. No—the office first. There are letters."

Yara closed the door on her and walked around to the driver's side, and in those four steps she allowed herself one breath that shook, because Edith must never see it shake. Then she took the wheel, and the Daimler pulled away from Holloway's gates, and behind them the prison resumed its business of swallowing women.

* * *

The Cat and Mouse Act had passed in April, and the newspapers had treated it almost as a kindness. The Prisoners' Temporary Discharge for Ill-Health Act, they called it, in the way that men name things to make them sound like medicine. A suffragist on hunger strike would be released when her health failed; she would recover at liberty; and then, when she was strong again, she would be rearrested to serve out the balance of her sentence. The cat, the cartoonists explained with a chuckle, playing with the mouse. Letting it run a little. Catching it again.

No one in the papers asked who would drive the mouse.

That was the question Yara found herself answering, day after day through that wet spring and into summer, with her hands on the wheel and her foot on the clutch. Because the Act had a flaw the Home Office had not anticipated, or had anticipated and dismissed as beneath consideration: a released prisoner was free until rearrested, and rearrest required finding her, and finding her required that she stand still long enough to be caught. The women learned to run. And running, in 1913, meant motors—and motors meant chauffeurs.

Yara became, without ceremony or discussion, part of the machinery of evasion.

It happened the second morning after the release. Edith had slept twelve hours, eaten two eggs and half a slice of toast, argued correspondence until midnight, and risen at dawn looking—if not restored—at least reassembled, the way a chair is reassembled after mending: recognizably itself, but you knew where the breaks had been. She was at the hall table signing letters when Yara brought round the car, and there was a knock at the front door, and through the frosted glass came the silhouette of a bowler hat.

"The police," said Mrs. Pankhurst's girl, who had arrived on her bicycle ten minutes earlier with a warning that was already out of date. "Two of them. They have the warrant."

What followed Yara would later enter in her ledger in her smallest, most careful hand, because it seemed to her then—and seemed to her still—that history had happened in her garage, between the axle grease and the spare tyres, and nobody had thought to write it down anywhere but here:

*April 24th. Rearrest warrant served, Marlowe, E., Holloway sentence, balance outstanding. Subject removed via service entrance, 8:41 a.m., concealed beneath motor blanket in rear of Daimler, 20/25 h.p., registration mark [she gave the number, then struck it through—some entries even a ledger should not keep]. Driven to Hampstead address of Mrs. Whitcombe. Warrant officers searched premises, King Street, 9:15–10:02 a.m. Found: empty house, cold kettle.*

The police had knocked; the household had answered with silence and a made bed. Edith had gone out through the coal cellar and into the back of the Daimler while Yara stood at the front door being polite to two constables who kept looking past her at the staircase, at the coat rack, at all the places where a gentlewoman might plausibly be, because neither of them—not once—looked at the car. Or rather: they looked *through* the car, at the chauffeur standing beside it, and saw only what the uniform told them to see. A servant. Machinery with a cap on.

"Morning," said the older one, touching his helmet brim. "Is your mistress at home?"

"I believe she went out, sir."

"Know where she went?"

"No, sir." This was true, in the sense that all true things were true: she did not know where Edith went, because Edith had not yet been driven anywhere, because Edith was at this moment curled under a travelling rug three feet from this man's elbow, breathing through her mouth to keep the cough down.

He nodded, satisfied, and wrote something in his own notebook—everyone kept ledgers now, Yara thought, the whole empire ran on them—and the constables went up the steps and rang the bell and were admitted by the maid into a house that was already a stage set, and Yara turned the crank, and the Daimler coughed once and caught, and drove out of the mews at a decorous twelve miles an hour with its cargo silent under the rug.

At the top of the road, when they were clear, a voice emerged from the rugs, slightly muffled, entirely composed:

"You might have told me you were going to drive so quickly over that last set of cobbles."

"My apologies, miss."

"Don't apologize. Well done." A pause. The rug shifted. "Though if I am to travel as freight, I would request in future that you inform me before cornering. Freight is entitled to notice."

And Yara, watching the road, felt something crack open in her chest that was either laughter or grief, and suspected it was both, and drove on.

* * *

They developed a system, because systems were how you survived. Yara mapped the city in her head the way she had once memorized the letters in Edith's desk: safe houses became points on a circuit, each with its signals—a flowerpot on a windowsill, a curtain drawn to a particular width, a lamp lit or unlit. She learned which streets had constables posted after dark and which magistrates' courts sat on which days, and she learned the rhythm of the Act itself, its terrible pendulum: arrest, refusal of food, eight days, ten days, twelve, the doctors' notes growing urgent, the discharge, the recovery, the warrant, the knock at the door. Twelve days free, roughly. Sometimes nine. Never enough.

She kept it all in the ledger, which had grown from a curiosity into a chronicle. *May 3rd. Released, Holloway. Weight reported lost: 1 st. 4 lb.* *May 11th. Rearrest attempted, Victoria station. Evaded. Miss M. distressed; hands shaking; ate nothing at luncheon though food offered twice.* *May 19th. Recaptured, Kensington. Third feeding ordered.* She recorded the dates the way she recorded petrol consumption and tyre wear, because numbers were how she understood devotion, and because if she wrote it all down—every gate, every tube, every hour spent idling outside a hospital window—then it was accounted for, and if it was accounted for then it had weight, and if it had weight then someone, someday, would have to reckon with it.

Tobias helped when he could. He had taken lodgings two streets over and appeared most mornings with bread he had queued for since five, and he had a clerk's genius for paperwork: he could forge a doctor's certificate well enough to buy Edith forty-eight hours, could copy a railway timetable into a plausible itinerary that sent the warrant officers chasing to Brighton while Edith lay in a attic in St John's Wood. But it was Yara's hands on the wheel, night after night, that actually moved the mouse ahead of the cat.

She grew thin herself that spring, though no one starved her but arithmetic. She slept in snatches. She learned to eat a full meal in four minutes standing at the kitchen table, because four minutes was what the schedule allowed. Her hands acquired new skills: changing a tyre in the dark in under six minutes; reading a street by lamplight at speed; holding a woman's forehead steady while she was sick from the motion of the car after a force-feeding, saying nothing, because nothing was the correct thing to say.

Once—only once—Edith wept in the car.

It was in June, on the way back from a meeting in Croydon that had been broken up, and Edith had been free for six days and knew the clock was running, and somewhere south of Norbury she put her hand over her eyes and cried without sound, the tears coming down between her fingers in a straight line, and Yara drove and watched the road and did not look, because not-looking was also a form of care, perhaps the purest kind available to a chauffeur.

"I'm sorry," Edith said finally. "You didn't sign up for this."

"No, miss."

"When you came to me, I offered you wages and a uniform and a roof. Not—" she gestured vaguely, taking in the whole year, the warrants, the cell, the tube, the running. "Not this."

Yara considered her answer with more care than she had given any sentence in her life. The truth was layered: that she had come to Edith at sixteen with nothing, disowned and landed, and been taken in; that the uniform was the first thing she had ever owned that fit; that she had read every letter in that house and sealed them again and called it loyalty when it was really loneliness wearing loyalty's coat; that somewhere across the last months the distinction had dissolved, and she no longer read the mail secretly, because there was no longer any *her* apart from the household to keep secrets from.

"You underpaid me anyway," she said.

There was a silence. Then Edith laughed—a real laugh, cracked and rusty, the laugh of a convalescent—and it filled the car the way sun fills a room, briefly, moving on, and Yara held the wheel and let it wash over her and thought: *this. Whatever this costs. This.*

* * *

The fourth release came in July, and it was different, because Edith was worse.

Twelve days inside, seven of them fed by tube, and the governor's medical officer had signed the discharge order with visible reluctance, as though releasing her were a defeat in a war he intended to win by attrition. When Yara collected her at the gates, Edith could not manage the step into the car unaided. Yara took her arm and felt through the sleeve how little of her remained—an arm like a bundle of kindling wrapped in cloth—and guided her in, and Edith sat with her head back and her eyes closed and said, "Home," in a voice sanded down to the grain.

At home she slept for two days. On the third day the bowler hats came back.

Yara saw them first from the kitchen window—two men across the road, standing by the railings with the studied idleness of men who have been told to stand somewhere, smoking and pretending to read a newspaper that had gone soft in the damp. Surveillance, casual and permanent. The Act's other face: the mouse released, but watched, so that recovery could not happen in private, so that the moment she was strong enough to run she could be caught again, the wheel turning, turning.

"They're waiting for me to get better," Edith said, when Yara reported it. She was propped in the morning room among her correspondence, working, always working, a blanket over her knees like an admission of weakness she refused to otherwise make. "That's the elegance of it. They don't have to break me. They only have to wait for me to mend, and then collect me, and let me start starving again, forever. It's quite brilliant. I'd admire it if I weren't the mouse." She signed a letter. Her hand wobbled; she steadied it against the blotter and continued as though it hadn't.

"Miss," Yara said. "You should rest."

"I am resting. This is resting. Do you know what I did in Holloway? I rested. I rested so thoroughly I forgot the names of half my correspondents." She looked up, and her eyes had that brightness again, that terrible borrowed light. "Yara. Sit down."

Yara sat. It was the first time Edith had ever asked.

"I want to say something before they come again, because next time I may not be in a state to say it, and because it ought to be said by someone sober and starving, which is the only honest condition I have." Edith folded her hands on the letters. "I have treated you as staff because treating you as family would have been a liberty I had no right to take. You came to me with nothing, and I gave you a roof, and I have never once asked myself whether what I gave was enough, because asking would have obliged me to answer, and the answer is no. It was shelter, not belonging. I know the difference. I have watched you know it too, every day, politely, for years."

"Miss Marlowe—"

"Let me finish; I'm rationing my strength." A ghost of the old dry smile. "What I mean is this. You have driven me through police cordons and hidden me under motor blankets and lied to constables with a straight face that I could not better myself, and you have done it without being asked, without thanks, without any claim on me at all. And I want you to understand that I see it. That it is not invisible to me. That whatever happens—whether they take me tomorrow or next month or never—you are not staff in this house. You are what this house is *for*. All of this—" she touched the letters, the movement, the whole ragged enterprise—"is only the argument. You are the point."

Yara did not trust her voice. She looked at the carpet, at the worn place by the hearth where she had knelt a thousand mornings to lay the fire, and she thought about her father in Yokohama who had written her out of his life in four lines, and she thought: *he disowned one daughter. She has just adopted one. Both transactions took less than a minute. Only one of them is real.*

"Thank you, miss," she managed.

"That's all," Edith said, already reaching for the next letter, the audience concluded, the fortress of composure rebuilt brick by brick in the space of a breath. "Send the post. And Yara—" she did not look up, "—the car will need petrol. Tomorrow may be busy."

Tomorrow was busy. Tomorrow the men across the road were joined by a third, and a plain motorcar parked at the corner with a man in it who smoked forty cigarettes a day, and Yara, loading the luggage boot with Edith's emergency kit—the change of clothes, the bread, the flask of beef tea, the forged certificates in Tobias's beautiful clerk's hand—understood that the net was closing by inches, that this release would be shorter than the others, that the pendulum was swinging faster.

That evening Vera Calloway's note arrived, unsigned as ever, tucked into a prescription envelope at the chemist's: *Renwick has asked the Commissioner personally for standing surveillance authority. He means to catch you in the act of harbouring, not merely arrest her. Be careful whom you trust at the gates.*

Yara read it twice, burned it over the kitchen sink, and stood a long while watching the ash spiral down the drain.

Then she went out to the garage, and checked the petrol, and checked it again, and laid out Edith's coat over the back seat where a coat was nothing but a coat, and waited for the knock she knew was coming, counting the hours the way she counted everything now—in ledger entries, in miles, in days of borrowed freedom remaining.

*July 14th,* she wrote that night, in the small careful hand. *Released 6 days. Recovering. Watched. Next rearrest expected within 48 hours. Fuel: full. Routes: three. Courage:*

She stopped. The pen hovered. There was no column for courage; she had never found the unit of measure.

She wrote instead: *Sufficient. Barely. Entry continues.*

And downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, came the sound of Edith's typewriter, still going, still going, keeping its own reckoning against the dark.
