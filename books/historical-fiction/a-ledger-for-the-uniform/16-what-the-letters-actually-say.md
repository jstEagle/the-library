## 16. What the Letters Actually Say

The dining room at Marlowe House had not been used for dinner since the spring, when Edith had declared that formality was a tax levied by the dead upon the living and refused to pay it any longer. The long mahogany table stood bare of linen, its surface scarred by candle wax and the rings of a hundred teacups, and it was here—under the one lamp they could afford to burn without guilt—that Yara laid out the correspondence in order, as she might lay out accounts.

Thirty-one letters. She had numbered them herself, in her small precise hand, on slips of paper folded beneath each one. The earliest bore a date from October of 1912; the most recent was postmarked Malta only four months past. Between them stretched a history that officially did not exist: a dead man writing letters to a living woman, a death notice forged, a name struck from the rolls of the Mediterranean Expeditionary Force with the tidy efficiency of an ink blotter.

Edith sat at the head of the table. She had dressed for the occasion—not for mourning, Yara noted, but for work. Her grey wool dress was buttoned to the throat, her hair pinned back severely, and before her lay a sheet of foolscap ruled into columns. Whatever she intended, she intended to take minutes.

Tobias sat opposite her, his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he had not touched. He still moved like a man negotiating with his own body, each gesture costing something, but tonight there was a steadiness in him that had not been there when he first appeared at the service entrance, soaked through and shaking. He had shaved. Someone—he would not say who, but Yara suspected Vera Calloway's landlady—had lent him a collar that nearly fit.

Vera herself had been invited and had declined, in a note of two lines that managed to be both courteous and absolute. *I will hear what is said secondhand. Some doors I have already closed behind me.* Yara understood. There were things a forger could not bear to witness: the reading aloud of what her forgery had purchased.

"Begin," Edith said.

Yara drew the first letter toward her and unfolded it along its original creases. Alistair Marlowe's handwriting leapt up at her from the page—that confident, slightly leftward slant she had seen on three years' worth of household memoranda, shopping instructions, a note once pinned to the pantry door reading *Miss Nakamura is not to be overworked, see to it.* A dead man's hand, warm and familiar and impossible.

She read aloud.

*My dearest Edith—if you are reading this, then someone has disobeyed me thoroughly, and I find I am glad of it.*

Edith made a small sound, quickly suppressed. Yara kept her voice level and went on.

*You will want to know first why I am alive, so I will give you the facts plainly, in the manner you always demanded of me and I so rarely supplied. On the ninth of March, 1911, I did not die at Gallipoli. I was reported to have died, and the report was accurate in every particular except the essential one.*

*Here are the facts. Captain Hargreaves-Moore, our commanding officer, has for two years run a private trade in the discharge papers of enlisted men—selling exemptions, altering medical records, and holding over the heads of perhaps forty soldiers a set of debts, some financial and some of a nature I will not commit to paper even now. Among those forty are men whose families I know. Men who wrote to their mothers from hospital beds while Hargreaves-Moore sold their discharges out from under them to men who wished never to have served at all.*

*I found the ledger. That was my error and I do not retract it. He gave me a choice: face a court-martial for the theft of documents—which he would have arranged, and arranged convincingly—or die properly. He preferred the second. It was tidier. A dead lieutenant asks no questions, and my family name carries enough weight that no one at the War Office would think to look twice.*

Yara paused. In the silence, the mantel clock counted off eleven strokes of ten o'clock, though it was nearer midnight; the clock had been wrong since February and no one corrected it, as though time in this house were a courtesy rather than a fact.

"He chose this." Edith's voice came out stripped of all its polish. "He *chose* it."

"He chose to protect forty men," Tobias said quietly. "Read on, Miss Nakamura. It gets worse before it gets better. Most true accounts do."

* * *

They read until two in the morning, taking turns—Yara, then Tobias, then Yara again, because Edith discovered somewhere around the fourth letter that she could not trust her own voice to hold, and rather than surrender the room to weeping she surrendered the pages instead. This was, Yara thought, its own kind of bravery: Edith Marlowe, who had addressed crowds of ten thousand without a tremor, handing over the letters about her brother because her brother's sentences undid her.

And the letters undid her thoroughly, because they were not what any of them had expected.

Yara had prepared herself, privately, for a story of cowardice or disgrace—the desertion chapter plan she had built in her own head, the dishonorable dead man. What emerged instead was something far more difficult to forgive or condemn: a man who had spent three years erasing himself, letter by letter, so that other people could go on existing in full.

*Hargreaves-Moore believes I am dead, which means I must remain dead, which means every kindness you might send me becomes a weapon pointed at the men in that ledger,* Alistair wrote in the winter of 1911. *Do not search for me. Do not grieve publicly beyond what is seemly—I know how much I ask, asking you to perform grief, you who never performed anything in your life. But if you rage, if you demand inquiries, if you make my death a question rather than a fact, he will begin to wonder whether the fact holds. And the first place a frightened man looks is at whoever benefited from the fiction. Vera Calloway signed my death notice with her own hand. If I fall, she hangs. If I speak, she hangs. So I keep silent, and I ask you to keep silent, and I know that of the two of us you were always the one better at silence, being constitutionally incapable of it.*

Tobias laughed—a short, startled bark of laughter—and pressed his hand to his mouth.

"You knew him," Edith said. Not a question.

"I clerked for the field hospital outside Valletta for eight months." Tobias looked down at his untouched tea. "He came in with dysentery the first week I was posted. Should have died; the ward expected it. He spent the whole fever dictating letters to men in the next beds who couldn't write—letters home, letters to the pay office, one truly magnificent complaint to the Admiralty about the coffee. He'd dictate lying flat with his eyes shut like a man conducting business from a coffin." His voice caught. "When I told him later what Renwick asked me to do—to burn the file, all of it—he said, 'Then you'll be the last man alive who knows the story, Finch. Mind you tell it badly. Badly told stories get argued with.'"

Edith turned her face away, toward the dark window where the garden was a rumor of black against blacker black. When she turned back, her eyes were dry and bright and terrible.

"Go on," she said. "All of it. Every word. I have spent three years mourning a lie, and I intend to audit it to the last farthing."

* * *

It was the eleventh letter that broke the night open—the letter dated June of 1913, written from a convalescent ward in Sliema, in handwriting gone thinner than the hand Yara remembered from pantry notes but still unmistakably, stubbornly his.

*The ledger is copied. Forty-one names, forty-one sums, forty-one dates, in Vera's fair hand and mine both, and lodged in three places you need not know. Here is what matters: those names are not names to me. Private Ellis Dunning, nineteen, whose mother scrubs steps in Portsmouth—his discharge was sold for thirty pounds while he lay with a smashed knee in our own ward. Sergeant Okafor, who took a Turkish bullet through the shoulder and was marked "deserted" so his pension could be redirected. I could recite them all. Some nights I do, like a litany, like a prayer I am not entitled to.*

*You will ask why I do not simply publish. You will be right to ask; you have never met a secret you didn't try to vote into the open. But publication kills the witnesses, Edie. It does not kill Hargreaves-Moore—he has friends above my reach—but it kills Vera, it kills the forty-one, it hands him the broom to sweep the floor clean. So the ledger waits, and I wait, and the waiting is the hardest soldiering I have ever done. I was trained to advance. Nobody trains you to hold a position made entirely of your own absence.*

*Tell me about your marchers. Tell me about the women. I read the papers they let me have. When they write about your movement, half in mockery, I think: there is my sister, being history again, and here am I, hiding inside a filing cabinet. Perhaps between us we make one whole person.*

Yara stopped reading. She had to. Not because her voice failed—her voice was the steadiest thing in the room—but because Edith had risen from her chair without any apparent decision to rise, and stood with both palms flat on the scarred table, looking down at the pages as though they might rearrange themselves under scrutiny into something survivable.

"Being history again," Edith repeated. "He wrote that. He sat in a fever-ward in Malta thinking of me as—" She stopped. Started again. "I spent three years being ashamed of how I mourned him. Do you understand? I mourned him *inefficiently.* I kept his study locked and told people it was damp. I could not say his name at meetings. And all the while he was alive and *thinking of me*, and protecting strangers, and asking nothing—" Her hand came down on the table, not a blow, more a settling, like a bird landing. "Not nothing. He asked one thing. Silence. And God forgive me, I nearly bought a berth to Canada and sailed away from the whole question."

"You didn't know," Tobias said.

"I knew *something*. Sisters know. I knew his last letter to me, the real one, from 1910, was wrong somehow—too cheerful, too finished, a letter written by a man closing accounts." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Yara had heard from her yet. "Closing accounts. Even my metaphors are bookkeeping now. Miss Nakamura, you've infected this house."

Yara gathered the letters back into their numbered order, and her hands, for once, were not entirely steady either. Because she had heard it too—had heard it from the first page—what none of the others seemed to have said aloud yet: that Alistair Marlowe had done the very thing she herself did every morning at the service entrance. He had read what he should not. He had copied what he was meant to destroy. He had kept a ledger of other people's crimes in the belief that records, rightly kept, were a kind of armor.

*History happens elsewhere,* she had written in her own creed, her own private ledger, months ago. And here was a man who had learned the opposite lesson at the cost of everything: history happens exactly here, in hospital wards and service entrances and coal cellars, to whoever is standing nearest and willing to write it down.

"The forty-one," she said. Everyone turned. She had not meant to speak; the words arrived anyway, in her careful, accented English. "The men in the ledger. They are alive. Some of them. They are in England, perhaps. They do not know why Lieutenant Marlowe died for them."

"No," Edith said slowly.

"Then he is still absent for nothing." Yara laid her palm flat beside the eleventh letter. "Or—for them. Which is not nothing. But it is a thing chosen *for* him, by Major Renwick, by Captain Hargreaves-Moore, by everyone who agreed to the fiction. He has been dead three years because other people filed the paperwork. I think—" she searched for it, the thought arriving whole, like a train pulling into a station she hadn't known she was waiting at—"I think he would prefer to file his own papers now."

Silence. Then Tobias, softly: "Badly told stories get argued with."

Edith looked from one to the other of them—the clerk who kept copies, the driver who read sealed mail, the sister who had performed grief for a living man—and something moved across her face that Yara had no word for in either of her languages. Grief, transmuting, in real time, into something harder and hotter. Pride, maybe. Or ammunition.

"Yes," Edith said. She sat down again and pulled the foolscap toward her, and began, in her swift secretary's hand, to rule new columns. "Yes. We shall want copies. We shall want them made carefully, in several places, by people who cannot easily be arrested." She glanced up, and for a moment the marcher and the mourner and the sister were all one woman, and she was smiling. "Miss Nakamura. You read everything anyway. You may as well start copying."

Outside, the first grey suggestion of dawn touched the rooftops of Hampstead. Inside, a dead man's words lay spread across a scarred table, forty-one names strong, and the household that had gathered around them was no longer a household at all.

It was a committee. It had an archive. And somewhere across the city, a man in a good coat was about to discover that the dead keep much better books than the living.

Yara reached for a fresh sheet and began to write.
