## 17. Renwick's Card

The card arrived on a Thursday, delivered not by post but by hand—a boy of perhaps fourteen in a livery too large for him, who stood at the service door holding it out on a small silver tray as though he had been drilled for the gesture and was terrified of getting it wrong. Yara took the card from the tray, read it, and felt the temperature of the morning change.

*Major C. E. Renwick. War Office. At your convenience.*

No address beyond Whitehall. No message. No time requested. Just the name, printed in heavy black type on cream stock, and beneath it, in ink that had dried with the slight tremor of a hurried hand: *Regarding the Marlowe correspondence.*

Yara stood in the doorway long after the boy had gone, the card pinched between her fingers like something that might bite. The garage smelled of petrol and cold iron behind her; the Daimler sat with its bonnet up, waiting for her hands to return to it. She did not return to it. She carried the card through the scullery, past Mrs. Hobbes's domain where the kettle had just begun its low complaint, up the back stairs and along the corridor to Edith's study, where Edith sat at her desk surrounded by the morning's campaign letters, reading glasses pushed up into her greying hair, a pencil held between her teeth like a woman chewing on her own patience.

Edith read the card once. Her face did nothing at all.

"So," she said. "He's come to us at last."

"You know him?" Yara asked, though she already knew the answer. She had spent three weeks now inside this mystery—the ledger open on her lap each night, its columns growing fat with dates and postal marks and the names of clerks—and Major Renwick's name appeared in it eleven times. Eleven times in Tobias's halting account, eleven times in the margins of the Malta letters' routing, eleven times like a stain that would not lift.

"I know *of* him," Edith said. She set the pencil down and took off her glasses, folding them with the deliberate care she used when she wanted the world to believe she was calm. "Alistair spoke of him once, in his last letter before Gallipoli. 'Renwick keeps everything,' he wrote. I thought it was praise." She looked out the window toward the street, where the plane trees were dropping their first bronze leaves onto the pavement. "It wasn't."

"Shall I tell him he may call?"

"He'll call whether we permit it or not. Men like Renwick don't request entry, Yara—they announce it." Edith turned the card over in her fingers. "Thursday afternoon. Let him come while the light is still good. I want to see his face clearly."

* * *

Yara spent the intervening hours doing what she always did when fear arrived without an appointment: she worked. She cleaned the windscreen until it squeaked. She checked the tyre pressures twice, though they had been fine the first time. She re-greased the wheel bearings that did not need re-greasing, because her hands needed occupation or they would shake, and shaking hands were a luxury she could not afford—not here, not in this house where competence was the only currency she had ever been certain of carrying.

Tobias found her at half past two, crouched at the rear axle with a rag in her fist.

"He's here," Tobias said quietly. He had taken up a kind of station-keeping in the household since his arrival—drifting between the kitchen, the garden, the coal cellar where the archive now slept behind its false wall—always useful, never quite belonging, a man practising how to be a person again. His hands, Yara noticed, had begun to steady themselves these last weeks. Now they trembled again, and he pressed them flat against his thighs to hide it. "At the front door. Cook let him in."

"Is Edith—"

"Dressed for battle." A ghost of a smile crossed his tired face. "She asked me to fetch you. Said you'd want to hear it yourself." He hesitated. "She said you'd want to *write* it yourself."

Yara set down the rag. In her apron pocket, always, was the small notebook—the ledger's field companion, its portable twin. She touched it once, the way another woman might touch a locket, and followed him inside.

* * *

They did not put him in the drawing room. Edith received him in the study, among the papers, because she wanted him to understand whose territory this was. Yara stationed herself by the window with the pretext of adjusting a blind, close enough to see everything, far enough to be furniture. Tobias remained in the corridor, within earshot of the open door. It was arranged without a word being spoken about arrangements, the way things in that house had begun to be arranged—as though the household had developed, without anyone announcing it, a collective instinct for siege.

Major Charles Edward Renwick was not what Yara had expected, which was itself a lesson. She had built him in her mind from the machinery he represented: a man made of filing cabinets, of rubber stamps, of red tape wound tight enough to strangle. What entered the study instead was a broad, comfortable-looking man of perhaps fifty, silver-haired, with a ruddy outdoor complexion and the easy bearing of someone who hunted grouse and was generous at Christmas. He wore civilian clothes—a good tweed coat, well-cut trousers, shoes polished to a soft military gleam—and he carried his hat in one hand and a walking stick in the other, though Yara doubted he needed either for support.

"Fear arrives wearing a good coat," she thought, and the phrase settled into her ledger's margin even as he crossed the room.

Mrs. Marlowe," he said, extending his free hand. His voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it. "Charles Renwick. Forgive the intrusion. Your brother spoke of you often—I feel as though I know you already."

"Alistair is dead, Major." Edith did not take his hand. "You'll forgive me if I don't pretend otherwise."

Something flickered behind Renwick's pleasant eyes—appraisal, recalibration—but his manner did not shift by a degree. "Of course. Forgive me." He seated himself in the chair Edith indicated, crossing one ankle over his knee with the ease of long habit. "I'll come directly to the purpose of my visit, since I suspect your household values directness." His gaze moved briefly—briefly—to Yara at the window, then returned. "There is a matter of correspondence. Letters, Mrs. Marlowe. Letters that ought, by rights, to have been destroyed."

"Letters are difficult things to destroy," Edith said mildly. "They have a way of surviving their authors. Ask any historian."

"Or any widow," Renwick agreed pleasantly, and Yara saw Edith's spine stiffen by a fraction. "I'm not here to accuse anyone of anything. I want to be very clear about that. Whatever misunderstandings exist—and I understand there has been some distress regarding Captain Marlowe's service record—that distress arises from paperwork, and paperwork can be corrected. Amended. Closed." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners. "That is all I've come to offer. Closure."

"The closure of a file," Edith said, "or the closing of mouths?"

"Mrs. Marlowe." He leaned forward slightly, and the warmth drained from his voice so smoothly, so completely, that it might never have been there at all. What remained was not cruelty—it was worse than cruelty. It was administration. "Your sister-in-law's charity work. The WSPU subscriptions paid from accounts that were not strictly yours to draw upon. The incident at the warehouse in Limehouse in March—witnesses can be produced, or witnesses can become confused. Your driving companion's immigration status—" and here his eyes did move to Yara, deliberately now, resting on her with the mild weight of a man examining a horse's teeth, "—which I understand is somewhat irregular, given certain circumstances surrounding her father's business failures in Yokohama. Papers go missing, Mrs. Marlowe. People go missing. Records, however, endure. Records are my profession." He settled back into the chair. "I should hate to see yours become complicated."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted perhaps four seconds. Yara counted them, because counting was what she did, because numbers were the solid ground beneath everything else that swayed. Four seconds in which she watched Edith Marlowe, whom she had served for six years, whom she had driven through jeering crowds and past broken windows and once, memorably, through a police cordon with the tyres screaming, sit absolutely still in her own study while a man in a good coat dismantled her life item by item, politely, the way an accountant reads out a bill.

Then Edith laughed.

It was not a large laugh—a short, dry exhalation, almost a cough—but it filled the room more thoroughly than shouting would have done.

"Complicated," she repeated. "You rehearsed that word. I can tell. You've used it before—in other studies, with other families. You sit in their chairs and you drink their tea and you recite their sins back to them in that reasonable voice, and then you leave, and they spend the rest of their lives looking over their shoulders." She rose from behind her desk and stood looking down at him, small and grey-haired and utterly unintimidated. "My record, Major Renwick, is already complicated. I am, at this moment, liable to arrest under a statute that treats me as a cat treats a mouse. I have been force-fed twice. I expect to be again. There is nothing you can add to my file that the Home Office hasn't already stamped, filed, and celebrated." She folded her arms. "So let us dispense with the theatre. You are afraid of what Alistair's letters contain. You burned the original file rather than risk it, and now you discover copies exist, and you cannot burn those without knowing where they sleep. That is why you're here. Not for closure. For inventory."

For the first time, Major Renwick's composure moved. Not broke—moved, the way a still pond moves when a fish turns beneath the surface. He uncrossed his legs. He placed his hat carefully on his knee.

"You mistake me," he said quietly. "I serve the Crown, Mrs. Marlowe. The Crown requires stability. A dead captain resurrected by paperwork, a nurse who falsified documents, an officer's conduct called into question three years after the fact—all of it would serve no one. Least of all the men who died believing their army was worth dying for." He rose, retrieving his stick, and settled his coat across his shoulders. "You are a woman of principle. I respect that. Principles, however, are expensive. I would consider whether this one can be afforded—by you, or by those under your roof." His gaze touched Yara again, briefly, at the door. "Some of them have further to fall than others."

He paused at the threshold and turned, and for a moment the pleasant mask slipped entirely, and Yara saw the thing underneath: not anger, but a kind of weary, bureaucratic patience, the patience of a man who had erased many files and knew that erasure was simply a matter of time.

"The boy who kept the letters," Renwick said. "Finch. He was meant to be transferred to a posting in India in August. The orders went astray. They can be un-lost, Mrs. Marlowe—or he can be found. Deserter, thief, malingerer—the charges write themselves. I shouldn't have to write them at all." He fitted his hat to his head. "Good day."

The front door closed with a soft, civilized click.

* * *

For a long moment no one moved. Then Edith sank into her chair, and Yara saw—for only a second, before the mask came down again—the cost of what the performance had extracted. Her hands, folded on the desk blotter, were not quite steady.

"He knows about the cellar," Tobias said from the doorway. His voice was thin. "Not the cellar itself—he doesn't know where—but he knows the letters survive. And he knows about me." He laughed, a hollow sound with nothing in it. "India. He lost my transfer orders himself, didn't he? I was never meant to reach Bombay. I was meant to disappear somewhere between here and the docks, and no one would ever ask why, because no one asks after a clerk."

"No one is disappearing," Edith said, and her voice came back to her like a blade returning to its sheath. "Sit down, both of you. We are going to think."

But Yara was not listening. She had opened her notebook, and her pen was moving, fast and precise, recording every word Renwick had spoken, every inflection, every threat dressed as concern. *Witnesses can be produced. Records endure. Some of them have further to fall.* When she reached the final line she stopped, and read it back, and understood suddenly—with the cold clarity that comes after fear passes through and leaves its outline behind—what Renwick had actually told them.

He had not come to threaten Edith primarily. Edith was already ruined in the eyes of the state; threatening her was merely courtesy. He had come to threaten *them*—the driver, the clerk, the people with irregular papers and lost transfer orders, the ones who could vanish without a newspaper noticing. He had come to make her look at him and understand that history did not happen elsewhere. It happened in studies, on Thursdays, to women standing by windows pretending to adjust blinds.

She looked up. Edith was watching her over the desk, and something passed between them—recognition, perhaps, or resolve.

"He gave us a gift," Yara said quietly.

Edith raised an eyebrow. "Did he."

"He told us his timetable." Yara turned the notebook around so Edith could read the last line. "'The orders went astray. They can be un-lost.' Un-lost means issued. Issued takes signatures. Signatures take days." She closed the notebook. "We have less time than we thought. But we have more than none."

From the hallway came the sound of Tobias rising slowly from his chair, and above the house the afternoon light was beginning to fail, and somewhere in Whitehall a file was already moving through the machine, gathering stamps like a stone gathers moss, and Yara Nakamura—who had believed once that history happened to other people—began, with a steady hand, to draft the next column of the ledger.

*What must be decided before the orders are un-lost:* she wrote. *And who.*

Outside, the first drops of October rain struck the window, patient as bureaucracy, and did not stop.
