## 18. The Berth

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope the colour of weak tea, and Yara read it twice before she sealed it.

*Canadian Pacific Steamship Company, Liverpool. Re: Standing Arrangement, Account No. 1147-M. We write to confirm that the berth reserved under the above account remains held for the season ending 31st October next, after which date it must be either taken or released...*

She stood in the morning room with the letter flat on her palm and the sealing wax warming between her fingers, and she understood three things at once, the way one understands a room's contents in a single glance: that Edith Marlowe had been paying for this for years; that whatever the arrangement was, it had a deadline now, October, which was closer than October ever seemed when you were not counting toward it; and that she, Yara Nakamura, was holding a piece of paper that belonged to someone else's future, and reading it anyway, because that was what she did, that was who she had become without ever quite deciding to be it.

She sealed the letter. She carried it in on the salver with the others, and set it second from the top, where Edith's eye would find it before the bills, and she watched her employer's hands go still over the toast rack when she saw the envelope.

Edith read it standing at the window. She read it the way she read everything — once quickly, then again slowly, her thumb pressing the crease as though testing whether the paper would confess something the ink had not. Then she folded it along its original lines, precisely, and put it in the drawer of the little mahogany desk by the window, the one she locked with a key she wore on a ribbon under her blouse, against her skin, where no constable searching the house would think to look first.

"Yara," she said, without turning. "Would you bring the car round at eleven? I have an errand I'd rather drive myself to."

"Very madam."

"And wear your good gloves. The ones without the grease."

* * *

They drove north-west out of London, past the gasworks and the terraces, past the new estates going up in rows like teeth, until the streets thinned and the hedges began, and Edith directed her with two words at a time — *left here, carry on* — until they came to a lane with a brick wall on one side and a field of hay gone to seed on the other, and a gate, and beyond the gate a house that might have been handsome forty years ago and was now merely dignified, the way old officers are dignified, upright out of habit rather than hope.

There was no one else about. That was the point of it, Yara realised. There was never anyone else about.

Edith got out of the Daimler herself, which she almost never did, and walked to the gate, and stood there with her hand on the iron latch without opening it, looking at the house the way one looks at a photograph of the dead.

"My father bought this place in 1881," she said. "He said a family ought to have somewhere to put its embarrassments."

Yara switched off the engine. The silence that followed was enormous, country silence, full of small sounds — a dog somewhere behind the wall, bees in the hedge — and she understood that she was not meant to answer yet, that Edith had brought her here to say a thing that could not be said within earshot of the house in Kensington, where the walls had begun, in both their private reckonings, to have ears.

"Alistair stayed here, the summer before he shipped out," Edith went on. "He and Father quarrelled in that drawing room — you can see the windows from here. Father called him a coward for refusing to stand for Parliament on the family ticket. Alistair called Father a fossil." Her mouth did something that was nearly a smile and mostly grief. "I sat on the stairs and listened and thought them both magnificent fools. I was twenty-two and certain of everything."

She opened the gate. They went through together, Yara half a step behind, the chauffeur's distance, though nothing about the morning felt like service anymore.

The housekeeper — or the woman who was nominally the housekeeper, though Yara suspected her duties ran to more than linen — met them at the door and took Edith's coat and asked nothing, which told Yara that this woman too was part of the arrangement, whatever it was, one more person keeping a ledger nobody signed.

Edith led her down a corridor smelling of cold plaster and lavender bags to a small back room with one window over the garden. On the desk lay a tin document box, army green, unmarked.

"I want to show you something," Edith said, "and then I want to ask you something, and I want you to understand that you may refuse the asking without any change in your position here. Do you hear me? None whatsoever."

"Yes, madam."

"Don't *madam* me, girl, not today." But there was no anger in it. She unlocked the box with the ribbon key from around her neck.

Inside, on top of a thin folder of papers, lay a single ticket. Not a ticket exactly — a passage document, heavy cream stock, embossed at the head with the steamship company's anchor-and-crown, the kind of paper that cost real money even to look at. Yara read it upside down first, the clerk's trick Tobias had taught her without meaning to, and then Edith turned it round and laid it flat and let her read it properly.

*One berth, first class, Liverpool to Quebec. Issued against standing account. Name of passenger to be inserted at time of booking. Valid until 31st day of October, 1913.*

A blank line where a name should go.

"There was a scandal," Edith said, "before your time and before mine, properly. An aunt of mine — my mother's sister — a woman named Constance, who did a thing that cannot be spoken of in our family except as *the trouble*, and the thing she did, when you strip away all the shame they wrapped it in, was fall in love with the wrong man and refuse to give him up. That is all. That was the whole of it. But it was enough, in those days, to end a life as it had been arranged." She touched the edge of the ticket but did not pick it up, as though it were hot, or holy. "My grandfather — Constance's father, not the one who bought this house — set aside money for her passage. He never forgave her, mind. He simply made sure that if the world closed on her, there would be one door left open, and paid for, and nobody need know whose hand turned the key."

"And it has passed down," Yara said quietly.

"It has passed down. My father renewed it. I renew it. Every year a letter comes from the shipping company, and every year I pay, and every year I tell myself it is sentiment, an eccentricity, a Marlowe folly." She looked up then, and her eyes were dry and very steady and very tired. "And then last month I stood in a police court and watched a nineteen-year-old girl sentenced to six weeks hard labour for shouting the word *votes*, and I watched her mother faint in the public gallery, and I thought: the door. The door is open. It has always been open. And I have been keeping it for ghosts."

The room was very quiet. Out the window, the garden went on being a garden, indifferent, green, absurd.

"Why are you showing me this?" Yara asked. Her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Because of what happened in Hyde Park." Edith's jaw tightened. "Because I have had a telegram from Renwick's office — did you know that? A *telegram*, as though we were arranging a dinner — requesting another conversation. Because Vera Calloway wrote to warn us that his patience is not infinite. Because Alistair is coming home to a country where his name is a loaded weapon, and I am fifty-one years old and my heart gives me trouble on stairs, and the government has learned that it can starve women in cells and call it law." She drew a breath. "Because someone may very soon need to disappear, Yara, cleanly and completely, the way Constance disappeared, alive and safe and erased — and I find I cannot decide who. And I am sick to death of deciding things alone."

Yara looked at the ticket. One berth. One blank line. All that money, all those decades of quiet annual payments, converging on a single seat on a single ship, and the ship would sail whether the seat was filled by the right person or the wrong one, and there was no second seat, and there never had been. Safety, quantified. Love, reduced to arithmetic. She thought: this is what families do instead of saying what they mean. They buy a door and keep it locked and call the locking itself the love.

"The asking," she said. "You said you wanted to ask me something."

Edith straightened. For a moment she looked every year of her age and every year of her courage at once.

"If it comes to it — if Renwick moves, if the Act closes on me again and this time they don't let go, if Alistair's return makes this house untenable for any of us — will you help me decide? Not as a chauffeur. Not as a secretary. As—" She stopped, and Yara watched her reach for the word and flinch from it. "As one of us."

One of us. Yara felt the phrase land in her chest like a coal dropped down her collar. Three years she had worked in this house. Three years of reading letters through sealed envelopes and telling herself she stood outside the story, that history happened elsewhere, to other people, to women with banners and men with titles, never to a Japanese-English girl in a chauffeur's uniform whose father had crossed her off his ledger at sixteen and left a blank line where a daughter used to be.

A blank line. Like the one on the ticket.

"You're asking me to help choose which of you leaves England," Yara said. "You. Mr Finch. Mrs Calloway. Perhaps yourself."

"I'm asking you to help choose who gets to live."

"They are not always the same question."

"No," Edith said softly. "No, they are not. That is rather the whole horror of it."

* * *

They drove back through the afternoon, the light going long and gold across the fields, and neither of them spoke for the first ten miles, and the silence was not uncomfortable, which was new, and Yara thought about that: how silence changes its weight depending on who shares it.

It was somewhere past Watford, stuck behind a cartload of hay while the Daimler idled and the horses ahead plodded their ancient pace, that Edith spoke again, not turning her head, watching the road.

"Tobias doesn't know yet."

"No, madam."

"He'll argue. He'll count himself out — he's already begun, I can see it, the way he stands slightly behind doors now, as though practising for disappearing." A pause. "And Vera won't leave at all. I've asked. She laughed at me. Actually laughed."

"She has her own war here."

"She has her own war *everywhere*, that woman. She carries it like other people carry umbrellas." Edith's hands flexed once on her lap. "Which leaves you and me, Yara. And Alistair, God help us, who will refuse on principle because he has spent three years learning to make grand gestures out of wreckage. So. You and me."

The hay cart swung into a gate. The road opened. Yara changed gear and let the car breathe, and thought about the blank line, and about her father in Yokohama, whom she had not written to in four years and who would not know, if she vanished across an ocean tomorrow, whether to grieve or to feel relieved. She thought about a berth on a ship going west, toward a country she had never seen, where no one would know her name and no one would need to. Safety. The word had begun to taste strange in her mouth, like fruit from a foreign market — beautiful, and possibly rotten at the centre.

"Madam," she said, "if I may — the thirty-first of October."

"What of it?"

"That is nine weeks. Less, if the company means business days." She kept her eyes on the road. "Whatever we decide, the clock has already started. It started years ago. We've only just heard it ticking."

In the corner of her vision, Edith Marlowe turned her face to the window, to the passing hedges and the sinking sun, and said nothing at all, which was the loudest agreement Yara had ever heard.

* * *

That evening, after the household had gone up, Yara sat in the garage with the lamp turned low and opened her private ledger to a fresh page. Outside, the London night pressed close — a milk float rattling somewhere, a drunk singing two streets over, the enormous ordinary machinery of a city that did not yet know what was coming for it.

She ruled the columns as she always did: Date. Item. Value. Note.

Under Date she wrote *Tuesday, 12 August*. Under Item: *One berth, first class, Liverpool–Quebec. Held since 1891. Expires 31 October.* Under Value she paused, pen hovering, and finally wrote: *Everything anyone in this house owns.*

Then she sat back and looked at the page, and at the space beneath it, waiting for the entry that would come next, the entry she could not yet make because it required a decision, and decisions, unlike dates and sums, could not be copied from someone else's records.

Nine weeks, she had told Edith. But lying awake later, listening to the house settle around her, Yara corrected herself. The clock had not started with the shipping company's letter, nor with Renwick's telegram, nor even with a soaked stranger hammering on the service door with a dead man's handwriting in his arms.

It had started in 1891, with a grandfather buying a door for a disgraced daughter. Eighteen hundred weeks ago, more or less. Ticking all that time in a locked box in a forgotten house, patient as empire.

And now, at last, everyone she loved was standing inside the room with the ticking in it, and only one of them could walk out through the door.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the ledger lay open on her knees like a small sleeping animal, and downstairs, beneath the floorboards, beneath the coal cellar's false wall they had built with their own hands, the archive waited — Alistair's letters, Edith's movement papers, Vera's careful forgeries — all of it evidence, all of it ammunition, all of it reasons why none of them was safe and one of them must be.

The next morning, a note arrived from Major Renwick.

Not a telegram this time. A card, personally written, proposing Thursday, four o'clock, "to conclude our unfinished conversation." And beneath his signature, in ink a shade darker than the rest, a postscript that Yara read three times before her hands stopped shaking enough to carry it upstairs:

*I am given to understand the Marlowe account holds certain standing arrangements abroad. I do so admire foresight in a family. It saves such unpleasant improvisation later.*
