## 19. Everyone Counts Themselves Out

Edith Marlowe had kept the passage documents in her writing desk for eleven years, through two moves and one bankruptcy scare and the whole slow rot of her marriage, and she had never once told anyone they existed.

She took them out now on a Tuesday evening in October, when the house had gone quiet in that particular way quiet houses do after supper—the clink of dishes finished below stairs, the fire settling, the gas turned low—and she spread them on the blotter beneath the lamp like a hand of cards she was finally tired of holding.

One berth. Second class, Cunard line, Liverpool to Saint John. Paid in full, 1902, by a man named Hollis whose name meant nothing to anyone anymore but whose money had bought Edith's first printing press and, as it happened, one guaranteed escape from England for any Marlowe who ever needed to vanish. The ticket agent's ink had faded to the colour of weak tea. The berth did not expire; it waited. It had waited through Alistair's departure and Alistair's death and Alistair's impossible resurrection, and it sat there now on the desk like a patient animal, waiting still.

*Someone must use it,* she thought. *Before winter. Before Renwick finds a second pretext. Before this house becomes a place where people are taken.*

She thought of Yara first, because she thought of Yara first about everything now, though she would have denied it under oath.

The girl had come to her at sixteen with a trunk, a letter of introduction written in beautiful English by a father who wanted nothing further to do with her, and hands that could take an engine apart in the dark. Seven years. Seven years of early mornings and late drives and letters sealed without comment—letters Edith knew perfectly well had been opened and read, because the gum on the envelopes never quite matched, and because Yara Nakamura had the decency to be bad at only one thing in her life, which was pretending not to care.

Edith had let her read. That was the truth she carried privately, the small vanity of her mercy: she had let the girl read everything, because a person locked out of history will find a way in through windows, and Edith preferred to leave the windows open where she could watch.

And now the windows were becoming doors, and men like Renwick were walking through them, and the girl needed somewhere to be that was not here.

*Yara should go,* Edith wrote in her head, composing the argument the way she composed all her arguments, in clean lines with no wasted motion. *She is young. She has no record, no file, no name in any ledger that matters. England has given her nothing but work, and Canada at least gives her distance. And I—*

She stopped. The pen hovered.

*I am sixty-one years old,* she wrote instead, *and my body is a ledger of debts already incurred. Two hunger strikes. Forty-one days in Holloway across three arrests. My lungs remember the cells the way other women remember their wedding days. If I go, the movement loses its voice and gains nothing. If she goes, the movement loses nothing it cannot survive and one person survives entirely.*

It was tidy. It was even true. She signed it mentally, dated it, filed it.

But her hand stayed flat on the paper a long moment after, and what she did not write down, what she permitted herself only in the dark of the study with the lamp turned to its lowest flame, was simpler and less defensible: *I cannot lose another one. Not the brother, not the cause, not the girl. Let it be someone. Let me be the one who stays and pays.*

Love, in Edith Marlowe's experience, was mostly bookkeeping conducted at a loss, and she had never once balanced her own column.

She put the papers back in the desk, under the false bottom, beneath the bundle of unsent letters to Alistair that she had written during the three years he was dead. She turned off the lamp. In the hallway, passing Yara's closed door, she paused—only a moment, only the length of a breath—and then went up to bed.

* *

Yara heard the pause outside her door.

Seven years of listening to engines teaches you to hear hesitation in anything—a valve, a footstep, a heartbeat. She lay in her narrow bed above the garage with her eyes open and catalogued it: the weight of Edith's tread stopping, the creak of floorboards as weight shifted from one foot to the other, the half-second of silence that meant *I almost knocked*, and then the resumption, slower than before, up the stairs to the main house.

She had been thinking about the berth all day. Tobias had let it slip that morning over the radiator hose—that there was a passage, held in reserve, one seat—and the knowledge had sat in her chest since like a stone in a carburettor, small and precisely wrong, throwing the whole engine of the household out of time.

Because everyone was doing arithmetic. She could see it in all their faces at supper. Edith counting years. Vera counting risks. Tobias counting sins. Each of them looking at the others across the cold mutton and thinking, with terrible politeness, *not me—them.*

Yara got out of bed and lit her candle and took out the ledger.

It lived now behind a loose brick in the garage wall, wrapped in oilcloth, because a ledger of evidence could not sleep under the same roof as its subjects. But she kept a second book—smaller, blacker, unnumbered—for things that were not evidence, and she opened it now and wrote, in the handwriting her father had taught her and disowned in the same year:

*Considered: the berth, and who ought to fill it.*

Then she made a list, because lists were how she prayed.

*Edith Marlowe. Reasons:* She wrote them out. Sixty-one. Failing lungs. The movement needs her voice more than it needs her body, and her body is the price of the voice—if Holloway takes it again under the Cat and Mouse Act, there may be no third release. *If anyone leaves, it should be the one the future most requires.* She looked at the sentence and found it insufficient and added: *Also, she is the closest thing to family I have, and family is supposed to be protected. I have read enough letters to know that much.*

Then, below it, in smaller characters:

*Counter-argument: she will never go. She would set herself on fire before she boarded a ship away from this fight. Naming her is therefore a way of loving her, not a plan.*

She sat with that. The candle guttered and righted itself.

*Tobias Finch. Reasons:* hunted, though not yet charged; a clerk's memory full of things Renwick wants erased; twenty-six years old and owed approximately one entire life by a country that spent his and gave back a tremor and a bundle of stolen letters. *He should go. He has the least here and the most ahead.*

*Counter-argument: he will refuse. He has begun calling this house by a word he thinks no one hears him use. It is "home."*

Her pen slowed.

*Vera Calloway. Refused in advance. Will not board. Has said so twice, once politely and once not.*

*Myself.* The pen hovered longest here. *Reasons: none sufficient. England has been cruel in the ordinary ways and kind in one extraordinary one. What I know of history I learned at this kitchen table. What I love of it lives within four miles of this candle. To leave would be to become again what I was at sixteen—someone to whom history happens elsewhere, on another continent, to other people. I am done being that person.*

*I name Edith. I expect she has named me. This is how we say it, we Marlowes—* 

She caught the word. *We Marlowes.* She had written it before she thought, the way blood moves before permission. She looked at it a long time. She did not strike it out.

Below, at the bottom of the page, she added one line more, in the space where accountants write the balancing figure:

*Note: every member of this household believes the berth belongs to someone else. Therefore it belongs to whoever loves the others least, or most—I have not yet determined which. To be resolved.*

She blew out the candle. Above the garage, through the frost-furred window, the lights of the main house went dark one room at a time, like a sentence being crossed out.

* *

Tobias Finch could not sleep in beds. He had explained this to no one, but the household had noticed anyway—the folded blanket on the drawing-room sofa each morning, the way he drifted toward chairs rather than rooms—and Edith, who noticed everything, had simply ordered a better lamp for the corner he favoured and said nothing, and this, more than anything anyone had said aloud, was why the word *home* had gotten loose in his head.

He sat in that corner now, near midnight, watching the coals, and did his own accounting.

The berth. One seat. He turned the fact over the way he'd once turned over requisition forms, checking figures against figures, and every sum came out the same way.

*Not me.*

It wasn't humility. He distrusted his own humility, had learned in the field hospital at Malta that a man's modesty is often just fear wearing borrowed clothes. No—it was arithmetic, clerk's arithmetic, the only kind he trusted.

*Miss Marlowe goes, or Yara goes.*

If Miss Marlowe went, the movement lost its spine, and the movement was the only thing standing between Renwick and the smooth erasure of everything Alistair had bled for. Also—and here the arithmetic got warm and unprofessional—she had fed him soup with her own hands when his hands wouldn't stop shaking, and had said, the second week, without ceremony, *You'll stay until you're steady, Finch, and then longer if you like,* and no one since his mother had issued him permission to exist in quite that tone.

If Yara went—

He found he could not complete that entry. His pen, so to speak, skidded.

There was a thing he had noticed about Yara Nakamura and had decided, with great discipline, never to notice again: that she listened to engines the way other people listened to music; that she laughed exactly once per joke, quietly, as if laughter were a document requiring countersignature; that when she stood next to him at the workbench in the garage, close enough to pass the same spanner back and forth, some clenched thing behind his sternum loosened by degrees, and he slept—actually slept—afterward.

*She goes,* he wrote firmly in his head. *She is young and she is not hunted and she deserves a country that hasn't spat at her. Or Miss Marlowe goes. Either. Both are correct entries.*

*And if neither will board?* asked some junior clerk in the back office of his mind, the impertinent one.

*Then we are four fools in a burning archive,* he answered, *and I will burn with them, and God forgive me, I think I would rather.*

He pulled the blanket higher and watched the fire and understood, with the clarity that comes only past midnight, that this was what Alistair must have felt in Malta—this exact arithmetic, this exact refusal, this exact warm damnation. The dead man had counted himself out too. Perhaps that was what the letters were, in the end: a ledger of self-erasure, kept in a beautiful hand.

* *

Vera Calloway received the question on Thursday, put to her directly over tea in her lodgings off the Euston Road, and she answered it before the cup touched her saucer.

"No."

"You haven't heard the—" Yara began.

"I've heard everything. One berth, paid passage, expires or doesn't, someone must go." Vera set the cup down. She was thirty-four and looked forty-five and had the hands of a woman who had washed a thousand bedsheets in a colonial hospital and forged one death certificate, and those two facts had, in her face, achieved a kind of truce. "And my answer was composed three months ago in Malta, at sea, on the train from Dover, and every day since. No."

"Renwick knows your name now."

"He's known my name since 1912." She smiled without warmth. "That's rather the point of me."

"You could be safe."

"Child," Vera said—not unkindly, but with the weariness of a woman correcting a junior nurse for the tenth time—"I have been safe. Safe is Malta. Safe is a ward of dying men and a commanding officer who owns you because he owns your signature, and safe is watching Alistair Marlowe decide to disappear so that I don't hang for a piece of paper, and safe is three years of knowing that a living man walks the earth as a dead one because of me. I have done safe. It is the least safe place I have ever lived."

She leaned forward.

"If I board that ship, I confirm everything. The fugitive flees; ergo there was something to flee from. My absence becomes his confession. Whereas if I stand in London, in uniform, in my ward at St Bartholomew's, with my references and my landlady and my church pew, and look Major Renwick in the eye every Sunday I please—then his story has to fight mine in daylight, and daylight is the one theatre where I can beat him."

"And if he comes for you anyway?"

"Then I am arrested for forging the death notice of a man who is alive, which will require the Crown to prove, in open court, that Alistair Marlowe lives—which is precisely the revelation Renwick has spent three years burying." She lifted her cup at last and drank, unhurried. "I have arranged matters so that my destruction is also his. It is the only kind of architecture I'm good at. Forgers build that way."

Yara looked at her—at this woman who had altered a record and thereby altered a world, who had paid for it daily and would pay more, and who spoke of it the way Yara spoke of engine timing: as mechanics, not martyrdom.

"You're like us," Yara said, before she could stop it. "You keep ledgers."

Vera's face did something complicated then—something old and briefly young.

"I keep the only kind that's ever mattered," she said. "The ones nobody's supposed to read."

* *

By Friday, the arithmetic had become weather. Everyone felt the pressure change; no one spoke of it.

Edith drafted, in her head, the speech by which she would press the ticket into Yara's hands. Yara drafted, in her ledger, the counter-speech by which she would refuse and redirect. Tobias rehearsed, against the ceiling of the darkened drawing room, the simple sentence *Give it to Miss Marlowe, or to Yara, and I'll carry the luggage to whichever door.* And Vera, in Euston, re-ironed her nurse's uniform for morning shift and felt the strange peace of the condemned who have chosen their own ground.

Four people. One berth. Four refusals, each signed and sealed in the privacy of a separate skull.

It might have gone on for weeks—might have curdled into the polite, lethal stalemate of families who love by subtraction—had the post not come early on Saturday morning, and had the envelope not been cream-coloured, heavy-stock, and franked with the crest of the War Office.

Yara carried it in on the salver, as she carried everything, her face arranged into the competent blankness of seven years' practice. Edith slit it with the bone-handled knife. Her eyes went down the page once, quickly, the way they went down hostile newspapers.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was level, which was how the household now knew it was bad. "Major Renwick requests the pleasure. Sunday lunch. He writes"—her finger moved along the line—"*to discuss arrangements for the winter.*"

She looked up. Yara met her gaze. Somewhere upstairs a door closed; Tobias, awake at last, had heard the tone through the floor.

"Arrangements," Edith repeated softly, folding the card along its crease, once, precisely. "How very like a soldier. Even his threats come with a timetable."
