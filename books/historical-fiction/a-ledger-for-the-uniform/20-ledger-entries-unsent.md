## 20. Ledger Entries, Unsent

The house had gone quiet in the particular way it did after ten o'clock, when Mrs. Pell retired with her hot-water bottle and Edith's lamp behind the study door burned down to its last inch of oil. Yara knew every sound of this silence: the tick of the hall clock, the settling of the pipes, the small sigh the Daimler made as its engine cooled in the garage below like a sleeping animal. She had catalogued them all without meaning to, the way she had catalogued everything else about this house—its smells, its rituals, its people—as though she might one day be asked to account for them.

Tonight she sat at her narrow desk in the room above the garage, a sheet of good paper before her and the inkwell open, and found that her hand would not begin.

She had written ledgers before. She had written hundreds of pages of dates and names and sums, the neat columns of evidence she kept locked beneath the false bottom of her sewing box: *March 4th — letter from Glasgow branch, three names redacted by censor's stamp; March 9th — Renwick, Major H., War Office, visitor to House of Commons, 2:15 p.m.* Numbers were honest. Numbers did not require her to say anything true.

But a letter required a salutation, and she did not know how to address the man who had disowned her.

She tried. *Honoured Father.* The words looked absurd on the page, formal as a funeral wreath. She crossed them out. *Father.* Worse—it was an accusation dressed as a word. In the end she wrote nothing at all above the first line, and let the letter simply begin, which was perhaps more honest than any greeting could have been.

*I have been reading your letters.*

Not his. Not really. But the phrase was true in a way she liked—the way it sounded like a confession and a theft at once—and she had learned from watching Edith Marlowe work that a sentence which does two things at once is worth ten that do only one.

*You will want to know what I mean. You will think I have been opening other people's post again, as you accused me of doing when I was fourteen, though what you actually said was that a daughter who reads what is not hers will never keep what is. You were wrong about the second part. I keep everything now. It is my profession, or nearly one.*

The pen moved. Outside, rain had begun against the little window, soft as fingertips.

*I drive a motorcar for a suffragist. You would find this funny, or shameful, depending on which version of you is doing the finding—the father who taught me to love engines, or the one who told the neighbours his daughter had died of fever rather than admit she had run away to England. I am very good at driving. Mr. Daimler himself would approve of my gear changes, if he cared about such things, which I suspect he does not. The car is a 1911 model, black as a beetle, and I know its temper better than anyone alive except possibly the mechanic who built it, and I know him too, because I sit on a crate and watch him work whenever he lets me.*

*Her name is Mrs. Marlowe. Edith Marlowe. You will not have heard of her. You read the wrong newspapers, and even if you read the right ones they would call her worse than she is, which is to say they would call her a hysteric instead of a mathematician of injustice, which is what she actually is. She counts things. She counts arrests and sentences and the price of bread for a widow against the price of a racehorse for a lord, and she puts the sums where the country cannot avoid seeing them. I learned that from her. Everything I know about keeping accounts of the truth I learned in this house, which means you are no longer the source of anything in me, and I hope that lands somewhere in Yokohama like a stone dropped down a well.*

Yara stopped. Read it back. It was crueler than she had intended, and less true than it pretended to be—for of course he remained the source of things in her. Her hands were his hands. Her patience with machines, her suspicion of easy answers, the habit of checking a thing twice before trusting it once: all of that had come across the water inside her like stowaways, uninvited and permanent. A person could not disown their own inheritance any more than Alistair Marlowe could disown his handwriting.

She thought of the letters upstairs, in Edith's study drawer, in that unmistakable slanting hand—*dead these three years*, and writing anyway, dated from a hospital in Malta, describing the colour of the harbour in November. History happened elsewhere. That had been her creed since she was old enough to form one. And then a soaked stranger had stood at the service entrance holding proof that history had been happening, patiently, secretly, three years deep into a grave, waiting for someone to come and read it.

She dipped the pen again.

*Tobias says I should write to you. He said it without knowing he was saying it—he said everyone has someone they owe a letter to, and that most people die owing it. He is a clerk, or was, before the army finished with him, and he keeps his guilt the way I keep my ledger: itemized, dated, reviewed nightly. He was in a field hospital at Gallipoli. Alistair Marlowe saved him there—you remember I told you about the dead brother? Well, he is less dead than advertised, and the whole story of how that came to be is sitting in a drawer upstairs in forty-one letters that a clerk was ordered to burn and did not burn, and I cannot tell you why that moves me so much except that it proves something I need proven: that paper survives people. That what is written can outlast the person who wanted it destroyed.*

*That is why I write to you tonight, though I will never send this. Because you destroyed nothing, Father. That was your cleverness. You simply stopped writing, and let the absence do the burning for you. Six years now. I have counted. Of course I have counted—I count everything—but I want you to know that the counting is not bitterness. It is bookkeeping. Somewhere in my ledger there is a column with your name at the top, and every year I enter another line in it, and the line says only:* still nothing, *and I do not know whether I am keeping the account open because I hope it will be paid or because closing it would mean admitting what was owed.*

The rain thickened. Down in the garage, the Daimler sighed again, or she imagined it did.

She wrote until the candle guttered low—pages she would fold and put in the box with the others, the unsent correspondence of six years, addressed to a man in Yokohama who believed his daughter dead of fever. There was a strange comfort in it, a comfort she was ashamed of: the letters piled up like stones over a well, and each one she wrote made the silence between them more permanent, and more hers. She was building a wall out of words. She was very good at walls.

* * *

The second letter took longer to begin, because its recipient had no address at all.

*Alistair,* she wrote finally, and felt the strangeness of using a dead man's Christian name—except he was not dead, which was the whole trouble, and yet some part of him had died anyway, the part that could walk up the front steps of his sister's house and ring the bell. Whoever Alistair Marlowe had become in Malta, in whatever hospital ward or hiding place Vera Calloway had spirited him to, he was not the man whose farewell dinner Edith still set a place for in her memory. Ghosts, Yara was learning, did not require death. They required only absence long enough for the living to finish their portrait of you.

*I have read all forty-one of your letters,* she wrote. *I expect you would mind that, if you knew. I expect you would mind it very much, being a soldier and a gentleman and all the other categories of person who believe privacy is a kind of honour. But I am the one who seals them, and sealing is a funny sort of promise. You hold the folded paper, you press the wax, you watch the flame go out, and all the while you carry the contents inside yourself like a swallowed key. Edith knows. I have suspected for two years that she knows, and she has never said a word, and I have decided that this is the truest kindness either of us has ever done the other: we pretend not to see each other reading, and in pretending, we agree that some things matter more than rules.*

*Here is what I cannot work out, and I am writing to a ghost because ghosts do not interrupt:*

*Why did you stay hidden?*

*It is the question under all the questions. Tobias asks it about Renwick. Edith asks it about the censors' stamps. Vera Calloway—whom I have not met yet but whose name sits in your margins like a held breath—she must ask it of herself every morning. But the question is yours. A man declared dead is free. Free of the army, free of the scandal you mention in fragments, free of whatever your commanding officer held over you. All you had to do was remain a rumour. Instead you wrote forty-one letters to a woman who believed you buried at Gallipoli, and you dated them, and you posted them through channels that must have cost you dearly, knowing every stamp was a risk. Dead men do not write letters. Living ones do not write to their own funerals. So which were you?*

The clock downstairs struck one. Yara flexed her writing hand, stiff now, and looked at the page.

She had watched Edith read those letters. She had watched grief and hope fight openly on that composed face, round after round, like two boxers neither of whom would fall. And she had understood something in that watching that she had no language for until now, sitting alone above a garage with an unsent letter in front of her: that Edith had spent three years mourning a quarrel, not a man. That the funeral had been for the unreconciled thing between them—the dinner in 1910 where Alistair had called her work unwomanly and she had called him a coward in finer words, and neither had written to mend it, and then Gallipoli had made mending impossible. Or apparently impossible. The universe, it turned out, kept poor records. The universe reported deaths it had not confirmed and closed accounts it had no right to close.

*Your sister keeps your place at table,* Yara wrote. *Not literally—Mrs. Pell would riot—but in every other way. There is a version of you in this house, maintained daily, dusted and polished, and I think when you come back—and I have begun to believe you will come back, because the letters are recent and the handwriting is steady and men who intend to stay ghosts do not describe the colour of harbours—I think when you come back you will find that you have to fight that version of yourself for the right to replace it. She will not surrender him easily, the Alistair she built out of grief. He is kinder than you. He agrees with her about the vote. He never left.*

*Forgive me. I am lecturing a ghost, and I am not family, and I have no right.*

And there it was. The word. *Family.* She stared at it on the page the way one stares at an error in a column of figures that refuses, upon rechecking, to be an error at all.

Because that was the thing she had never said aloud, not to Edith, not to Tobias, not to the candle: that she had a family, and it was this one, and it had assembled itself out of accidents—a dismissed daughter, a widowed agitator, a broken clerk, a forged nurse, a resurrected soldier—and none of them shared blood, and all of them kept each other's secrets, which was a stricter bond than blood ever managed. Blood families assumed. Chosen families *decided*, daily, and the deciding was the love.

She thought of her father's house in Yokohama: the polished floors, the silent meals, the way disagreement was handled by removal—of privileges, of names, finally of persons. She had been erased cleanly. There was no fever; there was a decision, entered in the family's private ledger, and she had spent six years believing that such erasure was the natural law of families, that love was a licence revocable at will.

Then she had come to this house, where a woman kept a place for a dead brother, and a clerk kept letters he'd been ordered to burn, and a nurse forged documents out of mercy, and a chauffeur kept a secret ledger in a sewing box—and slowly, entry by entry, she had learned that record-keeping was not the opposite of love. Done properly, it *was* love. To keep someone's account open. To refuse to mark them paid-in-full-and-gone. To write *still here, still here, still here* in the margin of every year.

*I will tell you what I decided tonight, Alistair,* she wrote, the candle nearly gone. *I decided that if you come back—and you will—I will not resent you for the years your sister lost. I will not resent the space you occupy, though I earned my place here and you abandoned yours. I will feed you at this table, because that is what this household does: it feeds people back into existence. And I will keep reading the mail, and you will never know, and someday you may thank me, because the letters I seal are the only reason half of us are still speaking to the other half.*

*There is one more thing, and I hardly dare write it, but a ghost deserves honesty: there is a berth. One berth, on a ship to Canada, held under an arrangement older than me, for whichever member of this improbable family needs to disappear before winter. Everyone is counting themselves out of it. Edith has named me. I have named her. Tobias would give it to both of us if ships worked that way, and Vera won't leave England at all, and Alistair—if you come back, you will be the reason someone must use it, and I do not yet know how to forgive that, and I do not yet know that forgiveness is required. We are arguing ourselves into shape, this family of mine. Cold suppers and raised voices and tears in the kitchen, and underneath it all the same question, ticking like the hall clock:*

*Who goes?*

*Who stays?*

*Who gets to be the one who remembers?*

She signed nothing. Unsent letters needed no signatures; they were addressed to the writer as much as anyone.

* * *

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and the dark was full of the house breathing around her—Edith's window going dark at last across the yard, the rain easing, the city beyond the wall holding its thousand unsent letters in its thousand drawers.

Tomorrow, she thought, the letters could not stay in a drawer. Tomorrow Renwick's polite threats would arrive again wearing his good coat, and the Cat and Mouse Act would keep turning Edith out of Holloway only to reel her back, and papers that could hang a family could not live forever in a sewing box and a study desk. They would need stone and mortar. They would need a place with a false wall and a lock of a different kind—the kind made of silence and coal dust.

She knew exactly where. Every house has a heart it has forgotten, and this one beat down in the cellar, under the chute where the coal came clattering in each autumn.

Yara rose, folded the two unsent letters into the sewing box with their fifty-three sisters, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the lid, as if taking an oath over a Bible of blank pages.

Then she went down to wake Tobias, because the digging would take all night, and there was no one else she trusted to hold the lamp.
