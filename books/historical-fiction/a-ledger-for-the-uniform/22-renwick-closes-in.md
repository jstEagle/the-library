## 22. Renwick Closes In

The car came at half past nine on a grey Tuesday, and Yara saw it before she understood it.

She was polishing the Daimler's bonnet when she noticed the Humber parked across the street, angled so that its windscreen caught a clean reflection of the Marlowe house. That alone meant nothing; delivery vans angled themselves for all sorts of reasons. What meant something was that the man inside it had not moved in forty minutes. She had counted. She was good at counting—she had been keeping ledgers since she was twelve years old, and a ledger was nothing but the discipline of noticing what repeated.

The man wore a brown cap and read a newspaper he never turned. The page had been the same page since she first looked: the shipping news, or what passed for it in October, column after column of departures and arrivals that no one read unless they were waiting for someone. She went back to her cloth, worked the polish into the paintwork in slow circles, and let her eyes travel sideways every third pass.

At eleven o'clock the Humber was relieved by a second car, an older Wolseley, and the newspaper man got out, stretched like a man who had been sitting too long, and walked away without looking back. His replacement did not even pretend to read.

Yara set down the cloth and stood very still in the middle of the mews, listening to the ordinary sounds of the city—the rag-and-bone man's cry two streets over, a church bell marking the hour badly, rain starting somewhere above the roofs and arriving in fat, unhurried drops. It occurred to her, standing there with polish on her hands and her heart going strangely quiet, that this was what history looked like when it came for you. Not cavalry. Not fire. Two parked motorcars and men who were very good at appearing to do nothing.

She thought: *I have read enough letters to know how this begins.*

* * *

Edith took the news standing at her study window, arms folded, watching the Wolseley with the expression of a woman appraising a rival's hat.

"Renwick," she said. "He's wasting his money. I've been watched by professionals—the police, the Home Office, twice by journalists who thought they'd catch me at something worse than politics. These two are clerks playing at detection." She turned. "But clerks are worse than detectives, Yara. Detectives want results. Clerks want paper. And Renwick is building a file."

"A file against you?"

"Against all of us. He doesn't need to prove anything. He needs to assemble enough of *something* that a magistrate will sign whatever he asks for next." Edith came away from the window and sat behind her desk, and Yara saw—for one unguarded moment before the mask came down—that her employer's hands were not quite steady. "A warrant pretext. He'll have one already drafted. Failure to register a resident alien, perhaps. Suspicion of harbouring a deserter—that one would be deliciously ironic, given we're harbouring no such thing. Yet."

The word *yet* sat between them like a coal on the carpet.

"You should burn them," Yara said quietly. "The letters."

"No."

"Mrs Marlowe—"

"Yara." Edith's voice did not rise, but it acquired that quality it took on at public meetings, the one that carried across a hall without amplification. "Alistair spent three years being erased. They wrote him out of the world with a rubber stamp and a lie, and they did it because it was *tidier* that way. If I burn those letters, I am doing Renwick's work for him. I am agreeing that paper matters more than people. I will not." She pressed her palm flat against the desk, as if steadying something beneath it. "We keep them. We simply get cleverer about where."

That evening they moved the archive again—from the coal cellar false wall to a new cavity Tobias built behind the cistern in the scullery—and then, on Yara's insistence, moved it back, because Tobias's hammering had been heard by the girl from the corner shop, and a hiding place known to three people was a hiding place known to everyone who might squeeze any of the three. The letters ended up wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare tyre well of the Daimler itself, which Yara proposed with a straight face and which made Edith laugh for the first time in days—a short, startled sound, quickly put away.

"They'll search the house," Yara said. "They won't search the car. A chauffeur's car is furniture. It has no secrets worth a warrant."

"And if they take the car?"

"Then I will be driving it," said Yara, "and they will have to explain to a magistrate why they impounded a lady's motorcar on the strength of two men reading newspapers."

* * *

Vera's warning came on Thursday, and it came the way Vera did everything: precisely, and disguised as something else.

A parcel arrived for "Miss Nakamura, care of Mrs E. Marlowe"—which was itself a message, because nobody sent Yara parcels, and because Vera knew perfectly well that anything addressed to the household would pass through Edith's hands first. Inside, under a layer of straw, was a tin of Maltese honey biscuits, the kind sold from a cart near the hospital gates, and beneath the biscuits, folded into a rectangle small as a stamp, a strip of paper torn from a nurse's chart.

Yara read it at the kitchen table with her back to the door, the way she'd learned to read other people's letters, and felt the floor tilt gently under her feet.

*Warrant applied for Monday last, grounds of "papers prejudicial" — refused. Second application Friday, new ground: alien registration irregularity. Will succeed. They mean to take the house papers AND the woman. R. has told the Commissioner your brother-in-law is a deserter living under this roof. He is not, but truth stopped mattering to R. some time ago. Watch the post. V.*

Yara read it three times. The first time for content. The second time she noticed *brother-in-law*, and understood that Vera was telling her, in the same breath, both the danger and the shape of the lie: Renwick had told the authorities that Alistair was *already here*, hidden among them, so that any search would be justified and any denunciation pre-made. The third time, she noticed *watch the post*, and went cold in a warm kitchen, because the post had been arriving late all week, and she had blamed the fog.

She burned the strip of paper over the gas ring and fed the ash down the drain, and then she stood a moment with her hands flat on the cool enamel, breathing, while the kettle came slowly to a boil behind her and did not know, as she did, that the world had narrowed by several feet on every side.

* * *

Tobias found her in the garage that night. He came in without knocking—he had earned that much, though he still hovered at the threshold like a man unsure whether rooms wanted him in them—and he stood turning his cap in his hands until she looked up.

"You've seen them too," he said. "The cars."

"Since Tuesday."

"I saw one outside my lodgings yesterday." He said it flatly, but his hands kept working the cap. "Different street. Same trick with the newspaper. I nearly went up and asked him the time, just to watch him not know it." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Old habits. In the field hospital you learn to spot the ones who aren't really there to help. They stand wrong. Too settled. Men who are waiting always look like they're about to leave."

"Are you going to leave?"

The question came out more bluntly than she intended, and she watched it land—watched him go still, the way he did when something touched close to the wound. He looked at the Daimler instead of at her, at the tyre well where three years of a dead-then-not-dead man's correspondence slept in oilcloth.

"I thought about it," he said. "Last night, walking home past that car. I could be in Birmingham by Saturday under another name. There's work in the motor works. Nobody in Birmingham cares what a clerk did in Malta." He swallowed. "And then I thought about Miss Marlowe going through that door on Monday morning with a warrant in one policeman's hand and my name in the other, and I thought—I did that. Not on purpose. But I'm the one who kept the letters, I'm the one Renwick can find, I'm the thread he'll pull to reach all of you. Running doesn't cut the thread. It just leaves the rest of you holding it."

"Tobias—"

"I'm not saying it nobly," he said quickly. "I want to run. Every part of me wants to run. I just—" He stopped, and started again, quieter. "In the hospital, when the shelling started, Alistair Marlowe had time to get under the table himself. Instead he lay over me. Three minutes of shellfire, and a man I barely knew lay over a clerk because clerks don't get dugouts." His voice frayed. "I've been trying to pay that back ever since, and the interest keeps compounding. So no. I'm not leaving. Not yet. Not while leaving costs more than staying."

Yara looked at him—this damp, trembling stranger of a month ago, this man who had walked into her ordered world carrying the handwriting of a dead man—and felt something shift in her chest that she did not have a ledger column for. Fear, yes. But also a fierce, inconvenient tenderness, and underneath it a thought forming with the clarity of an entry in her own hand: *when the berth question comes again, I will not let it be answered by arithmetic alone.*

"We should tell Mrs Marlowe about the second application," she said aloud. "Friday. That gives us the weekend."

"The weekend for what?"

"For getting cleverer," said Yara, "about where."

* * *

Saturday the fog came off the river and swallowed the city whole, and in the fog the watchers multiplied—or perhaps only seemed to, but Yara counted four now, two cars and two men on foot who walked the mews in opposite directions and passed each other with the smallest nod, like clock hands meeting at the top of the hour. She drove Edith to a committee meeting in Kensington with the archive riding in the spare tyre well and a constable's shadow trailing them at a respectful distance, and Edith, to her credit, conducted the entire journey as if unaware, discussing tariff reform and prison conditions in a voice pitched for eavesdroppers, feeding them nothing, daring them to write it all down.

It was on the drive home, fog thickening against the windscreen, wipers ticking, that Edith said, without preamble:

"He'll come Monday. With the warrant and two sergeants and a great show of legality. And he'll find a house full of suffragist literature, which is legal, and a Japanese-born chauffeur whose registration papers I renewed myself in March, which is legal, and no trace of any soldier dead or alive." She glanced sideways. "You moved them again?"

"Yesterday. Third place. Only I know it now."

"Not even Tobias?"

"Not even Tobias." Yara kept her eyes on the road. "It isn't distrust. It's arithmetic. Each person who knows is a door that can be opened."

Edith was quiet for a long moment. The fog pressed at the glass. When she spoke again her voice had lost its meeting-hall steel and gone low and tired and human.

"When I was a girl, my father kept his accounts in a room no one entered. I used to think the locked door meant shame. It took me thirty years to understand it meant fear." Her gloved hand rested briefly, warmly, on Yara's sleeve on the gear lever. "Whatever happens Monday, Yara—you have been the best of us. Better than blood. Remember that I said so, in case I forget to say it again in front of witnesses."

Yara drove on through the fog with her throat tight and her hands steady, because hands must be steady, that was the whole of her creed, and the creed was cracking, and still the hands held.

* * *

Sunday night, the telephone rang at eleven.

The household was abed; Yara answered it herself in the hall, in the dark, the receiver cold against her ear, and for a moment there was nothing but the sea-sound of the line and the fog against the windows. Then Vera's voice, low and fast and stripped of all its usual precision:

"They moved it up. The warrant's signed—it was signed this afternoon, I saw the docket, I was delivering charts to the Commissioner's office. They come tomorrow. Not Monday. Tomorrow, seven o'clock, before the household's awake, before anyone can warn—" She stopped. Breathed. "Yara. Listen. It's worse than papers. Renwick's added a name to the docket. He's told them there's a witness to be secured along with the documents. A male witness. Former army clerk."

The hallway seemed to tilt. Yara gripped the edge of the hall table.

"He means to take Tobias tomorrow," Vera said. "With or without cause. Once he's in custody, the questions start, and Tobias is brave but he is not cruel, and Renwick will be cruel enough for both of them. Get him out tonight. Tonight, do you hear me? Anywhere. I'll meet you if I can, but get him out of that house before seven o'clock or everything we've done—all of it, the letters, the ledger, Vera Calloway's whole forged life—was for nothing."

The line clicked dead.

Yara stood in the dark hallway with the receiver still at her ear, listening to the hum of the dead connection, and upstairs she could hear Tobias coughing in his room—the small, damaged-lung cough that had followed him out of Malta—and beyond the windows, faint through the fog, the slow footsteps of the watcher making his round.

Seven o'clock. She looked at the hall clock. Half past eleven. Seven hours, and a fog thick enough to lose a city in, and a Daimler in the mews with a full tank, because she always kept a full tank, because competence was the only prayer she had ever trusted.

She set down the receiver and went, quietly, up the stairs.
