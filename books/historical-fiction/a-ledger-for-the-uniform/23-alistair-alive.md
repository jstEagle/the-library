## 23. Alistair Alive

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which Yara would later think was fitting—Tuesdays were the days the household did its ordinary arithmetic: coal delivered, linens counted, letters sealed. Ordinary things. The wire arrived at half past nine in the morning, folded twice by a boy in a cap too large for him, and it sat on the silver tray in the hall for eleven minutes before anyone touched it.

Yara knew it was there. She had watched the boy hand it to Mrs. Petch; she had watched Mrs. Petch set it down with the particular care people gave to things that might be bills or might be catastrophes. She was polishing the Daimler's bonnet at the time, or pretending to, her cloth moving in slow circles while her eyes kept returning to the hall window, to that small pale rectangle lying on the tray like a playing card turned face down.

Eleven minutes. She counted them without meaning to. Counting was what she did instead of breathing when something mattered.

Then Edith Marlowe came down the stairs with a letter in one hand and her reading spectacles pushed up into her hair, took the telegram off the tray as an afterthought, opened it standing in the hall with the morning light behind her—and did not move again for so long that Yara set down her cloth and came inside without deciding to.

Edith read it three times. Yara could see her lips moving on the third pass. When she finally looked up, her face was doing something Yara had never seen it do in four years of service: nothing at all. Edith Marlowe's face in repose was always working—judging, planning, armoring itself. This was blank as fresh snow.

"Yara," she said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "Fetch Mr. Finch. And then fetch Vera Calloway, if you can find her. And then—" She stopped. Looked down at the paper again, as though it might have changed in her hands. "And then come back here. I don't want to be alone with it."

* * *

The telegram said eleven words. Yara read them perhaps forty times that day, and by evening they had worn grooves in her mind like a coin rubbed smooth.

*ALISTAIR ALIVE. SAILING FROM MARSEILLE. ARRIVE VICTORIA THURSDAY. —V.C.*

Alistair alive. Three words that unmade three years. Yara stood in the kitchen doorway holding the wire out toward Tobias, who was mending a kettle handle because his hands could not be idle, and she watched him read it, and she understood that she was watching a man learn that a ghost had been walking around Marseille buying cigarettes and complaining about the weather.

"No," Tobias said. Not *what*, not *how*. Just *no*, quietly, the way a man says no to weather.

"From Vera," Yara said. "The initials."

He took the paper from her. His hands were steady—he had taught them steadiness in field hospitals, she supposed—but the rest of him trembled faintly, like a struck bell past hearing. "Thursday," he said. "Day after tomorrow." He looked up. "Do you understand what this means? If Alistair comes back—if he sets foot in England—the record can't hold. You can't keep a man dead who keeps turning up at Victoria Station asking after his sister."

"You can try," Yara said. "Renwick will try."

That was the thing about joy, she was learning. It never arrived alone. It came through customs like everything else, and there was always a duty to pay.

* * *

The household spent Wednesday preparing for a resurrection, and it prepared the way it prepared for everything: with work.

Edith went to the wardrobe and stood before it for a long time, and Yara, laying out towels in the corridor, heard her say—to no one, to Alistair, to 1910—"You'll have put on weight. You always did, when you were miserable." Then she chose the grey suit, the good one, and made Mrs. Petch brush it twice, and did not explain why she wanted to look composed rather than beautiful. Composed was armor. Beautiful was surrender.

Mrs. Petch cleaned the blue room at the back of the house, the one that had been a linen store these three years because no one could bear to make it anything else. She stripped the dust sheets and found beneath them the room exactly as it had been left: the brass bedstead, the shelf of Kipling and Henty with their spines cracked from a boyhood reader, the watercolor of Dover cliffs that Alistair had bought for sixpence because, he'd said, it was the only honest painting of England ever made—it showed the country as a thin white edge over enormous grey nothing. Mrs. Petch beat the pillows out the window and wept into them where the street couldn't see, and when she came downstairs her eyes were red and her voice was perfectly level and she asked whether they'd want the good china, and no one remarked upon any of it, because that was how the household loved: laterally, through chores.

Tobias wrote a list. Of course he wrote a list; he had been an army clerk, lists were his liturgy. What to tell Renwick, if Renwick asked. What not to tell. Which of the letters should go back under the coal cellar floor and which should stay in the house for Alistair to confirm. He pinned the list above the kitchen table and by evening it had grown amendments in three different hands—Edith's decisive slashes, Yara's small precise additions, even Mrs. Petch had contributed a note about food—and it looked less like a plan than like a family arguing on paper, which was, Yara thought, the only way this family knew how to argue.

Yara herself did the thing she always did with unbearable news. She entered it in the ledger.

*Received, 10:42 p.m., telegram via V.C., Marseille. Subject: A.M. Status amended from deceased to living. Effective date of correction: Thursday.*

She sat with the pen hovering after that last line. *Effective date of correction.* That was ledger language. That was the language of accounts, where a mistake was not a tragedy but an entry to be reversed, where a dead brother could be written back into existence the same way you'd correct a transposed figure. She thought about the girl she had been at sixteen, arriving in this country with one trunk and a father's silence packed around her like straw, learning that history was something that happened in newspapers to strangers. She had built her whole life on that wall. And now history had climbed over it, whistling, carrying a telegram.

She wrote one more line beneath: *Cost of correction: unknown.*

* * *

Vera Calloway came at dusk, let in through the service entrance by arrangement, her coat still smelling of the train from King's Cross. She was a compact woman with nurse's hands and a face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and Yara had met her only once before, over tea and the ethics of forgery, and had liked her the way you like a blade you've been handed in a dark room: gratefully, and with caution.

"He insisted on wiring ahead," Vera said, sitting in the kitchen with tea untouched before her. "I told him silence was safer until he was ashore. He said Edith had waited three years for a letter to arrive late; she wasn't waiting another day past necessity." A ghost of a smile crossed her mouth. "He's stubborn. It's a family complaint."

"Is he well?" Edith asked. She had come down still in the grey suit though it was evening, as if dressing for Thursday early might hasten it. "Truly well?"

"He is thin. He is older than his years. There's a stiffness in his left side from the wound that he pretends isn't there." Vera wrapped both hands around the teacup without drinking. "But he is alive, Mrs. Marlowe, and he is lucid, and he has spent three years composing in his head exactly what he means to say to you, and I warn you now—I have heard fragments of it, and it is not an apology. It's an argument. He intends to arrive already fighting."

"Good," Edith said, and meant it, and Yara saw Vera register that—a flicker of reassessment, the nurse recalibrating a patient she had misread. "We quarreled properly once, my brother and I, and neither of us finished it. I'd rather finish it than forgive him unfinished."

"There's more," Vera said. "And I'll say it plainly, because plain speech is all I've got left. The moment that ship docks, Major Renwick knows. His people watch the passenger manifests the way monks watch prayer hours. Alistair coming home is not a private joy. It is a flare over open ground."

"Let him look," Edith said.

"Mrs. Marlowe—" Vera set down the cup. Her voice stayed level but something underneath it shifted, tectonic. "You have not spent years inside the machinery. I have. Renwick doesn't rage and he doesn't bluster; he files. By Friday there will be a memorandum. By Monday, charges—or worse, quiet arrangements, men paid, papers lost, your brother detained at some port 'pending clarification.' I have forged one death already to keep him free. I am telling you that keeping him free is about to become the hardest work either of us has ever done."

The kitchen clock ticked. On the wall, Tobias's list fluttered faintly in the draught from the door, thirty items long, none of them sufficient.

"Then we'd best enjoy Thursday," Edith said at last, "while it's still ours."

* * *

Yara slept badly and rose early, and on Thursday morning the house woke like a bride and a defendant at once.

She laid fires that didn't need laying. She checked the Daimler twice, then a third time, wiping the windscreen though rain hadn't fallen. At ten o'clock she drove Edith to Victoria—not to meet the train, which wasn't due until nearly four, but because Edith said she could not bear to spend the waiting hours indoors, and because Yara suspected the truth was that Edith could not bear to spend them anywhere but near the rails along which her brother would come.

They sat in the car on the approach road, engine off, and watched the station breathe. Luggage carts. Soldiers on embarkation leave moving in loose bright clusters, their uniforms still smelling of factory starch. A band somewhere playing something martial and cheerful. Porters shouting destinations like prophets: *Southampton! Boat train! Folkestone!*

"He used to meet me here," Edith said abruptly. "When I came up from school. He'd be fourteen and furious about having been made to come, holding a bunch of violets he clearly hadn't chosen himself." She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her. "He'd thrust them at me and look away, mortified by his own tenderness. Even then. Even at fourteen, tenderness embarrassed him."

"And now?" Yara asked. It was forward of her. Four years ago she would not have dared.

"Now I imagine he's learned what tenderness costs when you withhold it." Edith looked out at the tracks. "Three years of believing him dead, Yara. Do you know what I did with that? I made it useful. Grief makes excellent fuel. Every march, every hunger strike, every night in Holloway—I told myself Alistair was why I couldn't stop, that I owed the dead that much. And now the dead man writes to say he's taking the boat home, and I find I'm—" Her hands closed in her lap. "Terrified. Not of seeing him. Of discovering the grief was easier company than the man."

Yara said nothing. There was nothing in her creed for this. History happened elsewhere, she had believed for so long; but here it was, sitting beside her in the front seat of a Daimler, wearing a grey suit and confessing.

At a quarter to two, a man in a bowler paused near the car—too near, too long, his eyes making a small professional circuit of the registration plate, the luggage rack, Yara's chauffeur's cap. He walked on without hurry toward the taxi rank, where he lit a cigarette and did not smoke it.

"Inside?" Yara said quietly.

"Yes," Edith said. "And Yara—drive us home the long way afterward. If anyone follows, I should like to know it before supper, not before disaster."

* * *

The train was twenty minutes late, which everyone agreed later had been unbearable beyond description.

They stood on the platform among the crowd—Edith upright in the grey suit, Tobias a step behind with his cap in his hands, Yara at the edge of the family group, driver and witness both, belonging to it entirely and permitted to stand apart, which was, she realized, the position she preferred in every room of her life. Steam rolled down the platform in great white curtains. Doors banged. Voices called names.

And then, through the steam, a figure.

He was gaunt—that registered first, the gauntness, a body that had argued with hunger for years and partly lost. He moved with a careful asymmetry, the left leg trusted slightly less than the right. His hair had gone grey at the temples though he could not have been much past forty. He wore a civilian coat that fit him badly, the coat of a man dressed by other people's charity, and he carried one canvas bag and nothing else, because everything else he owned had been surrendered or buried or burned.

He stopped three feet from Edith.

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the platform roared around them—whistles, porters, a child crying somewhere, the vast indifferent machinery of arrival—and Yara, watching, thought: *this is what it looks like when history stops happening to other people.*

"Alistair," Edith said.

"I'm told," he said, and his voice was roughened and lower than she remembered, "that I've been dead three years. I hope you held a decent funeral. Knowing you, the eulogy was probably better than most sermons."

Edith made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and then the Marlowes did what Marlowes did with feeling—they shook hands. Formally, briefly, both of them gripping hard enough to hurt, both of them letting go too soon, both of them looking away. Tobias stepped forward and took the canvas bag, and Alistair looked at him with a question, and Tobias said, "Finch, sir. I kept your letters," and Alistair's face changed entirely, and he gripped the younger man's shoulder and said nothing at all, which between certain kinds of Englishmen is the longest sentence there is.

It was Vera who broke the tableau, practical to the last, steering them toward the exit with murmured instructions about tickets and luggage. And it was Yara who, falling into place a few steps behind, glanced back once down the receding platform—

—and saw the man in the bowler hat. Not smoking. Watching. Making a small note in a pocket book, unhurried as a clerk, closing the record of their reunion with one neat line.

By the time she turned to speak, he had melted into the crowd, and the platform doors swallowed them all, and the drive home began.

Yara drove the long way, as instructed. Twice she caught the gleam of a motorcar holding three cars' distance behind them, patient as paperwork. Edith saw it too, in the mirror, and said nothing, and straightened her back, and put her hand once—briefly, wordlessly—on her brother's knee, as the city slid by outside, gilded and oblivious, and somewhere ahead of them the evening waited with its questions, its accusations, and whatever Major Renwick had already begun to file.
