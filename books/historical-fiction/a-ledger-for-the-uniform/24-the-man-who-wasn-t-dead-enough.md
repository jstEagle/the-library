## 24. The Man Who Wasn't Dead Enough

The telegram had said the boat train from Marseille would arrive at Victoria at twenty past three, and by half past two Yara had already driven the Daimler around the block four times because Edith could not bear to be early and could not bear to wait.

"Again," Edith said from the back seat, though Yara was already turning. Her gloved hands lay flat on her knees in a posture Yara recognized from the march days — the stillness of a woman holding herself together with discipline alone. "I want to see the front of the station when he comes out of it. I want him to see me first."

"He will see you first, ma'am."

"I've imagined it three years." Edith's voice was dry as old paper. "In the imagining he steps off a ship at Southampton and I am there on the quay and neither of us says anything clever for once. In reality I expect we shall quarrel before we reach the car."

Yara said nothing. She had learned that Edith's prophecies were usually accurate, and that silence was sometimes the only kindness available between people who knew each other too well and not at all.

The house had spent the morning preparing itself the way households do when a ghost is expected: extra coal on the fire, the good sheets aired, Tobias — released only six days, thin as a winter branch but upright — carrying chairs about at Vera's direction until Vera finally told everyone to stop fussing because dead men did not notice the position of furniture. Yara had polished the Daimler until she could see her own face in the bonnet, which was a foolish thing to do and yet she had done it, because if history insisted on arriving at Victoria Station then it could arrive in a clean motorcar at least.

Now she brought the Daimler to rest beneath the iron ribs of the station forecourt and cut the engine, and the sudden quiet was enormous.

Edith did not get out. Neither did Yara. They sat in the ticking warmth of the cooling machine while porters' barrows rattled past and a boy sold evening papers shouting something about the Balkans, and Yara thought: *this is what it is like when history comes home.* Not elsewhere. Not to other people. Here, in a motorcar smelling of petrol and lavender water, with a woman beside her whose hands had gone white at the knuckles.

The train was late. Of course it was late. Trains from Marseille were always late, Vera had said, because French timetables were suggestions rather than promises, and this observation had been delivered with such flat certainty that no one had asked how she knew.

At ten to four the board clattered and changed, and the crowd on the platform side began to seep out through the barriers — sailors, commercial men, a family with too many boxes — and then, among them, a man who walked as though the ground owed him money.

Yara saw him before Edith did, or perhaps Edith saw him and refused to confirm it. He was gaunt. That was the word the telegram had prepared them for and it was insufficient; gaunt suggested thinness, whereas Alistair Marlowe looked as though someone had removed a layer of him entirely — skin over a frame that had once been broad, sun-darkened in a way no Englishman was darkened in England, his hair grey at the temples though he could not have been more than forty. He wore a coat that had been good five years ago and carried nothing but a canvas bag. No stick. His gait was even. Whatever had happened to him, he had walked out of it under his own power.

Edith opened the car door herself, before Yara could move, which Yara noted and filed away: *she will not be waited upon today.*

They stood facing each other across eight feet of wet paving stones, brother and sister, and did not speak.

Yara, still seated, hands on the wheel, watched. It was what she did. But watching had stopped being safe months ago; watching now meant feeling, and she felt the whole scene enter her like cold water.

Alistair moved first. He crossed the distance and stopped just short of touching range, and said, "You look well, Edie."

"You look," said Edith, "like a man who has been dead three years."

"That's roughly accurate."

"You might have written."

"I wrote constantly. You received my letters — I understand you received all of them eventually." His eyes went briefly to the Daimler, to Yara, acknowledging without greeting. "It seems everything arrived except me."

"Yes." Edith's voice cracked down the middle and she repaired it instantly, mortar over stone. "Well. Get in the car. You'll catch your death standing in a draught, and it would be inconvenient to bury you twice."

* * *

He sat in front, beside Yara, which caused a small seismic event in the order of things. Employers rode behind; chauffeurs drove. But Alistair climbed into the passenger seat as naturally as if he'd been riding in motorcars all his exile, dropped the canvas bag at his feet, and said, "Left at the corner. Or right. Whichever avoids the tram lines."

"You know London?" Yara asked, because someone had to say something and Edith had retreated into an offended silence behind them.

"I know London of 1910," he said. "Whether it's kept."

"It's kept some things."

He glanced at her sideways. Up close his eyes were pale grey, almost colourless, set deep in a face mapped with fine lines that had no business being on so young a man. "You're the driver who reads the mail."

Yara's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the car and into the road. "Ma'am—"

"Don't," Alistair said mildly. "Vera told me. She tells me everything now; three years of letters buys a man a confessor. She thinks you're the most honest person in that house, because you never pretended not to read." He turned back to the windscreen. "I approve. Anyone who reads other people's letters understands that the truth is never in the official version."

Behind them, Edith said, "Alistair, don't interrogate my staff within the first mile."

"I'm not interrogating. I'm being interrogated by circumstances. There's a difference."

"There is no difference with you. There never was."

And they were off — Yara could hear it coming, the collision gathering force behind her seat like weather — and she drove carefully through the Saturday traffic and let it happen, because stopping it was not her place and possibly not possible.

"You might have written to me directly," Edith said. "One line. *Alive. Well.* Two words. You cost me three years, Alistair. Three years of grieving a living man."

"And if I had written? If one letter had slipped past the censor, past Renwick's clerks, past whoever opens post in your circle — where would Vera be? Where would the eleven soldiers whose names are in that ledger be?" His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You of all people should understand that a secret kept is a life preserved. Your whole movement runs on it."

"My movement does not ask my own sister—" Edith stopped. Started again. "My movement does not require me to mourn my brother falsely while he drinks wine in Malta."

"Hospital tea," said Alistair. "Mostly. And I wasn't in Malta for the last year. I was in Marseille, in a room above a chandlery, learning French badly." He paused. "I'm sorry, Edie. I am. I've rehearsed this apology on trains for nine hundred miles and every version of it sounds exactly as inadequate as that one."

"Then say the true thing instead of the adequate thing. Why did you let them report you dead?"

Silence filled the car. Rain had begun, a fine drifting mist that beaded on the windscreen, and Yara switched on the wipers mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"The true thing," Alistair said finally, slowly, "is that Major Renwick held a document over Vera's head that would have seen her transported, and held another over mine that would have seen me shot, and the only exit either of us was offered was a closed file and a death notice. The true thing is that I took the exit. The true thing is that I have hated myself for it every single day since, and that hatred is not useful to anyone, least of all you, and yet here it is, arriving in your motorcar along with me."

Yara's hands were steady on the wheel. She was proud of that later.

* * *

The household assembled in the drawing-room at six, arranged with the accidental geometry of people who didn't know where to put themselves. Vera stood by the window with her arms folded, wearing the expression of a woman watching a verdict come in. Tobias sat on the edge of a chair near the fire, upright in the way of the recently imprisoned, hands flat on his thighs. Yara took up a position by the door — the servant's geography, the observer's post — with a tray of untouched sherry she was using as camouflage.

Alistair stood in the middle of the carpet, cleaned up somewhat, in a borrowed collar that didn't fit, looking at the room full of strangers who knew him better than most of his friends did.

"So," he said. "You're the clerk who wouldn't burn the letters."

Tobias rose. "Sir."

"Don't 'sir' me. I've been shot at by men who 'sir'd' me and it improved nothing." Alistair crossed to him and studied his face. "Renwick sent you to destroy the correspondence and you kept it instead. Do you know what that cost you?"

"Everything, apparently, sir. Sorry. Everything."

"It nearly cost me everything too," Alistair said, and then, unexpectedly, he put out his hand, and Tobias took it, and the two men shook once, hard, like men closing a bargain rather than greeting each other. "Thank you. That bundle of paper is the only reason I exist outside a filing cabinet."

Yara watched Tobias's ears go red and felt an absurd, fierce pride in him, as though she'd personally trained him to disobey orders.

Then Alistair turned to Vera, and the room's temperature changed.

"You shouldn't have come to England," he said quietly.

"Hello to you too."

"You know what I mean. Renwick knows your name now. He'll have known it for weeks — since the girl met you, since the letters started moving. You had safety in Malta. Distance."

"I had loneliness in Malta," Vera said. "Distance is not the same as safety, Alistair, whatever the army taught you. I forged a death certificate and a transfer order and eleven years of a nurse's reputation into that fiction. If I'm to hang for it, I'd rather hang where there's decent tea." She unfolded her arms. "Besides. You were coming whether I liked it or not. Someone had to meet you off the train who wouldn't cry."

"You're crying."

"I am not. It's the weather." But her hand went briefly to her face, and Yara looked away, suddenly an intruder in a moment she had no right to witness — and witnessed it anyway, because that was what she did, because she was building something out of these moments, brick by brick, a ledger of other people's love.

* * *

Dinner was laid for seven, which was absurd, and they ate it anyway: Alistair and Edith at opposite ends of the small table, Vera between them like sandbagging, Tobias and Yara flanking. Cook had produced a roast that nobody tasted.

The quarrel resumed over the soup, gently, and gathered force through the fish.

"—and I am telling you," Edith said, setting down her fork with a click, "that surrendering yourself is vanity dressed as honour. Renwick wants exactly that: a quiet court-martial, a closed door, the record sealed. You walk into his hands and he wins everything he's wanted since 1911."

"And I am telling you," Alistair returned, "that eleven families believe their sons deserted. Eleven women have lived with that. One of them died believing it — I read the parish record, Edie, Private Halloway's mother, 1912, heart failure, aged fifty-one. I killed her as surely as if I'd posted the lie myself." His hand trembled slightly as he lifted his glass. "You spend your life fighting for truth in public. Forgive me if I can't sit comfortably inside a private one."

"Truth in public gets women force-fed in Holloway," said Edith. "Truth needs strategy. Timing. Allies. You propose to stroll into the War Office with your confession and your honour and be devoured by lunchtime, and the record will stay shut regardless, because that is how records work — I have spent fifteen years learning how records work."

"Then teach me," Alistair said. "Instead of scolding me."

"I am teaching you. Scolding is my method. Ask anyone."

For a moment — one suspended, glittering moment — they almost laughed. Yara saw it happen: both mouths twitching, both gazes dropping to the tablecloth, three years of grief balanced on a knife-edge between fury and affection.

Then Alistair said, "You've grown hard, Edie."

"And you've grown righteous. We're both insufferable. Eat your dinner."

But the laughter had gone somewhere it couldn't easily be recalled from, and the rest of the meal proceeded in the strained politeness of a family performing itself for witnesses.

* * *

Afterward, Yara found him in the library, standing at the window with a glass of the good whisky he'd apparently located without help.

She had come to build the fire. That was her stated errand and she stuck to it, kneeling at the grate, laying kindling, striking the match, all the while aware of him watching her with those pale eyes.

"You're the one keeping the ledger," he said.

Her hands stilled on the poker. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I deduced it. Vera writes to me in a code we invented in Malta — trivial things, the price of eggs, the health of imaginary aunts — and in her last letter she mentioned that the household had acquired 'an accountant.' Then the telegram came from this address, signed with initials I've never heard of, in a hand far too controlled for Edith's." He smiled faintly. "Also, you read everyone's mail and drive a motorcar and haven't once asked me a question about myself, which means you already know the answers. That's ledger behaviour."

Yara set the poker down and stood. Outside, the rain had thickened against the glass, and the lamplight made amber coins of it.

"When you were reported dead," she said, "a woman in this house set a place for you at Christmas for two years. Cook did it. Nobody instructed her to. And Edith wore black for eleven months and then took it off abruptly on a Tuesday and worked harder than ever, and everyone pretended not to notice that she never once said your name aloud again." She turned to face him fully. "I have been in this family four years, Mr. Marlowe, and I am not in it, technically, I am employed by it, but I watched it lose you, and I watched what the losing did. So I will tell you what I'd tell any account in arrears: the debt isn't to your honour. Honour doesn't eat. The debt is to the people who set your place at the table."

Alistair regarded her for a long moment. The fire crackled up behind her and threw moving light across his ruined young-old face.

"Edith said you were a spectator," he said. "That night in October, after Holloway — she wrote to me of it, in code, before I understood I could trust her. She said her driver watched history through a windscreen."

"Did she." Yara kept her voice level. "And what do you say?"

"I say spectators don't forge passage documents." He raised his glass slightly, a salute. "Or keep ledgers. Or stand in libraries telling ghosts the truth about themselves. Whatever you are, Miss Nakamura, you're not a spectator. You may want to inform my sister before she loses another argument to you without knowing it."

Downstairs, faintly, the hall clock struck ten. And beneath its chime Yara heard the front-door bell — a single, deliberate peal, the kind of ring that belonged to callers who arrived by appointment rather than affection.

Vera appeared in the doorway of the library, very still, her face drained to paper.

"That will be the police," she said.
