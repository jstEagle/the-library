## 26. Vera's Gamble

Vera Calloway had learned to measure a man's danger by the way he held his hat.

Major Renwick held his like a man who expected the world to be handed to him brim-first—by the crown, fingers loose, as though it were an object of no consequence that happened to belong to him. He sat in the back of her lodging house parlour on a Tuesday morning in October, his grey coat brushed, his boots polished to a dull military shine, and he looked around the room at her second-hand furniture and her nurse's clock and her single framed photograph of Valletta harbour with the expression of a man taking inventory for a confiscation.

"You've done well for yourself," he said. "Considering."

Considering. The word did more work than a whole regiment. Considering you were nobody. Considering you were a woman. Considering you forged the death notice of Lieutenant Alistair Marlowe and set three years of machinery grinding after a ghost.

Vera poured tea she did not want and he did not intend to drink. Her hands were steady. She had practised this—the steadiness—in hospital corridors where men screamed and one's hands had no business shaking, because shaking hands dropped things, and dropped things cost lives. She thought of it now as she had thought of it then: *steady hands, steady hands*, and beneath it, older, the Maltese lilt of her training sister's voice, *Sister Calloway, your patient is dying and you are admiring your own composure.*

"Major," she said, "you've come a long way from Whitehall to tell me something I could read in the papers."

"I've come," Renwick said, "because you've been writing letters."

The tea went cold in her mouth before she swallowed it.

* *

Here is what the world knew of Vera Calloway: that she was thirty-one years old; that she had trained at the Bighi naval hospital in Malta, where the light came off the Grand Harbour like hammered tin and the wounded came up from the Dardanelles smelling of iodine and salt; that she had come to London in 1912 with excellent references and taken a post at a private nursing home in Kensington, where she was praised for her discretion. Discretion. That word again, always attached to women who kept things.

Here is what the world did not know: that in March of 1911, in a ward that smelled of carbolic and fig trees through the open shutters, a clerk named Tobias Finch had been carried in with his thigh opened to the bone by shrapnel, screaming not from the wound but from the fear of it, and that Lieutenant Alistair Marlowe—who should by every rule of rank have been elsewhere—had sat by the boy all night with his hand flat on the uninjured knee, saying nothing, doing nothing, merely being there, which was sometimes the whole of medicine. And here is what only three people alive knew: that Alistair Marlowe had died in that war after all—not at Gallipoli, not under Turkish fire, but in a small room above the hospital stores, at the hands of his own commanding officer's necessity, when he refused, for the third time, to sign away the names of soldiers he'd caught Colonel Hartwell extorting.

Not shot. Not even struck. He had simply been given a choice between two kinds of death, and the paperwork was drawn up before he finished choosing. *Killed in action, body unrecoverable.* The cleanest lie in the empire, four words long, stamped and filed.

Except that Vera Calloway had been in the room next door, and had heard everything, and had made her own choice about whose paperwork would survive.

She had not planned it. Planning implies leisure, and there had been none. There had been only Alistair Marlowe, alive against all orders, hiding in the linen store because Hartwell wanted him dead properly or gone permanently, and Vera standing in the doorway with a death certificate in her hand—a real one, for a private named Ellison who had indeed died that morning and whom no one in England would miss or identify—and hearing herself say, in a voice she barely recognised as her own:

"There's a ship leaving for Marseille on Thursday. You'll be on it as Private Thomas Ellison, deceased as of Friday. I'll write the notice myself. I write very convincing notices."

He had looked at her the way men look at water in a desert—wanting to believe, afraid to drink.

"Why?" he had asked.

And she, who had rehearsed no answer because she had not known she would need one, said the true thing: "Because I'm tired of being the person who watches them decide things and then cleans up after. This once, I'd like to decide something."

* *

Renwick set down his untouched cup with a small ceramic click that sounded, in the quiet parlour, like a lock turning.

"Mrs. Calloway—Miss—I'll be direct, as between professionals."

"We're not colleagues, Major."

"We are both in the business of records," he said mildly. "You keep them. I keep them. Occasionally we amend them. The difference is that mine carry the authority of His Majesty's Government, and yours carry the authority of—" he gestured at the room, at her, with two fingers, a small contemptuous fan "—a nurse."

Vera folded her hands in her lap so that he would not see them do anything interesting.

"And what record is troubling you today?"

"Marlowe." He said the name the way one reads a file number. "The household in Hampstead has developed an interest. The sister is a suffragist with more courage than sense, and she has acquired letters. Recent letters. In a dead man's hand. There is a young man involved—Finch, formerly of my own section, a clerk, forgettable—and a driver, some foreign girl, clever beyond her station. Between them they are assembling a story. Stories, Miss Calloway, are like unexploded shells. It isn't the explosion that worries me first. It's who's standing near it when it goes off."

"You're worried for us," Vera said. "How touching."

"I am worried for order." For the first time something moved behind his face—not warmth, never warmth, but the flicker of a man showing you the engine room because he believes you cannot understand it. "Do you know what happens if it becomes known that a lieutenant of the Norfolk Regiment was declared dead at his commanding officer's discretion? Not the scandal—scandal washes out. The precedent. Every widow with a grievance, every Member with a grudge, every crank in Christendom demanding to see the actual bodies. The whole architecture of confidence rests on the public believing that our paperwork describes reality. We cannot have a living demonstration that it doesn't."

"So the truth must be buried deeper than the man."

"The truth must be *managed*," Renwick said, with the patience of a schoolmaster. "That is all governance is, madam. Management. Now. I am authorised to offer you terms. You will cease correspondence with the Marlowe house. You will provide me with copies of any documents in your possession relating to Lieutenant Marlowe, Private Ellison, or the period of your service at Bighi. And you will consider yourself, henceforth, observed. If these terms are met, nothing unpleasant attaches to you. Your references remain intact. Your little life continues." He smiled. It was a good smile, well maintained, like his boots. "If not—well. Forging the death notice of an officer of His Majesty's Army carries a certain penalty. And a woman alone, foreign-trained, no family, no protector—" he spread his hands, apologetic, "—juries are sentimental about nurses but not about forgers."

There it was. Laid out on the tea table between them like instruments on a tray: her life, itemised, priced, and found cheap.

Vera looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood, went to the bureau in the corner, unlocked the bottom drawer with a key from her chatelaine, and returned with a slim black ledger.

Renwick's eyes changed. It was very slight—the pupils widening a fraction, the stillness of a man who has heard a floorboard creak in an empty house—but Vera Calloway had spent eleven years reading faces across bedsheets, reading the face that lied about pain and the face that lied about hope, and she saw it, and she let him see that she saw it.

"What," Renwick said carefully, "is that?"

"This," said Vera, "is Colonel Hartwell's accounts book. The original. Not a copy. The one in his own hand, kept in the strongbox behind his quarters at Bighi, which he believed was secure because he assumed nurses can't pick locks." She laid it on the table between the teacups. "Dates. Names. Sums. Which officers paid him, how much, for which silences. Two majors, one member of Parliament's brother-in-law, a shipping contract, and—" she turned a page delicately, like turning the page of a hymnal "—the entry for March the ninth, nineteen-eleven. *Disposal, Marlowe, Lt. A.—settled.* Settled, Major. As one settles a debt. Your Colonel was a tidy bookkeeper. It's why I trusted the figures."

Renwick did not touch it. He had stopped moving entirely, the way men stop when they step onto ice and hear it speak.

"That book is a fabrication," he said.

"Of course it is," Vera agreed pleasantly. "I'm a forger. I told you—I write very convincing notices. Paper, ink, hand, ageing, the lot. I learned it making death certificates, and I improved upon it out of boredom and principle. So here is your difficulty, Major, and I do encourage you to take your time with it, because I certainly took mine." She sat down again, unhurried, and folded her hands. "Either this ledger is genuine, in which case your Colonel's crimes are sitting on my tea table and you will do anything—*anything*—to prevent it surfacing. Or it is a fabrication, in which case you must prove it false, publicly, in a court, with experts and testimony, which means opening the entire Bighi record to daylight, which means Hartwell's real books—which I assure you exist, and which I have seen, and which I have described in detail to a solicitor who holds a sealed copy of my affidavit and instructions to publish upon my death, arrest, deportation, or prolonged absence from my lodgings." She picked up her cold tea and sipped it, grimacing faintly. "It's rather stewed. Would you like fresh?"

For a moment—one long, glittering moment—she saw the possibility pass through his eyes that he might simply have her killed, here, in this parlour, and search the rooms after. She had accounted for that too; the affidavit was not in the rooms. But she met his gaze steadily, and something in her steadiness told him what he needed to know: that this woman had already imagined this exact moment, had rehearsed it, had perhaps even wanted it, and that people who want the confrontation are dangerous in ways calculation cannot price.

"Blackmail," Renwick said at last, quietly.

"Insurance," Vera corrected. "Blackmail is when you ask for money. I'm asking for silence. Yours, specifically. And the withdrawal of your attention from the Marlowe household—all of them. The sister. The clerk. The girl who drives the motorcar. You will lose their addresses, Major. Lose them thoroughly. Because if so much as a police constable calls at that house without cause—if Miss Marlowe is re-arrested under your new Cat-and-Mouse arrangement with unusual efficiency, if Mr. Finch is detained over some pretextual warrant—if anything happens to any of them that smells of Whitehall, my solicitor publishes, and the empire learns that its paperwork describes reality rather less than its citizens have been led to believe." She smiled at him then, and was surprised to find the smile felt warm, almost tender, though nothing in her was warm. "You see, I spent years cleaning up after men who decided things. I told one of them that I wanted to decide something instead. I find I've become quite decided since."

* *

After he left—the ledger staying on her table, because she gave him nothing and he dared not demand it, though his eyes went to the drawer twice on his way to the door—Vera stood at her window and watched him cross the street below, hat held correctly now, gripped, like a man carrying something heavier than it looked.

Then, only then, her hands began to shake.

She sat down on the hard chair by the window and let them, because there was no one to see, and this too was part of the discipline: a trembling allowed only in private, budgeted for, scheduled like medication. She thought about what she had just done. She had threatened a major of the British Army in his own empire, in his own language, on his own terms, and won—or appeared to win, which in her experience was the same thing right up until it wasn't. She had spent the last coin of her safety. The affidavit existed; the sealed copy existed; but paper protects no one, paper only promises, and she had just promised Renwick a war if he touched her people.

Her people. When had they become that? The word sat strangely in her chest, like a coin of the wrong currency. She had come to England alone, as she had done everything alone, because alone was safe, alone was portable, alone was the one possession no one could confiscate. She had built her life the way she built her forgeries: thin layers, careful aging, nothing that could be traced back to a single indisputable origin. No family. No history that could be checked. A name chosen from a shipping manifest because it sounded English enough and meant nothing to anyone. She had spent eleven years becoming a woman with no past, and now she had gone and acquired one—a sister in Hampstead with ink on her fingers, a soldier's ghost in her letters, a chauffeur girl with watchful eyes and a ledger of her own (oh yes, Vera had noticed; recognising a fellow archivist was the one skill her old life had left her), a trembling clerk who kept what he was ordered to burn. A household of people who kept things. Who wrote things down. Who, each in their own clumsy, brave, doomed way, were refusing to let the world's paperwork stand unchallenged.

She laughed aloud, once, a short sound with no humour in it, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

The cost of forging a life, she thought, is that eventually you must live inside it. And the cost of deciding things—she had warned herself of this in Malta, in the linen store, in the moment before she handed a dead man's name to a living one—is that decisions have consequences, and consequences arrive wearing good coats and holding their hats wrong.

Outside, the October light was going thin and yellow over the rooftops. Somewhere north, in Hampstead, Edith Marlowe was weakening between hunger strike and cell door, and a girl named Yara was driving prison gates like a ferry-woman, and somewhere in Whitehall Renwick was at this hour dictating a memorandum, altering a file, managing the truth—and somewhere south, God help them all, a man who had been legally dead for three years was preparing to board a boat at Marseille.

Vera rose, took the black ledger from the table, and weighed it in her hands. Hartwell's real entries were in it after all—she had copied them faithfully, every damning figure, because a forgery anchored in truth was the only kind worth the gallows risk. One book. One small black book against the whole machinery of an empire's confidence.

It would have to be enough.

She locked it away, then took out paper and pen, and in a disguised hand—the hand of a fictional correspondent, one of her best—began the letter she had hoped never to send:

*To Miss Yara Nakamura, care of the Marlowe residence, Hampstead.*

*Madam,—He came on Tuesday. I have bought our silence at a price he does not yet believe I can pay, and he will test whether I can. Expect his attention to turn from you toward the source of my leverage, which means, in time, toward Malta, and toward a boat arriving from Marseille. Whatever you have planned, plan faster. And whatever you do, do not let the old man surrender quietly—that is exactly the ending he wants, because quiet men disappear, and I have spent my whole life ensuring that this one doesn't.*

*Yours, in the trade of records,*

*V.C.*

She sealed it, addressed it in a third hand altogether, and set it on the hall table for the evening post. Then she went to the window again and watched the street, where the lamps were coming on one by one, each a small act of defiance against the dark, and waited—as she suspected they all were waiting, all of them, the whole strange family of keepers—for the knock, or the telegram, or the dawn, whichever arrived first.
