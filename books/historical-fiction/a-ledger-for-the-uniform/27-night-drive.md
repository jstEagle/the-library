## 27. Night Drive

The Daimler left the mews at half past eleven, its headlamps hooded to two yellow slits, and Yara drove it the way she had learned to drive everything: as though she were merely furniture that happened to be moving. That was the trick of the uniform, she had decided years ago—buttons, cap, goggles pushed up on her forehead like a second set of blind eyes. Nobody looked at a chauffeur. They looked *through* a chauffeur, the way they looked through footmen and parlourmaids and the girls who sold matches outside the Underground stations. Invisibility was not a gift; it was a trade, and tonight she intended to spend every coin of it.

Alistair Marlowe sat behind her, in the seat he had once owned by right before the sea took his name. He had insisted on sitting forward, in the open, beside her, and Edith had overruled him with one sentence: *"You will ride like luggage, Alistair, because luggage is not questioned."* So he rode in the enclosed rear compartment with Vera Calloway, and Yara heard them only as murmurs through the glass partition, two voices braided tight, saying things that did not need to be said aloud because three years and a forged death notice lay between them.

The night was the colour of wet slate. Rain had come in off the estuary around ten and now hung in the air without committing itself, fine as sieved flour, beading on the windscreen until the wipers smeared it into long grey commas. Yara knew the route by heart because she had driven it in her head forty times since Tuesday: out past Hammersmith where the gasworks breathed their orange light into the clouds, along the Bath Road through Brentford, then south before Guildford onto lanes that narrowed to the width of a coffin. Ninety miles to Newhaven. The packet boat for Dieppe sailed at four-forty, whether or not the world approved.

In the toolbox under her seat, wrapped in an oily rag beneath the jack, lay the ledger. Not all of it—she had left the earliest pages hidden in the coal cellar with the rest of the archive—but the last month of entries, copied out in her smallest hand. Dates. Names. Sums. The postal marks from Malta. The times Renwick's grey saloon had passed the house, and how slowly, and on which nights. If the car was stopped, if the night went wrong, the rag and the jack were supposed to go into the river at Farnham and the ledger with them. She had practised the motion in the garage until her wrist knew it without her mind's permission.

She had not told anyone about that arrangement. Some things a chauffeur keeps the way a priest keeps confession.

* * *

They cleared London without incident. At Chiswick a constable stood under a lamp with his cape shining like a beetle's back, and he waved them past with the bored majesty of a man whose whole evening consisted of waving things past. Yara kept her speed exactly lawful. She had learned from Edith that the powerful are rarely stopped, and from Vera that the caught are almost always stopped for some small sin first—a lamp out, a turn signal forgotten, a hat worn at the wrong angle. She committed no small sins. The Daimler purred through the checkpoint like a well-bred animal passing other dogs.

It was after Brentford that Alistair knocked on the partition glass.

"Miss Nakamura." His voice came thin through the speaking tube. "May we talk?"

"You may talk," she said. "I'm driving."

A pause, and then something that might have been a laugh, quickly smothered. She liked him better for it. In the four days since Marseille had sent its telegram and Alistair had walked up the area steps looking like a man assembled hastily from spare parts, Yara had catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: he apologised too much and too precisely, as though courtesy were a debt he could still service; he flinched at the smell of antiseptic; he watched doors. He was, she thought, what happened when a decent man tried to erase himself and mostly succeeded.

"Vera says you read everything," he said. "Letters, ledgers, the lot."

"Someone has to know what's going in and out of that house," Yara said. "I seal most of it unread."

"And the rest?"

"The rest I read twice."

Another smothered sound from the tube—Vera this time, unmistakably—and then Alistair again, quieter. "Then you know more about my death than I do. I only ever saw the inside of it."

Yara let that sit. The road unspooled ahead, black ribbon stitched with rain. Somewhere beyond the hedgerows the war was still a rumour, a gathering weather front, but here in the car it was already inside everything: in the way Alistair spoke of himself in the past tense, in the way Vera's hand had shaken signing the false register at the nursing home, in the berth—one berth—that sat purchased and waiting in Edith's strongbox like a loaded gun.

"You were dead three years," Yara said at last. "That's longer than some marriages."

"I know what I cost." The words came flat, rehearsed by solitude. "I've cost everyone. My sister aged ten years in my absence. Vera gave up—" A rustle; Vera must have touched his arm. He started again. "What I mean to say is that whatever happens tonight, the fault line runs through me. You're driving us to the coast for my sake, and you barely know me, and I'd understand if you'd rather—"

"Mr. Marlowe." She said it crisply, the way she announced arrivals at the front door. "I have driven Mrs. Pankhurst through a mob in Kensington. I have driven your sister to Holloway eleven times, counting the time the matron made us wait four hours in the rain. I once changed a tyre on Putney Bridge while two detectives watched and pretended not to, because watching was their job and pretending was mine." She adjusted the choke. "I am not driving you to the coast for your sake. I am doing it because the record is wrong, and I don't tolerate wrong records. Ask anyone. It's a character flaw."

Through the glass, faintly, Vera said something in what sounded like approval, and Alistair said nothing at all, which Yara took as agreement or surrender, and found both acceptable.

* * *

The first real checkpoint came at Guildford, where the road crossed under the railway bridge and someone with authority had decided that authority should be visible.

Two lanterns. Three men. A motorcycle with its engine ticking as it cooled, and a sergeant with a clipboard who stepped out into the road with his palm raised, unhurried, a man stopping traffic the way he might stop a clock. Yara brought the Daimler down through the gears so smoothly that the body barely nodded. Behind her, through the partition, she felt rather than heard the silence change quality—the difference between people resting and people holding their breath.

"Evening, sir." The sergeant bent to the driver's window, and his lantern threw his face into planes. Rain dripped off his cape brim. "Where from?"

"The Marlowe residence, Richmond," Yara said. Truth, or the shape of truth. Lies were heavy cargo; she preferred ballast that floated.

"Where to?"

"Brighton. My mistress's brother is unwell. We're fetching him to a specialist in town tomorrow." She gestured vaguely rearward. "He can't abide trains just now. Nerves."

The sergeant wrote something. The word *nerves* performed quiet miracles on officialdom; it explained slowness, pallor, odd hours, and any reluctance to make eye contact, all at once. He moved along the flank of the car, and Yara watched him in the wing mirror the way she had learned to watch everything—peripherally, patiently, a woman built entirely of attention.

He paused at the rear door. Knocked once.

Yara's hand drifted below the dash, rested on the cold metal of the toolbox latch, and stayed there. Under her palms the wheel hummed with the idling engine. She thought, with strange clarity, of the coal cellar, of the false wall she and Tobias had mortared by lamplight, of the archive sleeping behind brick like a heart behind ribs. Whatever happened in the next ninety seconds would become an entry either way. She found she preferred to write the entry herself.

The door opened. Lamplight spilled into the back of the car.

"Good evening." Vera's voice, pitched low and tired, with the precise flatness of a nurse who has been awake too long. "If you're collecting names, Sergeant, mine is Calloway, and I'd be grateful if you'd collect it quickly. This gentleman has been given morphia and I'm to have him in Brighton before his pain returns, and I'd rather not explain to Dr.—" she produced a name, plausible, local, "—why his patient spent an hour talking to the police."

The sergeant looked at Alistair, who was doing the finest piece of work Yara had ever witnessed from him: he simply *wasn't there*. He sat slumped against the upholstery, eyes half-closed, mouth slack, a man hollowed out by illness and opiates, and it struck Yara that this was the truest thing he had done in three years—playing dead was the one role he knew by heart.

"Nerves, is it?" the sergeant asked.

"Shell shock," Vera said, and let the word fall between them like something surgical.

The sergeant closed the door gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom. He came back to Yara's window, consulted his clipboard, and said, "There's a naval party at Newhaven checking papers. If you're only going to Brighton you'll want the Lewes road instead."

"Thank you, Sergeant. Brighton. Of course."

He waved them through, and the lanterns shrank in the mirror, and Yara held the lawful speed for a full mile before she allowed herself to breathe properly. Then, into the speaking tube: "Newhaven is being watched. Naval party. Papers."

A silence. Then Vera: "Then we go to Shoreham. There's a coasting steamer, the *Sussex Belle*, calls at Littlehampton Thursdays. It's slower. It's also nobody's business."

"It's Wednesday," Yara said.

"I know," said Vera. "We'll wait in an inn and be dull. Dullness is the best disguise I know. I've worn it for years."

* * *

They took the lanes after that, and the rain thickened, and the world contracted to the reach of the headlamps. Yara drove with the concentration of a surgeon and the memory of a cartographer; she had memorised the Ordnance Survey of Sussex the way other women memorised hymns, in the garage, by lamplight, during the long hours when the household slept and she did not. Left at the burnt oak. Right where the chalk cut through. Every crossroads was a decision, and for perhaps the first time in her life she understood that she had been making decisions all along—only they had been small ones, sealed envelopes, unread letters, and now they had grown large enough to carry passengers.

At a gate near Storrington they stopped to let a flock of sheep pass, and in the pause Alistair leaned forward to the tube.

"Miss Nakamura. When this is done—when we're away—I intend to stand up publicly. Court-martial myself, if that's what it takes. Clear Vera's name. Clear Tobias's." A breath. "Edith will hate it. But I spent three years letting other people hold my story for me. I won't spend a fourth."

Yara watched the sheep stream white through the lamplight, hundreds of small decisions moving in one direction because one dog had decided.

"Good," she said. "Wrong records are a kind of theft. I keep accounts."

The sheep passed. The gate swung. She drove on.

* * *

The inn at Littlehampton was called the Anchor & Hope, which Yara considered the most honest pub sign in England, and she put the Daimler behind its stable block where the grooms' lanterns wouldn't find it. She registered at the desk as "Mrs. Nakamura, travelling with her brother and his nurse"—a fiction so thin it was nearly lace, but the landlord was half-asleep and the rain was doing the rest of the work.

They took tea in a private room upstairs, and for an hour the three of them were merely tired people in a warm place, and Yara felt the strangeness of it settle over her like the coat she wasn't wearing: she was *inside* the evening now, not observing it through glass. For six years she had stood at the edge of history with a chauffeur's cap pulled low, believing it happened elsewhere—to marchers arrested, to brothers drowned, to sisters imprisoned—while she remained a spectator with excellent posture. Now she had lied to a sergeant, rerouted a fugitive, memorised a coastline, and chosen an inn, and the machinery of the night turned on those choices. History had not happened *to* her tonight. It had been *driven* by her, at a lawful thirty miles an hour, through the rain.

"Do you ever write it down?" Vera asked suddenly. She sat across the table, hands curled round her cup. "All of it. What you see. What you do. People like us—we alter the record, you and I. Forgers, drivers. We decide which version survives." Her eyes were steady and very dark. "Somebody ought to keep the true one."

Yara thought of the ledger under the seat, wrapped in its oily rag, riding through police checkpoints like contraband scripture.

"Somebody does," she said.

Vera nodded slowly, as if a suspicion had been confirmed, and said nothing more, and the two of them drank their tea in the companionable silence of women who understood each other's crimes.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when Yara went down to check the car, and the yard was empty except for the rain and one figure standing by the stable block with an umbrella he clearly didn't need, because he wore it tilted back like a man who wanted his face known.

Grey coat. Grey hat. The face from the slow passes outside the house in Richmond, seen through windscreen glass a dozen times, always at the edge of vision, always patient.

Major Renwick smiled the way a bank manager smiles.

"Miss Nakamura," he said pleasantly. "Lovely night for a drive."

Her mind did what it had trained itself to do: itemised. Distance to the kitchen door, twenty feet. Distance to the road, sixty. The weight of the toolbox. The position of the stable boy's lantern. And beneath the arithmetic, cold and rising, a single entry writing itself:

*R. appeared at Littlehampton, 2 a.m. Someone talked.*

"Major," she said, and did not move from between him and the stable block.
