## 29. Holloway Again

The morning of the visits began with ink.

Yara filled the fountain pen twice before she trusted it, laid three sheets of paper on the kitchen table, and wrote in her smallest hand—the hand she had learned copying figures in Yokohama, the hand that could fit a whole sentence into a space no wider than a thumbnail. The warders at Holloway would read whatever passed through those gates; she had no illusions about that. But small writing discouraged reading. Small writing looked like nothing. A shopping list, an account, a servant's arithmetic. She had spent four years making herself look like nothing, and it was, at last, going to be useful.

*Miss M— is well. She eats what they bring and keeps the rest for argument. Her hands are steady. She asked after you by name.*

She blotted it, folded it into eighths, and slid it inside her glove, where it lay against her palm like a second pulse. Then she took up the motor keys, checked her collar, checked her face in the dark glass of the pantry door—a woman of no particular consequence, uniform pressed, expression set to neutral—and went out to the Daimler.

Frost had come in the night. It lay on the bonnet like a skin of salt, and when she cranked the engine the cold bit through her gloves into the bones of her hands. She let it bite. Pain was a kind of bookkeeping too; it told you you were still present, still liable, still owed something to the day.

Two visits. One morning. One man held at the toll house on a charge of documents he had never touched, and one woman held in a cell on a charge of being inconvenient to the Empire. And between them, all day, herself: the courier, the ledger, the living line drawn from one cell to another with a chauffeur's cap pulled low over her eyes.

She drove north through streets still blue with early cold, past hoardings papered with recruiting appeals that hadn't been there in September, past boys in scout uniforms drilling with broom handles in a churchyard. The world was rehearsing. Everyone could feel it underfoot, like distant artillery heard through water. Yara kept her eyes on the road and did not rehearse. She had decided, somewhere between the coal cellar and the quay, that she was done watching history from the kerb. Now she simply carried it, in her glove, against her palm.

* * *

They kept Tobias Finch in a holding annex behind the magistrate's court—not Holloway proper, though the papers said otherwise—but the visiting procedure was the same everywhere in London that winter: a corridor smelling of carbolic and wet wool, a grille, a bench, a matron with a ledger of her own who wrote down names with the satisfaction of a woman adding figures she already knew would balance.

Yara signed herself *M. Nakamura, secretary to Mrs. E. Marlowe*, and waited forty minutes on a bench whose slats had been polished by decades of waiting. Through the high window she could see a slice of grey sky and the top of a wall. Somewhere beyond it, men were counting days the way she counted mileage.

When they brought him in, her first thought was that he had shrunk. Not his body—he sat as tall as ever on the far side of the grille—but something around him had been pared away, the way a pencil wears down doing honest work. His cuffs were unbuttoned. His hair had been cut badly, by someone in a hurry, or someone making a point.

"Miss Nakamura." He almost smiled. "You found the right building. I half expected them to turn you away."

"They don't turn away secretaries," she said. "Secretaries are furniture. It's one of the few advantages of the position." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing laundry. The matron stood within earshot, pretending to read her ledger. "I've brought news. You'll forgive me if I deliver it like an inventory. I find inventories travel well through locked doors."

"Please." He leaned toward the grille. "Inventories are my mother tongue."

"Then: item one. Mr. Alistair Marlowe reached Marseille on the ninth and sailed on the twelfth. He is alive, he is angry, and according to Miss Calloway's last letter he has grown a beard that makes him look like a disreputable apostle. Item two. Major Renwick has not renewed the watch on the house. Whether that means he has given up or changed tactics, we cannot yet say. Item three—" and here she allowed her voice to drop, so that only he could hear it through the mesh, "—Mrs. Marlowe knows what you did at the toll. She knows you claimed the documents were yours alone. She has instructed me to tell you that this was unnecessary, reckless, and exactly what she would have done herself, and that she is furious with you, which is how she loves people."

Tobias laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered—and then his face did something complicated, grief and gratitude fighting for the same ground. "Three years ago," he said quietly, "I watched a man carry a wounded soldier two miles on his back because the stretcher-bearers wouldn't come into the fire. He never once said it was unnecessary. He just did it, and swore at me the whole way for bleeding on his collar." He looked down at his hands. "I keep thinking about that. About how some people carry things and never make the carrying into a debt."

"He'd say the debt works the other direction," Yara said. "He'd say the carrying is the payment."

"And what do you say?"

Yara considered the question properly, because Tobias was the sort of person who noticed if you answered carelessly. "I say I'm tired," she said. "And that tiredness is not a reason to stop. That's my whole philosophy this winter. It fits on a coin."

He smiled then, fully, and for a moment the room was warmer than its pipes could account for. Then the smile faded into something more careful. "There's something else. You should know—they questioned me again yesterday. Not about the documents. About the household. Who visits. Who sleeps there. Whether the Japanese girl reads the post." He said it plainly, without apology, because he had learned that plainness was the only mercy worth offering. "Renwick isn't hunting me, Yara. I'm the excuse. He's mapping your house."

"I know." She had known since Vera's warning, since the man who stood too long outside the tobacconist across the road, since the second visit from the insurance assessor asking questions no assessor would ask. "Let him map it. Maps go out of date. We move faster than his clerks."

"And Edith? Truly? They're force-feeding the women in there. You know that. It's in the papers now, they can't hide it."

"She is not on hunger strike at present," Yara said. "She is eating strategically. Her word. She says hunger is a weapon and weapons should be fired at the correct moment, not discharged wildly in the dark." She paused. "That is nearly verbatim. She'll be pleased to hear she frightened you."

"She frightens everyone," Tobias said, "including, I suspect, herself. It's why she wins."

A bell rang somewhere down the corridor, flat and final. The matron closed her ledger with a snap like a small verdict. Five minutes' warning.

Yara drew off her left glove and, under cover of passing him her handkerchief—for prisoners were permitted handkerchiefs, and no one inspected a handkerchief closely—slid the folded paper from inside it into the linen's hem. "For your pocket. Read it when they take you back. It's nothing. An account of small domestic matters. The cat next door, the state of the roses." She met his eyes through the grille. "You understand me."

"I understand," he said. "Item four: the roses are fine." And his voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he covered it by coughing, and she pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was also a form of love, and she was learning all its dialects.

At the door he turned. "Yara. When this is over—whatever over looks like—I owe you a debt I can't itemize."

"No," she said. "You don't. That's the whole point of us." And she walked out before either of them could ruin it with more words.

* * *

Holloway itself was a different country.

The prison rose out of Camden like a monument nobody wanted, its brick walls stained with soot and older weather, its gates framed in stone carved with pious sentiments about repentance that had long since worn smooth of meaning. Yara parked the Daimler where a constable indicated, and sat for a moment without moving, hands on the wheel, engine ticking as it cooled.

She had driven Edith Marlowe to a hundred meetings. She had waited outside a hundred halls, listening through doors, learning the movement's vocabulary the way you learn a language by immersion—quorum, resolution, deputation, the Cat and Mouse Act, that obscene little name, as though the women were animals and the law merely playful. But she had never before come here. Prisons, in Yara's private cosmology, belonged to the category of things that happened to other people. She was about to walk through the gate of that category and find out what it cost.

Inside, the air was stone-cold and disinfectant, and every sound arrived late, muffled by walls built to swallow noise and never quite succeeding. A wardress with a face like a closed ledger took her details. *Name. Relationship to prisoner. Purpose of visit.* Yara wrote *secretary* and felt the word do its work, felt herself pass through the machinery of the place as something beneath notice. There was power in being underestimated, she thought. It was the only power the century had offered her so far, and she intended to spend it well.

They brought Edith Marlowe into the visiting room ten minutes later, and Yara had to school her face not to react.

Edith had lost weight before her arrest—hunger strikes did their work in installments, and the body remembered each one—but prison had refined the loss into something architectural. Her cheekbones stood out like struts. Her hands, folded on the table before her, were steady, but the steadiness had a quality of effort about it, like a bridge holding in wind. Her hair had gone greyer at the temples in six weeks. And her eyes—her eyes were exactly the same, grey and level and faintly amused, as though the entire apparatus of His Majesty's Prison Holloway were a mildly disappointing lecture she was enduring until the good speaker came on.

"Well," Edith said. "The cavalry arrives, and it's five foot two and drives a Daimler."

"The cavalry sends its regards," Yara said. "It couldn't get the afternoon off."

They both smiled, and the smile cost them both something, and neither mentioned it.

"How is the house?" Edith asked. Meaning: how is everything, meaning: Alistair, Vera, Tobias, Renwick, the letters in the coal cellar, the whole precarious architecture of their conspiracy. Yara understood the grammar perfectly. They had been speaking in code since the first sealed letter, and prison had made them fluent.

"The house stands," Yara said. "Your brother writes weekly. His handwriting has deteriorated, which suggests recovery of health and decline of manners. Miss Calloway has taken a position at the Free Hospital and reports that the ward sister is a tyrant, which Miss Calloway considers a compliment to her own standards." She paused. "Tobias sends his love. He is bearing up. He asks whether you have eaten."

"Tell Tobias I have eaten, and that he is a fool, and that I intend to say so at length when they release me." Edith's voice was dry, but her hand moved on the table—an inch toward Yara, an inch back, a motion too small for the watching wardress to log. "And you? How is my driver surviving without employment?"

"Poorly. The Daimler misses you. It sulks. It refuses to start before nine."

"Liar. That engine would start in a flood."

"It would start out of loyalty," Yara agreed. "Which is worse."

The wardress shifted by the door. Time was being counted even here, especially here; the Empire rationed even affection. Yara lowered her voice and let the inventory run, quick and quiet: Renwick's silence and what it might mean; the shipping line's confirmation, received that morning, that the berth was held until the end of the month and no longer; the lawyer's opinion that Tobias's charge would likely resolve to a fine or bound-over order, unless someone chose to make it worse, which meant Renwick was keeping him as a card to be played later. She delivered it all in the tone of a woman reciting a grocery list, and Edith absorbed it with small nods, filing each item behind those unchanged eyes.

"You've become rather good at this," Edith said softly, when she finished.

"At what?"

"At carrying the whole ledger in your head. At deciding what matters." Edith turned her hands over on the table, palms up—a gesture Yara had seen her use at meetings, when she wanted to show the audience she had nothing hidden. Here it looked different. Here it looked like surrender, or prayer, or both. "I used to think you were a spectator, Yara. I said so once, cruelly, and I have regretted the cruelty if not the error. I was wrong about the error too. You were never watching history. You were memorizing it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Yara found, to her surprise, that her throat had tightened. She looked down at the table, at the grain of the wood worn smooth by other visitors' griefs. "Someone has to remember accurately," she said. "Everyone else in this family is busy being dramatic."

"My brother, you mean."

"I mean all of you. Dramatic, magnificent, exhausting people." She raised her eyes. "Someone has to write it down while you're all being heroic. Otherwise the heroes get to decide what happened, and heroes lie."

Edith laughed—really laughed, a sound that startled the wardress into looking over—and for a moment the cell-block smell and the stone cold fell away, and they might have been back in the kitchen at Hampstead, arguing over cold supper, which was where all their best arguments lived. "God help the future," Edith said, wiping her eye. "You'll be its accountant."

"Better than being its victim," Yara said. Then, quieter: "Eat what they give you. Keep your strength for the correct moment. And when they ask you to sign anything—anything at all—read it first. Twice. Promise me."

"Twice," Edith agreed. And then, as the bell rang and the wardress stepped forward, she added, very fast, in the voice she used for the sentence that ended every speech: "Yara. Whatever happens at the end of the month—whatever you decide about that berth—you should know that this household is not an arrangement. It never was. Blood is an accident. This is a decision. Mine was made years ago, the day I found a sixteen-year-old girl alphabetizing my correspondence because no one had ever taught her that she was allowed to matter."

The bell rang again. The wardress touched Edith's shoulder. And Yara, rising, pressing her palm once briefly against the older woman's knuckles—two seconds of contact, logged nowhere, owned by no state—said the only thing that was true:

"I'll be at the gate every visiting day. Count on the countable."

Then she walked out through the swallowing corridors, past the pious carved stones, under the gate, into the cold bright street, and did not let her breath shake until she was inside the Daimler with the doors shut and the engine running and the frost melting on the windscreen in long silver tears.

* * *

She drove back south slowly, taking the long way along the park, because her hands needed the work of the wheel and her mind needed the rhythm. Late light came down through bare plane trees and lay across the road in bars, like lines ruled on a page waiting to be written on.

In her glove, Edith's reply to Tobias—three lines, smuggled out in a returned library book, pressed now against Yara's palm—instructed him to accept whatever plea spared him time, to trust the lawyer, and to stop apologizing to people who hadn't asked for apologies. In her breast pocket, Tobias's message back, written that morning on toilet paper in pencil no thicker than regret: *Tell her the carrying goes both ways. Tell Yara the roses are fine.*

By tomorrow evening both messages would be delivered. By Friday, Vera's counter-evidence would be copied and placed where the lawyer could reach it. By the end of the month, the berth expired, and winter came down like a lid, and someone would have to be on that ship.

Yara pulled up outside the house as the first lamp was lit in the hall window, and sat a moment in the gathering dark, doing what she always did at the end of a hard day's entries: closing the books.

Two visits. Two cells. One courier, intact. She spoke it aloud to the empty motor, quietly, the way her father had once closed his shop accounts in Yokohama, in a language she no longer let herself dream in.

*Balanced,* she said. *For today.*

Then she saw the figure standing under the plane tree opposite the gate—a man in a good coat, hat brim low, who had been standing there, she realized with a slow cold turning in her stomach, long enough to have watched the Daimler return from Holloway. As she watched, he tipped his hat—not to her, precisely, but to the house, to the whole fragile enterprise of it—and turned, unhurried, toward the corner, and was gone.

Yara sat with the engine idling and her heart going like a telegraph key, and understood that Renwick had stopped sending clerks.

He had come himself.
