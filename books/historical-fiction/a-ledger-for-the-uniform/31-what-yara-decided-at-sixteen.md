## 31. What Yara Decided at Sixteen

There was a particular silence her father made when he decided a thing was finished.

Yara had learned to hear it before she could name it: the small click of his chopsticks laid parallel across the bowl, the breath drawn through the nose, the pause before speech that meant the matter was already closed and whatever followed would be only the announcement of it. She heard it on an evening in March, seven years ago, in the front room of a house above the harbour in Yokohama, when he set down his chopsticks, folded his hands, and told her that she had shamed the Nakamura name beyond repair, and that she would be leaving for England in the spring, and that she should not write.

She was fifteen then. Sixteen by the time the ship sailed. Old enough, he said, to understand what she had done.

What she had done — though it took her years to say it plainly even to herself — was exist in the wrong proportions. Her mother had been English, a governess named Alice Pemberton who had come out east full of Kipling and gone home to God within a year of Yara's birth, leaving behind a daughter with her own grey eyes set in a face that Yokohama read as foreign and England would later read as something else again. Her father, Kenji Nakamura, a shipping agent of considerable propriety and considerable pride, had endured the marriage as a commercial necessity and the child as a compromise he believed he could manage. He managed it for fifteen years by keeping Yara indoors, unphotographed, unmentioned, schooled at home in both languages but celebrated in neither.

The scandal, when it came, was almost laughably small. A photograph. A school essay printed in the *Japan Gazette* under her name — *A Girl Between Two Empires* — in which sixteen-year-old Yara had committed the unforgivable sin of being articulate about her own condition. She wrote that she belonged fully to neither country and therefore might belong, one day, to herself. Her father read it aloud at breakfast in a voice like a verdict. His business partners had English wives. His competitors had sons. There were arrangements pending, a match proposed with the daughter of a silk importer, and here was his household's private arithmetic published in ink for anyone to count.

*You will go to your aunt in England,* he said. *There is no aunt. I have invented one. You will write to this address and they will receive you because I have paid them to. And you will not write to me.*

Yara remembered thinking, with a clarity that frightened her even then: *he has rehearsed this.* The sentences had the smoothness of things said over many times alone. That was the worst part — not the cruelty, which was ordinary, but the preparation. He had sat in this room, perhaps in that very chair, practising how to disown his daughter until the practice wore the feeling off it.

She did not cry. She noticed that she did not cry, and filed the noticing away, the way she filed everything, and later that habit would become her whole character without her ever choosing it.

* * *

The ship was called the *Tenyō Maru*, and Yara spent thirty-one days aboard it learning that the ocean does not care what you have lost.

She shared a cabin with two missionary sisters going out to Shanghai and a Mrs. Fairweather, a widow of formidable tweed, who took one look at Yara — the Japanese name on the manifest, the English face above it — and asked, with the frankness of the very comfortable, "And which are you, dear?"

It was not a cruel question. That was the discovery that undid her. It was asked the way one asks whether one takes sugar. Mrs. Fairweather genuinely wished to sort her into a drawer so the rest of the voyage could proceed smoothly, and there was no drawer, and the absence of the drawer interested nobody but Yara.

"I'm going to England," she said finally.

"Everyone on this ship is going somewhere," said Mrs. Fairweather, satisfied, and turned back to her knitting.

Yara went up on deck and watched the water for a long time. Somewhere below her, in the hold, were the two trunks her father had packed with a provisioner's thoroughness — winter clothes, medicines, a lacquered box of letters of introduction, and, sewn into the lining of her coat, four hundred pounds in notes, which was either generosity or the purchase price of never seeing her again, and she suspected it was both, and she suspected he would have been offended if she'd tried to separate the two.

She thought about the sea in the way she thought about everything: as entries. *Day four. Gulls gone; we are properly alone now. Day nine. The missionary sisters sing hymns badly. Day twelve. I have stopped expecting my father to appear at the rail.* She kept the account in her head because she had no notebook yet, and because the habit of itemising grief made it portable. A thing entered can be carried. A thing carried can be put down later, in some other place, at some other hour, when one has the strength.

What she did not know — could not have known, standing at that rail with the whole grey Pacific turning beneath her — was that she was already rehearsing the woman she would become. The competent one. The one who notices everything and says little. The one who keeps accounts because accounts do not abandon you.

History, she decided somewhere in the Indian Ocean, happened to other people. To girls with unambiguous faces. To families that kept their members. She would disembark at Southampton, she would present herself at the invented aunt's address, and she would build a life so quiet and so well-ordered that no empire, no father, no sea would think to trouble it.

This resolution lasted eleven days past her landing.

* * *

The address in Pimlico belonged to a Miss Hargreaves, who was not an aunt and barely a landlady, being chiefly a woman who took Japanese money and provided a narrow bed, bread and dripping, and instructions to be invisible. Yara was invisible with great skill for five months. She learned the bus routes, the price of coal, the exact angle of bow that made English shopkeepers relax. She learned that her English was too correct, her Japanese too present in her vowels, and that she could sand down either edge with practice, and she practised.

In September, the money from her father stopped.

She learned it the way she learned most things — indirectly, by inference. Miss Hargreaves's manner cooled by degrees, like a stove banking down overnight. The bread became thinner. Then one evening Miss Hargreaves stood in the doorway of the narrow room holding an envelope that had clearly been steamed open and resealed with more hope than craft, and said, "Your remittances have ceased, miss, and I'm sure you'll understand I can't run a charitable institution."

Yara understood perfectly. What she felt was not surprise. It was something colder and more useful: confirmation. The four hundred pounds had not been generosity after all. It had been a term of years, like a sentence, like a lease. Her father had paid for her absence up to a date certain, and the date certain had arrived, and somewhere in Yokohama a man was laying his chopsticks parallel across his bowl and considering the matter closed.

That night she counted what remained. Eleven pounds, six shillings. She entered it in the back of her prayer book, which had become her first ledger, under the heading *Assets*, because if you called it assets you could look at it.

Then she did the thing that saved her life, though it did not feel like courage at the time. It felt like arithmetic. She listed what she knew: engines. Her father had taught her engines — outboard motors, the little generator in his office, the motor car a client once left at the house, which Kenji Nakamura, despite every other austerity, had permitted himself to examine with something close to love. Yara had knelt beside him on the gravel and handed him tools and absorbed, without either of them remarking on it, the whole liturgy of the combustion engine. Petrol, compression, spark. A machine did not care whose daughter you were. A machine started or it did not, and its judgments were final and fair.

She wrote to six garages. Five did not answer. The sixth wrote back to say they did not hire women, but that Mrs. Marlowe of Kensington, who was looking for a driver for her new Daimler, was, they understood, less conventional, and here was the address, and please do not mention where you got it.

* * *

Kensington, October 1908. The house had white stucco peeling at the cornices and a basement kitchen that smelled of onions and decades. Yara was shown into a morning room crowded with papers — she would learn later that the crowding was permanent and principled, that Edith Marlowe regarded an empty surface as a moral failure — and the lady of the house looked up from a draft of a pamphlet with the bluest, most appraising eyes Yara had ever seen.

"You're the mechanic," Edith said. Not a question.

"I'm the applicant, ma'am."

"Miss Hargreaves writes that you are seventeen, discreet, and friendless." Edith set down her pen. "I assume she means the last as a warning. I take it as a qualification. Friendless people work hard and stay."

"Is that experience or cynicism, ma'am?"

Something moved at the corner of Edith's mouth — not quite a smile; a reconnaissance. "Both. They're the same thing after forty. Show me your hands."

Yara showed her hands. They were small, scarred at the knuckles, permanently shadowed with grease in the creases that no pumice ever fully lifted. Edith turned them over with the impersonal authority of a horse trader, then released them.

"The Daimler is in the mews. The last three men who drove it treated it as a carriage with opinions. If you treat it as an engine that happens to be beautiful, the position is yours. Room found. Wages modest. And one further condition."

"Ma'am?"

"I am about to become notorious. You may have gathered." Edith gestured at the papers, at the window facing the street, at the whole approaching decade with a small economical movement of one hand. "There will be marches. Police. Newspapers saying ugly things. Possibly worse than newspapers, before it's done. My drivers — my staff — will be photographed standing next to me. Whatever you imagine your life was going to be, it will not be that. So consider carefully. You may decline and I will pay your fare anywhere in England and think none the less of you."

Yara stood in the paper-strewn room and performed, silently, the accounting she would perform all her life. Assets: eleven pounds, six shillings, minus the fare already spent coming here. Liabilities: a father across the world who considered her a closed account; a city that could not decide what drawer to put her in; a face that opened no doors and closed none. Against this: a roof, wages, an engine, and — she examined this entry carefully, because it was new — a woman who had looked at her friendlessness and called it a qualification.

"Ma'am," she said, "I've already been disowned by one household for the crime of being seen. I don't imagine yours will be worse."

Edith Marlowe laughed — one short syllable, surprised out of her — and wrote a line on a card, and that was that. Yara Nakamura, aged seventeen, entered service. History had not troubled her. She had walked straight into its waiting room and taken a seat.

* * *

She learned the household the way she learned engines: by taking it apart.

Cook, Mrs. Brand, who sang hymns while she worked and swore in Welsh while she rested. Annie, the maid, seventeen and romantic, in love with the idea of the movement the way one is in love with a poster of it. Mr. Tredwell next door, who tipped his hat to Edith and wrote anonymous letters to the *Standard*. The rhythms of the place, its tolerances and detonations. And always, everywhere, the papers — arriving, accumulating, being answered, sealed. Yara carried them to the post and, from the first week, read them first.

She told herself it was caution. A driver must know what she is carrying; if a parcel contains sedition, better the driver knows before the police do. This was true. It was also, she admitted on the bad nights, incomplete. The letters were the first documents in her life in which history was addressed to someone she could see. In Yokohama, history had been the war news her father skimmed, the treaties discussed in rooms she was sent out of. Here it arrived on a silver salver with the morning post and said *Dear Mrs. Marlowe, they arrested six of us outside the Treasury, here are the names.* Names. Dates. Sums. She copied the important ones into a notebook she kept beneath her mattress, telling herself it was professional discretion, knowing it was hunger.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, the entry she kept in no ledger: Yara Nakamura wanted to matter somewhere. She had been subtracted from one family's sum and added, at wages, to another's. Competence was her rent. She paid it daily — the Daimler ran like a sewing machine, the routes were memorised, the police sergeants at the march gates knew her face and grudgingly nodded it through — and she told herself the arrangement was sufficient. One did not need to be loved. One needed to be necessary.

Necessary was safer. Necessary could not be disowned, because necessary could not be spared.

But she was not a fool, and she knew the difference between the two words even if she preferred not to read it. When Edith fell ill with influenza in the winter of 1911 and Yara drove herself, unasked, to the chemist through sleet at midnight, and sat up all night with barley water and the medical columns of back issues of *The Lancet*, and Edith surfaced from fever once and said, "You needn't have stayed up," and Yara said, "Someone had to keep the accounts of your temperature," and Edith, half-delirious, had gripped her wrist and said, "You impossible girl, that's not what I mean" — on that night, Yara lay awake till dawn understanding exactly what Edith meant, and refusing, with all her considerable discipline, to enter it in the ledger. Some figures, once recorded, change the total. She was not ready for the total to change.

And then there was the berth. Though she did not learn of it for years yet, it existed already, older than her service — an arrangement Edith had made quietly with the Cunard office in 1906, one paid passage to Canada, held indefinitely, renewable annually, against the day someone in her orbit might need to be *elsewhere*, quickly and permanently. Edith called it, among the few who knew of it, the family contingency. It had never been used. It sat in the files of the shipping line like a held breath.

Seven years Yara would serve before she learned of it. Seven years of sealing letters she had already read, of driving Edith to gates and courts and prisons, of perfecting the competence that covered the wound the way paint covers damp — adequately, and only from the outside.

* * *

Now it is the spring of 1913, and the wound has begun, very slightly, to talk.

It talks in the garage at night, while she works on the Daimler by lamplight and Tobias Finch sleeps badly upstairs in the room that was Annie's. It talks when she catches Edith watching her across the breakfast table with that appraising blue gaze, the same one from the morning room five years ago, and wonders what entry her employer is making in whatever ledger Edith keeps. It talked loudest of all three nights ago, when Edith mentioned — casually, folding linen, not looking at her — "You knew, of course, that I hold a passage? Canada. One berth. It renews in June. I've never told you because it's never been relevant, and I find I dislike telling you now, because the moment I tell you it becomes relevant."

One berth. Yara had stood very still with the teapot in her hands and heard, underneath the words, the sound of chopsticks laid parallel across a bowl — her father's sound, the sound of a decision already made — except that this time the decision had not yet been made, and that was the unbearable novelty of it. All her life, other people had decided her allocations. Her father had allocated her absence. Miss Hargreaves had allocated her eviction. England had allocated her a uniform and a grease rag and the title of *the driver*. And now here was a woman holding one seat on a ship to safety, looking at her people — at Yara, at Tobias with his hunted eyes, at Alistair returned from the dead and Vera with her forged life — and asking, silently, *who goes?*

Yara had said, "The tea's getting cold, ma'am," and poured it, and fled to the garage, and stood with her hand flat on the Daimler's warm bonnet for a long time.

She was sixteen once, on a ship, deciding that history happened elsewhere. She was twenty-three now, and the elsewhere had a warrant out for one of her people, and a cell reserved for another, and a shipping deadline, and her own handwriting on a hundred sealed letters that proved she had never once been merely a spectator.

That night, after the house slept, she took out the old notebook from beneath the mattress — the prayer book was long retired, but the habit had migrated faithfully — and began a fresh page. At the top she wrote, in her smallest, most controlled hand:

*Question under review: the berth.*

Below it, she drew the column she had drawn since the Indian Ocean. *Assets. Liabilities.* And then, because she was honest even when it hurt, a third column, one she had never allowed herself before. She titled it, after a moment, *Who deserves it* — and then crossed that out, and wrote instead: *Who would survive losing everyone else.*

She stared at the third column for a long time. The lamp hissed. Upstairs, faintly, Tobias turned over in a bed that was not his, dreaming of hospitals.

She sharpened her pencil, and began to fill it in.
