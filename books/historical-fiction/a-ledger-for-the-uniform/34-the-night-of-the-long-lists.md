## 34. The Night of the Long Lists

The supper was cold because Cook had left, and because no one had thought to light the range until it was too late to matter. A ham that had gone stiff at its own edges. Bread from Tuesday. A jar of pickled onions Vera had brought in the summer and nobody had opened. The dining room at Belgrave Row had not seen a full table since before the Cat and Mouse Act had turned Edith Marlowe's life into a schedule of arrests and releases, and now four places were laid — five, if one counted the chair at the head that Alistair refused on principle, pacing instead between the sideboard and the window like a man walking off a fever.

Yara had laid the table herself. She noticed, as she set down the knives, that her hands did not shake. They had shaken in July, when Renwick's card first arrived. They had shaken outside Holloway in August. But tonight they were steady, and she understood why only later: tonight she had already decided.

The berth sat among them like an uninvited guest. It existed as a document in Edith's writing desk — a paid passage to Canada aboard the *Corinthic*, sailing from Liverpool on the twenty-third of October, one berth, held under an old arrangement with a shipping clerk who owed Edith a debt older than the war. One berth. One name. And ten days, now nine, in which to write it.

"Someone must go," Alistair said, for perhaps the sixth time since the soup course that never was. "That much is arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Tobias repeated. He sat very straight in his chair, his hands flat on either side of his plate, the way he still sat at every meal as though a sergeant might pass through and inspect him. He had filled out since Holloway; there was colour in him again, and something else, something that looked almost like standing ground. "You've said that three times, sir, and each time you leave out the rest of the sum."

"The rest of the sum being?"

"That whoever goes must be someone Renwick wants gone." Tobias's eyes came around the table, unhurried, landing last on Yara. "Or someone who wants to be gone. And I don't believe we have either person at this table."

"We might," said Alistair.

"No," said Yara, and was surprised by her own voice.

It stopped him mid-stride. He turned and looked at her properly — her employer's brother, the dead man resurrected, gaunt as a November tree and twice as stubborn — and she held his look without lowering her chin, which was more than most people managed with the Marlowes.

"You were going to say yourself," she said. "Again. You said it on Sunday, and Miss Calloway dismantled the argument, and you said it Tuesday, and I dismantled what was left of it. You intend to surrender yourself publicly regardless. A man who has already given himself away cannot also be given a berth. There isn't enough of you left over."

Vera laughed. It was a small laugh, quickly folded away, but it escaped, and Yara saw Alistair glance at her with an expression that in another man, in another house, might have been called fondness.

"And who would you send, Miss Nakamura?" Alistair asked. "You've opinions on everyone else's claims. Let's hear yours."

"I have none," Yara said, which was true in the way that a locked drawer is empty. "I keep the accounts. I don't spend them."

* * *

The accounts, of course, were hers. Had been hers since that first night in the garage when grief had made her clever and she had begun the private ledger — dates, names, sums, the whole machinery of Alistair's false death entered line by line in her small even hand. She kept it still, though its purpose had changed. It was less a record of a mystery now than a record of them: Edith's releases and re-arrests, Tobias's detention and discharge, the cost of coal, the cost of silence, the price of Vera's gamble paid out in a blackmail ledger surrendered to a major who had promised, in his polished way, to forget everything.

Renwick had forgotten nothing. They all knew it. The exchange in September had bought Tobias's freedom and Edith's temporary quiet, but a man like Renwick did not forgive; he filed. Somewhere in a drawer in Whitehall there was a folder with all their names in it, waiting for weather.

Which was why the berth mattered, and why the arguing had gone on for a week, and why Edith had finally declared tonight that the matter would be settled at table or not at all.

Edith herself had been uncharacteristically quiet through the early part of the evening. She sat at the foot of the table — she had given up the head years ago, saying a chair should face the door — with her hands folded and her hunger-striker's thinness hidden under a grey dress that Yara had mended twice. Twice this month. When Edith began wearing out her clothes faster than usual, it meant she was moving faster than usual, and when she moved faster than usual, the police took notice, and when the police took notice, Yara drove to Holloway.

Now Edith unfolded her hands.

"I will settle one thing before anyone speaks again," she said. "It will not be me. I am aware that Yara believes—" a small dry pause— "and that Alistair believes, and that Vera has been polite enough not to say aloud, that I ought to take the passage myself. Rest, recover, sit out whatever is coming across some Canadian prairie where the nearest policeman is a Mountie with better manners." She looked around the table. "I am fifty-four years old. I have been force-fed twice. I have buried a brother who then inconveniently stood up out of his grave, and I have spent eleven years teaching this country that women are citizens. If I sail away now, every word I have said becomes a performance. I will not do it. That is settled. Argue with me and I shall simply repeat myself until you tire."

"Tire," said Alistair, "is the operative word. You're running on—"

"On what, Alistair? Say it. Say I'm running on fumes, say I'm killing myself by inches, say whatever you've been rehearsing by the window all evening. Then remember that you are about to walk into a court-martial with your name in your mouth and your honour in your hat, and ask yourself which of us has the stronger claim to self-preservation."

The clock on the mantel ticked into the silence. Yara watched the two of them — the siblings who had quarrelled unreconciled in 1910 and were making up for lost time now with compound interest — and felt the strange ache she always felt watching them: that this was family, the real article, love and fury in the same breath, and that she had learned what it looked like only by standing just outside its light.

Tobias broke it, gently, the way he did everything.

"Then it's between the rest of us," he said. "And I'll say my piece plainly, because I've been a clerk and clerks are plain. The berth should go to Miss Nakamura."

Yara's fork stopped halfway to the plate.

"Renwick's folder has my name in it twice — once for keeping the letters, once for taking blame at the toll. It has Mr. Marlowe's name so many times the folder may need a second volume. It has Mrs.—Miss Calloway's name for the forgery. It does not have Miss Nakamura's name at all, because she has done nothing wrong, ever, except drive beautifully and read other people's post." A faint smile touched him there, and she let him have it. "She is the only person at this table who could walk out of England tomorrow without looking back once at the customs officer. That is worth more than any argument about deserving. Deserving is how people talk themselves onto sinking ships."

"Thank you," Yara said, when she trusted her voice. "No."

"You can't just say no."

"I can. I've been practising." She put the fork down. "Tobias. You were detained fourteen days in a cell smaller than our pantry. You took the blame for documents that were half mine — I carried them, I chose the route, I talked my way past the first checkpoint because a chauffeur is furniture and furniture isn't searched. If anyone at this table has an account open with the law, it is I. But more than that." She looked down at her plate, at the cold ham, and made herself look up again. "More than that. My father disowned me at sixteen for wanting a life larger than his house. I arrived here with one trunk and a letter of introduction and a surname nobody could pronounce, and this household—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and continued— "this household taught me to read history instead of merely dusting it. I am not going to Canada to be safe. I am going to stay here and make sure this house keeps its memory, because memory is the only thing Renwick cannot confiscate without our help."

"That," said Vera, "was a speech."

"It was a ledger entry," Yara said. "Spoken aloud. Forgive me."

* * *

They argued after that, but it was a different kind of arguing — the kind that happens inside families, where the object is not victory but proximity. Vera maintained, as she always maintained, that she would not leave England at any price; that a woman who had forged one document could forge a thousand, and that Malta had taught her that records outlive the men who write them, and she intended to outlive hers. Alistair maintained that the berth belonged to Vera, and Vera told him, with a nurse's bluntness, that if he tried to give her anything she would return it broken. Tobias maintained his case for Yara quietly and repeatedly, the way water maintains its case against stone. Edith maintained nothing further; she had stated her position and, having done so, ate her cold ham with the satisfaction of a woman who has won by refusing to fight.

At some point the candles burned low and someone — Yara suspected Tobias — fetched the lamp from the hall, and the quality of the light changed the quality of the argument, softening it, drawing them inward around the table the way lamplight draws moths.

It was Alistair, of all people, who broke the tension with laughter.

He had been trying to calculate the weight allowance on the *Corinthic* — one trunk, he said, one trunk and no more, imagine compressing a life into a steamer trunk — and he gestured too widely with the bread knife and knocked the pickle jar, which spun, wobbled, and came to rest pointing directly at Tobias like a compass needle finding north.

"There," Alistair said. "The matter is decided by Providence and a jar of onions."

And Tobias — hollow-cheeked, cell-marked Tobias, who six months ago had arrived at the service entrance soaked through and shaking — laughed. Really laughed, helplessly, until his shoulders shook and his eyes watered and Vera pounded his back and Edith said, "Really, Alistair," in a voice that was entirely delighted, and Yara sat among them and felt something crack gently in her chest, the way ice cracks on a pond in late winter: not breaking, thawing.

This, she thought. This is what I chose. Not the berth, not the escape, not the clean arithmetic of survival. This table. These voices. The laughter of damaged people at a cold supper while the century gathers itself like a fist.

She excused herself to fetch the cheese — there was no cheese; there had been no cheese for a fortnight — and stood a moment in the dark of the pantry with her hand flat against the cool shelf, breathing.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and the others had gone up — Vera to the spare room she'd occupied since September, Alistair to the attic room that had waited three years for him, Tobias to the cot in the converted maid's room that he insisted was luxurious and had begun, disturbingly, to mean it — Yara sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low and opened the ledger.

She read back through it. March: the marginal note, *V.* April: the censors' stamps, traced and matched. June: the toll, the detention, fourteen days entered in figures that did not begin to hold what they held. September: the exchange. Each entry in her neat hand, each one a moment when she had stopped being a spectator of history and started carrying it.

On a fresh page she wrote today's date. Beneath it:

*Berth, Corinthic, 23 Oct., one. Claimants: four. Withdrawn: two (E.M., V.C.). Contested: two (A.M., T.F.).*

She sat looking at those last initials for a long time. Alistair would surrender publicly within the week; he had said so in front of witnesses and Alistair Marlowe did not unsay things. His claim to the berth was therefore void, whatever he argued. Which left the arithmetic brutally, perfectly simple, and had been simple for days — she had simply needed the noise of the argument, the long lists drafted and redrafted, the laughter and the tears, to be certain that the simple thing was also the right thing.

Tobias's name was in Renwick's folder twice. Her name was in no folder at all.

But a ticket written to Yara Nakamura could be questioned, delayed, examined. A ticket written in a firm, familiar, frequently-forged hand — a hand she had studied across a desk for six years, whose loops and crossings lived in her fingers the way a hymn lives in a throat — that ticket would pass without a second glance. Edith signed the household accounts; Edith signed the petrol chits; Edith's signature opening the passage to *herself* would raise no eyebrow anywhere between Belgrave Row and Liverpool.

Yara turned to a blank leaf. She took up her pen. She did not write yet; she only rested the nib against the paper and let the decision finish setting, like plaster poured into a mould.

Tomorrow she would practise the signature. Tomorrow she would fill the inkwell, and warm the paper, and do the thing she had sworn when she first came to this house never to do again — put her hand to another person's name — because some names, used rightly, are not thefts but gifts.

In the ledger, at last, she wrote a single line beneath the date, and closed the book on it:

*One berth. One name. Mine to write.*

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — Tobias, awake, she guessed, doing his own arithmetic in the dark. Outside, October pressed its wet face against the window, and somewhere east of the city the world went on loading its guns.

Nine days.
