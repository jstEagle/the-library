## 35. Berth for One

The house had gone quiet in the way houses do when everyone inside has stopped pretending to sleep. Yara sat at the kitchen table with the passage documents spread before her beneath the single lamp she'd angled so its light fell only on her hands and the paper and nothing else. Past midnight. The coal range ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above her, Edith Marlowe lay awake—Yara was certain of it, certain the way one is certain of weather—and somewhere above that, in the attic room Tobias had occupied since autumn, a man who believed he had three more days of England left was also not sleeping.

Three documents. The Canadian Pacific arrangement Edith had kept folded in her strongbox since before the war in anyone's imagination—a berth, paid in full, held against calamity. The family's private lifeboat, launched years ago and never once used. Edith had laid it on the table two nights ago during the long supper of lists and said, plainly, *Yara, it is yours,* and Yara had said, just as plainly, *No,* and the word had hung between them like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

She had thought about that bell for two days.

Now she uncapped the ink bottle, and her hand did not shake, which surprised her. She had expected trembling. What she felt instead was something colder and more deliberate—the same stillness that came over her behind the wheel of the Daimler when the streets turned ugly and every instinct screamed to look at the crowd while her training insisted she watch the road. Competence as a kind of armour. She had built her whole self out of it.

*Edith Marlowe.* She knew the signature better than her own. Six years of driving this woman through London while letters were sealed in the seat beside her; four years of signing chits, receipts, subscriptions, petitions that passed through the chauffeur's hands because no household ran itself. Edith wrote her name with an impatient elegance—the capital E thrown forward like a woman stepping into traffic, the double l's looped high, the final e reduced to a flick, almost contemptuous, as though finishing were an indignity.

Yara practised eleven times on the butcher's brown paper she'd laid over the table first. Eleven attempts, each one examined under the lamp, each one destroyed by tearing and folding into her apron pocket for burning later. On the twelfth attempt she stopped practising.

Here was the arithmetic she could not escape, the ledger line she had been carrying since the night of the long lists: Renwick wanted Tobias. Not arrested, precisely—Renwick's methods had never required anything so vulgar—but *found*, *named*, *filed*. A clerk who kept copies, a name attached to documents that should have burned, a loose thread in a tapestry Renwick had spent three years weaving over Alistair's grave. Every day Tobias remained in England was a day the noose drew tighter, and the exchange Edith had bargained in Holloway's shadow had bought his freedom but not his safety. Freedom and safety were different accounts entirely. Yara understood that better than anyone in the house. She had been free since she was sixteen and safe never once.

And there was exactly one berth. One. The universe had arranged its cruelty with an accountant's precision.

So: she would sign Edith's name, assign the berth to herself, and then—

Then what? That was the part she hadn't let herself think through fully, because thinking it through meant feeling it through, and she had an appointment with her own grief scheduled for the dockside and she intended to arrive composed.

She dipped the pen. Wrote the name. It was good. It was very good—it was, she thought with a strange cold pride, better than Edith had managed in months, since the hunger strikes had begun teaching her hands new tremors. Yara looked at the forgery for a long time. Somewhere in the walls the house settled, exhaled, kept her secret the way it had kept all the others.

*I'm sorry,* she said silently, to the woman upstairs. *You named me. I'm naming myself back.*

That was the thing about being given something, she realised. Everyone in this house had spent a week performing the same noble arithmetic—Edith counting herself out for Yara, Yara counting herself out for Edith, Tobias refusing even to hold the ticket, Vera declining to leave Malta's shadow at all—as if love were a currency you proved by refusing it. But love that only ever declined was just another form of hiding. Yara had hidden behind a uniform for six years. She was finished with it.

She signed the second copy. Blotted both. Folded them into the oilskin wallet that had once held Edith's suffrage correspondence and now held, in effect, a human life.

Then she fed the practice sheets into the range, watched them curl and go, and went upstairs to lie beside sleep without touching it.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of river. The shipping office had confirmed it twice now: the *Corinthic* sailed on the evening tide from the Royal Albert Dock, and the berth expired with it. Ten days had become three days had become today, and the city moved around the fact of their departure with sublime indifference—costermongers crying fish, trams shrieking, a newsboy shouting something about the Balkans that nobody paused to hear because everybody had learned to half-listen to catastrophe.

Edith met her in the hall at seven, dressed for the dock in the severe grey coat she wore to funerals and parliamentary galleries, indistinguishable purposes. Her eyes went at once to Yara's hands, then to the oilskin wallet, and something moved across her face—recognition, perhaps, or the beginning of a suspicion she chose, visibly, not to finish.

"The documents are in order?" Edith asked.

"Signed," Yara said. And let the truth sit there, plain as a lamp in a window, for Edith to find or not find.

Edith found it. Yara watched her find it—the small stillness, the fractional narrowing, the way her jaw set and then, remarkably, unset. For a moment the hall held nothing but the two of them and six years of sealed letters between them, and Yara braced for the storm, for *how dare you*, for the full Marlowe fury that had faced down magistrates and Home Secretaries.

"You write my name better than I do," Edith said at last, dry as August. "I expect that's treason of some kind."

"It's bookkeeping," Yara said.

Something cracked open in Edith's face then—not tears, nothing so simple, but a loosening, a letting-down of weight she had carried so long it had become posture. "I signed that berth over to you," she said quietly. "Whatever name is on it now, whatever hand wrote it—that was my intention. You've merely... expedited the paperwork." She straightened her collar with a brisk, furious tenderness. "If you tell anyone I approved of forgery in my own house, I shall deny it with my dying breath."

"I wouldn't dream of exposing you," Yara said. "You're far too useful."

For one absurd second they nearly laughed, and then neither of them did, because laughter would have opened the floodgates and the car was waiting and there would be time—no, Yara corrected herself, there would not be time, there was never again going to be time like this time—and so they simply stood a moment longer in the hall where Yara had first arrived at sixteen with one suitcase and a father's silence stitched into her coat lining, and then they went out to the Daimler together.

Tobias was already at the car, cap in hand, wearing the good suit Vera had procured for him and looking like a man dressed for his own trial. Which, Yara thought, was not far wrong. His eyes found hers across the bonnet, and in them was the question he had been asking with increasing desperation for two days: *who goes?* He had made his own position unambiguous—he would not take the berth, he had said, he had *insisted*, with the stubborn honour of a man who owed too many debts—and so he believed, poor gentle fool, that the choice lay between Edith and Yara, and that whichever of them boarded, the other would remain, and he could not bear either version of it.

"East India Dock Road," Yara said, and took the wheel.

* * *

London unspooled eastward in layers of smoke and commerce. Edith rode in the rear seat with the oilskin wallet on her lap—Yara had handed it over without comment, and Edith had accepted it without opening it, a mutual courtesy of liars—and Tobias sat beside Yara up front, his knee an inch from the gear lever, watching the city go past as a man watches a country he is leaving.

"You're quiet," he said, somewhere past Poplar.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet *differently*."

She glanced at him. He had filled out somewhat since autumn, the field-hospital hollows in his cheeks partly shadowed over, but his hands still betrayed him—they moved constantly in his lap, counting things, sorting things, a clerk's hands that had never recovered from holding a dying man's sleeve. She thought: *three weeks ago I memorised safe-house addresses in a garage while he slept in the loft, and I didn't know yet that he would become the centre of everything.* She thought: *the ledger has forty-one entries now and the last one is blank.*

"There's something I need to do at the dock," she said. "When we get there. I need you to trust me and not argue."

"That's asking rather a lot," Tobias said. "Arguing is the only exercise I get."

"Tobias."

He heard it then—whatever it was in her voice, the steel under the softness—and went still. "All right," he said. "I trust you."

It was, she thought, the most expensive sentence anyone had ever spent on her.

They came down onto the dock road where the cranes stood like patient herons over the forest of masts and funnels, and the smell was tar and rope and cold salt and the particular iron breath of ships that carry people away from their lives. Third-class passengers were already queuing with tin boxes and wicker baskets and children with names pinned inside their coats. The emigrant trade ran on hope the way the mills ran on coal.

Yara brought the Daimler to rest near the gangway, killed the engine, and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, listening to the metal tick. Across the water a tug groaned. A gull screamed its one opinion. Everything was ordinary. Everything was unbearable.

"Right," she said, and got out.

At the shipping line's little booth by the gangway, a purser with side-whiskers and a ledger of his own—everyone in this story, Yara thought, keeps ledgers—checked the documents she presented. He read the name on the berth assignment. He looked up at her. He looked down again.

"Nakamura," he said. "Berth is confirmed. Single occupancy, second cabin, starboard side. You'll want to board within the hour, miss; we sail on the tide."

"Thank you," Yara said. "There's been a change."

* * *

She did it twenty feet from the gangway, with Edith standing at her shoulder and Tobias holding the luggage he would not be needing, and she did it quickly, because she had learned from watching this family that hesitation was the only unforgivable sin.

She put the ticket in Tobias's hand and closed his fingers over it.

His face went through confusion, comprehension, refusal—all of it in the space of a heartbeat—and he began to push the ticket back, his mouth opening around the word *no*, and Yara gripped his wrist with both hands, the way one holds a man on a ledge.

"Listen to me," she said. "Your name is on Renwick's list. Mine isn't—I'm nobody, I'm a chauffeur, I'm invisible, and that invisibility is worth more here than it ever will be on a ship. You saved copies of papers that were ordered burned, and that makes you dangerous, and dangerous men need distance. I am staying because staying is the thing I can do. You are leaving because leaving is the thing you must. There's no argument in it. I've done the sums."

"You don't—" His voice cracked. "Yara, you *signed yourself on*. You forged your way onto that ship, I saw the name, you—" He stopped. Recalculated. She watched him arrive, appalled, at the true shape of what she'd done: that the berth had been hers by fraud and sacrifice combined, and she was spending both at once.

"Yes," she said simply. "And I'm giving it to you. That's how a berth works. It belongs to whoever holds the ticket." Her eyes stung and she forbade them, absolutely, to do anything further. "Alistair stayed hidden for three years to protect people. Vera forged a death to free him. Edith starved herself in Holloway and traded her silence for your release. Do you really imagine you get to be the only person in this family who doesn't pay?"

"This isn't payment, this is—you'll be here when it turns, all of you, when whatever's coming comes—"

"Someone has to keep the archive," Yara said. "Someone has to drive her to prison gates and bring her home. Someone has to remember all of this properly, itemized, dated, in a fair hand. I'm good at that. It's the one thing I'm best at in the world." She pressed the ticket deeper into his palm. "Go and live somewhere the century hasn't found yet. Write to us under the name we agreed. And Tobias—" her voice did finally break, one hairline fracture, quickly mended— "when you get to Canada, buy a notebook. Keep a ledger. So that somewhere, on some other shore, our account stays open."

Behind her, Edith made a sound—not quite a word, not quite a sob—and stepped forward, and the three of them stood in a knot on the dock amid the tin boxes and the gulls and the great indifferent machinery of departure, and Tobias Finch, army clerk, survivor, keeper of dead men's letters, wept openly into the collar of his borrowed good suit while a ship's officer politely pretended not to exist.

He pulled back. Held the ticket. Looked at it as though it might change its mind.

"The last entry," he said hoarsely. "In your ledger. What does it say?"

Yara smiled at him, and it cost everything, and she paid it gladly.

"*Balance outstanding*," she said. "Now go. Before I commit a second forgery and put you on that ship bodily."

He kissed her hand, absurdly, formally, like something out of a century that was ending. He shook Edith's hand and she ignored the hand and embraced him instead, fiercely, whispering something Yara did not hear and would not ask about. Then he walked up the gangway with his cardboard case and did not look back, because men who look back miss the tide, and at the top he turned anyway, of course he turned, and raised one hand, and Yara raised hers, and then the deck swallowed him.

* * *

They stood on the quay as the mooring lines came off and the *Corinthic* leaned away from the world. The funnel smoke rolled out brown and thick and drifted west over London, over Holloway, over the War Office where a major with a good coat was presumably reading files that no longer contained a clerk named Finch. The ship grew smaller with the patience of all departing things.

"He's gone," Edith said at last, unnecessarily.

"He's gone," Yara agreed.

"And you are still here."

"I'm still here."

Edith was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had the flat brightness she used for verdicts. "Renwick won't like it. A berth bought under my name, assigned under my hand, surrendered to the one man he wants found—he will consider it a trick played on him personally. Men like Renwick always do." She turned from the water, and the light caught the iron in her hair. "We should go home. Quickly."

Down the quay, near the customs shed, a black motorcar that had been parked there since before they arrived slid smoothly off the kerb and began, unhurried, to follow them.

Yara opened the Daimler's door.

"Hold on tight," she said.
