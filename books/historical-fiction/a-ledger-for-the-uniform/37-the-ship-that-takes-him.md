## 37. The Ship That Takes Him

The tide turned at twenty past seven that morning, and Yara knew it without looking at the water. She had learned the Thames the way she had learned the Daimler—by its sounds, its temperaments, the small mechanical complaints it made when pushed too hard—and this morning the river sounded different. Fuller. Restless. A river gathering itself to carry something away.

She stood at the window of her narrow room above the garage, dressed in her uniform though no one had asked her to drive anyone anywhere today. The grey skirt, the white collar, the cap she set on her head with both hands as if placing a crown or a verdict. The uniform was the only costume in England that had never lied about her. When she wore it, people saw exactly what they expected to see: a chauffeur. Invisible. Efficient. Nobody's daughter, nobody's sister, nobody's heart.

*You could still go with him,* said a voice inside her that sounded suspiciously like Tobias Finch's, quiet and stubborn as he was quiet and stubborn. *There are other ships. There is always another ship.*

But there wasn't, and they both knew it. There was one berth, paid for years ago out of Edith Marlowe's private account against some future catastrophe, and Yara had forged the signature that put it in her own name, and then—at the dock, with the gulls screaming and Tobias's face going from confusion to understanding to something that broke her open—she had handed it to him like a woman passing a lit match across a gap in the floorboards.

She had not let herself think about it since. Thinking, she had discovered over three days, was a country she could not afford to enter. So instead she polished things. She cleaned the garage until the tools gleamed. She re-gapped the plugs on the Daimler and torqued every nut she could reach and wiped down the bonnet twice, though the car had gone nowhere since the night of the checkpoints except into the police impound and back out again, dented along one wing where a constable's knee had pressed it, carrying Tobias's confession in its boot like contraband.

He had confessed to everything. That was the word the solicitors kept using—*confessed*—as if Tobias had committed a sin rather than an act of loyalty. He told the magistrate at Bow Street that he alone had copied the War Office file, that he alone had carried the letters north from Malta, that he alone had organized their concealment, and that neither Mrs. Marlowe nor Miss Nakamura nor any member of her household had known anything about it until long after. It was a lie so complete and so generous that Yara had wanted to strike him when he first said it aloud, there in the cell at the toll station, his hands cuffed and his voice perfectly level. *I acted alone,* he said, and looked at her, and made her complicit in his sacrifice whether she willed it or not.

The exchange had freed him. Edith's silence on Major Renwick's commanding officer—the blackmail ledger, Vera Calloway's careful hand, all of it locked back in its drawer—had purchased Tobias's release with conditions attached. Conditions that included departure. *Before winter,* the man from the Home Office had said, in the tone of a clerk reading a laundry list. *And not to return.* As if a human being were a stain that could be sponged out of the empire's ledger if one scrubbed promptly enough.

Today was the last day of autumn's grace. Today the *Missanabie* sailed for Saint John, New Brunswick, and Tobias Finch was aboard her, or would be within the hour.

* * *

Edith met her on the front steps at half past eight, wrapped in the dark coat she wore to funerals and to prison gates, which by now amounted to nearly the same wardrobe.

"You don't have to come," Yara said.

"Of course I have to come." Edith pulled her gloves on finger by finger, unhurried, a woman who had spent a year learning that hurry was what they wanted from you. "I held the berth for eleven years, Yara. Eleven years, waiting for someone in my family to need saving badly enough to use it. I rather think I'm entitled to watch it do its work."

"It isn't doing its work," Yara said. "I did."

"Yes," Edith agreed mildly. "That too." She descended the steps and paused beside her, and for a moment the great suffragist, the terror of two Home Secretaries, simply looked at her driver the way one looks at a painting whose meaning has changed since last viewing. "You know I'd have given it to you."

"I know," Yara said. "That's why I couldn't take it."

Alistair came around the corner of the house with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, gaunt still, three years of hiding worn into him like weather into stone. He moved differently now than he had in the first days after Marseille—steadier, as though having decided to surrender himself publicly he had somehow been given back his own skeleton. His trial was set for February. He spoke of it the way other men spoke of dental appointments.

"The cab's at the gate," he said. "Vera's already in it, arguing with the driver about the route."

"She'll argue us all the way to Canada given half a chance," Edith said.

"She offered again," Alistair said quietly. "Last night. Her passage, her savings, everything—to go with him. He refused. Said one exile per family was quite enough." He almost smiled. "He called us his family, you know. Standing in the hall with his bag packed. I didn't correct him."

Nobody corrected him. That was the whole point of him.

Yara drove them herself. Not in the Daimler—that felt wrong, too grand, too much the machine of the life Tobias was being cut from—but in the hired cab, squeezed between Edith and Vera while Alistair sat facing them with his knees nearly touching hers, four adults folded together like a letter meant for someone far away. London slid past the windows in its grey October dress: costermongers shouting, a newsboy waving a paper with FRENCH MOBILIZATION ORDER across five columns, children chalk-hopping on a pavement that might, before spring, be full of men in uniform marching somewhere east and south. The century was turning under their wheels. Yara watched it go by and thought: *I am done watching.*

At Euston they took the boat train. At the terminus, under the iron ribs and the smoke-stained glass, the porters' trolleys rattled and the departures board clacked its letters into new arrangements, and Yara understood suddenly why Tobias loved stations—had always loved them, had told her once, on a night drive through Hertfordshire with the rain hammering the roof, that a railway station was the only place in England where leaving was considered respectable. Everyone here was in the act of becoming elsewhere.

They found him on the platform at Liverpool Street—no; the boat train went from Liverpool Street, yes, and there he stood by the third-class carriage with his single canvas bag at his feet, wearing the good coat Edith had bought him and looking, in the crowd, exactly like what he had been before any of it: a clerk. An ordinary man. The kind history happened to.

He saw them and his whole face changed—not brightened, nothing so simple as that; it *opened*, the way a door opens when you stop guarding it.

* * *

There was perhaps six minutes. Yara counted them the way she counted everything now, entered them in the mental ledger that had become her second heartbeat. Six minutes of platform noise and steam and Edith gripping both his hands and saying things too low for the rest of them to hear, and Alistair clapping his shoulder with a brotherly roughness that had arrived fully formed despite never having existed before, and Vera pressing a small paper packet into his pocket—"ginger cakes, don't argue, you get seasick, I remember"—and none of it enough, none of it ever going to be enough, and all of it exactly what they had.

Then he turned to Yara, and the others drew back with the tact of people who had learned, all of them, when a moment belonged to someone else.

"You gave me your ticket," he said. It wasn't an accusation. It was more like a man checking a sum.

"You needed it more."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have." She looked up at him—he was half a head taller, this soldier who trembled at loud noises and kept copies of everything—and made herself hold his eyes. "Tobias. Listen to me. I spent my whole life believing history was something that happened to other people. Other people got arrested. Other people got erased. Other people got shipped across oceans because the paperwork required it." Her throat tightened; she pushed through it. "I gave you that berth because I finally understand it doesn't work that way. History happens wherever somebody decides to write it down. And I'm staying to write it down. Someone has to keep the archive. Someone has to keep *her* archive, and Alistair's letters, and Vera's ledger, and the record of every baton and every force-feeding tube and every name in the margin. That's not a consolation prize. That's a post. I'm taking up my post."

"And I'm supposed to just—go. Start over. Be the survivor."

"You're supposed to *live*," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the word like ice on a pond. "Do you know how many people in this story died of other people's decisions? Alistair died of a report. Vera died of a lie she had to tell daily. Edith is dying by inches in a cell every time they rearrest her. You are the only person in this entire affair who gets to walk off the page alive. Take it. Take it for all of us."

He was crying. He didn't hide it; he had never learned to hide it and she loved him for it, loved the undamagable honesty of his face. "I'll write," he said. "Careful letters. Coded, if I have to. I read enough censored mail to know how."

"Do," she said. "Address them to the garage. The service entrance. No one watches the service entrance—that's the entire principle of my life."

The whistle blew, long and silver, cutting the platform in half. Around them the crowd compressed toward the train like water finding its level. Porters slammed doors. Somewhere a child wailed with the pure outrage of the very young confronted by departures.

Edith embraced him last, briefly, fiercely, and said something Yara caught only the end of: "—and when it's over, and it will be over, sooner than any of us wish, you will come home. That is not an invitation. It is an instruction."

Then he stepped up onto the carriage step, one hand on the rail, and looked back at all four of them standing in the steam, and Yara thought: *this is the photograph no camera will take. This is the entry no ledger holds but mine.*

"I acted alone," he said suddenly, loudly, to all of them, with the ghost of a grin. "Remember that. All of you. I acted entirely alone."

"Entirely alone," Alistair agreed solemnly. "We never saw the man before in our lives."

"Who?" said Vera.

The doors closed. The train began to move.

* * *

She did not wave. That surprised her. She had imagined waving—had rehearsed it, absurdly, in the garage at night—but when the moment came her hands stayed at her sides, because waving was what spectators did, and she was finished being a spectator, and besides, he wasn't looking back. He had wedged himself at a window further down the train, and as it gathered speed along the platform she saw his palm press flat against the glass, and she raised her hand after all, too late, uselessly, perfectly, holding it up as the carriage carried his face past her and away.

They rode the lift down to the river terrace and walked out onto the pier at Wapping because Edith insisted—*if we're losing him to the sea we shall at least see the sea take him properly*—and there they stood in the cold wind among the cranes and the barge-smoke while the great ships lay at their moorings like sleeping animals.

The *Missanabie* left on the afternoon tide. They watched the tugs nudge her bow around. They watched her funnel begin to pour its first brown smoke. Vera cried silently, without wiping her face, a Maltese woman who had forged a death notice with steadier hands than she could manage a goodbye. Alistair stood with his cap in his hands like a man at graveside, and perhaps he was; he was burying, after all, the version of himself that had stayed hidden. Edith held Yara's arm, her grip surprisingly strong, and said nothing at all, which from Edith Marlowe was the loudest speech she owned.

The ship moved. Slowly at first, then with the terrible smoothness of large things, down the channel, past the cranes, past the moored lighters, growing smaller and more general—a ship, then a shape, then a smudge—until all that remained of Tobias Finch was the line of his smoke, unwinding eastward along the low grey sky, thinning, stretching, dissolving.

Yara watched until it was gone. Watched until the place where it had been was only sky, indistinguishable from every other part of the sky, so that a stranger would never know anything had passed through it at all. That, she thought, was the whole art of the empire: making passages vanish. Making exits look like weather.

Well. She knew how to fight that now. She had a coal cellar with a false wall and a growing archive behind it. She had a ledger with entries in three colours of ink. She had a habit of reading other people's mail before sealing it, and she intended to teach it forward.

Grief and rightness rose in her on the same breath, exactly as she had known they would, twin tides in the same channel, and she let them. She stood on the pier in her chauffeur's uniform with her employer's hand on her arm and the wind pulling her hair loose beneath her cap, and she grieved, and she was right, and both things were true, and neither cancelled the other.

"He'll be all right," Alistair said at last. It wasn't certainty. It was a decision, made aloud, the way their family made everything.

"He'll be better than all right," Vera said, blowing her nose with tremendous dignity. "He'll be *Canadian*."

Edith laughed—one short, startled bark of it, the first laugh Yara had heard from her in weeks—and then, recovering herself, turned to Yara with eyes that were red-rimmed and absolutely clear.

"Well," she said. "The berth is used. The debt is paid. And on Monday, Miss Nakamura, Alistair surrenders himself at Bow Street, and Vera's name goes into the record, and everything we buried comes up into the daylight whether the War Office likes it or not." She squared her shoulders against the wind. "We shall need a very thorough account of events. Itemized. Dated. In your best hand."

Yara looked at her—the fierce, exhausted, unbreakable woman who had taken in a disowned girl off the street nine years ago and never once asked for a receipt—and felt the next chapter of her life assemble itself, entry by entry, like type falling into a composing stick.

"I'll begin tonight," she said.

And far out on the grey water, where the smoke had been, the first lights of the evening were beginning to show, steady as entries in a ledger that no fire, no warrant, no major in a good coat would ever be permitted to erase.
