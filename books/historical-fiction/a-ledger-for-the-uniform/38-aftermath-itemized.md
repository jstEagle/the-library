## 38. Aftermath, Itemized

The hearing was held in a room that smelled of beeswax and old paper, on the third floor of a building near Whitehall whose windows had been painted over with a pale wash of lime—so that the men inside could deliberate about the truth of a man's existence without the distraction of the world outside. Yara noticed this first, before she noticed anything else: that they had literally painted out the daylight to conduct their inquiry. It seemed to her the most honest thing about the whole proceedings.

She sat in the public gallery with Tobias's coat folded over her arm—he had left it with her that morning with a small apologetic gesture, as though a coat were a thing one surrendered before battle—and she watched Alistair Marlowe give his testimony in a voice like dry gravel.

He had dressed for it in Edith's insistence and Vera's needlework: a dark suit that hung off him where the prison camps and the hiding years had eaten away at his shoulders, a collar so clean it looked accusatory. He stood because they had told him he might sit and he had refused. His hands rested on the rail of the witness box, and Yara saw that they did not tremble. Whatever else the last three years had taken from him, they had not taken his stillness.

"State your name for the record."

"Alistair James Marlowe. Late Captain, Eighth Battalion." A pause, deliberate as a signature. "Declared dead, the eleventh of March, 1911."

The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere behind Yara a man coughed into a handkerchief, and the sound went through the room like a shot.

"You understand," said the chairman—a grey man named Hallowes whose face suggested he had been assembled entirely from committee minutes—"that the record of your death was entered upon sworn testimony. That testimony has now been shown to be false. You are asking this board to accept an account of events which reflects severely upon a serving officer of senior rank."

"I am not asking you to accept it," Alistair said. "I am telling it to you. Whether you accept it is a matter between you and your conscience, sir, and I wouldn't presume to manage either."

Yara bit the inside of her cheek to keep her face still. Beside her, in the gallery, a woman in a brown hat made a small sound—not quite a laugh—and pressed her gloved hand to her mouth.

It took four hours. Yara counted them against the ledger she carried in her handbag, entering times in pencil in the margins of a page she had ruled that morning at the kitchen table while the house still slept: *9:14, chairman opens; 9:31, Alistair names Renwick; 9:40, objection, overruled.* She had learned, these months past, that history was not a weather that happened elsewhere. History was a room. History was chairs and ink and a clerk's pen going scratch, scratch. And history, therefore, could be itemized—could be entered line by line by anyone patient enough to hold the account open.

Alistair told it plainly, without ornament, and the plainness was what gave it weight. How his commanding officer, Colonel Ashworth—Renwick's friend, Renwick's protector—had discovered that Alistair had been collecting evidence of supply fraud that starved the field hospitals; how the discovery had turned; how blackmail had come dressed as patriotism, *keep silent or be ruined*; how Alistair had chosen instead to ruin himself, deserting rather than sign the false report that would have buried the hospital deaths under the word *fever*. How Vera Calloway, a nurse who had seen the same ledgers from the other side of the same beds, had done the one thing within her power: she had written him dead.

"She falsified a death certificate," Hallowes said, "and thereby committed—"

"She saved a life," Alistair said, "with the only weapon the Empire left in her hands. A pen. I'd have thought this board would recognize the instrument, if nothing else."

The silence after that lasted eleven seconds. Yara timed it.

* * *

Vera testified in the afternoon, and the room changed around her the way a room changes when a lamp is lit.

They had tried to make her small. Yara watched it happen—the way Hallowes addressed his questions slightly over her head, as though speaking to some male relative who ought to have accompanied her; the way the word *forgery* was deployed twice in the first five minutes, laid down like caltrops on the road ahead. But Vera Calloway had spent six years being underestimated by men in uniform, and she had developed, in consequence, a manner that Yara could only describe as unhurried mercy. She answered everything. She volunteered more than they asked. She produced, from a plain canvas satchel, the blackmail ledger itself—the original, the true account, kept in Colonel Ashworth's own cramped hand: sums paid, sums owed, names of dead soldiers converted quietly into line items marked *stores written off*.

"Do you know," Vera said, setting it on the table with both hands, the way one sets down something fragile or sacred, "in Malta we had a phrase for paperwork like this. We called it *the second war*. The first war killed the men. The second war killed the truth about how. I only ever fought in the second one."

"And you admit altering official records."

"I admit writing a death notice that was truer than the official one," Vera said. "The official record said Captain Marlowe was dead. Captain Marlowe, as far as the Army was concerned—as far as his pay, his pension, his family's grief was concerned—was indeed dead. Killed by his own side, administratively, which is the commonest way officers die. My forgery simply caught up with the facts."

Yara, in the gallery, felt something move through her chest that she would later, in the ledger, call by no name at all, because no name she knew was adequate. It was recognition. It was seeing herself at a distance of a few yards and a whole world of difference: another woman with a pen, another keeper of accounts, deciding alone at midnight what the record would say. The difference was that Vera had acted and Yara had only ever read. But the distance, she understood suddenly, was closable. It had perhaps always been closable.

The vindication, when the board delivered its findings three days later, came wrapped in the thinnest possible language, the way institutions wrap everything they are ashamed of. Colonel Ashworth's conduct was referred for further examination. Major Renwick's name appeared in the final paragraph, in a clause about *lapses of administrative judgment*, which everyone in the room understood to mean that he would resign quietly and keep his pension and never be spoken of again—which was, Yara thought, its own kind of death notice, and better deserved than most. Vera's actions were described as *technically irregular but materially justified*, a phrase Edith read aloud at the breakfast table the following morning with such relish that she read it three times, once for each of them, like a toast.

"Technically irregular," Edith said, buttering toast with the flourish of a woman knighted. "Materially justified. Yara, write that down somewhere permanent. If they ever put it on my headstone I shall consider my life a complete success, and I haven't even got the vote yet."

* * *

Because that was the other aftermath, the one that began before the hearings ended: Edith resumed.

Not gently. There was no gentle available to her. The Cat and Mouse Act had taken twelve pounds off her frame and left her with a cough that lingered into October, and the movement's organizers had wanted—had *begged*—her to take a quiet season, a restorative winter, a chair by a fire. Edith Marlowe received these entreaties the way a locomotive receives suggestions about timetables.

By the second week of November she was speaking again. Yara drove her to halls in Islington and Camberwell and a Methodist chapel in Poplar where the roof leaked rain onto the front row and nobody moved out of it. The crowds were different now—larger, younger, angrier in a way that had stopped being fashionable anger and become something with teeth. The war talk was everywhere, in the newspapers, in the public houses, in the way men on street corners looked at the Daimler as though assessing it for requisition. Edith spoke about the vote, but underneath the vote she spoke about time. *How long,* she asked them, *how long will you be told to wait? How long is a woman's patience meant to run? Until the guns start? After?*

In the car afterward, wrapped in rugs, her voice hoarse to a whisper, she would say almost nothing. Once—only once—she broke the silence.

"They'll declare it before spring," she said, looking out at the lamplight sliding along the wet streets. "You've seen it too. Everyone's seen it. And when it comes, they'll tell us all to be quiet, that there's a war on, that our little grievances can wait. They always say wait. Waiting is what they're *for*." She turned her head then and looked at Yara directly, and her eyes were tired and absolutely lit. "So we must not wait. Whatever comes. Promise me you remember that, when I'm not here to say it loudly enough."

"I promise," Yara said, and found that she meant it in the marrow-way, the way one means things that cost.

* * *

Tobias stayed through November. There was no longer any need for him to hide—Renwick's fall had lifted the hunt off him like weather clearing—but he stayed anyway, sleeping in the narrow room above the garage that had been meant as temporary and had quietly become otherwise. He mended fences that didn't need mending. He learned the Daimler's temperaments. He sat with Edith over the correspondence files and catalogued them in his neat clerk's hand, and the two of them developed a shorthand of grunts and raised eyebrows that Yara found, against her will, deeply endearing.

On the last evening of the month, he found her in the garage.

The Daimler was up on blocks; Yara had the front axle stripped and was lying half beneath the chassis with a spanner in her fist and grease to her elbows, and she heard him come in by the particular drag of his boot on the concrete—the step of a man who had learned to walk carefully in hospitals and had never unlearned it.

"The boat train's Tuesday," he said.

"I know."

"I've been thinking I might stay. Actually stay. There's work—I could get work, clerking, and the house—" He stopped. She heard him stop, the way one hears a door close partway. "Edith says I'm a coward if I go and a fool if I stay, and that both options suit her perfectly because she enjoys having someone to insult at breakfast."

Yara slid out from under the car and sat up. Her hands were black to the wrist. She looked at them, and then at him, and the thing she had decided weeks ago—the decision she had forged into existence with borrowed ink and a steady hand—stood between them in the garage like the Daimler itself: solid, serviced, waiting to be driven.

"Tobias," she said. "Do you trust me?"

"With my life," he said, and had the grace to look embarrassed immediately, and she loved him for the embarrassment, and said nothing about any of it.

"Then don't ask me any questions until Tuesday," she said. "Tuesday, everything makes sense. I promise you that much. Tuesday, you'll see the whole account, balanced."

He frowned at her—the frown of a clerk confronting a column that won't sum—but he nodded. Above them, through the small garage window, the London sky went the color of cooling iron, and somewhere across the city a church bell rang out the hour, indifferent and exact.

* * *

That night, long after the house had gone dark, Yara sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open before her and a single candle burning, because electric light seemed wrong for this—too modern, too easy. The ledger had begun in June as an exercise, a clerk's game, dates and stamps and sums. It had become, page by page, the true history of the year: the letters, the march, Holloway, the coal cellar, the berth, the quay, Renwick's face when Edith had unfolded the blackmail ledger on the dock like a fan. Everything itemized. Everything accounted for.

She turned to a fresh page and ruled it, and wrote the date at the top in her careful hand, and sat for a long moment with the pen lifted, listening to the house breathe around her—the tick of the range, the settle of beams, Edith's cough, faintly, two floors up, and then the silence that meant she'd slept.

Then she wrote:

*Final entry.*

*Item: one captain, restored to the living. Item: one nurse, vindicated. Item: one brother and one sister, quarreling again, which is to say, reconciled. Item: one soldier, safe passage granted—by whom, the record shows a signature; the signature shows a hand; the hand was mine. Item: one chauffeur, remaining.*

*I began this book believing that history happened elsewhere—to other people, in other rooms, at other ends of wires I would never hold. I was wrong. It was never elsewhere. It was in the mail I sealed. In the garage. In the cellar. In every door I opened or did not. History happened here.*

*History happened here. I was in it.*

She blotted the page, closed the book, and set her palm flat upon the cover for a moment, as though taking an oath upon it or feeling for a heartbeat.

And it was only then, in the hush after the pen stopped, that she let herself think clearly about Tuesday—the berth expiring, the ticket bearing a forged name, the young man upstairs who believed he owed her his life and did not yet know she intended to spend hers making the account balance. Outside, the wind had turned. It came off the river smelling of salt and coal smoke and, faintly, unmistakably, of snow.

Ten days, the shipping line had said. She looked at the calendar on the wall, at tomorrow's square, and picked up her pen again.
