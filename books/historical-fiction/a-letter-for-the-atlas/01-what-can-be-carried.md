## 1. What Can Be Carried

On the morning the Ministry of Roads burned its own archives, Freya Sandoval was measuring her life in a small room above the Courier's Hall, and finding it heavier than she expected.

Not that it weighed much. That was the point of a cipher-case: brass-bound, palm-sized, designed to hold everything a courier needed to be trusted and nothing a courier could afford to lose. Hers had been issued eleven years ago, when her hands were still soft enough to blister on a hard ride and her superiors still bothered to learn her name. Inside it lived three things: a set of rotating letter-plates for the old republic cipher, a wax seal stamped with the double star of the Chancellery, and — though no inventory would ever list it — the particular silence of every person who had ever handed it to her and said *don't open this*.

She had carried treaties. She had carried pardons. She had carried, once, a single line informing a garrison commander that his brother had died at sea, which was not encrypted at all because there was nothing in it anyone could steal.

Freya laid the case on the narrow bed beside her packed satchel and looked at both of them for a long moment, the way a woman might look at two suitors, one of whom had been lying to her for years.

Below her window, the Ministry of Roads was feeding itself to a brazier.

This was official policy now, or as close to policy as the republic still came. The Archive Act of the Provisional Session — passed at midnight, by candlelight, by ministers who had already sent their families south — required the destruction of all records that might "aid hostile administration." In practice this meant that clerks who had spent thirty years filing road surveys were now tearing them into strips and feeding them into iron baskets while soldiers stood around looking embarrassed. The smoke over the Ministry quarter had become so constant that the city's pigeons had relocated, all at once, in what one of the hall's stable boys described as "the only orderly evacuation this government has managed."

Freya watched a page of something — a bridge schedule, maybe, or a survey of the eastern passes — spiral up past her window, catch the wind, and land burning on the cobbles like a dying orange bird.

Eleven years of carrying things for people who were now carrying armfuls of their life's work to the fire.

She closed the shutter.

* * *

The Courier's Hall had been built, a century ago, to impress foreign visitors with the reach and permanence of the republic. It had marble columns carved with the faces of famous couriers — men and women frozen mid-stride, dispatch cases in hand, expressions of serene purpose that Freya had always found faintly insulting. Nobody looked serene on day nine of a hard ride. The sculptors had clearly never met an actual courier, or had met only the ones who'd survived.

The hall was nearly empty now. Of the forty-two couriers listed on the duty board, nine remained. Six of those were too old to ride and too stubborn to say so; one was Freya; two were boys of seventeen whose main qualification, as far as she could tell, was being too young to remember any government but this one.

Master of Dispatches Corvin Hett presided over the empty room from behind a desk built for four clerks, reading a document with such concentration that he had clearly read it forty times.

"Hett," Freya said.

"Sandoval." He didn't look up. "You're early."

"I'm always early."

"You're always early." He finally raised his head. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been awake for several days in several different centuries. "Sit down."

She sat. Around them, the great hall echoed with its own emptiness. Somewhere in the back, the two young couriers were arguing about whether a horse could be taught to swim, which under the circumstances struck Freya as exactly the right thing to argue about.

Hett slid a folder across the desk. It was sealed twice — once in plain wax, once beneath the Chancellery's double star pressed into black.

"Commission," he said. "Sealed orders. Destination is inside."

"Which ministry signed it?"

"The Chancellery."

"The Chancellery evacuated three days ago. Half of it went south and the other half went into the river, figuratively speaking."

"Then you'll appreciate the irony of delivering its mail." He pushed the folder another inch. His hand, Freya noticed, did not entirely want to let go of it. "There's a cipher-case going with it. Yours."

"It's already mine."

"This one's yours *properly*. New plates. Old cipher." He said the last part carefully, the way a man says *the doctor says it's benign* — technically good news, delivered badly.

Freya took the folder. The wax was warm from his keeping. Underneath the seals, she could feel the stiff shape of the commission papers, and folded within them — she knew the thickness by heart — the authorization that would make every checkpoint, toll, and garrison between here and the Atlas Workshop answer to her name.

The Atlas Workshop. She had been expecting this assignment for a week, ever since the rumors started moving faster than the couriers. The Workshop held the treaty plates — the engraved masters from which the republic printed its accords, its currency of trust, its whole paper architecture of promises. Whoever held the plates held the ability to make anything look agreed-upon. Everyone in the capital understood this. Which meant everyone outside the capital was riding toward it.

"How long do I have?" she asked.

"Before what?"

"Hett."

He rubbed his eyes. "Before the general's outriders close the northern road, I'd guess six days. Before the city falls, ten. Before the ministries finish evacuating—" he gestured at the smoke-stained light coming through the high windows, "—well. You've seen the pace we move at when we're motivated."

"And after I deliver?"

"After you deliver, you'll receive new instructions through the Workshop's dead-drop, same as always." He almost smiled. "Assuming there's still someone on the other end of the drop."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm a reassuring man. Ask anyone. They'll say, 'him? No.'"

Despite everything — despite the smoke and the empty hall and the folder in her hands getting no lighter — Freya laughed. It came out cracked from disuse, but it was a laugh. Eleven years of riding with Corvin Hett signing her dispatches had built up a certain tolerance in each of them, the way a lock and key wear smooth together.

"Freya." He said it quietly, and she stopped smiling, because in eleven years he had used her first name perhaps four times, and never indoors. "The orders are sealed. You know the rule."

"I know the rule."

"Not even a courier reads her own commission. Not even now. Especially not now."

"I know the rule," she said again. "I wrote half the rules."

"I know." He looked at her across the desk with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — not pity, not apology, something older than either. "That's why they chose you."

She stood, tucked the folder into the oilcloth inner pocket of her coat, where it sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and picked up her cipher-case. New plates, old cipher. Everything a courier needed to be trusted.

At the door of the great hall she paused, because one of the seventeen-year-olds had won the swimming argument and was now trying to demonstrate on the other one, and for a moment the marble couriers on their columns seemed to lean down and watch, mid-stride forever, carrying their beautiful empty cases to destinations that no longer existed.

*Don't open this*, said the silence in the case.

She went down to the stables to see about her horse.

* * *

Her horse was called Biscuit, which she had not chosen and had given up protesting years ago. He was a dun gelding of moderate opinions and excellent endurance, and he greeted her by attempting to eat the tassel off her shoulder.

"No," she told him.

He ate it anyway. She let him. There seemed little point these days in defending institutions nobody else was defending.

The stable master, a broad woman named Greta who had outlasted three ministers and one actual fire, was mucking the next stall and watching her with the frank assessment of someone who had watched couriers leave for twenty years and knew the arithmetic of returns.

"North road?" Greta said.

"North road."

"They burned the granaries at Millbrook yesterday."

"I heard."

"Heard from who? The couriers stopped coming through Millbrook Tuesday."

"From the pigeons," Freya said. "They're very well-informed since they left the Ministry quarter."

Greta snorted and leaned on her fork. "You'll want the gray blanket off the top shelf. Cold nights already, up past the ford. And take the extra shoes. Farrier's gone south with his family and I'm not letting you ruin my horse's feet because the republic can't keep a farrier."

"Your horse."

"My horse. You're just the legs." But her voice had gone soft in the middle of it, and when she handed over the blanket her hand rested a moment on Freya's sleeve. "Come back, girl. I've buried enough of you this year."

"Greta—"

"Don't. I hate the speeches. Go on."

So Freya went, which was its own kind of speech, and understood — walking out into the ash-flecked afternoon with Biscuit stamping behind her — that this was what a courier actually carried. Not the case. Not the orders. The weight of every hand that had rested on her sleeve and decided not to say the thing. Greta's. Hett's. The seventeen-year-olds, who had stopped arguing about horses to watch her cross the yard, wearing the particular expression of boys doing sums about survival.

Orders, guilt, nothing else. That was the allowance. She had always traveled light.

Mostly.

* * *

She spent her last evening in the capital doing what she always did before a long ride, which was nothing. This was a discipline she had learned in her second year, from a dying courier named Anselm who told her, with the confidence of a man who had been wrong about many things but not this, that the road punished preparation and rewarded rest. *The night before a ride*, he'd said, *you eat, you sleep, and you let the world get worse without your help.*

The world obliged. From the window of her room she watched the fires of the Ministry quarter pulse against the low clouds, orange and patient, and somewhere to the north a bell began ringing — not the alarm bell, but the other one, the one the churches rang when there was no longer anything useful to say.

She ate. She tried to sleep.

What kept surfacing instead, in the dark behind her eyes, was a memory she had not visited in years: her first delivery, aged nineteen, standing in a farmhouse kitchen in the hill country while a farmer read her sealed letter by lamplight and then sat down very slowly on a bench, because the letter conscripted his sons. She remembered how he had looked at her — not angry, not pleading, just *reading* her, the way you'd read a weather sign, trying to determine whether the disaster was her fault or merely passing through her.

*It's just paper*, she had wanted to say. *I don't write it. I carry it.*

And he had nodded, eventually, and thanked her, because the republic's people were polite even to its weather, and she had ridden away understanding for the first time that a courier was not neutral. A courier was the last inch of every order. The pen wrote it, the minister dreamed it, but her boots brought it to the door. There was no inch of distance anywhere in the chain of command smaller than the space between her hand and the latch.

She had made her peace with that inch, more or less, over eleven years. You carried what you were given. You didn't open it. You didn't ask. The rule wasn't bureaucracy; it was mercy — mercy on the courier, mostly, because a courier who knew what she carried became something else, something with opinions, and a courier with opinions was just a person on a horse, and persons on horses got shot at borders by men with questions.

But tonight the peace sat crooked, like a saddle packed wrong, and she kept adjusting it and it kept slipping.

Because the ministries weren't evacuating. She had watched them all week and told herself *evacuating*, but that wasn't the word. Evacuation implied somewhere to go. What the ministries were doing was *shedding* — dropping records, dropping clerks, dropping pretense — the way a fleeing animal drops weight. And a republic that had begun shedding its paperwork, its roads, its bridges, its names — what exactly remained, at the far end of her ride, to honor the paper she carried?

Who would enforce her commission? Who was the *republic* anymore — the ministers on the southern road? The clerks feeding the fires? The pigeons?

*Stop*, she told herself, in the flat tone she used on green recruits. *You have a destination and a deadline. Everything else is weather.*

But the thought wouldn't quite die, and it lay down beside her in the dark like a third traveler: *a courier who starts asking what her paper means is already opening the case.*

She woke before dawn without having decided to, dressed in the dark, and went down through the sleeping hall with her satchel and her case and her coat with its double-star badge, past the marble couriers striding nowhere, past Hett's empty desk where someone had left, neatly centered, a single unsealed envelope marked *for whoever comes back*.

She didn't read it. She wanted to. She noted, clinically, that wanting to was new.

In the yard the sky was the color of dirty wool and the air tasted of smoke and rain that hadn't committed yet. Greta was there, of course, pretending to check hooves in the dark, and pressed a wrapped parcel of food into the saddlebag without being asked — bread, hard cheese, and, inexplicably, a jar of honey.

"For the cold mornings," Greta said. "And because if you die I want to feel I spoiled you a little first."

Biscuit ate another tassel. The gates stood open. North, beyond the roofs, the road ran out toward the ford and the hills and whatever the general's outriders were doing to it, and Freya Sandoval swung up into the saddle with the case against her ribs and the sealed orders against her heartbeat, carrying — as she had always carried — orders, guilt, and nothing else.

She was three streets past the walls when she realized she was checking the seal on the folder with her thumb. Pressing it, the way you press a bruise. Black wax. Double star. Unbroken.

Unbroken, and warm from her own hand, and lying flat against her chest like something alive that hadn't yet decided what to be.

Six days to the Workshop, if the road held. She gave Biscuit his head, and the city fell away behind them smoking, and neither of them looked back — though Freya would maintain, for the rest of her life, that the horse did.
