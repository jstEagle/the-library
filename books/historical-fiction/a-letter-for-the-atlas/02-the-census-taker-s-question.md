## 2. The Census Taker's Question

The border checkpoint at Vell's Crossing had been, in better years, a toll booth.

Freya Sandoval remembered it from her first commission, six years ago — a cheerful little wooden hut with a striped awning and a clerk who charged two coppers per cart and waved couriers through for free, on the theory that anyone carrying government seals was doing the republic a favor by existing. There had been a pot of geraniums on the sill. She remembered the geraniums specifically, because she had thought at the time: *this is what the republic looks like when it is healthy. It grows flowers at its borders.*

Now the awning was gone, the hut was sandbagged to the windows, and the geraniums had been replaced with a machine gun whose crew looked like they'd rather be growing flowers somewhere themselves. A queue of perhaps forty travelers stretched back along the road — farmers with carts, a family strapped to their own furniture like refugees from a house fire that hadn't happened yet, two monks arguing quietly about whether their vow of poverty covered the bribe the guards were likely to want.

Freya joined the queue and did what she always did in queues: she measured her life against the case slung across her chest.

The cipher-case was a flat iron box the size of a prayer book, strapped to her ribs under her coat, and she was aware of it the way other people were aware of their own heartbeats. It contained the diplomatic cipher of the Republic of Vessany — the final one, the ministry clerk had said, as though ciphers came in seasons like fruit. Inside the case, folded into oilcloth beside the codebook, was her sealed commission: orders from the Ministry of Continuance (a name that had seemed inspiring four weeks ago and now sounded like a joke told by a drowning man) directing her to deliver the case to the Atlas Workshop in Correnvale, there to be opened only by the master printer or her successor.

She had been traveling nine days. She had slept in ditches twice, in a barn once, and once — memorably — in a chapel where the priest had asked no questions and accepted payment in salt. Her boots had begun to develop opinions about her feet, none of them charitable. And the road behind her had grown steadily quieter in the way roads do when the news traveling down them gets worse: first the gossip stopped, then the singing, then the children, then everyone.

Ahead of her in the queue, the two monks had settled their theological dispute by deciding that poverty covered bribes but not humiliation, and they would pay if humiliated, which struck Freya as the most honest reading of scripture she'd heard all year.

The line moved. The line stopped. Somewhere ahead, a soldier laughed at something that wasn't funny.

It was while the queue was stopped that Freya noticed the man at the table.

He sat apart from the soldiers, at a folding desk dragged out onto the road itself, with a ledger open before him and an inkwell weighted down with a stone. He was perhaps forty, thin in the way of men who forget meals rather than skip them, with ink on three fingers and the fourth finger of his left hand wrapped in a grubby bandage — a pen callus gone wrong, she guessed, a wound earned honestly by paperwork. He wore the grey coat of the Census Bureau, which was a coat Freya knew well, because the Census Bureau was the one arm of the government that had never once lied to her. Numbers didn't flatter. You were either counted or you weren't.

What struck her was that he wasn't asking the usual questions.

She watched him work through the family with the furniture. A soldier asked their names; the man at the table waited politely until that was done, and then he leaned forward and said something too quiet for Freya to hear. The father of the family answered. The man wrote it down. The family passed through.

Then the monks. Same pattern. The soldier asked names and business; the census taker asked *something else*; the elder monk answered after a long pause, looking unhappy about it; and through they went.

Freya found, to her mild alarm, that she was rehearsing answers to questions she couldn't yet hear.

*You're overthinking,* she told herself. *He's a census taker. He's counting heads because the Bureau still exists on paper, and paper is the last thing this republic will surrender.* She had spent her career believing that paperwork was the skeleton of civilization — that as long as forms were filed and ledgers kept, some part of a country went on breathing even after the body politic stopped moving. It was a belief that had gotten her through the fall of three provincial capitals without losing so much as a signature.

The queue advanced. Three people. Two. One.

"Next."

Freya stepped up to the sandbagged booth. The soldier there was young enough that his uniform fit him like a borrowed opinion. He took her travel writ, glanced at the courier's seal, and visibly lost interest — couriers were, apparently, still doing the republic favors by existing.

"Courier," he said, to nobody, and waved her past.

She got exactly four steps before the voice at the folding desk said, "Madam Courier. A moment, if the road permits."

She turned. The census taker had risen from his chair — a courtesy she noted; most officials made you come to them these days, as if standing were a resource to be conserved. Up close he had the particular stillness of a man who had learned to listen for a living, and eyes that were doing precisely that.

"Ilan Voss," he said, "Bureau of Census and Registry." He didn't offer credentials. Perhaps he'd run out. Perhaps, she thought later, he simply felt the coat was credential enough.

"Freya Sandoval," she said, and gave him nothing else, because giving nothing else was the job.

"One moment of your time, madam. The Bureau requires it."

"The Bureau," Freya said, "is aware that courier traffic is exempt from enumeration."

"The Bureau is aware of a great many things." He smiled — a small, tired, genuine smile, the kind that doesn't try to win anything. "I'm afraid I've been instructed to ask additional questions. New directive. From the Ministry of Continuance, as it happens."

That landed somewhere under her ribs. Her own ministry. Sending directives to border checkpoints while its own building was being packed into crates. Either the bureaucracy was admirably committed to its duties or it was gathering information for someone, and Freya had seen enough of the war's early months to know those two possibilities were no longer mutually exclusive.

"Ask your questions," she said.

He opened his ledger to a fresh page. Dipped his pen. And then he looked up at her with an expression of mild professional apology, the look of a man about to ask you to remove your shoes at a funeral, and said:

"Whom do you serve?"

The question hung there in the dust of the road.

Not *what is your name*. Not *your age, your trade, your district of origin, your household size* — the litany of the census, the questions that treated every citizen as a fact to be recorded. Not even the newer questions, the ugly ones creeping into checkpoints across the country: *your faith, your province of birth, whether any member of your family had served the old Assembly.*

Just that. Whom do you serve.

It was, Freya would reflect afterward, a beautifully constructed trap, or possibly just an honest question, and the trouble was that she could not immediately tell the difference — which was itself an answer.

"The Republic of Vessany," she said.

It came out wrong.

She heard it come out wrong even as she said it — half a beat too slow, pitched a fraction too high, the way a child says a memorized lesson when the teacher has wandered off-script. Six days of burned villages and silent roads, of ministries evacuating around her like organs leaving a body, and it turned out that when the moment came to say the words plainly, her mouth hesitated. Her mouth, which had never once hesitated over a delivery confirmation, a customs declaration, a sworn statement. Her mouth had opinions now. Her mouth had been reading the countryside.

Ilan Voss wrote something in his ledger. It was not, Freya suspected, *Republic of Vessany*.

"You hesitated," he said, conversationally, not looking up.

"I was considering the formality of my answer."

"You were considering whether it was true." He set down the pen. "Forgive me. That's not a question I'm supposed to follow up on. But I've asked it forty-one times today, and yours is the first answer that arrived late. Everyone else answers instantly. Farmers say 'my family.' Soldiers say 'the General' now, mostly, and don't lower their voices. Monks say 'God,' and then usually add 'and also whoever isn't shooting at us.'" He tapped the page. "You said the Republic the way people say the name of someone at a funeral. Correctly, and sadly, and a moment after they should have."

Freya stood very still. Under her coat, against her ribs, the cipher-case seemed to grow warmer, though she knew perfectly well that iron boxes did not have temperatures and the heat was entirely hers.

"That hesitation," she said carefully, "could be reported."

"It could."

"A courier traveling under ministry seal, answering allegiance questions badly at a border checkpoint. There are regulations about that. There are probably several regulations about that. I've read most of them."

"There are indeed," Ilan agreed. "Regulation 44-C, if we're being precise: misstatement of allegiance to a census official carries a penalty of detention pending investigation." He paused. "Pending investigation by the Bureau of Census and Registry. Which currently consists of me, a broken inkwell, and a bandage that has stopped being medically relevant."

"And how many investigations are pending?"

"None." He closed the ledger. "Do you know what I did before the war, madam? Twenty-two years. I counted people. I counted births and deaths and marriages and the number of chickens per household in the eastern valleys, because someone had to know how many chickens there were, and apparently that someone was me." He flexed the bandaged finger absently. "And in twenty-two years, I learned one thing above all others: the ledger doesn't care what you feel. It cares what's true. A lie in a census poisons everything built on it — the grain allotments, the tax rolls, the vaccination schedules. Everything downstream of a lie runs sick."

He looked at her then, directly, and there was nothing in his face except a kind of weary professional curiosity.

"So when a woman carrying a ministry seal tells me whom she serves, and takes a full second to answer — I don't think *traitor*. Traitors lie smoothly. I've met smooth liars; they're very efficient at the checkpoint." He picked up the stone, weighed it, set it back on the inkwell. "I think: here is someone who used to be sure, and isn't anymore, and hates it. And I would very much like to know why, because I have been watching this country be unsure for eight months, and I am starting to suspect that the uncertainty is not an accident."

The machine gun crew behind him had begun an argument about card games. One of the monks was trying to sell the other his half of a cheese wheel. The queue grumbled forward like weather. The world, indifferent as ever, continued.

"You're proposing," Freya said slowly, "that instead of detaining me, you... walk away from your post."

"I'm proposing," Ilan said, "to file myself as a statistic."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Emigration. Outbound. One adult male, occupation: census taker, reason for departure:—" he considered— "'professional disagreement with the questions.' That's accurate. That's honest. It'll poison everything downstream of it, of course, but the Bureau's downstream is a burning archive, so I find I can live with the corruption."

"You're deserting."

"I prefer 'reclassifying.'"

Despite herself — despite the case against her ribs and the road ahead and nine days of discipline worn down to the studs — Freya felt the corner of her mouth move. She smothered it. Smothering smiles at checkpoints was practically regulation.

"Mr. Voss," she said, "you don't know anything about me. I could be carrying anything in that case. I could be anyone."

"You're a courier," he said. "Your boots are ministry-issue, resoled twice, which means long roads and short budgets. Your coat has a faint rectangle pressed into the left breast where something heavy rides against your ribs — a document case, iron, by the wear on the strap. You checked the strap twice while waiting in line, both times with your right hand, which means you're left-handed, which means you keep your writing hand free, which means whatever you carry matters more than your comfort." He shrugged, apologetic. "Also you have ink on your left cuff and you smell faintly of sealing wax. I notice things. It's the whole of my training and most of my personality, I'm afraid."

Freya looked at him for a long moment.

Here was the arithmetic of the road, laid out plain: a stranger who saw too much, who could report her with a word and ruin nine days of work with a sentence, standing in the dust asking — not demanding, *asking* — to come along. Every protocol she owned said refuse. Every protocol said trust no one, carry alone, deliver alone, and let the republic's secrets die with you if it came to that.

Every protocol had also, she was beginning to suspect, been written by people who assumed the republic would still exist at the end of the journey.

"Why?" she asked. It wasn't the smart question. The smart question was *what do you want*. But she found she wanted to know the true one more than the useful one, which was, she reflected, probably how the whole country had gotten into this state.

Ilan Voss gathered his ledger, his broken inkwell, his stone. He tucked the chair under the table with a small housekeeping gesture, the gesture of a man leaving a room he expected to see again and knowing he wouldn't.

"Because I spent twenty-two years recording what this country was," he said. "And in eight months I've watched it stop being that, and nobody — not the ministers, not the soldiers, not the priests — will tell me what it's becoming. They all claim to know. None of them show their work." He settled the ledger under his arm like a book he intended to finish. "But you're going somewhere with a sealed case and a straight back, and you hesitated when you said whom you serve, which means you might actually care about the answer. So I'd like to walk with you and watch. Call it fieldwork. Call it a final survey." He smiled that tired smile again. "Call it, if you like, taking the census of wherever it is we're all going next."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll write down 'courier declined interview' and sit back down, and we will never have had this conversation, and I will go on asking forty strangers a day whom they serve until someone shoots me or the questions stop mattering." He adjusted the bandage on his finger. "Though between us, madam — I don't think the questions ever mattered. I think what mattered was always whether anyone answered honestly. And today, out of forty-one people, exactly one of them did."

"You said I lied."

"I said you hesitated." He picked up his bag. "Lying is what comes after the hesitation. You haven't decided yet. That's the most honest thing I've seen at this checkpoint since the war started, and I include the monks."

Somewhere up the road, a bell began ringing in the village beyond the crossing — not an alarm, just the noon bell, marking the hour for a town that still bothered to keep time. Freya looked north, toward Correnvale, toward the Atlas Workshop and the delivery that defined her, and felt the strange double weight of the case against her ribs: iron on the outside, and something less nameable underneath, something that had shifted slightly during the conversation, the way cargo shifts when a cart turns.

"One condition," she said.

Ilan waited.

"You carry your own bag. I don't share rations with Bureau personnel. Bureau personnel," she added, "eat the rations meant for Bureau personnel, and then file a requisition for mine."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, and then delight, quick and unguarded, the expression of a man who has spent months among people too frightened or too angry to make jokes. "Madam, that is the single most bureaucratic insult I have received this year, and I once had a minister call me 'statistically inconvenient' to my face."

"High praise. Walk or don't."

He walked.

They passed the sandbags together, past the young soldier who didn't look up, past the monks and their cheese wheel, and Freya did not look back at the checkpoint, because couriers did not look back, because looking back implied doubt about the direction, and she had a delivery to make, and a schedule, and a republic to serve.

*The Republic of Vessany,* she said inside her own head, testing the words.

They came out on time this time. But they came out, she noticed, in the tone Ilan had described — correct, and sad, and a moment late — and she wondered, with a chill she filed away under *later*, under *things to examine when there was a roof and a lock and no witnesses*, exactly what it was that her mouth knew that her orders did not.

Beside her, Ilan Voss opened his ledger against his forearm and began, walking, to write. She caught only the first line before he angled it away.

*Outbound, one adult male. Reason: professional disagreement with the questions.*

"Where to first?" he asked cheerfully.

"North," Freya said. "Correnvale."

His pen stopped moving.

"Correnvale," he repeated. "The printing town."

"The Workshop," she said. "Why? What do you know about Correnvale?"

Ilan looked at her for a moment, and then down at his ledger, and when he spoke again the cheerfulness had drained out of his voice as neatly as water out of a cracked cup.

"Three days ago," he said, "the last outbound traveler through this checkpoint from the east told me the General's engineers had reached the river valley. Madam — they weren't marching on Correnvale's garrison." He closed the ledger slowly. "They were measuring the bridges. All of them. As if planning to hold the town rather than take it. Nobody holds a printing town unless there's something in it worth holding."

Freya walked on. Against her ribs, the iron case rode steady and silent, keeping its secret, keeping its schedule — and for the first time in nine days, she found herself wondering not whether she would arrive in time.

But what, exactly, she would be arriving with.

* * *

They made twelve miles before dusk, walking fast through country that grew more beautiful the more ruined it became — which was, Freya was learning, simply how the land did grief: orchards blossoming over abandoned farms, poppies crowding the roadside ditches as if the flowers had held a vote and decided color was a form of resistance.

They camped in the lee of a fallen boundary marker, an old stone post with the republic's crest worn nearly smooth, and shared a fire the size of two cupped hands, because smoke traveled far in flat country and neither of them wanted company.

Ilan ate his rations — Bureau rations, hard biscuit and dried figs — and then, with the transparent casualness of a man attempting sleight of hand for the first time in his life, set three of the figs on the flat stone between them.

"Bureau surplus," he said. "Requisitioned from no one."

"You said you don't share rations with—"

"You said *you* don't. I made no such pledge. I am, after all, reclassified." He lay back against his pack, ledger under his head like a pillow, and stared up at a sky already filling with stars. For a while the only sounds were the fire ticking and, distantly, a dog barking at some farm that might or might not still have people in it.

"Madam Sandoval," he said at last, to the stars rather than to her. "May I ask a census question?"

"You may ask. I reserve the right to hesitate."

That earned her a breath of laughter in the dark. Then, quietly: "When you answered me today. At the table. When you said whom you serve." The pause stretched. "What did you almost say instead?"

Freya fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch.

She thought about lying. She was, after all, extremely good at it in every format except spoken aloud to observant men at folding desks. And she thought about the truth, which was that she had almost said nothing at all — that for one full second her mind had presented her with a blank page where her allegiance was supposed to be written, and the blank had frightened her more than any soldier at any checkpoint.

"I almost said," she finally replied, "'ask me when I've finished the delivery.'"

"Hm." Ilan turned this over, professionally, the way he must have turned over harvest numbers and chicken counts. "That's not an evasion, you know. That's actually a coherent position. Service deferred to duty completed." He yawned. "Most people can't manage even that much structure anymore. The whole country's answers have gone soft at the edges. Like overripe fruit."

"Is that in the ledger? 'Country: soft at the edges, like overripe fruit'?"

"It will be." A rustle; the scratch of his pen in the dark, working blind, trusting his hand. "Entry four hundred and six. Witness: one courier, ministry seal, resoled boots. Statement: reliable, hesitant, shares nothing, eats figs under protest."

"I ate them under *no* protest whatsoever."

"Noted. Amendment entered." The scratching paused. "Goodnight, madam courier."

"Goodnight, Mr. Voss."

She took the first watch, as couriers did — trust was a currency she spent in installments, and tonight's installment was letting him sleep within reach of her pack while she sat awake. Above the dead boundary marker the stars came out in their cold thousands, indifferent as ledgers. Below the ridge, far off to the southeast, there was a glow on the horizon that might have been a burning town and might have been a kiln and might have been nothing, and she watched it for a long time, unable to decide which possibility she was rooting for.

Behind her, Ilan murmured something in his sleep — numbers, she thought. Reciting numbers. Even dreaming, the man was counting.

Freya pulled her coat tighter, touched the strap of the cipher-case once, with her right hand, out of habit, and settled in to guard a fire, a stranger, and a delivery whose contents she had never once doubted —

until now, when the doubt had arrived, punctual as the noon bell, and refused to be filed anywhere.
