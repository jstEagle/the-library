## 3. Road of Ash

The first village was called Merrow's Bend, and it had stopped existing on a Tuesday.

Freya knew this because Ilan told her. He had a way of knowing things — not the dramatic things, not the movements of armies or the fall of ministries, but the small administrative facts that made up the skeleton of a country. Merrow's Bend had a tax assessment of forty-one households, two mills, and a chapel whose roof had been re-slated in the spring. Now it had none of those things. It had smoke, and ash that rose in soft grey ghosts when the wind moved through what had been streets.

"You can tell how long ago by the color," Ilan said, standing in the cart as they rolled down its one road. "Fresh burns are black. After three days they go grey. After a week, white." He paused. "This is four days old."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because the bread ovens are still warm underneath." He crouched at the ruin of one and pressed his palm flat against a scorched brick, then held his hand up like a man taking an oath. "See? The heat holds. Four days, maybe five. Whoever did this didn't stay. They burned and moved on."

Freya looked at the village and felt the numbness she'd been cultivating since the capital — a careful, disciplined frost, like ice over a well — crack just slightly along one edge. She pushed it back down. A courier carried orders, guilt, and nothing else. Grief was not on the manifest.

"Who burns a village and doesn't even occupy it?" she asked.

"A general who wants people to run ahead of him carrying the news," Ilan said quietly. "Terror travels faster than cavalry. It's free, and it never deserts."

They walked the rest of the way through in silence, because there was nothing else to do. In the chapel — or where the chapel had been — someone had set out a row of stones, and on each stone a name scratched in charcoal. Not an official record. Just neighbors counting their own.

Ilan stood before the stones for a long time. Then he took out a small notebook from inside his coat, licked the tip of a pencil stub, and began to write.

"What are you doing?" Freya asked.

"Recording them." He didn't look up. "Forty-one households. Somebody should know who lived here."

* * *

The census ledgers were the first thing about Ilan Voss that genuinely frightened her, and it took her two more days to understand why.

He traveled with seven of them — fat, canvas-bound volumes, each one labeled in a neat bureaucratic hand: *District Nine, Enumeration of Persons, Year of the Republic 214.* At night he would sit by whatever fire they'd dared to build and read in them, not for pleasure but the way a priest reads scripture, moving his finger down columns of names, ages, trades, dependents.

On the third night, unable to sleep, Freya sat across from him and asked to see one.

He hesitated — the first hesitation she'd ever seen in him — then passed it over.

She opened it expecting numbers and found people. Every entry was precise: name, age, occupation, number in household, livestock held, taxes assessed. And beside some of the entries, in a different ink, added later in a cramped hand: *deceased, autumn fever.* *Deceased, conscription.* *Deceased, fire, Merrow's Bend.* *Household fled, destination unknown.*

Page after page after page.

"This is the whole district?" she said.

"Every soul I could count."

"And these annotations—"

"The ones I went back for." He fed a stick into the fire. "A census isn't a photograph, you know. It's a promise. You write down that a person exists, and the republic promises that this matters, that they'll be schooled and taxed and defended and buried under their own name. I used to believe the paperwork itself was the service. Write the name correctly and you've done your part." He smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile. "Then the fever came to Kessel's Hollow, and I wrote *deceased* next to sixty-two names, and I understood that I hadn't served those people at all. I'd just filed them."

Freya closed the ledger carefully, as though it might bruise.

"In the ministry," she said, "we believed paperwork was neutral. That was almost our religion. Ink has no allegiance. A form filled out honestly serves whoever holds power, and the forms don't care which."

"Yes," said Ilan. "That's exactly what frightens me about you."

She laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound — and then realized he hadn't meant it as a joke.

"I mean it kindly," he said. "You carry sealed orders across a dying country and you don't open them. You trust the seal. You trust the system that sealed them. I spent fifteen years trusting the same thing, and the system taught me, entry by entry, exactly what my trust was worth." He nodded at the case strapped to her chest, the cipher-case she measured her life by. "So when I look at you, courier Sandoval, I see a woman carrying something she believes is neutral. And I wonder what it will cost her to learn otherwise."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley, a dog barked at nothing, or at everything.

"I could have reported you at the checkpoint," Freya said. "One word from me and you'd be in irons."

"Yes."

"Why didn't I?"

Ilan considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, as if it were a column requiring accurate addition.

"Because when I asked your allegiance, you lied badly," he said. "And people only lie badly about things they still believe in."

* * *

The refugees moved west along the river road like a slow brown river of their own — carts, handcarts, families on foot, an entire poultry coop lashed to a wheelbarrow and squawking its objections at every rut. Rumor traveled among them faster than they did, passed cart to cart like a contagious disease.

By noon of the fourth day, Freya had collected eleven versions of the same news.

The general was three days away. The general was a week away. He had taken Hallow Ford; no, Hallow Ford still held; yes, Hallow Ford had fallen with barely a shot. He had ten thousand men. Twenty thousand. He had promised mercy to towns that opened their gates, and everyone who repeated this detail lowered their voice, as though mercy were something shameful to be caught discussing.

An old woman walking beside their cart for a mile delivered the most detailed version, and also, Freya suspected, the least true.

"He was born in a storm, you know," the woman said. "Marked from birth. His mother drowned herself rather than nurse him." She said it comfortably, the way one recites a favorite hymn.

"That's not true," said Tam — no. Not yet. That comes later. It was another boy, a refugee child trotting alongside, all elbows and indignation. "My gran says he was born in a dry summer and his mother sells fish in Corvess and beats him regular, which is why he's cruel."

"Both can be true," Ilan offered mildly.

"They cannot both be true."

"They absolutely can both be true. Cruelty has excellent funding."

The boy glared at him, decided he was either mad or safe, and trotted off to spread his version elsewhere.

When the road emptied, Freya said, "You collect rumors too now? First ledgers, now gossip."

"Rumors are a census of fear," Ilan said. "Count them long enough and you learn where the truth lives — usually somewhere in the middle, looking embarrassed." He glanced back at her. "What do the rumors say where you're going?"

"To the Atlas Workshop? That it's already fallen. Or that it's fortified. Or that the plates inside can mint legitimacy for whoever holds them, which makes it the most valuable building left in the republic."

"And do you believe any version?"

"I believe my orders," Freya said, and heard how it sounded — like a child insisting the dark isn't there. Ilan had the grace not to say anything at all, which was somehow worse than argument.

* * *

It rained on the fifth night, a cold steady drizzle that made every fire a confession, visible for miles. They sheltered in the shell of a tollhouse — ironic enough that Ilan remarked on it ("the last honest tollgate in the district; it charged everyone equally right up until it burned") — and sat close together over a fire the size of two cupped hands, because anything larger would have been visible from the hills.

They shared silence the way other travelers shared bread. Freya found she didn't mind it. With most people silence was a debt accruing interest; with Ilan it was simply a room they occupied together.

It was Ilan, eventually, who broke it.

"My wife died in Year 210," he said, to the fire. "Before any of this. Fever, not war — I want that recorded accurately, since accuracy is apparently my religion now. Her name was Betha. She thought the census work was absurd. She used to say, *you go around writing down the living, Ilan, and I'll stay home writing down the dead,* because she kept our family Bible's death register, births and deaths going back four generations in her handwriting." He turned his hands toward the small fire. "After she died I couldn't stop annotating. Every name I wrote down, I started hearing in her voice. Every *deceased* was a line in her register. I thought I'd gone gently mad. Then the war came and showed me I'd merely been practicing."

Freya watched the rain come down past the broken doorway, silvering in the firelight.

"I had someone," she said. It came out before her discipline could catch it. "In the capital. He worked the dispatch room. We weren't — it wasn't announced. Couriers aren't supposed to leave attachments behind, it's poor operational practice." She heard herself and winced. "Listen to me. *Operational practice.*"

"It sounds very tidy."

"It was very tidy. Right up until the evacuation, when I got my commission and he got assigned to the archive battalion, and we said goodbye in a corridor full of clerks burning files, and neither of us said anything real because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because saying something real would have meant admitting the republic was ending. As long as we kept it official, kept it *procedural*, it was just another assignment. Temporary. There'd be a recall order eventually."

"And do you believe there will be?"

The rain answered for a while.

"I don't know what I believe anymore," Freya said. "That's the honest answer. I believe in the weight of this case on my chest. It's the only fixed point left. Everything else is rumor."

Ilan reached into his coat, and for a moment she thought he was going for a ledger, but instead he produced a twist of paper — actual paper, precious as silver now — unwrapped it, and revealed a handful of dried apples, wrinkled and sugar-browned and clearly hoarded for weeks.

"Betha dried these," he said. "Three years ago. They're probably terrible. Eat one anyway."

"Why are you giving me your dead wife's apples?"

"Because you look like a woman carrying something heavy, and I've noticed heavy things get lighter when divided." He held them out. "Also because if we die tomorrow, I refuse to let my last act on earth be hoarding fruit."

Freya took one. It was terrible — leathery, faintly of smoke, tasting mostly of patience — and she ate it slowly and completely, down to the last thread of skin.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't mention it. Literally. If anyone asks, we ate rats."

She laughed again, the second time in five days, and this one lasted longer. Across the tiny fire, Ilan permitted himself the smallest satisfied expression of a man whose arithmetic had finally balanced.

* * *

They came over the ridge above Vetchling at dawn, and Freya knew before Ilan said anything that this burn was different.

White. A week old, maybe more. But unlike Merrow's Bend — burned and abandoned — Vetchling had been *worked*. The fields outside town were stripped to stubble. Every wagon wheel had been taken, every door, every hinge, every length of rope. Soldiers' camps, orderly and vast, had flattened the grass to the east in geometric patterns still readable in the dew.

"They quartered here," Ilan said, scanning the ground with his pencil already out, making notes, because of course he was. "Regimental layout. Cook lines here, horse lines here — and look, latrine trenches on the far side, downwind. Disciplined. This wasn't raiders. This was the general's regulars, provisioning for a march."

"Marching where?"

Ilan traced the flattened traffic patterns with his eyes, north and east, following them to the horizon.

"Toward the ford," he said. "Toward the river crossings. Which means toward the Workshop, unless I've forgotten my geography."

"You haven't," Freya said. Her hand had gone, without her permission, to the case on her chest. "Vetchling sits on the only practical route. Anything headed for the Atlas country crosses the river within twenty miles of here."

"Then we should hurry," Ilan said. Then, because he was Ilan, he added: "Also, we should be careful, and also, I've counted the camp latrines and estimated troop strength at roughly nine thousand, which I'd like entered into the record."

"Noted," Freya said. "Nine thousand."

"Give or take a regiment. There's always a regiment nobody accounts for. Armies are like censuses that way." He pocketed his notebook and looked down at the burned town, at the neat geometries of the camp, at the road running east toward the ford and the river and everything waiting on the other side of it.

"Courier Sandoval," he said, and there was something new in his voice, something that wasn't quite humor anymore, "may I ask you a professional question?"

"You may ask."

"When we reach wherever you're going — when you deliver whatever's in that case — what happens to the rest of us? Me, specifically. The deserter traveling with the republic's last honest seal." He smiled to soften it, but his eyes didn't join in. "I've been telling myself you're escorting me as far as the river. I'd like to know if that's true, or if I'm just cargo that hasn't been weighed yet."

Freya looked at the case on her chest. Sealed. Neutral. Silent.

"I don't know," she said, and was surprised to find the honesty didn't sting. "But ask me again at the river. I have a feeling I'll know more by then."

Ilan studied her for a long moment, then nodded once, as a man does when a figure comes in close enough to accept.

Down in the valley, the wind shifted, and brought them the smell of the burned town — and beneath it, faint and impossible and utterly distinct, the smell of fresh ink.
