## 4. The Widow Who Drew the World

The river town of Callowmere had one bridge, two churches that disagreed about everything except the price of candles, and — as of three months ago — one coffin that would not stay buried.

Marta Hale made a point of visiting it every morning.

She went down through the yard behind the chandler's shop, past the bee skeps her husband had never gotten around to tending properly, to the little plot beneath the willow where the ground was supposed to hold what she'd put in it. She knelt. She pressed her palm flat against the soil the way you'd check a fever. And then she did the thing that would have gotten her hanged in any town the general's men had reached yet: she listened.

Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth and wax and every trick her husband's trade had taught her about preserving things against damp and time, lay forty-one master maps of the republic. Every river gauge from the northern highlands to the delta. Every ford, every ferry right, every toll schedule, every bridge rated for load by Aldous Hale's own careful hand. Forty-one sheets of vellum and ink that represented thirty years of a man walking the country with his boots wearing out and his pencil stubs multiplying in his pockets like rabbits.

The general wanted them. The republic's own ministry had sent a man in a gray coat to want them too, politely, twice. And so Marta had done the only sensible thing available to a widow with a shovel.

She had buried her husband alive with his life's work.

"You're talking to a hole in the ground again," said Nettie from the chandler's fence, not unkindly.

"I'm conducting an inspection," Marta said. "There's a difference. A hole in the ground doesn't answer back."

"Neither does he."

"He never did when he was alive either, if the maps were involved." She stood, brushed off her knees, and permitted herself the small luxury of a joke she'd have been shot for making anywhere east of here. "Besides, if anyone asks, I'm mourning. Grief is very respectable this year. It's the only crop still paying."

Nettie laughed the way people in Callowmere laughed now — quickly, glancing at the road first, as though humor were contraband and someone might smell it on your breath. Which, Marta thought, was more or less accurate. The war hadn't come to Callowmere yet. It was coming the way weather comes: rumors from the north, prices climbing like smoke, and strangers on the river road who asked questions with too much interest in the answers.

She walked back up through the yard, past the bees — *Aldous, honestly, they're starving, I've told you since June* — and let herself into the map room.

That was what the town called it, anyway. In truth it was the back room of their house where Aldous had kept his working copies, his field notebooks, his instruments. The master plates and finished sheets were under the willow; these were the sketches, the drafts, the beautiful mess of a cartographer's mind spread across every surface. Marta could not bring herself to tidy them. Tidying them would mean admitting no one was coming back to make new messes.

She sat at his desk. She took out the letter she'd received that morning — the third this month — and read it again even though she knew it by heart.

*By order of the Provisional Directorate for the Security of the Republic, all survey materials, hydrographic records, and cartographic holdings are hereby requisitioned for the national defense. Compliance is expected within ten days. Non-compliance will be treated as—*

Treated as what? The sentence trailed off into legal fog, which was somehow worse than a threat. Threats you could defy. Fog you could drown in.

Marta set the letter down and looked at the wall where Aldous's best work hung: the great chart of the River Vane, drawn when they were young and foolish enough to think a map could be a love letter. He'd put their wedding date in the margin disguised as a depth sounding. *Seven fathoms at the narrows, 14th of the Harvest Month.* She'd found it years later and cried into a chart case for an hour, and he'd pretended not to know why, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"They want me to hand them over," she told the chart. "Your maps. To whichever uniform arrives first."

The chart said nothing, being ink. But she imagined what Aldous would have said, which was: *Marta, my darling, a map is not paper. A map is knowledge, and knowledge does not care whose flag is flying — but the men carrying the flags do, so use your head.*

Use your head. That had been his second-favorite phrase, after *hold the line steady*, which he'd shouted at apprentice surveyors wobbling with the theodolite on windy days until the whole crew chanted it like a hymn.

So she used her head. And her head said: ten days. And her head also said: the requisition man in the gray coat had counted her sheets wrong, or believed his own paperwork over his eyes, because he'd written down *thirty-eight* when there were forty-one. Bureaucracy, that great blind ox, had stumbled past the truth and left it standing in the road.

Three sheets unaccounted for. Three sheets that existed, officially, nowhere.

Her head had more to say, but a knock at the front door interrupted it — the particular knock of a stranger, firm but not pounding, and Marta felt the old cold slide down her spine before her feet even moved.

* *

There were four of them at the gate, and Marta catalogued them the way she catalogued everything now, automatically, the way Aldous had catalogued landmarks: distance, bearing, threat.

Two were travelers, dusty, ordinary — a lean dark-haired man whose eyes moved over her house the way an assessor's did, and beside him a woman with the posture of a pikestaff and the tired eyes of somebody who had been awake longer than sleep could account for. Behind them, a boy of perhaps ten, small and watchful, standing slightly behind the woman's skirts as if they were a fortification. And a coffin-cart, which nearly stopped Marta's heart until she saw it was empty, riding light on its springs, a mule dozing in the traces.

"Good afternoon," the woman said. Her accent was capital-bred, worn down at the edges by weeks of road. "We're asking after passage north. The ferry, if it runs."

"The ferry runs when Ferro feels like running it," Marta said. "Which lately is when he can find passengers foolhardy enough to pay."

"We'd be such passengers," said the man, with a smile that admitted the situation.

"And who's asking?" Marta folded her arms. "Forgive me. It's been a season of askers."

The woman hesitated — a real hesitation, Marta noted, the hesitation of someone weighing truth against habit — and then produced papers from inside her coat with the crisp efficiency of long practice.

"Freya Sandoval. Courier, Office of State Dispatch, fourth rank." The seal was genuine. Marta had spent enough of her marriage around government men to know a real commission from a forged one; forgeries were usually *better*, ironically, because forgers cared. This document was authentic down to its smudged ink stamp and its faintly insulting bureaucratic phrasing. "This is Ilan Voss, formerly of the Census Bureau. The boy is Tam. We mean no harm and we'll be gone by morning."

Marta looked at the commission for a long moment. Then she looked at the woman.

"Do you know," she said slowly, "what a rare animal you are?"

Freya Sandoval blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"Honest paperwork." Marta tapped the page. "I've had three requisitions this month alone. Two were forged — badly, the seals upside down, whoever trained those men should be ashamed — and the third was real but signed by a directorate that I happen to know was dissolved six weeks ago. You are the first official document I've seen since midsummer that is exactly what it claims to be. Do you have any idea how unsettling that is? Like meeting a coin that's actually gold."

Something flickered across the courier's face — surprise, and beneath it something wearier and sadder, as if honesty were a burden she hadn't chosen. "I could show you my travel warrant as well."

"Don't strain yourself." Marta stepped back from the door. "It's going to rain and you look like you slept in a ditch. Come in before I reconsider. Not you," she added to the boy, who had frozen halfway to the step, "you especially come in. There's honey bread. Don't argue with a widow, it's bad luck."

* *

They ate in the kitchen because the parlor had dustsheets on the furniture and Marta refused, on principle, to lift them for anyone.

The boy ate the way children eat when they've learned not to count on meals: steadily, gratefully, without a single wasted motion, and with his eyes doing the counting for him. Marta caught Freya watching him with an expression she filed away for later study. The census man — Voss — watched everything, including Marta watching, and gave her a small nod of professional respect between bites, one keeper of records acknowledging another.

"So," Marta said, setting down her cup. "You're going north. The whole country's going north, or trying to. And you, Courier Sandoval, are carrying something."

Freya's cup paused halfway to her lips. "I don't discuss my commissions."

"No, of course you don't. Sealed case, chained to your wrist, I'd wager — I can see the strap mark on your sleeve, you've been wearing it so long it's rubbing." Marta leaned back. "I was married to a mapmaker, madam. My entire profession is noticing what's where and how far apart. You can't hide cargo from me any more than you can hide a river."

Voss made a sound that might have been a suppressed laugh. Freya shot him a look that could have curdled milk.

"I don't need to know what's in the case," Marta continued. "I mention it for one reason only. Because I have something too. And I've spent three months deciding what an honest woman owes to a dying republic, and I've come to a conclusion I'd like tested against someone whose paperwork is actually real."

The kitchen went quiet. Even the boy stopped eating, honey bread suspended, listening with the total attention of the young.

"There's a grave behind the chandler's shop," Marta said. "My husband's. Aldous Hale, master cartographer, died in the spring — truly died, I'll swear to that part on anything you like. What the parish priest buried, however, was not entirely my husband." She held Freya's eyes. "Forty-one master maps of this republic are lying six feet under that willow. Every river gauge, every ford, every bridge rating from the highlands to the delta. The general wants them. So does whatever is left of the ministry. And yesterday I received a requisition giving me ten days to surrender them, signed by an office that no longer exists."

She let that sit.

"So here is my question, Courier Sandoval, and I warn you it's the question I buried him with: when a country stops deserving its own maps, who do they belong to?"

* *

Freya Sandoval didn't answer right away. Marta respected her for it. Quick answers were for priests and liars.

The courier set down her cup and turned it slowly, once, the way a person turns a thing when they're really turning themselves over. Outside, the promised rain began, soft against the kitchen windows, and the bees grumbled in their skeps, and somewhere down the lane Ferro's dog barked at nothing in particular.

"When I was sworn in," Freya said at last, "I took an oath to carry what I was given and deliver it intact. That's the whole of the oath. Carry. Deliver. It doesn't say to whom the contents rightly belong, because the assumption —" her mouth twisted, just slightly — "the assumption was that the people giving the orders and the country were the same thing." She looked up. "I don't know anymore whether they are. But I know what I'd say if you asked me about a bridge."

"A bridge?"

"Aldous Hale rated the bridges," Freya said. "Load, span, condition. If those ratings end up in the general's hands, his artillery crosses rivers it has no business crossing. If they end up in no one's hands, they rot in the ground and a hundred years from now some poor engineer builds a bridge wrong and drowns a village. But if they belonged—" she paused, choosing, "—to the towns along the river. To ferrymen and millers and the people who live with the water. Then the maps would be doing what maps are for."

Marta sat very still.

It was, she realized, the first time in three months that anyone had answered the question correctly. The priest had said the maps belonged to God, which was pious and useless. The gray-coated man had said they belonged to the state, which was the same thing the general's scouts had said with worse manners. Aldous, in his way, had said they belonged to posterity, which was a fine word for *not my problem while I'm dead*.

But the people who lived with the water. Yes. That was it. That was the whole of it, and she could have wept, and instead she did something better.

She got up, went to the dresser, and took down the key she'd hidden inside the hollow handle of Aldous's old brass scale-weight — because no one robs a widow's kitchen scales, they're sacred — and came back and set it on the table between them.

"That opens the tool shed behind the house," she said. "In the floor under the plan chest, there's a trapdoor, and under the trapdoor there's a box with three master sheets in it. The ones the requisition man miscounted. The river Vane complete, the northern fords, and the delta channels." She sat back down. "You're going north. You'll pass every one of those rivers. I can't dig up my husband and run — I'm known here, the coffin's known, and the moment it moves, every informer between here and the border starts writing letters. But three sheets can travel in a map case. With a courier whose paperwork is actually honest."

Freya stared at the key. "You'd trust me with them."

"I'd trust anyone with them," Marta said, "who answers the question right. You did. Don't make me regret having standards."

"Marta," Voss began gently, "there's a war coming down that road—"

"There's a war everywhere, Master Voss. I checked." She gestured at the window, the rain, the whole gray shivering world. "I've spent three months burying the most important thing I owned and visiting it like a madwoman. I am *done* with hiding. Hiding is just grief wearing a practical coat." She heard her own voice rise and made herself stop, breathe, lower it. "Aldous drew those rivers so people could cross them safely. I intend to see that they keep doing that. Whatever else burns."

The boy — Tam, silent all evening — spoke for the first time. "Is it true you buried the maps with him?"

"It's true."

He considered this with enormous seriousness. "Did he mind?"

Marta laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, rusty, startling everyone including her. "No, sweetheart. He'd have insisted on it. He was a jealous man about his work. Buried him with his pencils too. He'd have wanted company."

Tam nodded gravely, satisfied, and returned to his bread, and Marta thought: *oh no*. Oh no, she knew that feeling already, the one arriving in her chest like weather. Three months alone and here came a child and a stubborn courier and a census man with kind eyes, and she could feel the exact shape of the mistake she was about to make, and she was going to make it anyway.

"Well," she said briskly, before the feeling could get fully organized. "It's raining, the ferry won't run till morning, and I have a spare room and half a goose. You'll stay the night. And in the morning—"

She stopped. Because through the rain-haze of the window, coming up the river road at an unhurried walk, she could see riders. Four of them. Gray coats, unremarkable horses, and the loose easy formation of men who were not in any hurry because they believed nothing ahead of them could possibly matter.

The requisition had said ten days.

They'd taken two.

Marta set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet kitchen, exactly like a decision being made.
