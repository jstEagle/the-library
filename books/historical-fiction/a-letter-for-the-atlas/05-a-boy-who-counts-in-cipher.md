## 5. A Boy Who Counts in Cipher

The boy was sitting on the coffin.

Not *in* it — Marta had been very clear about the distinction, twice, in the tone of a woman who had buried one husband and did not intend to bury a cartographer's pride in the same week — but *on* it, cross-legged, in the yard of the toll house where they'd stopped to water the mule. He sat with his hands folded and his chin lifted, watching them with the frank, unhurried attention of a magistrate reviewing evidence.

"I don't think he's dangerous," Ilan said quietly.

"He's been following us since the ash road," Freya said. "That's either danger or desperation, and I've learned not to guess which."

"He's been following us," Marta corrected from under the cart, where she was greasing an axle, "since the ash road, and he has eaten exactly nothing of ours, asked for exactly nothing of ours, and counted everything we own out loud at least four times."

Freya looked at the boy again. He was perhaps nine, small for it, with a burn-scarred forearm he kept turned inward as though ashamed of it, and eyes the color of river slate that never quite settled on anything — they moved, always moving, from Freya's boots to her satchel strap to the cipher-case's chain at her collarbone, and back.

He'd counted. Of course he'd counted. She thought of the times she'd caught the flicker of his lips behind them on the road, silent, quick — she'd assumed he was praying.

"What do you mean, counted?" she asked.

Marta rolled out from under the cart, wiping her hands on her apron. "I mean this morning he told me we were carrying eleven days of hardtack, a jar of goose fat gone slightly off, forty-two sheets of paper, three ink pots — one nearly dry — and," here Marta paused, and her voice went carefully flat, "a locked case on a chain that the lady checks seventeen times a day, usually when she thinks no one is watching."

The silence that followed had a particular quality, the quality of a room in which someone has just said aloud the thing everyone had politely agreed not to notice.

"Fourteen," Freya said, before she could stop herself.

The boy's mouth twitched. It was the first expression she had seen him make that wasn't watchful blankness, and it transformed his whole face — suddenly he was not a stray but a child, sharp-eyed and pleased with himself.

"Fourteen, then," Marta said, entirely too satisfied. "Well. That's settled. The boy can count better than the courier can lie about counting."

* * *

His name was Tam, or so he wrote in the dirt with a stick when Ilan crouched beside him — T-A-M, each letter drawn slowly, carefully, like a man copying shapes he didn't understand. Because he didn't. Ilan figured it out by the second letter and stopped, gently taking the stick.

"You don't read."

Tam shook his head.

"But you write your name?"

"Someone showed me." He spoke rarely and softly, with the careful diction of a child who had learned words from adults who were busy dying. "A schoolmaster. Before. He said a name is the only word you need written down, because everything else about you can be counted instead." He held up his fingers, folded two down. "I had a sister. I count her so I don't lose her. Two minus one."

Freya felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a millstone starting up.

Nobody asked what had happened to the sister. Nobody needed to. They were all carrying their own arithmetic — Marta with her husband in a coffin full of treasonous maps, Ilan with his ledgers of the dead, Freya with a sealed case she touched fourteen times a day because touching it was the only prayer she trusted.

"We're going north," Ilan said. "It's not safe. There's less food than there is road."

"I know how much food you have," Tam said. "Eleven days of hardtack. If you divide it five ways it lasts longer than four."

Marta made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Freya stared at the boy.

"Five ways," she repeated.

"If I come with you. Then it's five." He said it without pleading, without hope even — the way one states a theorem. "Eleven divided by five is two days each and one left over. The left over one should go to the mule, because if the mule dies we all walk, and walking uses more bread."

There was a long pause in which Freya became aware that she was being outmaneuvered by a nine-year-old with no shoes, and that part of her — the part she did not authorize, did not feed, did not acknowledge in any official capacity — wanted to laugh until she cried.

"That's sound logistics," Ilan said gravely.

"It's blackmail," Freya said.

"It's arithmetic," said Tam.

* * *

They took him in the way travelers take things in — sideways, without a formal vote, by the simple mechanism of nobody saying stop. By evening he had a place in the cart between Marta's map-chest and the water barrels, and by the second night he had, without anyone noticing the moment it happened, become simply part of the cargo that mattered.

Freya noticed it happening anyway. She noticed everything now; the boy had taught her that.

She watched him through the fire's edge as he sat with Marta, learning the roads. Not reading them — Marta had tried, patiently, tracing a finger along the river names, and Tam had listened politely and then said, "But which one is longest? How many bridges? Where does it bend?" — and Marta, to Freya's astonishment, had begun answering in numbers. Distances in leagues. Bends per mile. Ford depths in handspans. And Tam's whole face lit like a lamp.

"He doesn't want the map," Marta murmured to Freya later, over the dishes. "He wants the measure of it. He carries places in his head like I carry them on paper." She shook her head slowly. "My husband used to say every map is just numbers wearing a pretty dress. That boy is the dress taken off."

"And what do we do with him?" Freya asked. It came out harsher than she intended. "We're carrying things people kill for. Every mile we take him, he's worth more to whoever catches us — as leverage, as a hostage, as—"

"As a boy eating hardtack," Marta said, and handed her the dish towel, and that was the end of the argument, in the way that Marta ended arguments: by making continuing one feel ridiculous.

* * *

The third night, the chill came.

It came the way cold comes into a house — not all at once, but through some seam you didn't know was open.

They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed granary, and after supper Tam did what he always did before sleep: he counted. Aloud, softly, in the dark beyond the firelight, the way other children said prayers. Freya had heard it for three nights and thought nothing of it — a litany, a comfort, numbers as blankets.

Tonight she listened properly.

"—forty-one, forty-three, forty-seven, fifty-three," he was murmuring. "Fifty-nine, sixty-one, sixty-seven—"

Primes. Her spine went straight. Those weren't random; those were the prime sequence, and primes meant—

She rose without a sound and crossed the camp, and stood over him, and he looked up at her without fear, without guilt, only with that open slate-colored attention, and continued: "Seventy-one, seventy-three. Then it changes. After seventy-three it goes to intervals — seven, then eleven, then—" he frowned, working it, "—then thirteen, but the thirteenth one is wrong, it's been changed, it should be ninety-one but the case says eighty-eight."

The fire cracked somewhere behind her. Somewhere far off, a dog barked at the end of the world.

"Say that again," Freya said. Her voice came out level, which cost her more than screaming would have.

"After seventy-three it goes seven, eleven, thirteen. But the last one's wrong. Eighty-eight instead of ninety-one." He recited it the way he recited everything — clean, unhesitating, without comprehension, a song learned by heart from a singer who never explained the words. He tapped the chain at her collarbone, lightly, once, with one grimy finger. "You say them in your sleep sometimes. The first ones. You hold the case when you say them. Like my sister held her doll."

Freya sat down in the dirt beside him because her legs informed her that standing was no longer among the services they provided.

He had it. Fragments of it — the key-sequence, the interval pattern, and worse, the deliberate corruption at the end, the check-digit that proved authenticity, the one thing she'd have sworn no eavesdropper could ever extract, because no one listening would know which number was *wrong*. But Tam didn't need to know what was wrong. He only needed to remember that it *was* wrong, that the true sequence said ninety-one and the case said eighty-eight, and anyone who had both halves of that knowledge —

Anyone who had both halves could forge.

Her mind raced ahead of her down the dark road: what the fragments meant, who else might hear him recite in his sleep, what a captain at a checkpoint would pay, what the general's men would do to a child who carried state secrets in his skull without knowing their weight. The case at her breast suddenly felt less like armor and more like an eggshell, and the boy beside her like a crack running through it.

"Tam." She kept her voice gentle, and discovered that gentleness, too, was something she'd have to learn from scratch. "Those numbers you say at night. Do you say them to anyone else? Ever?"

He considered this seriously. "To the mule once. The mule wasn't interested."

Despite everything — despite the vertigo and the dread settling into her bones like wet snow — Freya laughed. It escaped her before permission could be sought, short and startled, and the boy looked pleased, and she understood that she had just done something irreversible. That somewhere between the hardtack division and the disinterested mule, she had stopped being a courier escorting cargo and started being something with a name she hadn't authorized.

A guardian. God help her. A guardian.

"Here's the rule," she said. "The numbers are yours. Keep them. But you don't say them to soldiers, strangers, priests, tax collectors, or mules. Only us. Can you hold that?"

"Like the maps in the coffin," Tam said, nodding at the cart. "Secret things go in quiet places." He patted his own forehead, matter-of-fact. "Mine's very quiet."

Freya walked back to the fire and sat down next to Ilan, who was watching her with the mild patience of a man who had already worked all of this out and was waiting for her to catch up.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard."

"How long has he known the sequence?"

Ilan stirred the coals. "Since the first night, I'd guess. He listens the way I used to record — completely, without deciding to." He glanced at her. "You're thinking about what it means. What he could be, in the wrong hands. What those fragments are worth."

"Yes."

"And what else?"

Freya looked into the fire. Beyond it, Marta had draped a blanket over the sleeping boy, tucking it with the fierce tenderness of a woman who had lost the habit of mothering and found it waiting intact, like a tool stored in a shed. Tam slept the instant sleep of the truly safe, lips already beginning to move faintly through his nightly litany — numbers instead of dreams.

"And else," Freya said slowly, "I'm thinking that I have spent my entire life believing that what I carry is safe because it is sealed, and coded, and chained to my body, and guarded by my life." She turned the cipher-case over in her hands, its familiar weight suddenly strange. "And a barefoot child cracked it in three nights by accident, while half-asleep, for entertainment."

Ilan nodded. "Paperwork was never neutral," he said. "Neither was secrecy. The republic built locks and called them walls." He fed a twig to the flames. "The question isn't whether your orders survive contact with the road, Freya. The question is whether *you* will — or whether you'll finally find out what you actually believe, now that the lock is broken."

She didn't answer. Across the dead camp, the boy murmured on in his sleep — forty-one, forty-three, forty-seven — a child counting his way toward morning, carrying the death of her certainties in the quietest place in the world.

Two days later, the road bent east toward the river, and they saw smoke rising over the ford, and soldiers at the crossing checking papers with a thoroughness that made Marta grip the coffin-rail until her knuckles went white.

The captain who stepped into their path had a memory like a ledger, and eyes that stayed on Ilan Voss a moment too long.
