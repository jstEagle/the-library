## 6. Checkpoint at the Ford

Ilan Voss had spent eleven years asking strangers questions they did not want to answer, so he knew exactly what it looked like when a man lied to him. He had a whole taxonomy of it, filed neatly in the part of his mind that had once organized census districts: the too-quick answer, the answer that arrived one beat late like a guest who had forgotten which house he was invited to, the answer that was technically true and therefore worthless. He could sort a lie the way a fishwife sorted herring — by smell, by weight, by the shine of it.

Which is why it troubled him deeply, standing in the queue at the ford with a widow's coffin-cart creaking behind him, that he was about to commit every category of lie he had ever catalogued, all at once, to an audience of armed men.

"You're muttering," Marta Hale said from the cart's bench.

"I'm rehearsing."

"Rehearsing sounds better when you do it silently."

"I rehearsed silently last night," Ilan said, "and dreamed I was arrested by my own supervisor, who confiscated my pen and made me fill out a form explaining why I needed a pen. The form required a pen." He adjusted the collar of his old census coat, brushing a fleck of ash from its shoulder. The coat had been his armor for eleven years — gray wool, brass buttons stamped with the republic's little wheel-and-quill, pockets deep enough for ledgers. Nobody searched a census officer. Searching a census officer was, administratively speaking, searching the state itself, and even soldiers hesitated before that particular absurdity.

"That coat," Marta said, "is going to get us killed."

"This coat," Ilan said, "is going to get us *through*. There's a difference, and the difference is approximately four hundred meters of river."

The ford at Callow's Bend was not much to look at — a brown river doing its honest best over brown stones, a ferryman's hut with its roof half caved in, and a checkpoint that consisted of six soldiers, a trestle table, and a captain who had arranged his men with more care than the setting deserved. That care was what worried Ilan. Sloppy soldiers were dangerous; tidy ones were worse. Tidy ones had been told to be tidy, which meant somebody important cared about this crossing.

Ahead of them, the morning's traffic shuffled forward: a tinker with his pack open and his dignity already rummaged through, a farm family whose cart of turnips received a bayonet's inspection that found nothing but turnips and left everyone feeling vaguely insulted. The refugees on foot were waved past with contempt — no use robbing people who had already been robbed by the war itself. It was carts that got stopped. Carts meant goods. Goods meant requisition, which was the general's army's favorite word after *victory* and slightly ahead of *breakfast*.

And theirs was a cart. A large one. A coffin-cart, black as a closed door, carrying a coffin that did not contain a body.

It contained, instead, forty-one master maps of the republic — Marta's husband's life's work, rolled in oilcloth and wedged into a coffin padded with the dead man's own winter clothes, because Marta had reasoned, with the terrible clarity grief sometimes grants, that no soldier in the world would open a coffin looking for paper. Soldiers would open anything else. They'd open your grandmother's biscuit tin and your baby's swaddling. But a coffin asked a question no sergeant wanted answered.

Ilan hoped she was right. He also noted, professionally, that hope was not a document he could file.

"Names," said the soldier at the trestle table, without looking up. He was young, with the raw pink face of a boy issued a uniform two sizes too serious for him. His spear wobbled when he shifted his weight, which meant his hands were cold, which meant he'd been standing there since dawn, which meant his patience had been used up hours ago.

"Census Officer Second Class Ilan Voss, traveling on district business," Ilan said, and produced his credentials with the weary flourish of a man who had done this ten thousand times, because he had. "With me, the widow Hale, transporting her late husband for burial in the northern parishes, where his family plot lies. And a ward of the road, Tam, age approximately nine, unregistered."

That last bit was a gamble. Unregistered children usually got waved along — nobody wanted to feed them — but naming the boy at all made the party look documented, complete, boring. Boring was the goal. Ilan had built his entire career on being boring. He had once put a tax auditor to sleep mid-inspection, and considered it his finest professional achievement.

The young soldier copied the names with his tongue poking out the corner of his mouth. "Census," he said. "You count people?"

"I record them," Ilan said. "Counting implies they hold still."

The soldier looked up at that, decided he had possibly been joked with, and wasn't sure how to feel about it. Behind him, the captain turned.

He was perhaps fifty, with a face like a folded map — creased in patterns that suggested long service and strong opinions — and he wore his rank the way some men wear good boots: comfortably, and with an air of having paid for them himself. He crossed to the table unhurriedly, read the credentials upside down, which was a trick only career officers bothered learning, and then looked at Ilan.

"Voss," he said. "Census. You'll have records of the eastern districts, then."

"I have records of everywhere," Ilan said. "It's the curse of the profession. People assume we're interested. We're obliged to be."

"Hm." The captain set down the papers. "Open the cart."

There it was. The sentence Ilan had rehearsed against for three days and never quite found an answer for, because there wasn't one. You could not talk a soldier out of opening a cart. You could only make him wish he hadn't.

"Captain," Ilan said, and here his voice did something he hadn't planned — it dropped into the register he saved for district supervisors and other varieties of weather, flat, procedural, faintly disappointed in advance. "The cargo is a body. A deceased man of the Hale family, bound for burial. Under Statute Eleven of the Sanitary Codes, transport of remains may not be delayed, as decomposition does not respect military schedules. If you wish to inspect, you are within your rights, and I am obliged to note your name in my report, along with the hour, and the fact that the inspection was conducted over my stated objection."

He said it the way he'd said a thousand true things, in the voice of a man burdened by accuracy. It was, Ilan reflected later, the most obscene thing he had ever done: using the language of truth, fluently, expertly, to protect a lie. His hands didn't shake. That was the part that frightened him.

The captain looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, Marta sat very still on the bench, her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral, which was method acting of the highest order given that her husband's maps were currently playing the role of her husband.

"The Sanitary Codes," the captain said.

"Chapter Four," Ilan said. "I can recite it if it would help. It wouldn't help. It has never helped anyone."

Something twitched at the corner of the captain's mouth — not quite amusement, but a relative of it, some cousin who visited rarely. "Open it," he said anyway.

Of course. Amusement didn't stop orders. Ilan stepped back, and two soldiers came around to the rear of the cart, and Marta rose, and for one horrible second Ilan thought she was going to throw herself across the coffin like a woman in a ballad, which would have ended everything, and he caught her eye and gave the smallest possible shake of his head — *no* — and she understood, somehow, and instead simply said, in a cracked and dignified voice:

"Gently. Please. He's all I have left of him."

And the soldiers, who would have pried open a church with their boots, went gentle. That was the thing about armies, Ilan thought, watching them work the coffin lid loose with something approaching tenderness — they were made of boys, and boys still had mothers somewhere in their heads, and mothers ruined more searches than any statute ever written.

The lid came up.

Inside: the dark wool of a dead man's coat, folded. A scarf wound where a face would be. The smell of camphor and old cedar, which Marta had seen to herself, because of course she had — Marta Hale missed nothing, least of all smells. And beneath the coat, invisible unless you lifted it, pressed flat against the coffin floor, the oilcloth rolls, thin as sleeping snakes.

A soldier prodded the coat with two fingers. Prodded again. Looked up at the captain.

"Body's wrapped, sir."

"Wrapped," the captain repeated.

"Per the codes," Ilan said helpfully. "Wrapping is chapter five. There's a great deal of chapter."

The captain ignored him. He walked around the coffin slowly, once, twice, the way a man circles a horse he's considering buying or hanging. Then he reached in — Ilan's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution — and lifted the edge of the coat, just an inch, just enough to see the pale gleam of oilcloth beneath.

One inch. One breath. The ford went very quiet. Downstream, the river kept up its idiotic cheerful babbling, oblivious.

"Maps," the captain said quietly.

Every plan Ilan owned burned to ash in that instant, and out of the ashes his mind — traitorous, tireless, eleven years of filing — produced, of all things, the truth, wearing a borrowed coat.

"Yes," Ilan said. "Burial maps."

The captain looked at him.

"The parish requires interment plots to be surveyed and recorded," Ilan went on, his heart hammering somewhere up near his teeth while his voice remained infuriatingly level. "The deceased was a surveyor by trade. His wife is bringing his own instruments and drawings to mark the grave's boundaries — northings, waterlines, the parish boundary stone. Sentimental, perhaps. Surveyors are like that about their own ground. You'd want your maps buried with you too, Captain, if you'd drawn them."

Silence. The captain withdrew his hand from the coffin. Slowly. He looked at the oilcloth, at the widow's white knuckles, at the boy Tam, who stood beside the cart eating a crust of bread with the serene obliviousness of childhood, and finally at Ilan.

"Surveyors," the captain said, "are exactly like that."

"I know," Ilan said. "I've recorded nine hundred of them."

Another silence, longer. Then the captain straightened, gestured, and the soldiers lowered the lid — gently, still gently, damn their mothers — and waved the cart forward. "Pass," he said. "Officer Voss."

They crossed. The wheels found the stones, the water parted around the axles, Marta drove with her eyes fixed on the far bank as though it might escape her, and Tam finished his crust. Only when the cart was fully on the northern side, when the checkpoint was a clutter of gray figures behind them, did Ilan allow himself to exhale, and discovered his lungs had apparently been holding that breath since the trestle table.

"Well," Marta said.

"Yes."

"*Burial maps.*"

"It came to me," Ilan said. "In the moment. I don't know where. Possibly the same place surveyors go when they die."

Marta laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter were also contraband — and Tam looked up from his crust and grinned at both of them without knowing why, which was the boy's special gift: grinning precisely when the adults most needed evidence that the world contained anything grin-worthy.

Ilan should have felt triumphant. He had bluffed six soldiers and a captain; he had protected the maps; he had, in the space of four minutes, committed forgery of manner, false statement to military authority, impersonation of integrity, and abuse of a dead man's profession. By any reasonable accounting, they had won the morning.

Instead, he felt the specific chill of a man who has just heard a door close behind him and realized there is no handle on this side.

Because the captain had memorized him. Ilan had watched it happen — had watched the man's eyes do the same work Ilan's own eyes did, the patient cataloguing, the folding of a stranger into permanent storage. *Voss. Census. Second Class. Gray coat, brass buttons, talks too precisely.* Somewhere in the general's spreading machinery, there was now a small drawer with Ilan Voss's name on it, and drawers like that did not stay empty forever. The captain would mention him at supper. The name would travel upward, the way names did, gathering questions as it climbed — *a census officer, this far west? Census officers don't escort coffins. Check his district. Where's his assignment?* And when the answers came back wrong — and they would come back wrong, because Ilan had abandoned his post eleven days ago and every ledger between here and the capital said so — the question would stop being *who is this man* and start being *where is this man*.

At the border, eleven days ago, Ilan had asked Freya Sandoval a single question too many — *who do you serve?* — and had followed her out of the old world because he couldn't bear to stay inside it. He had told himself he was still a traveler. A man between postings. A citizen of the long road.

Standing in the shallows of Callow's Bend, water tugging at his boots, Ilan understood at last that travelers have faces no one keeps. Deserter was the word for what he was now. Deserter, liar, accomplice — a man whose name lived in someone else's drawer.

Strangely, it did not feel like ruin. It felt like a form finally filled out correctly.

Behind them, faint across the water, he heard the young pink-faced soldier call to the next traveler in line, and the day went on, and the war went on, and the cart rolled north with its impossible cargo.

Tam tugged Ilan's sleeve. "Mister Voss. What's a deserter?"

Ilan looked down at him. "Who said that word?"

"The captain. To the other soldier. Quiet-like." Tam shrugged. "I remember quiet words too."

Of course he did. Ilan crouched in the cart's shadow, suddenly very tired and oddly calm. "A deserter," he said, "is a man who stops counting for one master and starts counting for himself."

"Is that bad?"

"It's the most honest paperwork I've ever filed," Ilan said, and ruffled the boy's hair, and did not tell him that honest paperwork, in Ilan's long experience, was exactly the kind that got a man hanged.

Far behind them, at the ford, the captain was writing something in a small notebook.
