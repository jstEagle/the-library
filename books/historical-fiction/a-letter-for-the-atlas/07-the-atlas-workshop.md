## 7. The Atlas Workshop

The city announced itself before we saw it — by smell first, ink and tallow and river mud, then by sound: the low industrial heartbeat of presses working past midnight. Tam heard it a full mile out, his head cocked like a bird's.

"That's not bells," he said.

"No," Marta said from the coffin-cart seat. "That's better than bells."

Ilan walked beside me with his hands clasped behind his back, the posture of a man pretending to be on official business, which after the ford was a costume that fit him worse every day. "You realize," he said quietly, "that we've been traveling toward this for eleven days, and I still don't actually know what's in your case."

"You know what I know," I said.

"Which is nothing."

"Which is nothing," I agreed pleasantly.

He gave me a look I had come to recognize as his particular gift: the look of a census taker confronted with an entry he knows is false but cannot correct without paperwork. Eleven days of road had taught me to read Ilan Voss the way he read ledgers — line by line, noting discrepancies. He noted mine daily. I noted his. We had reached a comfortable arrangement of mutual suspicion, which is more than most marriages achieve.

Marta drove us down the last hill at a pace she called dignified and Tam called slow as a funeral, which earned him a look over her shoulder that had ended stronger arguments. The coffin rode behind us, its false bottom holding her husband's master maps, and if any of us thought about how strange our caravan would look — a widow, a deserter, an orphan, a courier, and a coffin all bound for a printing house — none of us said it aloud. Grief makes strange processions. So does hope.

* * *

The Atlas Workshop stood where the river bent, a long stone building three stories tall with windows like a ship's, and above its door the old republic mark: an atlas open to no page in particular, flanked by the words *FOR THE RECORD OF ALL*. The paint had been refreshed recently. Someone had climbed up there with a brush within the year, which meant someone still believed the words were worth the climb.

A boy — an actual boy, twelve perhaps, ink to his elbows — met us at the gate before we'd knocked. He looked at our cart, our coffin, our faces, and delivered his message with the flat competence of someone who had been rehearsing bad news all week.

"She'll see the courier. Just the courier. She says the rest of you can water the horse, except you don't have a horse, so water yourselves."

"Charming household," Ilan murmured.

"I like him," said Tam, with the instant solidarity of one professional child recognizing another.

I handed my satchel to Marta — she took it without being asked, which by now felt less like trust than gravity — and followed the ink-boy through a yard stacked with paper bales under oilcloth. Everything smelled of metal and rags and something faintly sweet, like bread left too near a lamp. Through an open doorway I glimpsed the great press itself, a black iron animal the size of an ox, motionless for once, its bed empty. Even idle it looked like it was waiting to be told something.

Odile Ferrant received me in a room that had been an office and was becoming a sickroom, though she was clearly fighting the transition with everything she had. A bed had been installed against the far wall, made up with military neatness and pointedly unused. She sat instead at her desk, upright in a chair, a thin woman in a printer's apron gone gray with years of lint, her hands spotted with ink the way old maps are spotted with foxing. Her face was sharp and kind at once, the face of someone who has spent a lifetime catching errors and forgiving most of them.

"So," she said. "The republic finally remembers I exist. Sit down, courier, before you fall down. You have the walk of someone who has slept in ditches and is proud of it."

"There weren't always ditches available," I said. "Some nights it was hedgerows."

"Ah, luxury." She studied me with eyes that missed nothing, cataloguing the road-dirt, the split in my boot, the way I held my left arm a little carefully since the ford. Whatever she concluded, she kept it off the record. "Name?"

"Freya Sandoval. Courier of the second grade, commission under the Ministry of State." I set my papers on her desk. "And sealed cargo, to be opened only by the keeper of the Atlas Workshop or her designated successor."

Something moved behind her eyes at the word *successor*. She looked at the papers a long moment before touching them.

"And these people outside? Your escort?"

"They're not escort," I said. "They're—" I stopped. I had been about to say *nobody*, which was the official answer, and found I could no longer say it with a straight face even to myself. "They're with me."

"Mm," said Odile Ferrant, in the tone of a woman entering a discrepancy into a ledger for later review. She broke the outer seal.

I want to be honest here, because honesty became the currency of that room within the hour: I watched her face the way I had learned to watch faces on the road, and what I saw frightened me. It wasn't shock. Shock arrives fast and leaves fast. What crossed Odile Ferrant's face was slower — recognition, then confirmation, then a grief so practiced it barely disturbed her features, the way a river wears stone without anyone hearing it.

She read the outer page twice. Then she set it facedown on the desk, aligned its edges precisely with the desk's edge — I would learn that was how she steadied herself, putting things square — and looked at me.

"How much do you know, Freya Sandoval?"

"The commission is a treaty instrument," I said. "Ratification materials concerning the workshop's plates, to be executed upon safe delivery. That's what I was told."

"Told by whom?"

"The undersecretary. In the capital. The last week."

"The last week," she repeated softly. "When the ministries were burning their own files and calling it archiving." She folded her hands. "Courier. Do you know what these plates are?"

"Plates for the treaty edition. Historical documents. The founding accords, the boundary settlements—"

"The plates are the republic's memory," she said. "Every accord, every charter, every map of every border we ever swore to — cut in steel, kept here so that no minister, no general, no clever man with an army can say afterward, *it never happened, there is no record, you imagined your country.*" She tapped the desk once, lightly, like a press taking its impression. "Whoever holds these plates holds the proof of what we were. And your letter tells me who wants them."

She turned the page over again and let me see it. I read the seal — the true seal, unbroken, the one I had carried eleven days through ash and checkpoints with my life balanced on it like a plate on a pole — and Odile watched me understand it.

It was not the Ministry of State's seal.

It was close. Devastatingly close — same eagle, same stars, same border of tiny script. But the script was wrong. I knew the old cipher; I had measured my life against it since I was nineteen. And the motto running the seal's border was the old motto, the founding one, the one that had been retired thirty years ago when the third republic decided the first two had been embarrassing. No living ministry used it.

No living ministry. But a chancellery that wanted its orders to look ancient, authoritative, unquestionable — a chancellery that wanted a courier to carry them across a continent without asking questions — might reach back into the drawer of dead symbols and help itself.

"You see it," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I see a seal," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me. "Seals can be reissued. Restored. There were reforms—"

"Freya." She said my name the way you set type: deliberately, each letter placed. "In forty years at this bench I have verified ten thousand seals. This one was cut by someone very good, very recent, and very afraid of being caught. The republic did not send you this. Something wearing the republic sent you this."

The room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when a floor gives way somewhere beneath you, silently, before the falling starts. I heard the river through the wall. I heard the ink-boy in the yard laughing at something Tam had said. I heard my own heart doing arithmetic it didn't like.

Eleven days. Checkpoints. The ford, where a captain had looked at Ilan too long. Tam asleep against the coffin with my cipher-case under his head like a pillow. All of it carried forward by a woman who believed she was delivering her country's survival, and who had been, all along, a key turning in someone else's lock.

"I need to sit down," I said, with immense dignity.

"You're already sitting down," Odile observed.

"Then I need to sit down harder."

To my surprise, she laughed — a dry, real laugh, quickly rationed but genuine. She rose, crossed to a cabinet, and poured two glasses of something amber from a bottle with no label. "Printer's brandy. We distill it from the solvent runoff, which sounds worse than it is. Or possibly exactly as bad as it sounds. Drink."

I drank. It tasted like a decision I hadn't made yet.

"What happens now?" I asked. "Formally. If I deliver as instructed."

"Then in some weeks' time, men with wagons arrive, courteous men, and they load the plates, and they give you a receipt, and the receipt will be the last document this workshop ever issues." She sat back down, unhurried, a woman who had already done all her falling and landed. "And one morning the country wakes up with no record of what it was, and a general explaining what it is instead. It's very efficient. Tyranny loves a clean ledger."

"You've known," I said. "You read that seal and you weren't surprised."

"I've known for weeks," Odile said simply. "Word travels faster than couriers. Rumor rides horses; paper walks." She looked at me over her glass, and her eyes were not unkind, but they did not soften either. "I'm sorry, courier. I know what it is to find out your orders are not what you were told. I've spent a lifetime watching people discover what their work has been serving."

* * *

She showed me the vault that evening — I insisted, and she was too tired to refuse, though I suspected later that she wanted to show me, wanted a witness, wanted someone else to know where things stood before her body finished its negotiations with the disease chewing through her lungs.

The vault was beneath the pressroom, behind a door of green iron, and inside it the plates lived in oiled cloth on cedar shelves, row upon row, labeled in a small precise hand. Odile lifted one corner of cloth to show me: the Founding Accords, cut in steel, every signature of the first assembly rendered in lines fine as hair. It caught the lamplight and threw it back silver.

"Forty years I've kept these," she said. "My master kept them twenty before me. Do you know the joke of it? In all that time, no army ever came. No minister ever tried. The plates were safe because everyone agreed they should be safe — right up until the moment everyone stopped agreeing about anything." She smoothed the cloth down gently, tucking the accords in like a child. "Now everyone wants them. The general wants them melted, I expect, or locked in some private dark. And your letter—" she glanced at me, "—your letter opens the door politely."

"Then don't open it," I said. "Refuse delivery. Cite — cite irregularities in the seal. Buy time."

"With what authority, courier? Mine died with my lungs. Yours—" she smiled thinly, "—yours is currently a fiction wearing the republic's face. We could both stand in the doorway citing regulations. Men with guns are famously moved by regulations."

"Then we move the plates."

"Three tons of steel," she said, "and one dying woman. Yes. Let's draft that requisition."

We stood in the lamplight among the sleeping records of a nation, and for the first time since the capital I felt something other than numbness — felt the specific, terrible energy of a problem with no solution, which is, I have since learned, the soil where solutions actually grow.

"Your people," Odile said, as we climbed the stairs, slower now, resting twice. "The cartographer. The man who asks questions. The boy."

"They're not my people."

"No," she agreed. "Of course not." She paused on the landing, breathing, and looked at me with something that was almost gentle. "Courier. One piece of advice from a woman who is spending her last weeks learning what matters. Don't decide anything tonight. Decisions made at midnight always smell of panic by morning. Eat something. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow is soon enough to find out who you are."

"And who am I tomorrow?"

"That," said Odile Ferrant, opening the door to the lamplit pressroom where Marta was sketching the press's mechanism from memory, where Ilan was teaching Tam number games, where my found family — my unauthorized, undocumented family — waited for me without knowing they were waiting — "is the question your sealed letter was designed to prevent you from asking. I'd start asking it anyway."

* * *

I did not sleep. I told myself I was keeping watch, which was technically true in the way many lies are technical truths.

Past midnight, the presses started again below — Odile ran a skeleton crew of three, printing forms, receipts, the small bureaucratic wallpaper of a dying state — and I lay in the cot she'd given me and listened to the iron heartbeat and did arithmetic. If the general's envoys were coming, they were already coming. Letters travel; armies follow letters. Every hour I had spent walking east was an hour they had spent riding west, and somewhere on the roads between here and the ford, courtesy was advancing on us at the speed of cavalry.

I got up. I took my cipher-case from under my coat — I had not let it out of arm's reach in eleven days, and Marta had stopped teasing me about it around day four — and I sat by the window with the moon on the press-house roof and the case open on my knees.

I did not decode it. Not fully. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of women like me: I did not yet dare. I only held the case, and thought about Tam's fingers moving in his sleep, tracing numbers he'd memorized off my papers without knowing what they were, and thought about Odile saying *something wearing the republic*, and understood with a clarity that felt almost physical that the case in my hands was no longer a duty.

It was evidence. Or a weapon. Or a leash — and leashes, unlike duties, can be cut.

From the pressroom below came the rhythmic sigh of the great machine, and beneath it, fainter, Odile's cough, and beneath that, if I listened hard enough, the sound of a countdown nobody had wound on purpose.

Tomorrow, I promised the moon, the case, the boy asleep downstairs who could recite secrets he couldn't read. Tomorrow I would open it all the way, and see the whole of what I had carried, and decide who I was.

The presses ran until dawn. I counted every stroke.
