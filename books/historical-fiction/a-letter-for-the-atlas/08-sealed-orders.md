## 8. Sealed Orders

The Atlas Workshop had a guest room the size of a confession booth.

Freya discovered this after Odile Ferrant had shown them up a narrow stair that creaked like an old man climbing it himself, past shelves of type cases stacked to the ceiling, past a wall of locked drawers labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed. The room contained a cot, a washstand, a candle, and a window that looked out over the print floor below — a dark ocean of machinery waiting for morning to make sense of it.

"Printers sleep badly," Odile had said, setting down a candle stub and a jug of watered wine as though these were the only amenities the world had ever required. "So we give guests the same bed we'd want ourselves. Short enough that you can't lie awake wondering what you've forgotten."

"I never forget anything," Freya said.

"Then it will be very restful for you."

She had meant it as a joke. It was only now, lying on the cot with her boots off and her coat folded into a pillow, that Freya understood it hadn't been one. Odile Ferrant had looked at her commission case — the flat iron-banded box she carried against her ribs the way other people carried a heart — and had gone pale. Not startled. Pale. The color of someone reading their own sentence in a stranger's paperwork.

Freya had told herself all evening that there were a hundred explanations. The seal of the Chancellery of State frightened everyone now; half the republic's official wax had become, in the last year, less a promise than a threat. A dying master printer might well blanch at any document bearing the republic's authority, because documents bearing the republic's authority had lately arrived at printing houses in the company of soldiers.

But explanations were Ilan's trade, not hers. Her trade was carrying things without opening them. Twelve years of it. She could count on one hand — she had counted, more than once, on long roads, in worse rooms than this — the number of sealed commissions she had delivered unread. Forty-one. Forty-one little boxes of other people's intentions, carried faithfully from the hands that wrote them to the hands meant to read them, and not once had her curiosity cost the republic anything except, perhaps, a small private war inside herself that no ledger recorded.

Tonight the private war was winning.

She got up. There was no point pretending otherwise; she had been "resting" for two hours and her body had believed none of it. She lit the candle stub, dressed, and sat on the edge of the cot with the commission case across her knees.

It weighed what it always weighed. That was the obscenity of it. Four pounds, eleven ounces — she knew because she had weighed it once, early on, out of some clerk's instinct to quantify even the sacred — and it did not weigh one ounce more tonight than it had on the day the Deputy Chancellor pressed it into her hands and said, *Deliver it unopened or don't deliver yourself.* As if the republic were dying, the ministries evacuating around them like a body withdrawing blood from a limb, the sky over the capital the color of a bruise going bad — and still the box weighed four pounds eleven ounces, indifferent as scripture.

Outside, the river town murmured. Somewhere below her, water moved through the press-room sluices; somewhere beyond that, Marta slept in the cart-shed with her coffin of maps, and Tam slept wherever Tam had decided sleep happened — the boy treated beds as suggestions, and had been found at midnight twice already sitting among the type drawers running his fingers along the letterforms like a man reading braille in a language he refused to learn. And Ilan slept, if he slept at all, with his census ledgers under his head like a pillow stuffed with ghosts.

They trust me, Freya thought. All of them, in their various fashions, had arranged themselves around her and her iron box as though it were a hearth instead of a question mark.

She opened the case.

Not the seal — never the seal, not yet, maybe not ever — but the outer compartment, which was hers by right and habit: spare nibs, the decoding wheel's brass housing, flint, the thin knife she was technically not authorized to carry but which every courier carried, because the republic's enemies did not read its regulations. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth, the cipher key itself: the Republic Cipher, third revision, issued to seven living people, of whom she was perhaps the last one still walking toward anywhere.

The wheel was beautiful. She had always thought so, in the guilty way one thinks a weapon is beautiful. Two brass discs, forty characters each, inner ring and outer, turning against each other through twenty-nine settings keyed to the date. The Chancellery had commissioned it from the same workshops that made sextants, and it carried the same cold elegance: an instrument for finding your position when you are lost.

She set it to today's date. Her hands did this without consulting her, the way a musician's hands find a chord, and she hated how steady they were.

*One fragment,* she bargained with herself, in the voice of a woman negotiating with a hostage-taker who is also the hostage. *One fragment. Enough to confirm the destination protocol — the routing header, the authentication block. If the seal is genuine, the header will match the seal, and I will know my orders are what the Deputy Chancellor said they were, and I will sleep, and in the morning I will hand this thing to Odile Ferrant and be done with twelve years of carrying.*

It was a good bargain. It was, she recognized distantly, exactly the sort of bargain a person makes when they already know the answer.

The ciphertext began, as all Chancellery commissions began, with the routing block: fourteen groups of five characters that told any authorized reader who the message came from, whom it went to, and under what authority it traveled. She transcribed it onto the back of a laundry list Marta had given her — the only paper in the room — and set the wheel working.

The first three groups decoded clean.

ORIGIN: CHANCELLERY OF STATE. That was expected.

DESTINATION: ATLAS WORKSHOP, FERRANT. Expected. Her palms were damp anyway. She wiped them on her trousers and told herself it was the candle's heat.

AUTHORITY: REPUBLIC EMERGENCY STATUTE NINE. Also expected. Statute Nine was the provision under which the treaty plates could be moved, secured, or — in extremity — destroyed. Every courier who ever carried a Workshop commission memorized Statute Nine before they memorized their own mother's birthday, because the plates were the republic's signature made metal, and whoever held them could sign as the republic.

She stared at the three decoded lines for a long time. They matched. Everything matched. The seal, the routing, the statute. She felt the relief come up in her like water finding a level, and she was reaching to blow out the candle when she noticed the fourth group.

The fourth group should have been the subject line. In every commission she had ever carried, the subject line was formulaic — TREATY INSTRUMENT, or PLATES TRANSFER ORDER, or, in one memorable case, CONDOLENCES AND ACCOUNTS, which had turned out to be exactly as cheerful as it sounded. Fourteen characters, standard formulation, no ambiguity permitted. Ambiguity in a subject line was how wars started; the Chancellery had learned this in blood and codified it thereafter.

The fourth group decoded to fourteen characters. But they were not the formulation.

She ran it again. Same result. She checked the date setting — correct. She checked her transcription against the ciphertext itself, character by character, the way Ilan checked his ledgers, and found her transcription perfect.

SUBJECT: SURRENDER AND ACCESS.

Four words that had no business sharing a document with Statute Nine.

Freya sat very still. Below her, the sluice-water whispered its one syllable over and over, like a colleague clearing his throat before bad news.

*Surrender and access.* Surrender of what? Access for whom? There were, she supposed, innocent readings. Surrender of duplicate proofs. Access for inspection. The Chancellery loved inspections the way cats love boxes. But innocent readings were also Ilan's trade, and she was beginning to understand that she had spent the whole road south collecting people whose skills she would need for exactly this moment, though she'd have denied it under oath.

Her hands, traitorously steady, set the wheel to the fifth group.

She had meant to stop. Let the record show, she thought wildly, that she had meant to stop — that some version of Freya Sandoval, the version with the spotless record and the forty-one unopened commissions, had reached for the candle snuffer with every honest intention. But the fifth group was already transcribed, because her hand wrote while her conscience deliberated, and the wheel was already turning, and the decoded line came up out of the brass like a fish rising:

THE BEARER SHALL DELIVER TO THE ENVOYS OF GENERAL ASHAN VIREK ALL PLATES,

and stopped, because the sixth group required the next day's setting — the cipher rolled over at midnight, a design feature meant to frustrate exactly the kind of reader she was currently being — and Freya Sandoval, courier of the republic, fourth grade, commendation twice awarded, looked at the words *ENVOYS OF GENERAL ASHAN VIREK* glowing in her own handwriting on the back of a laundry list, and the floor of her profession opened beneath her like a trapdoor.

She did not scream. Screaming would have woken Tam, and Tam had heard enough.

But something in her chest performed a maneuver she had no name for — a lurch, a reversal, the sensation of a stair missing where the stair should be. She gripped the cot frame until the brass discs stopped blurring.

Envoys. Of the general.

Her commission — the commission she had bled for at the Kessin marshes, starved for on the ash road, measured her life by since the Deputy Chancellor put it in her hands and called it the republic's last hope — was addressed to the enemy. Not *protected from* the enemy. Not *delivered despite* the enemy. Addressed *to*. The grammar alone was treason, and she had carried it for nine hundred miles with the tenderness other people reserved for infants.

Unless. Unless it was a ruse. Commissions were sometimes seeded with false headers to mislead interceptors; she knew this; she had once been briefed on it by a man with ink-stained cuffs who had then vanished from the Ministry so completely that she'd assumed he'd been promoted. A false header pointing at Virek would be — no. No, it wouldn't. A false header pointed away from the truth, and if the true content were a treaty securing the plates for the republic, no forger on earth would advertise the general's name in the subject block, because the first soldier to decode it would march straight to Virek with the good news. You didn't bait a trap with the prize.

You baited a trap with the courier. With a woman so disciplined, so faithful, so gloriously incapable of opening a sealed box, that she would walk the plates directly into the general's open hands and call it duty.

Freya laughed. It came out wrong — one dry note, like a type case dropped on stone — and she clapped a hand over her mouth out of old habit, though there was no one to wake, no superior to offend, no rule left to break that she wasn't already breaking. The sound died in the little room and left her colder than before.

Midnight must have passed while she sat there, because when she reset the wheel to the new date, the sixth group yielded without resistance, and the seventh, and she read on in the candle-stub light, faster now, the way you read a diagnosis:

—ALL PLATES, MATRICES, AND PROOF IMPRESSIONS OF THE TREATY SERIES INTO THE CUSTODY OF SAID ENVOYS, RECEIPT TO BE SIGNED BY THE BEARER ON BEHALF OF THE REPUBLIC, THIS COMMISSION CONSTITUTING FULL AUTHORITY OF THE CHANCELLERY OF STATE FOR THE PURPOSES OF TRANSFER—

Full authority. Signed by her. On behalf of the republic.

There it was, then. Not a treaty. An instrument of surrender, dressed in the republic's wax, traveling in the republic's cipher, carried by the republic's most obedient servant — and requiring, for its completion, nothing more than the one thing she had never once withheld.

Her signature.

She looked at her right hand as though it belonged to a stranger. Twelve years of training lived in that hand. It could ride eighteen hours without blistering. It could forge — no. It could *authenticate*, the word mattered enormously suddenly — it could authenticate documents under fire. It had signed receipts for ammunition, medicines, prisoner exchanges, one litter of courier dogs whose paperwork had taken three days and remained, in her private opinion, the republic's finest administrative achievement. And it had been about to sign away the metal throat of every free press between here and the northern passes, and call it honor.

The vertigo came then, properly, the way Odile had probably known it would — Odile, who had gone pale at a seal, who slept badly in a house full of presses, who had looked at Freya's commission the way a doctor looks at an X-ray showing something the patient hasn't been told yet. The room tilted gently around the candle flame. Freya put her feet on the floor and pressed her palms flat against her knees and breathed, in the manner taught to couriers for shock, four counts in, six counts out, until the world agreed to hold still.

A leash. That was the word her mind finally offered, when the tilting stopped. Not orders — she had loved her orders, had built herself out of them the way a vine builds itself out of a wall, and had never once asked what the wall wanted, because the wall was the republic and the republic was the good and the good did not require inspection. But a leash implied a hand at the other end, and a hand implied a person, and persons could want things. The Deputy Chancellor's face rose in her memory: tired, kind, ink-stained — *Deliver it unopened or don't deliver yourself* — and she saw now, with a clarity that felt like a slap, that kindness had been the wrapping. He had chosen her precisely because she would not look. Forty-one unopened commissions. He'd have asked for her by name.

Or he hadn't known either. That possibility existed too, and was somehow worse — the thought of the old man signing whatever crossed his desk in the capital's final week, trusting the machine beneath him, while the machine was sold out from under the both of them. She didn't know which story was true. That was the cruelest part: the cipher could tell her what her orders said, but nothing on earth could tell her who had written them, or why, and she suspected the answer to *why* was simply that the general's chancellery had gotten there first, the way water gets into a cellar.

Below her window, the print floor lay dark and enormous. Somewhere in it, under lock, under Odile's dying vigilance, sat the plates. Forty years of treaties, charters, declarations — the republic's signature in steel, able to make paper into law and law into fact. And she was the lawful instrument of their surrender, and the law was a forgery wearing the republic's face, and she was —

She was what? Still a courier? A courier delivered. That was the whole of the office. Nothing in her instructions covered discovering that the instructions were the crime.

Tam's voice drifted up through the floorboards, or perhaps she imagined it — the boy talked in his sleep sometimes, numbers mostly, sequences recited in a dreamer's mumble. He had memorized fragments of her cipher weeks ago on the road, without knowing what they were, the way he memorized everything, because reading was closed to him and remembering was not. She had found it charming once. She found it now terrifying, and precious, and she could not yet say why both.

Freya folded the laundry list into eighths and burned it in the candle flame, watching the decoded words curl and blacken — *VIREK*, *SURRENDER*, *FULL AUTHORITY* — until only ash drifted onto the washstand. The ciphertext itself stayed safe in its case, untouched, the seal unbroken. To anyone who inspected it — and someone would inspect it, soon; the pale look on Odile's face promised that — the commission remained exactly what it had always appeared to be: the republic's last hope, four pounds eleven ounces, sealed and faithful.

Only now Freya knew what faithfulness was worth, and to whom it had been sold.

She pinched out the candle and lay down on the short cot in the dark, eyes open, listening to the sluice-water whisper and the house tick and settle around its secrets. Sleep was not coming; she had stopped expecting it. But something else was arriving in its place, slow and unfamiliar, taking shape in her chest like type being set one letter at a time — anger, she supposed, or its respectable cousin, resolve — and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, a question forming itself in the old formula of her trade, the routing block of a message not yet written:

*Origin: Freya Sandoval. Destination: unknown. Authority: pending.*

Down the hall, a floorboard creaked. Then again — deliberate, unhurried, the tread of someone who also did not sleep, coming along the corridor toward her door, stopping just outside it.

Through the gap beneath the door, lamplight wavered, and Odile Ferrant's voice, low and rough as unsized paper, spoke through the wood.

"You're awake, courier. I can tell. We should talk — you and I — about what your commission actually says."
