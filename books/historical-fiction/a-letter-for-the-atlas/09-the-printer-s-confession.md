## 9. The Printer's Confession

The trouble with a dying body, Odile Ferrant had decided some years ago, was that it kept insisting on its own schedule. Her lungs had opinions. Her hands shook at the worst moments — never when lifting a teacup, always when setting the smallest type, the six-point faces that made her feel like a jeweler threading a needle in mittens. And now, on the night after the courier arrived with her sealed commission, Odile's chest had decided to conduct a full rehearsal of its own funeral, wheezing and knocking around in there like an apprentice who'd found the brandy.

She sat up in her cot above the Workshop and listened to the house breathe.

A printing house breathes differently than other houses. It creaks in metal, not wood — the press frame cooling, the great iron screw settling by fractions. It smells of ink and oil and the particular sweetness of paper stacked deep enough to have its own weather. Forty-one years she had lived inside that weather. She knew every sound it made the way other women knew their children's voices.

Which was why she heard the footsteps below at two in the morning, light and hesitant, going toward the press room instead of away from it.

Odile dressed without hurry. There was no hurry left in her life anywhere; she had used up all of it decades ago and considered this one of her better trades. She took the lamp off its hook, checked that her shawl was over her nightdress because dignity mattered even to ghosts, and went down the stairs she'd been climbing since she was nine years old, when her father first set her on a stool and said, *You'll learn letters from the wrong end, girl — from the type side up.*

Freya Sandoval stood in the dark of the press room like a woman caught stealing bread.

She hadn't lit anything. The courier stood with her hand still on the shuttered lamp by the door, and the moon through the tall windows fell in long gray bars across the floor, across the press, across the woman herself — a tall, upright figure with the posture of someone who had been told at a young age that slouching was a form of treason.

"I woke you," Freya said. Not a question, not quite an apology either. A courier's honesty: here is what happened, do with it what you will.

"You woke the press," Odile said. "I only came along to see what it wanted." She shuffled forward, lifted the lamp shutter, and let a little light into the room. "Well. That answers that."

* * *

The press wanted nothing, of course. Presses are honest that way; they want exactly what you give them and no more. But Freya Sandoval had been standing near it the way a thirsty woman stands near a well, and Odile had spent forty-one years reading people the way she read proofs — quickly, and with a professional eye for errors.

"Sit," Odile said, indicating the stool by the composing stone. Then, because the courier looked as though sitting might be against regulations: "Sit or don't, but stop hovering. You'll scuff my floor, and I've scuffed it enough for both of us."

Freya sat. Perched, rather, on the front third of the stool, hands on knees, like a bird ready to take offense and flight simultaneously.

"You couldn't sleep," Odile said.

"No."

"Because of the letter you're carrying."

Freya's chin came up a fraction. "Because of the road. Because of the boy who snores. Because the widow cartographer has hung her husband's maps all over the drying loft like laundry and I keep expecting them to start a conversation."

"Ah," said Odile. "So it's everything except the letter."

Something flickered across the young woman's face — the specific look of a person who has just been outflanked by an old woman in a nightcap and finds herself almost enjoying it. "You read the outer seal today," Freya said. "You went pale."

"I'm eighty years old. Everything makes me pale. I once went pale at a good soup." Odile lowered herself onto her own stool, the one worn to the shape of her, and folded her hands. "But yes. I went pale."

The press room was quiet in the way only press rooms are quiet at night — not silent but attentive, full of waiting metal. Somewhere above them Marta Hale turned over in her sleep, and the coffin-cart outside creaked once in the wind, holding its secret maps the way it had held its secret man.

"I've been a printer for seventy years," Odile said. "Apprenticed at nine, master at twenty-six, widowed at thirty-four, and stubborn ever since. In seventy years I have printed treaties, tax rolls, marriage banns, death notices, three separate constitutions, one cookbook of genuinely criminal quality, and — during the Border Panic, which the histories politely forget — forty thousand copies of a pamphlet telling people the epidemic was over when it wasn't, because the ministry paid and my apprentices needed wages." She looked at her hands, liver-spotted and ink-stained down to the quick. "I tell you this so you understand I am not a sentimental woman. Paper is paper. Ink is ink. What's written on it is somebody else's sin."

"And?"

"And four weeks ago," Odile said, "a man came here from the chancellery. Not your chancellery — theirs. The general's. He was very polite. He wore a good coat and he smelled of nothing at all, which frightened me more than any smell would have." She smiled thinly. "He didn't threaten me. He *thanked* me. He thanked me for keeping the plates safe, and he said that soon they would be put to proper use, under new management, and that a loyal printer would find the transition painless. He said the word 'loyal' the way a butcher says 'tender.'"

Freya had gone very still. It was, Odile thought, her most impressive feature — that stillness. Most people fidget their way through bad news like water finding cracks. This one absorbed it whole.

"He showed you papers."

"He showed me courtesy. The papers were implied. Men like that don't carry documents; documents can be stolen. They carry *arrangements*." Odile leaned back. "And then, ten days later, a second visitor. This one from your republic — your ministry, your seals, your wax. Asking nothing, promising nothing, merely confirming that the Atlas Workshop remained secure, that its master printer remained reliable, that the treaty plates would be collected in due course by a trusted courier." She tapped the arm of her stool. "Two governments asking after the same plates in the same month, courier, in a country that is supposedly at peace with itself. I may be old, but I can count. It's the one thing printers and census men have in common."

"So you knew," Freya said quietly. "Before I came. You knew what the letter would say."

"I knew what the letter would *do*. Whether it says hand them over or sell them or trade them for a nice portrait of the general on horseback — I couldn't say, and I didn't need to. The plates leave this building and go to a man who burns printing houses that displease him. That much arithmetic even my lungs could manage." She coughed, briefly, as if summoned by the mention. "What I did not know was whether the courier carrying the order would be a functionary or a human being. I'd begun to fear the two categories no longer overlapped."

* * *

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Then she asked the question Odile had been waiting for, and it was not the question about the letter at all.

"What did you print?" the courier said, her eyes going past Odile's shoulder to the far corner of the press room, where a stack of fresh sheets sat under oilcloth, taller than a bread oven and smelling of last week's ink.

And there it was, Odile thought. Seventy years of reading people, and still it warmed her like good tea: the ones who noticed the paper first were the ones worth trusting.

"That," she said, "is the confession part."

She rose — slowly, giving her lungs fair warning — and walked to the corner, and drew back the oilcloth with the flourish of a woman unveiling a monument, though what lay beneath was humbler than that and grander too: broadsheets. Hundreds of them. Single sheets, printed on both sides, the type crisp and close-set, the headline running across the top in letters big enough to read from a galloping horse:

**THE ATLAS HERALD — Price: Nothing — Belongs To No One**

Below it, columns. News the ministries wouldn't print: the burned villages on the eastern road, counted by name. The requisitions the general's officers called "purchases." The ford checkpoint where soldiers searched a coffin. And at the bottom, a small box of type that Odile had set herself, late at night, shaking hands and all: *This sheet is free. Pass it on. If you cannot pass it on, read it aloud. If you cannot read, ask someone to. That is the whole law of it.*

"You've been printing a newspaper," Freya said. Her voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence.

"For three weeks. Two hundred a run, run twice a week, carried out in flour sacks by the baker's boy, who believes he's smuggling contraband cheese and is paid accordingly." Odile patted the stack. "It's the most illegal thing I've done in my life, and I once printed a cookbook that suggested boiling cabbage for four hours."

"Why illegal? It's — " Freya paused, choosing the word carefully. "It's true."

"Oh, courier." Odile laughed, and it turned into a cough, and she waved off the younger woman half-rising to help. "Truth is the most illegal substance in any country at war. Truth doesn't take sides neatly. Truth says the republic sold these people *and* the general burns them *and* the men who signed both orders drink from the same cellars. My broadsheet doesn't recruit for anyone. It simply refuses to lie. You'd be astonished how many people find that unforgivable." She sobered. "Also, strictly speaking, the republic licensed me to print treaties and official notices. A free sheet, priced at nothing, owned by nobody — there's no column in their ledgers for that. Unledgered things frighten governments more than armies do. Armies you can buy."

Freya stood and crossed to the stack. She picked up the top sheet with both hands, the way one handles a document — and then, catching herself, adjusted her grip to the way one handles a thing that matters. She read it all, top to bottom, standing in the moonlight, and Odile watched her read and thought: *there goes a woman finding out what she actually believes, and hating how comfortable it feels.*

"The village counts," Freya said at last. "These names. Where — " She stopped. Started again. "There's a man traveling with us. Ilan Voss. He was a census taker. He kept ledgers of — "

"Of the dead. Yes." Odile nodded. "His figures reached me through the baker's boy, who got them from his aunt, who got them from a refugee cart. Half the east road's news arrives that way now; the roads themselves have given up on it." She met the courier's eyes. "I printed his numbers. Every name he recorded, I set in type. It takes up nearly the whole second page of this run. Someone should be able to prove those people existed, and paper outlives governments — I have it on excellent authority, being older than several."

Freya set the sheet down with exaggerated care, as if her hands might otherwise do something unprofessional. "How many know?" she asked. "About the Herald?"

"Five. Me. The baker's boy. His aunt. A widow who draws maps and recently arrived with a coffin full of contraband geography and has been leaving me notes suggesting improvements to my distribution routes." Odile permitted herself a dry smile. "And now you. Which brings us to the difficult part, so I'll make us tea first. Bad news keeps better with something warm in the hand. Old printers' rule."

"We're in the middle of a civil war and you're making tea."

"Especially then. Tea is how civilization files its complaints."

* * *

The stove in the corner of the press room had heated type-metal for fifty years and knew its business; by the time the kettle muttered, Odile had arranged two mismatched cups on the composing stone and Freya had, without appearing to decide to, begun straightening a case of loose type. Odile watched her do it and felt something in her old chest squeeze tighter than the lungs managed on their own.

"Letter by letter," Odile said, nodding at the case. "Do you know it?"

"A little. Enough to know you're looking at it upside down and backward."

"Everyone does, at first. It's the trade's little joke — we spend our lives reading the world mirrored, so we notice sooner when the world itself starts printing things reversed." She poured the tea. "Now. The difficult part."

Freya took the cup and waited, and Odile admired that too — the discipline of it, the refusal to fill silence with noise. Somewhere in that rigid frame was a person who could sit still while the world ended, and Odile had built her life around precisely such people, having married one.

"I've known for weeks what was coming," Odile said. "And I have done nothing but print. I haven't moved the plates, though I could have — there's a cellar under the type-foundry that floods in spring and no inspector has entered it since the flood of the Year of the Bad Harvest. I haven't destroyed them, though God knows I've stood in this room at three in the morning weighing hammer versus conscience. I haven't surrendered them, obviously, or you'd have found a receipt instead of an old woman." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "I have printed truth and hidden nothing and changed nothing. Because I am one dying woman with bad lungs, and a broadsheet, however true, is a candle in a barn fire."

"You're not nothing," Freya said. It came out fast, almost angry, and she seemed surprised at her own volume. She lowered her voice. "Two hundred copies, twice a week, for three weeks. On the road east I saw what happens when people learn they aren't alone in knowing. It's — " She stopped again. This courier, Odile noted, stopped a great deal, as if her sentences required clearance from head office. "It's not a candle."

"It's kind of you to say."

"It isn't kind. It's assessment." And there she was again, upright as a fence post, and Odile laughed until the kettle rattled.

"Very well. Assessment received." She set down her cup. "Here is the difficult part, courier. Tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever you open that seal fully — and you will, because I watched you stand next to it tonight the way a dog watches a door — you will have to decide what to do with your orders and my plates and your career and possibly all our necks. And I am going to ask you for one thing, and it is not obedience, and it is not secrecy, and it is not help."

"Then what?"

"Time." Odile held her gaze. "Don't decide tonight. Sleep on it — badly, probably; nobody sleeps well in this house anymore, the press snores worse than the boy. But don't decide tonight. Decisions made at two in the morning in a press room smell like the lamp oil and feel like fate, and fate is nearly always someone else's paperwork. Decide in daylight, after tea, with your feet cold on my floor and the boy demanding breakfast and the widow redrawing my distribution routes without being asked. Decide as a person among people, not a courier among orders." She raised her cup slightly. "That's all I ask. One night. Whatever you choose afterward, I'll set the type for it myself."

Freya looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she turned her cup in her hands and said, in a different voice entirely — smaller, younger, stripped of the courier's varnish:

"When I opened the outer seal tonight — just a corner, just enough to see the first line — I hoped I'd imagined what I saw. And then I came down here, and I didn't know if I came to check on your press or to stand near something honest before I have to go back to lying." She looked up. "Why are you telling me all this? You've kept your secret for weeks. I'm the one carrying the order to end it. By every sensible measure you should have sent the baker's boy to steal my cipher-case while I slept."

"Every sensible measure," Odile agreed. "Sensible measures are how we got the war." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register she used for corrections on a final proof — gentle, exact, leaving no margin for misunderstanding. "I'm telling you because you stood in my doorway tonight and lied to me about why you couldn't sleep, badly, the way honest people lie. Because you straightened my type case without being asked, and put every letter back facing the wall, which means someone taught you right even if it wasn't the republic. And because I have spent seventy years watching what people do around presses, and the dangerous ones are always the ones who touch the paper gently."

She drank her tea. Outside, far off toward the river, something boomed — distant artillery, or thunder, or the century turning over in its sleep — and neither of them remarked on it, which was its own kind of companionship.

"One night," Freya said finally. "All right."

"All right," Odile echoed, and meant: *thank you*, and meant: *forgive me*, and meant, beneath both, the oldest prayer of her trade: *let the impression come out clean.*

They washed the cups together in the stone sink, in silence, like women who had done it all their lives.

It was Freya who broke it, at the foot of the stairs, pausing with her lamp relit. "Master Ferrant. The second page. The names — Ilan's names. When the next run prints —"

"Tomorrow night," Odile said. "If the lungs allow."

"If it prints," Freya said, and her face in the lamplight was unreadable and entirely legible at once, "print double. I think I know where we can get more paper."

She climbed the stairs. Odile stood a moment in the dark press room, listening to the house breathe, and then she did what she had done every night for three weeks and would do tomorrow: she pulled the oilcloth back over the Herald, patted the stack twice — *good work, hold fast* — and went to wind the clock.

At the top of the stairs, faint through the floorboards, she heard the drying loft door open, and Marta Hale's low voice murmuring, and then — unmistakably — the boy Tam's voice, awake at this hour, reciting numbers in the dark. Steady. Rhythmic. Like type falling into place.

Odile stood with her hand on the banister and frowned, because she knew number-sequences when she heard them, and those were not a child's counting games.

Those were a key, being learned by heart.
