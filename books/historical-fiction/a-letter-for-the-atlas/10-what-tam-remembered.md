## 10. What Tam Remembered

Tam knew three things about the room above the Workshop: that it smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, that Freya sat in it at night with her case open like a wound, and that he was not supposed to be there.

He went anyway. This was not disobedience, exactly. It was more like water going downhill — you could call the water a troublemaker if you wanted, but it was only doing what it was made to do.

The door was ajar. Through the gap he could see her at the table, the cipher-case open, the little brass rods laid out in rows. She had her fists pressed against her mouth, which was how she looked when she thought nobody could see her being afraid.

Tam counted things when he was nervous, and he was always a little nervous around Freya, because she was the kind of person who noticed everything and said almost none of it out loud. He counted her breaths through the door. Fourteen slow ones, then a long one, then she closed the case with a click that sounded like a small bone breaking.

That was when he knocked.

"Tam." She didn't sound surprised. Freya never sounded surprised; she just sounded like someone who had already rearranged the world to fit whatever happened next. "It's late."

"I know." He came in anyway, because stopping halfway through a doorway is worse than either option. "I can't sleep. The press downstairs makes noises. Like a big animal dreaming."

"It does do that."

"I named it. The animal. I called it Odile Junior."

For one second — he counted it, one whole second — Freya smiled. Then she put the smile away somewhere safe and looked at him properly, which was worse. "You should be asleep."

"So should you," Tam said, and then immediately regretted it, because adults did not like being told they were the same as children even when it was obviously true.

But she didn't send him away. She moved a chair with her foot, the way you invite someone without making it a ceremony, and Tam sat down across from her and the open case and all those little brass rods with their tiny stamped marks, arranged like soldiers who had been standing at attention for a hundred years.

"What are they?" he asked, though he wasn't supposed to ask.

"Numbers," Freya said. "Each rod is a number wearing a costume."

"Numbers wear costumes?"

"All numbers do. That's what a code *is*. A number dressed up as something else so it can walk past guards without being stopped." She turned one of the rods between her fingers. "This one looks like a harmless little seven. But in the right order, in the right company, it means something entirely different."

Tam leaned forward. He couldn't read — letters slid around on the page and swapped places when he wasn't looking, like beetles under a stone — but numbers stayed still. Numbers were honest. Numbers held your hand.

"Do you want to hear something?" he said.

Freya looked at him over the tops of the rods.

"When I can't sleep," Tam said, "I count. And I don't just count sheep or anything boring. I count everything I heard during the day. Doors. Boots. And—" He hesitated now, really hesitated, because part of him understood, even without words, that what he was about to say might be a kind of breaking. "And the sounds your case makes. When you turn the rods. Each rod has a little voice. A different click."

The lamplight made a small orange lake on the table between them. Somewhere below them, Odile Junior the press-dreaming-animal groaned in its sleep.

"Go on," Freya said quietly.

* * *

Here is the thing about Tam that nobody had quite believed yet, though Ilan suspected it and Marta had started keeping a slate of his sequences on the cart like other families kept drawings: Tam's head was a room with no doors, and once something walked into it, it never walked out.

Letters escaped him. Words dissolved. But sequences — number-strings, rhythms of clicks, the particular music of a thing done in an order — those stayed. They nested in him like swallows under an eave. He hadn't chosen this. It wasn't a skill anyone had taught him. It was simply the shape of him, the way some people are tall and some people are kind and Tam was a boy who could hold a thousand numbers the way other boys held a ball.

So he recited.

He started with the easy ones, the ones he'd gathered from the road like a magpie: the number of steps from the ford to the checkpoint (two hundred eleven, and four of them wobbled), the count of soldiers' boot-heels (he'd given each man a number based on his limp), the rhythm of Marta stirring the pot (long-long-short, like a bird with a limp too, apparently everything limped).

Freya laughed at that one — a real laugh, startled out of her — and Tam felt the warm feeling he always felt when he made an adult forget to be heavy.

Then he took a breath and did the other thing.

He tapped the table. Slowly. In the pattern of the clicks.

*Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap-tap.*

"That's the third rod," he said. "When you check it first, every night before you sleep. You always check it first. Tap-tap, tap, tap-tap-tap-tap. And then—"

He did the second one. The fifth. The ninth, which had a funny double-click at the end because the rod was worn, he could tell it was worn by the sound, and he'd asked himself a hundred times why a worn rod would matter until he realized it wouldn't, except that it meant he could tell the ninth apart from every other rod in the dark, in his sleep, upside down.

Freya had gone very still.

Not angry-still. Tam knew angry-still; he'd grown up around it. This was different. This was the stillness of a person watching a wall they trusted develop a crack.

"How many," she said. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something breakable.

"All of them," Tam said. "I mean — all the ones you've opened near me. Twenty-two nights. You do the same fourteen rods mostly, in mostly the same order, and then sometimes extra ones after, depending. I think the extras are the important parts? Because you do them slower. People do important things slower." He shrugged. "Like cutting bread for guests."

Freya put both hands flat on the table, as if the table might otherwise drift away.

"Tam. Can you do it again? All of it. From the beginning."

"From the beginning of which night?"

"Any night. Every night."

So he did. He closed his eyes and became, for a while, nothing but a sequence — taps and pauses, the worn ninth rod with its stutter, the long rod she handled last every single time, the one that got its own silence afterward, like a period at the end of a sentence. When he finished, the lamp had burned down a finger's width, and Freya was writing — actually writing, fast, on the back of one of Marta's spare maps, filling the margins with figures.

"You can't read," she said, half to herself, "but you've memorized my cipher."

"I didn't mean to."

"I know you didn't."

"Is it bad? That I did?"

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Tam watched her decide something. He was getting good at watching people decide things; it looked different in everyone. Ilan decided things like a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for years. Marta decided things the way a door closes — firm, no creak. Freya decided things the way ice decides to melt: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once.

"No," she said finally. "It might be the opposite of bad. But we need Ilan. Tonight. Can you wait while I fetch him, or will the numbers fall out?"

"They don't fall out," Tam said, a little offended. "They *nest*."

* * *

Ilan Voss arrived with his collar crooked and his ledger under his arm, because Ilan carried that ledger everywhere now, the way other men carried guilt — which was fitting, since the ledger was full of it. He listened to Tam recite the sequence twice, standing by the door with his mouth slightly open, and then he pulled up the third chair and did the thing he did best in the world, which was look at paper and understand it.

Except there was no paper. There was a boy.

"Say the ninth rod again," Ilan said.

Tam tapped it, complete with its worn-stutter.

"And the last one. The long one."

Tam tapped that too, and added, "She always leaves a quiet after it. A big quiet. Bigger than for the others."

Ilan and Freya looked at each other over the boy's head, the way adults do when the conversation has left the room where children are allowed. Tam hated that. He decided, on principle, to keep counting.

"He has the structure," Ilan murmured. "The opening key-sequence, the body order, the closing seal. Freya — the closing seal is the part that authenticates. It's the part that says *this document comes from the republic and may be trusted.*" His finger hovered over the map-back where she'd written. "If these are right — if he's right —"

"He's right," Freya said flatly. "You heard him. Fourteen rods, twenty-two nights. Nobody performs that consistently unless it's real."

"But that means—" Ilan set down the pencil and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and for a moment he looked every year of his tired age. "That means this child, this nine-year-old—"

"Ten," said Tam.

"—this ten-year-old is carrying enough of your commission in his head to reconstruct the outer cipher. Which means he could expose it. Read it aloud to the whole Workshop. Or—" Ilan stopped. Tam watched him step off the ledge. "Or write a new one."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, Odile Junior groaned in its dream, and Tam privately revised his estimate of how much the press slept versus how much it listened.

"Nobody is writing anything," Freya said. But she said it the way people say *nobody is panicking*, which is to say, in a tone that causes panic.

Ilan leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Freya. Listen to me. I spent eleven years counting the dead for a republic that filed them neatly and forgot them by spring. I know exactly what paperwork can do, because I watched it bury people who were still breathing. And now the most dangerous document in this country is walking around inside a boy who thinks numbers nest." He glanced at Tam, and his voice softened without changing its meaning. "No offense intended."

"None taken," said Tam, who had no idea what offense was but enjoyed being included.

"The envoys come back in two days," Ilan said. "The general's men, with their white flag and their patient faces. And when they do, Freya, they'll expect either your compliance or your surrender. What will you have for them? Because right now the truth in your case is a leash, and the only person in this building who can't read it is the one who's memorized it."

Freya stood up. She walked to the window, which was cracked for air, and stood looking down into the dark yard where Marta's coffin-cart sat under sacking, holding maps instead of a husband, which everyone agreed he would have found funny.

"If I forge a letter in the republic's cipher," she said slowly, "using the seal-sequence a child memorized off my own case—"

"—it would carry the republic's full authority," Ilan finished. "As far as any captain or clerk could verify. And by the time anyone discovered otherwise, the plates would be gone, or the broadsheet would be loose, or—" He spread his hands. "Or all of it. Or none of it. Forgery is a door, Freya. It doesn't care which way you go through it."

Tam raised his hand, because at the orphanage raising your hand had occasionally worked, and because he had a question that was burning a hole in his chest.

Both adults looked at him.

"If I'm carrying it," Tam said, "then isn't it mine? A little bit?"

The room went quiet in a new way.

"I mean," he hurried on, because the quiet was getting heavy, "you said numbers wear costumes so they can get past guards. Well. I'm the guard they got past. So now I know the secret, and knowing a secret means—" He searched for the word and couldn't find it, so he built it out of smaller words, the way he built everything. "It means you have to let me help carry it. That's the rule. That's the fair rule."

Freya came back from the window. She crouched down next to his chair, which adults almost never did, so that their eyes were level, and Tam saw that she was looking at him the way Marta looked at the coffin-cart: like something precious being carried toward a fire.

"Tam," she said. "What you have in your head could save everyone in this building. It could also hang every one of us. Do you understand that?"

"No," Tam said honestly. "But I understand that you're scared, and Ilan's scared, and Odile talks to the press like it's her son, and Marta sharpens the axe at night when she thinks we're sleeping. Everybody here is scared of something. Except me. I'm not scared of anything except the river." He paused. "And moths. But moths don't count."

Something happened to Freya's face then — a crack in the disciplined numbness, a small collapse in the direction of tenderness — and she reached out and touched the top of his head, briefly, like sealing a letter.

"Then we'll teach you what you're carrying," she said. "All of it. Before you carry it another step. That's my condition. No secrets from you anymore, and no decisions without you in the room. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Tam, who had never been offered a seat at any table in his life and was determined to act like this happened every day.

Ilan was already pulling fresh paper toward himself, and Marta's spare maps, and the stub of a pencil. Outside, far off toward the ford, a dog barked twice and stopped, and Tam — who counted everything — noted that it barked in the rhythm *tap-tap, tap*, the same as the third rod, and decided that was either a coincidence or the night itself trying to learn the code.

"First lesson," Ilan said, tapping the paper. "We start with what the old cipher *says*, so you know what we'd be changing. Freya — decode it. All of it. If he's going to hold the key, he holds the whole key."

Freya drew the case toward her and began laying out the rods, one by one, and Tam leaned in, and began — softly, under his breath, in the rhythm only he could hear — to count.

* * *

They were still at it when the lamp guttered low, and it was Odile who found them at dawn: the courier, the census taker, and the boy, bent over a scatter of maps covered in figures, like three conspirators who had forgotten which side they were conspiring for.

Odile stood in the doorway a long moment, her shawl around her shoulders, looking at the papers, at the case, at Tam.

"Well," she said at last, in the voice of a woman who had spent forty years setting type and recognized a headline when she saw one. "I suppose somebody had better put the kettle on. It looks like we're committing treason before breakfast."

Tam counted the seconds it took Freya and Ilan to stop looking guilty.

It was nine. He decided they needed practice.
