## 11. The General's Envoys

They came on the third morning, under a white flag so clean it looked laundered for the occasion.

Freya saw them from the Workshop's upper window: six riders in dove-grey uniforms, no insignia but a single brass pin shaped like a compass rose — the general's mark, she'd learned, though he had never been a navigator and his armies had never once gone where they said they would. The riders did not gallop. They did not form up at the gates with muskets. They simply arrived, dismounted, tied their horses to Marta's fence posts as if they were guests expected for supper, and waited.

"Polite ones," Marta said beside her, arms crossed. "That's worse."

"It is worse," Freya agreed. She had spent eleven years reading men across negotiation tables. The shouting kind wanted something small and told you so. The polite kind wanted everything and intended to be thanked for taking it.

Odile appeared at her elbow, smelling of ink and camphor, her cough held down by visible effort. "The tall one in front. That's Envoy Corvane. He negotiated the grain treaties before the war. He once talked a starving province into paying more."

"I know him," Freya said, and was surprised to find she meant it — not by acquaintance, but by type. Corvane was the sort of man who existed in every ministry she had ever served: patient, well-read, genuinely convinced that he alone understood what the situation required, which was always, conveniently, whatever he had already decided.

Below, Tam was in the yard stacking firewood, because Tam stacked things when he was nervous — wood, stones, spoons — and he had gone very still, watching the grey riders with the fixed attention he gave to anything that counted.

"He's counting them," Ilan murmured from the doorway. "Six horses, six men, one flag. He'll know their saddle order by tonight." A pause. "I'd rather he didn't have anything of theirs memorized at all."

"We can't unsee what we see," Freya said. It was the closest thing to comfort she had, and Ilan took it anyway, because that was the arrangement between them now: thin comforts, honestly offered.

* * *

They received the envoys in the Workshop's front room, which Odile had cleared of everything except a table, four chairs, and the enormous silent presence of the master press behind a curtain, like an elephant hiding behind a bedsheet. Freya sat at the head of the table with Odile at her right hand. Marta refused to sit at all and stood by the window sharpening nothing in particular with a knife that was already sharp. Ilan and Tam were sent upstairs, over both their objections, on the reasoning that a deserter census taker and a boy who could not read were the two least useful faces to show the general's men.

Envoy Corvane entered first. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that suggested it had been arranged, and he removed his hat with a flourish so practiced it looked sincere. Behind him came a younger man with a ledger-case — of course a ledger-case; power traveled with its paperwork — and two soldiers who stationed themselves by the door with the studied blankness of furniture that could become weapons.

"Mistress Ferrant," Corvane said, bowing to Odile. "And — forgive me — you would be the courier Sandoval."

"You have me at an advantage," Freya said.

"The general makes a study of advantages," Corvane said pleasantly. "It is his only hobby. May we sit?"

"You may."

He sat. He crossed his ankles. He accepted tea from Marta — who brought it with the expression of a woman pouring acid — thanked her warmly, complimented the cups, and then let the silence sit for exactly long enough to demonstrate that he was not afraid of it.

"The situation," he began, "is delicate, and I will treat it delicately. The republic's ministries are evacuated or captured. Its army is a rumor traveling south faster than its soldiers can walk. Its capital is governed this week by whoever shouts loudest in the assembly hall, and next week by weather. And yet—" he spread his hands, "—here you are. A sealed commission, a courier of known reliability, and the Atlas Workshop, intact, staffed, and holding treaty plates whose value has not diminished by one coin since the government that commissioned them stopped existing."

"The government hasn't stopped existing," Freya said.

Corvane smiled at her the way one smiles at a child insisting the moon follows the carriage home. "Mistress Sandoval. I have read your service record. Eleven years, forty-one commissions delivered, zero losses. You are, if I may say so without flattery, the last functioning organ of the state you serve. Which means the state exists precisely as long as you say it does." He leaned forward slightly. "We are not here to take anything. We are here to ask you to do your job."

Freya felt the old machinery turn over inside her — gears oiled by years of exactly this language. *Duty. Continuity. The work itself.* It was seductive because it was almost true, and she hated, with a sudden physical nausea, how comfortable the chair felt.

"The commission I carry is sealed," she said. "Its contents are between the issuing ministry and the Workshop's master. Not envoys."

"Naturally." Corvane sipped his tea. "We would never ask you to break a seal. We ask only this: that when the moment comes, you deliver it to *us*. The general's chancellery is the successor authority. A courier who delivers a state document into chaos serves no one. A courier who delivers it into stable hands serves everyone. Including herself." Another smile. "There is a commission waiting for you in the new administration. Director of Couriers. Your own office. Authority to rebuild the dispatch network as you see fit. We have watched your career, Mistress Sandoval. We think you are wasted on roads."

Behind the curtain, Freya thought she heard the press shift — impossible, presses don't move — and realized it was Odile's boot pressing against the floorboards, deliberately, a small drumbeat of solidarity.

"And if I decline?" Freya asked.

Corvane's expression did not change at all, which was itself an answer. "Then we will be very sad, and we will wait. We are extremely good at waiting. The general says patience is just violence with the volume turned down." He stood, replaced his hat, bowed to Odile, nodded to Marta — "Mistress Hale; your late husband's river surveys are admired even in the north" — and Freya watched Marta go white and still as stone at the window, filing that away like a knife into a belt.

"We'll return in three days," Corvane said at the door. "Take whatever time you need. Duty should never be rushed."

When they were gone, Marta set down the teapot with a click. "He knew my husband's name."

"He knows everything," Odile said quietly. "That's how you know he's serious."

* * *

That evening they argued around the kitchen fire, which was less a council of war than a family disagreeing about the future, complete with interruptions and one thrown spoon (Tam's; it missed).

"Three days," Ilan said. "That's not generosity. That's accounting. He's giving us time to decide because a decision made freely holds better than one made under threat."

"Or he's waiting for reinforcements," Marta said.

"Both. He's a careful man. Careful men bring arithmetic and cavalry."

Freya sat apart from the fire, turning her commission case over in her hands. She had not opened it. She could not open it — not yet, not while the seal held and the fiction of her duty held with it, because the moment she broke that seal she would have to know whether she was a servant of a dying republic or a hired hand of its replacement, and some part of her understood that either answer would cost her the person she had been.

"You went quiet in there," Ilan said, settling beside her. "I've seen quiet like that. Census takers get it. When the numbers start telling you something you didn't want counted."

"What did you want me to say to him?" Freya asked.

"Honestly? Nothing. You said exactly the right thing. 'Not envoys.' Perfectly correct. Perfectly within protocol." He looked into the fire. "That's what frightens me. You answered him like a courier. Smooth, correct, stalling beautifully. I kept thinking — she's done this before. She's good at this. And being good at this is the trap."

Freya laughed, briefly and without much humor. "Yes. That's the horror of it. He offered me a directorship and my spine *liked* it. Eleven years of roads and rain and forged passes, and some part of me heard 'your own office' and sat up like a dog hearing its name." She shook her head. "I hate how easily the old answers come. He asks about duty and I reach for duty like a handrail. He doesn't need to threaten me. He just needs to speak my language."

"And what's wrong with your language?" Tam asked, from the floor, where he was arranging firewood into an exact spiral. He couldn't read the argument, but he could hear its shape, and children hear shapes better than adults do.

Freya considered the question seriously, because Tam deserved seriousness. "Nothing's wrong with it. That's the problem. It all sounds right. Duty sounds right. Orders sound right. Even surrender, if you dress it properly, sounds right. There isn't a word in it that warns you."

"There's numbers," Tam said.

"What do you mean?"

"When people lie, they count different." He placed another log with precision. "At the checkpoint, before the soldiers, the captain said 'just routine' twice and touched his sword three times. People who mean it don't count their words. Corvane — he counted. I watched his fingers under the table. Every time he said 'we' he tapped once. Sixty-one times." He looked up. "Nobody says 'we' sixty-one times unless they're practicing."

The fire crackled. Ilan stared at the boy with the expression of a man watching water flow uphill.

"Sixty-one," Freya repeated.

"Give or take. Maybe fifty-eight. He sped up near the end." Tam returned to his spiral, satisfied, a child who had dismantled an envoy's soul between the second cup of tea and the third and found it not worth keeping.

Freya looked across the fire at her strange, unlawful household — a deserter, a widow with knives and maps, a boy who heard the arithmetic beneath politeness, a dying printer somewhere upstairs pretending to sleep — and felt the thing Corvane could never understand, because it did not appear in ledgers and could not be promoted into: that she had already been offered a position, weeks ago, by these people, and had already accepted it, and that no office in any administration would ever outrank it.

But the letter was still sealed. And Corvane would return in three days. And whatever the seal contained, she would have to face it soon — alone, by lamplight, with no protocol left to hide behind.

She slept badly, and dreamed of offices.

* * *

On the second day, Corvane sent a gift.

It arrived in a cart with a driver who would not come inside: two crates of fine northern paper, a case of steel pens, and a jar of honey, with a card in beautiful script. *For the Workshop's continued excellent work. No reply necessary.* The courtesy of it was obscene. He was furnishing them, Freya realized, stocking their shelves the way one stocks a house one intends to own.

Marta carried the crates straight past the press room and out to the coffin-cart, where her husband's maps lay hidden under a false bottom, and began, methodically, to line the cart's interior with fine northern paper. "Waste not," she said, when Freya raised an eyebrow. "He gave it. It's ours. That's how property works until someone with guns explains otherwise."

Odile examined the pens and pronounced them adequate. Tam ate the honey with a spoon, standing guard over the jar, and shared exactly three bites with the rest of them, which by Tam's arithmetic was an act of staggering generosity.

And Freya climbed to the upper room where the lamplight fell on her commission case, and sat with it through the whole of the short grey afternoon, listening to the sounds of her household below — Marta measuring, Odile coughing, Tam counting something under his breath, Ilan's pen scratching at a page he claimed was a letter home to nowhere.

Tomorrow Corvane would return, courteous and patient, and speak to her again in the language of duty, and sixty-one times he would say *we*, and she would have to answer him as something other than a courier.

Which meant tonight, at last, she would have to open the seal, and learn what she had carried across a burning country — and whom she had carried it for.

She struck the match.
