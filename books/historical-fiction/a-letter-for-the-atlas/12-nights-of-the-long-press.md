## 12. Nights of the Long Press

Marta Hale had spent thirty-one years married to a man who talked in his sleep about contour lines, and she had learned from him that every map is an argument — a claim about where things are and who they belong to. So when Odile Ferrant unlocked the cellar of the Atlas Workshop on their third night in the city and showed her the press, Marta understood immediately that this was not machinery.

It was a border crossing.

"Mind the step," Odile said, holding her lamp low. "And mind what you say down here. The floorboards upstairs are older than my grandmother and twice as gossipy."

The cellar was long and low and smelled of ink and tallow and the particular dry sweetness of old paper — a smell that hit Marta somewhere behind the ribs, because it was the smell of her husband's study, the smell of the coffin she slept beside. In the middle of the room sat the press: squat, iron-jawed, magnificent. Along the walls ran cases of type, each compartment labeled in Odile's cramped hand, and above them shelves of paper wrapped against damp like loaves.

"Your husband drew maps," Odile said. It wasn't a question. "I've seen you looking at mine."

"Everyone looks at maps," Marta said. "Most people don't know what they're looking at."

"And you do?"

"I know that your wall map shows three bridges over the Vell that were washed out two springs ago." She let that sit a moment. "I know someone hasn't updated it because updating it would mean admitting the river moved, and the river moving means the surveyors lied, and if the surveyors lied then somebody paid them to."

Odile turned her lamp up. In its light her face looked older and considerably more amused. "I think," she said, "you and I are going to get along."

* * *

The broadsheet itself had been Odile's secret for six weeks before any of them arrived. She confessed it the way other people confess to smuggling brandy — with a shrug and a dare hidden underneath. The first run had been four hundred copies of a single page: the republic's own founding charter, printed whole, no commentary, no editorials. Just the words themselves, set in the largest type she owned, under a title that made Marta laugh out loud the first time she read it:

**REMAINDERS.**

"Because that's all this is," Odile said. "The leftover stock of a going-out-of-business nation. But remaindered books still sell, Mrs. Hale. Remaindered ideas still circulate."

The problem was distribution, and the problem was time, and the problem — though nobody said it plainly in the cellar — was that the general's envoys came each morning with their white flag and their courteous smiles, and each morning Freya went out to meet them wearing her courier's face, and each evening she came back to the Workshop looking like a woman who had been carrying something heavy up a long stair.

So while Freya stalled, the rest of them worked by night.

They fell into it without ever quite agreeing to. That was the strange mercy of the household, Marta thought later: nobody ever voted. Things simply happened the way water finds a channel. Tam hauled paper and learned the typecases by touch, running his fingers along the compartments the way other children ran fingers along fences, chanting the letters' positions under his breath until he could find a capital T blindfolded. He couldn't read a word set in front of him, but he could tell you that the E's lived next door to the F's and that someone had been sloppy with the Q's.

"That one's wrong," he said one night, tapping a locked-up form with total confidence. "There's an upside-down N in there."

"There is not," said Ilan.

Tam waited. Ilan checked. There was an upside-down N.

"How," Marta asked him, genuinely curious, "do you *do* that?"

Tam shrugged, pleased and embarrassed at once. "It's just remembering. Reading's hard. Remembering's easy. Everything remembers itself if you look at it right." Then, after a moment, with the gravity of a boy delivering state secrets: "Don't tell Freya I can't read. She thinks I'm learning. I don't want her to be sad."

"We won't tell her," Marta said. "But you should. Someday."

"Someday's got a lot of errands already," Tam said, and went back to hauling paper.

* * *

Ilan Voss turned out to have a compositor's hands — long, precise, restless — and a memory nearly as greedy as Tam's. He had spent his career recording other people's lives in columns; now he set other people's words in metal, and there was something in his face while he did it, Marta thought, of a man being quietly forgiven for something. He never said what the census ledgers held. He didn't need to. She'd seen how he handled the paper: gently, the way you handle a thing that has been wept on.

His contribution beyond the press, though, was the thing that saved them more than once: names. Ilan knew everyone. Sixteen years of knocking on doors meant that when Odile needed lamp oil without a receipt, Ilan knew which chandler owed the Workshop a grudge from a misprinted handbill. When they needed word of the soldiers' patrol schedules, Ilan produced a baker whose brother-in-law drove supply wagons and who talked the way flour talks — everywhere, softly, settling into everything.

"You collected all these people," Marta said to him over the press one night, "just by asking them questions."

"I counted them," Ilan corrected mildly. "Counting and knowing are closer than people think."

* * *

Freya's part in the nights was smaller than she wanted it to be and larger than she'd admit. She could not help set type — her hands were trained for seals and locks, not sorts — but she could read proofs faster than anyone, catching errors the way a hawk catches mice, and she could recite the charter from memory, which mattered when Odile needed a passage checked against a copy they dared not leave lying open.

She also stood watch at the top of the cellar stairs.

That was the arrangement, unspoken. Every night, once the press began to clatter, Freya took the third stair from the bottom with a candle snuffer in her hand — a snuffer, Marta noted, and not a weapon, which told you everything about how Freya Sandoval's mind worked — and listened to the house. If anyone knocked, she would answer as a tired clerk. If soldiers came, she would come down the stairs slowly, loudly, buying seconds.

"The famous courier," Odile teased her the first night, "guarding us with a candle snuffer."

"It's a very good snuffer," Freya said, without smiling, and Marta had to turn away to hide her grin, because it was a very good snuffer, brass, weighted, probably older than the republic, and Freya had clearly chosen it after actual deliberation.

But it was on the fourth night that Freya gave them the thing none of the rest of them could: she brought down the cipher-case, opened it, and copied out — by lamplight, in her small exact hand — the authentic format of an official republic communiqué. Headings, spacing, the little ritual phrases of authority, the way a genuine order breathed.

"If we're going to print sedition," she said, laying the sheet on the stone floor, "it may as well be well-formatted sedition."

Nobody laughed. They were all thinking the same thing, which was that Freya was handing them the shape of her own leash so they could learn its seams. Marta caught her eye across the cellar and saw that Freya knew she'd been understood, and that she'd done it anyway.

* * *

Marta's own work began, as most of her work did, with a complaint.

"I can't map on these," she said, slapping the stack of proof sheets. "The backs are blank. Do you know what it's like to draw a river route on nothing?"

"Use the wrapping paper," Odile offered.

"Wrapping paper tears, wrinkles, and screams 'contraband' louder than a stolen bell." Marta spread a broadsheet flat on the press bed and smoothed it with both palms. "But nobody searches a newspaper. Nobody even *reads* the back of a newspaper. If I ink the escape routes onto the backs of these — lightly, in the margins, disguised as smudges — then every copy we distribute is also a road out of this city. For whoever needs one."

Odile stopped cranking. "You want to print maps inside the seditious literature."

"I want to print the *seditious literature* inside the maps, technically. The charter takes the front. My routes take the back. A man carrying this sheet carries the law of the republic in one hand and the way north in the other, and if a soldier stops him he sees a farmer's newspaper with ink stains on it, and farmers always have ink stains."

Ilan set his composing stick down. "Which routes?"

"All of them." And here Marta felt the old pleasure rise in her chest, the one her husband used to call her "survey fever," the joy of a blank sheet becoming a world. "The mill road past the tannery — the sentries there change shift at a quarter past the hour and are reliably late. The culvert under the east wall, which is not on any official map because the city engineers pretended it didn't exist after the flood of the year of Two Harvests. The ford, obviously, though the ford will close soon. And the rooftops route through the dyers' quarter, which only works if you know which roofs bear weight, and I know, because I mapped them for a wool merchant eleven years ago who paid me in cloth and gossip."

She drew them over the following nights, working after the press went quiet, by feel almost, her pen whispering over the backs of the sheets. She disguised the roads as decorative flourishes, the landmarks as printer's flowers, the distances as stray marks of measurement that might be anything. A compass rose became a coffee stain. The culvert became a wrinkle in the paper that was actually a wrinkle in the paper — she simply drew it accurately and let everyone assume it was damage.

"Genius or madness," Odile pronounced, examining a finished sheet against the lamp.

"In cartography those are usually the same thing," Marta said. "My husband used to say the difference between a map and a treasure chart is whether you're honest about the sea monsters."

* * *

It was Tam who found the flaw, because Tam found everything, because Tam noticed the way other people noticed weather.

He was sitting cross-legged on the paper bales, ostensibly supervising, actually memorizing — Marta could see his lips moving as he counted the sheets — when he frowned at the broadsheet in his hands.

"This one's different," he said.

"They're all a bit different," Marta said. "Hand-inked."

"No. This one's different-different." He held it out. "The stain's in the wrong corner."

Marta took it. Checked it. Went cold in the pleasant way, the way she always did when the work suddenly got serious. He was right. On this sheet her disguised route marks clustered toward the top-left margin instead of the lower-right, meaning the sheet had been rotated when she drew it, meaning anyone comparing two copies side by side would see that some newspapers had "stains" in matching patterns — and matching patterns are not stains. Matching patterns are *codes*.

"You beautiful, terrifying child," she said.

"Is it bad?"

"It's bad the way finding a hole in a boat is bad — better now than mid-river." She sorted the whole stack that night, Tam calling off the pattern positions from memory faster than she could check them, until every sheet was verified or redrawn. "From now on, you check every copy before it leaves."

"Me?" Tam lit up like a struck match.

"You. You're the quality inspector. It's an important position. There's a title and everything."

"What's the title?"

"Keeper of the Backs."

Tam tried the phrase in his mouth, decided he liked it, and assumed the office with the solemnity of a knighting. From then on no sheet left the cellar without passing under his finger, and Marta privately reckoned their operation had just become safer than anything the ministries upstairs had ever run, because the ministries had never employed a boy who remembered everything and trusted nothing to look right twice.

* * *

On the sixth night, near the small hours, the press jammed.

It was a small thing — a slipped quoins, a form gone loose — but Odile swore in three languages and put her hip against the iron and Marta put her shoulder against Odile, and Ilan held the lantern, and Tam cheered them on with entirely unhelpful advice, and when the press gave a groan and resumed its rhythm all four of them laughed like children, breathless in the lamplight, ink to the wrists.

And Marta looked around the cellar — at Odile with her sleeves rolled and her hair escaping its pins, at Ilan cradling the lantern like something rescued, at Tam asleep now against the paper bales with a composing stick still curled in his fist — and felt the grief move through her, as it always did, but differently tonight. Less like a knife. More like a tide.

Her husband would have loved this room. He would have loved these people. He would have stood exactly where Ilan stood, holding the light so others could work, and he would have said what he always said at moments like this, which was: *Well. Now we know what we're for.*

Four hundred copies of the first new run lay drying on the lines overhead — the charter on the front, her hidden roads on the back, the whole thing breathing faintly of linseed. Tomorrow Ilan's baker would carry bundles out in flour sacks. Tomorrow the words would start walking through the city on their own legs, the way words do, unstoppable, unownable, remaining.

"Mrs. Hale," Odile said, wiping her hands. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just counting," Marta said. "What we can carry. What we can't." She touched one of the drying sheets, feeling the raised bite of the type through the paper, her routes sleeping invisibly on the other side. "We're richer than we look."

Upstairs, the front bell rang.

Once — soft, hesitant, the knock of a neighbor. Twice. Then a third time, harder, and Freya's voice came down the stairwell, stripped of everything warm: "Snuff the lamps. All of you. Now."

In the dark, listening to boots cross the floor above, Marta put her hand on Tam's shoulder and felt him wake without crying out — felt him go silent and still and *counting*, she'd have bet her life on it, counting the footsteps the way he counted everything.

Three sets of boots. Heavy ones. And a fourth tread she recognized, light and unwilling, walking where it was made to walk.

Ilan's voice, when it finally came, was very quiet in the dark.

"That's the captain from the ford."
