## 13. Ilan's Ledger

The trouble with a life spent counting people, Ilan Voss had learned, was that you could never stop doing it.

He counted them now from the window of the Atlas Workshop's upper room: Freya at the press, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to her second knuckle and pretending it didn't delight her. Marta Hale on the floor with the coffin-lid maps spread out like a scandalous picnic, tracing roads with a charcoal stub held between two fingers. Tam crouched beside Odile's type case, mouthing numbers — the boy counted everything too, which Ilan privately considered the finest inheritance he'd ever passed along, though he'd done it by accident and the boy had done it better.

Four people, one dying printer, one boy, one cart of maps hidden in a dead man's coffin. One broadsheet printed in secret through three nights now, its pages drying behind loose bricks like contraband love letters.

And one captain of the general's army who had looked at Ilan Voss across a checkpoint ford six days ago and memorized his face the way Ilan himself once memorized names in a ledger.

Ilan knew he would come back. He had known it since the moment he'd bluffed them across the water — since the moment his old census seal, stamped soft into wax that no longer carried any legal weight anywhere in the republic, had bought passage for a smuggled cart of master maps. He had felt the captain's eyes on him as they rolled away. Not suspicious eyes. *Recording* eyes. The eyes of a man who wrote things down because someone above him might ask later.

The republic had taught them all to do that. It was the last lesson still working.

"You're doing the face," Tam said, appearing at his elbow with the soundlessness of a child who has learned that being noticed is dangerous.

"What face?"

"The counting face." Tam squinted up at him. "You get your mouth all serious but your eyes go around the room like you're adding us up."

"And what do I come to?"

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Four. Plus the lady downstairs who coughs. Plus you." A pause. "Five and a half."

"I'm a whole person, thank you."

"You're older," Tam said, with the merciless honesty of eight years old, "so you count less."

Ilan laughed despite himself, and Tam grinned and ran off toward the stairs, and Ilan stood at the window feeling the laugh drain out of him the way warmth drains from a room when a door opens somewhere below.

Because the boy was right about one thing. Ilan had spent thirty-one years counting people, and he had gotten very good at it, and the counting had a way of turning everything — everyone — into arithmetic. Two hundred and forty souls in the village of Kerrin Marsh, spring census. Ninety-one remaining by autumn, after the fever. Eleven households dissolved. Fourteen orphans dispersed to kin or, where kin could not be found, to institutions whose column headings in his ledger were so blandly administrative that he had sometimes read them aloud just to hear how obscene they sounded in a human voice.

He had told himself for three decades that the ledger was neutral. That a man who only recorded was not responsible for what the recording was used for. That paper did not pull triggers.

Then the general's men had come through his district with copies of his own ledgers in their saddlebags, using his careful columns of household names to decide which villages could be starved into compliance fastest, and Ilan had understood, with the slow sick clarity of a man watching his own handwriting come back to hang him, that there was no such thing as neutral paper. There was only paper, and hands, and what the hands were ordered to do.

So he had walked away from his office with his boots on and his seal in his pocket, and he had told himself that made him different from the men who stayed.

The captain coming up the river road would have opinions about that.

* * *

Freya found him an hour later, sitting on the stairs in the dark the way old men do when they don't want to be found but also, secretly, do.

"You've been quiet all day," she said, settling beside him on the step. She smelled of ink and lamp oil. In the week he'd known her — had it truly been barely a month? It felt like a decade measured in burned villages — he had learned that Freya Sandoval asked questions the way other people drew knives: precisely, and only when necessary.

"Thinking about my ledgers," he said.

"The ones you left behind?"

"The ones that followed me." He turned the census seal over in his palm. The wax impression was cracked down the middle; it had been for weeks. "There's a captain. From the ford. He saw my face, and I saw him see it, and I let myself believe that seeing isn't keeping." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "A man who spent his life writing things down should know better than anyone that seeing is keeping. That's the entire profession."

Freya was quiet a moment. "You think he'll come here."

"I think men like that always come back. They're not cruel, mostly. That's the worst part. He'll be polite. He'll show me my own signature and call it a formality, and he'll mean it kindly, the way a clerk means it kindly, and none of it will matter, because the form is the execution." Ilan looked at her. "I wanted to warn you. Not to frighten you. So that whatever happens in the next day or two, you know it was coming, and you know it isn't the work. Don't let the work stop because of me."

Freya's jaw tightened in a way he recognized. It was the look of a woman being handed bad news who immediately begins rearranging furniture in her head to make room for it.

"How much time?" she said.

"A day. Maybe two."

"Then we finish the run tonight, and Marta finishes the escape routes, and—" She stopped herself. He watched her stop herself. It was, he thought, rather touching, watching a professional courier try very hard not to plan around the removal of a variable. "Ilan—"

"Don't," he said gently. "Don't build me into the plan. I've read enough ledgers to know how variables get handled."

"That's a horrible way to talk about yourself."

"It's an honest way. I've been a line item my whole career. Two hundred forty souls, ninety-one remaining." He smiled at her, and meant it, more or less. "Besides — I owe a debt. Thirty-one years of columns. Every name I entered was a person I reduced to a number, and every number got used by somebody with worse intentions than mine. This—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the press, the broadsheet, the whole improbable conspiracy of a free page — "this is the first entry I've ever made that I'd want read aloud."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, in the voice of a woman who has decided to lose this argument today and win it later, "You will not surrender yourself without telling me first. That's not negotiable. If we're going to handle variables, we handle them as a household."

"A household," Ilan repeated. The word sat strangely warm in his chest. "Is that what we are?"

"We're four people, a half-person, and a dying printer sharing one roof and committing treason together," Freya said. "If that's not a household, the word doesn't exist."

* * *

They finished the run at two in the morning, and it was — Ilan admitted it freely — the happiest work he had ever done.

Tam ferried damp sheets to the lines where Marta had strung them, moving between the adults like a small quick bird, and Odile Ferrant supervised from her chair with her blanket over her knees and her voice thin as onion skin but absolutely sovereign: *Slower on the lever. Wipe the plate again. You there, courier, you're smudging the margins.* And Freya, smudge-handed and unrepentant, pulled each sheet with the concentration of a woman defusing something, while Marta worked her charcoal routes onto the backs of selected pages — a miller's mark here meaning *ford passable*, a church spire there meaning *soldiers billeted, avoid after dark* — until every twentieth sheet in the stack was simultaneously a declaration of a free press and a map home.

Ilan folded. It was all he asked to contribute, and he did it beautifully. Thirty-one years of creasing official documents had given him the sharpest fold in the province, and he took an absurd, undignified pride in it, pressing each corner true while Tam watched over his shoulder and graded him.

"Better," the boy allowed, on sheet sixty.

"High praise."

"Don't let it make you proud."

Too late, Ilan thought. Far, far too late for that.

It was near dawn when he heard the horses.

Not many. Three, maybe four, moving at a walk — the unhurried pace of men with paperwork rather than weapons. He knew that pace. He had traveled at that pace himself for three decades, and some part of him, the part that had been counting things longer than it had been loving them, rose inside him like a man standing to greet an old colleague.

He went downstairs before anyone else woke properly. By the time the knock came — three measured raps, courteous even at dawn — he had the door open, the household shushed behind him, and his old census seal already in his hand.

Captain Drev stood on the step exactly as Ilan remembered him: young-ish, tired-eyed, clean about the collar, holding a leather document case the way soldiers hold things they've been told are important. Behind him, two troopers sat their horses in the grey light with the patience of furniture.

"Census-taker Voss," Drev said. Not a question. "Or should I say — former census-taker Voss. As of the eleventh of the month, you're listed as deserted." He opened the case. "I brought your ledgers."

And he had. Ilan looked down at them and felt the ground tilt slightly under his feet, because there they were — his own ledgers, volumes three through nine, his neat columns, his cramped annotations, the whole careful architecture of his thirty-one years, stacked in an enemy officer's arms like scripture recovered from a heretic's house.

"You went back for these," Ilan said.

"The chancellery requisitioned the district archives." Drev said it mildly. "Your hand was recognized. Your absence was noted. The general finds desertion distasteful — not for sentimental reasons, you understand. It's simply that a man who abandons one set of records may abandon another." His eyes moved past Ilan's shoulder, into the dark of the workshop hallway, and Ilan stepped deliberately sideways to block the view, an old man's body suddenly worth more than it had been in years.

"There's no one here but a widow and her son," Ilan said. The lie came easily, which frightened him a little. "We're sheltering from the road."

"Mm," said Captain Drev, who had memorized faces at a ford and was clearly memorizing this one now. "Well. I regret the hour, and I regret the errand. But the law is the law, even in times like these. *Especially,*" he added, with something that was almost, horribly, sincerity, "*in* times like these. Order is the last thing standing between people and the fire. You of all people must understand that. You kept records."

"I kept records," Ilan agreed quietly. "That's exactly why I understand."

Drev held out a folded warrant. "You'll come with us. There's a hearing at the ford garrison. It will be brief." A pause, and then, in a lower voice, almost apologetic: "Bring nothing. It goes faster."

Behind him, on the stairs, Ilan heard a floorboard creak — Tam, awake, frozen halfway down, those wide eyes taking in the troopers, the ledgers, the warrant, everything, filing it all away in that relentless little counting head. Ilan did not look at him. Looking would confirm. Instead he raised his voice just enough to carry up the stairwell and sound like nothing at all:

"Tell the widow not to wait breakfast."

* * *

He walked out to the horses with his spine straight and his hands empty, and the strangest thing — the thing he would remember afterward, if he was granted any afterward at all — was how light he felt.

Thirty-one years he had carried those ledgers. Not physically; physically they weighed almost nothing, paper and string and board. But he had carried them everywhere nonetheless, into every room, through every checkpoint, into every sleepless night: two hundred forty souls, ninety-one remaining, fourteen orphans, eleven households dissolved. He had carried them the way sinners carry saints' bones, convinced the weight itself was proof of devotion.

Now a soldier carried them, and Ilan's shoulders were bare, and the morning air moved against him like something new.

At the gate he stopped, and Captain Drev, courteous to the end, waited for him to speak.

"There's a woman upstairs," Ilan said. "She's carrying a letter. Official business — sealed commission, courier's authority, the whole liturgy. Whatever you may have heard about this house, whatever your chancellery believes is in this building, that letter is the only thing in it that matters, and it isn't yours, and it won't be the general's, no matter what anyone promises anyone." He met the young man's tired eyes. "Let her deliver it. That's all I'm asking. Not mercy. Just — one entry in your ledger done honestly."

Drev studied him for a long moment. Something flickered across the captain's face that might have been doubt, or might have been the memory of a village he'd once requisitioned grain from, or might simply have been dawn light doing dishonest things to a tired man.

"I'll write down what I find," Drev said finally, which was not yes, and not no, and was the most honest sentence either of them would exchange that morning.

Ilan climbed onto the trooper's spare horse, and did not look back at the workshop — looking back would confirm, confirm, always confirm — but as they rode out through the waking streets he found himself counting anyway, helplessly, faithfully, one last time. Windows. Chimneys. People stirring at doors. A dog. A girl with a bucket.

All present. All accounted for.

And somewhere behind him, in a house full of drying ink, Freya Sandoval was going to open her sealed commission to its final page — he had seen her wanting to, had seen her circling it night after night like a moth around a shut window — and what she found there would decide whether his surrender had bought them a week or a war.

Count it, he told the boy silently, riding north in the smoke-grey light. Count everything. Someone has to.
