## 14. The Question Freya Asks

Freya had been trained, years ago, in a bare room above the Ministry of Correspondence, to read documents the way other people read faces. Master Corvel — a small, dry man who smelled of lamp oil and disappointment — had taught her that every official paper told two stories: the story it wanted you to believe, and the story of its own making. The ink, the paper, the seal, the fold, the hand. *A lie,* he liked to say, tapping his cane against the floorboards, *is a very tiring thing to write. It cannot help but show its seams.*

She had believed him then. She had believed him for eleven years of service, through four ministries and one collapsing republic, because believing him made her work clean and her sleep untroubled and her life a tidy sequence of sealed cases delivered on time.

Now she sat alone in the Workshop's counting room at three in the morning with her commission open before her, and she thought: *Master Corvel, I am going to find every seam.*

* * *

The others slept. Odile had finally gone to bed after coughing herself hoarse over the press, insisting to the last that she was "perfectly well, merely dying slowly, there's a difference." Marta had curled up in the cart among the coffin-maps, which she claimed was the only furniture she'd ever trusted. Tam slept against Ilan's side on the pallet by the stove, and Ilan sat awake beside him, as he always did, keeping his quiet watch over the boy's breathing like a man guarding the last honest number in his ledgers.

"You should sleep," Ilan had said when she passed him.

"I will," she'd lied, badly.

He'd noticed. He always noticed. He'd said nothing, which was its own kind of noticing, and let her go.

The counting room smelled of paper dust and old glue. She lit two lamps, then a third, because what she intended required light the way surgery required knives. She laid out her commission on the long table — the case first, then the outer wrapper, then the letter itself — and arranged her tools around them like an altar: the magnifying lens Odile kept for inspecting type, a jeweler's loupe borrowed from Marta's cart, a sheet of blank Workshop stock for comparison, and a candle stub for holding flame to watermark.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud made it real. "Let's have it out between us."

The commission stared back, smug in its seals. The republic's great seal in red wax, unbroken. The chancellery countersign beneath it in violet. The hand of the Undersecretary of State, crisp and correct, instructing the bearer — Freya Sandoval, Courier First Class, eleven years of blameless service — to proceed to the Atlas Workshop and deliver the enclosed instrument into the custody of its master, and thereafter to render assistance to *such parties as may present themselves bearing the general's mark of safe conduct.*

She had read those words a dozen times since decoding the fragment. She had read them the way one reads a diagnosis — repeatedly, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

Tonight she would stop reading and start interrogating.

She began with the paper. This was the oldest trick in Master Corvel's thin book: government stock was government stock, milled at the state mill at Verren with a running-water mark of the republic's wheel. She held the first page up to the candle.

There was the wheel. She felt something in her chest loosen half a degree.

Then she held the second page up, and the wheel was there too, but it was *wrong.* Slightly wrong. The spokes were too even. The Verren wheel, she knew from years of handling, had a small irregularity in the lower rim — a flaw in the mould, famous among couriers, the kind of detail only a forger or a very bored apprentice would notice. She had once written three pages to the Ministry about a provincial clerk whose requisition forms bore a perfect wheel, and had received back a commendation and a note reading *we do not pay you to be interesting.*

This wheel was perfect. Both pages. Flawless, evenly spaced, identical down to the last fiber.

State paper didn't come in pairs that identical. The mill's marks drifted, page to page, batch to batch, like handwriting. These pages matched the way twins match when they've been copied from the same tracing.

Freya set the page down very carefully, as though it might bite, and discovered her hands were not entirely steady.

"Well," she said. "That's one."

* * *

The ink came next. Official correspondence used the ministry blend — iron gall with a specific blue-black depth that browned at the edges over months, a slow chemical confession of age. Her commission's ink was fresh, of course; the orders were weeks old at most. But fresh iron gall had a particular wet blackness, a bloom under lamplight that could not quite be faked, and this ink —

This ink was *too* good. Too uniform. No pooling where the pen had paused, no thinning where the hand had hurried. Every stroke of the Undersecretary's famously cramped script flowed with the serenity of something written slowly, deliberately, perhaps several times until it was right.

And here was the thing that made her sit back and laugh — actually laugh, a short ugly sound in the empty room — she recognized the hand. Not the Undersecretary's hand. The *imitation* of it. Because she had spent eleven years reading genuine Undersecretary letters, and the man made a small, consistent error: he crossed his double-t's with a single long stroke that drifted upward, lazy as a cat stretching. Everyone who dealt with the Chancellery knew it. It was in every courier's mental file, filed under *useful trivia, never expected to matter.*

Her commission's double-t's were crossed neatly. Individually. Correctly.

The forger had been too good a copyist. He had fixed the flaw.

"Oh, you poor careful monster," she murmured to the page. "You studied him so hard you made him better than himself."

Two seams. She was almost enjoying herself now, which frightened her more than despair had. There was something in the work itself — the close attention, the puzzle, the slow peeling back — that felt like coming home, and the homecoming was horrifying, because the house she'd returned to was built entirely of lies and she had lived in it happily for a decade.

* * *

The wax took longest, because wax forgery was genuinely difficult and whoever had made this seal had been a master of it. The republic's seal matrix was supposed to exist in exactly three copies: the Chancellery, the Ministry of State, and the archive vault. Three matrices, each with a microscopic identifying nick — Corvel had made her memorize their positions until she dreamed of them.

Under the loupe, the seal on her commission showed the archive vault's nick, precisely placed, precisely deep.

But the wax was wrong. Republic wax carried a grain, a faint swirl from the melting ladle. This wax pooled in the seal's recesses with a glassy evenness, and at its edge, where it met the paper, it had shrunk back a hair's breadth in cooling — a shrinkage that happened when wax was poured into a *mould of the seal*, not stamped by the seal itself. Someone had taken an impression, cast a duplicate matrix, and pressed fresh wax with it.

Which meant someone had access to an impression of the archive seal. Which meant someone inside the archive vault, or someone who had *been* inside it.

The general's chancellery had grown fat on defectors. She thought of Ilan at the checkpoint, asking travelers their allegiance instead of their age, and wondered how many clerks and archivists and seal-keepers had walked south over the past year carrying nothing but their expertise and their reasons.

Three seams. Paper, ink, wax. Any one of them deniable. Together they were a verdict.

But Freya was thorough the way some people are devout, and there was one test left, the one Corvel had called "the question behind all questions": not *is the document false,* but *where did it come from?* Forgeries left fingerprints of habit — phrasing, formatting, the little bureaucratic dialects that grew up inside individual offices like family accents.

She read the commission through again, slowly, aloud, listening with her whole body.

*"...thereafter to render assistance to such parties as may present themselves bearing the general's mark of safe conduct."*

There. And there. *Such parties as may present themselves.* She turned pages, checked her memory of a hundred genuine commissions. The republic's Chancellery wrote *officers duly accredited.* It wrote *envoys bearing proper credentials.* It was fussy, old-fashioned, allergic to vagueness — because vague language created disputes, and disputes created paperwork, and paperwork was the republic's true religion.

*Such parties as may present themselves* was not Chancellery dialect. It was military staff dialect — the language of men who expected obedience and found precision tedious. She had heard it in the mouths of garrison commanders. She had heard it, most recently and most chillingly, from the general's courteous envoys in the Workshop parlor, smiling over their tea: *our people will call upon you; we trust you'll extend every accommodation to such parties as present themselves.*

The phrase had crawled up her spine that day without her knowing why. Now she knew why. It was the same sentence. The same hand behind the hand behind the hand.

She put down the commission. She stood up. She walked to the window, opened it, and breathed the cold river air for a long time, because if she did not put cold air into her lungs she was going to break something, possibly the table, possibly the press downstairs, possibly herself.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a ledger line.

The order was forged. The signature was false. The instructions — surrender the plates, open the vaults, hand the republic's memory to the general — originated not in any ministry but in the chancellery of the man marching north to swallow the country whole. She had not been carrying the republic's last word. She had been carrying the general's first word, dressed in the republic's clothes, delivered across half a continent by the most obedient courier the Ministry had ever certified.

They hadn't even needed to corrupt her. That was the elegant part, the part that made rage rise in her throat like bile. They had simply *used* her — used her honesty, her discipline, her eleven years of blamelessness — as the vehicle. A courier with a flawless record and genuine papers passes every checkpoint. A courier with doubts gets searched. So they gave her no doubts. They gave her a clean case and a straight road and let her own virtue carry the knife.

Every burned village she'd walked through. Every checkpoint. Ilan risking his neck at the ford. Marta sleeping in a cart with her husband's coffin. Tam memorizing numbers he couldn't read, guarding fragments of a lie because the adults told him it mattered.

*I carried it for them,* she thought. *I carried the surrender through all of it, and I would have delivered it, and I would have slept well after.*

She gripped the windowsill until the wood bit her palms.

And underneath the rage — this was the shameful part, the part she would examine later, or never — underneath it, unmistakable, warm and terrible as brandy: relief. Liberation. The leash had snapped, and the strange thing was that it didn't feel like falling. It felt like standing up straight for the first time in years.

Because duty, it turned out, had never been hers. It had been *assigned.* Signed by a forger, sealed in cast wax, addressed to a woman who existed mostly as a function. And a function could be repurposed by anyone clever enough to hold the pen.

But a person — a person could choose.

Master Corvel's voice, dry as August grass: *A lie is a very tiring thing to write. It cannot help but show its seams.* He had meant it as armor for the republic's documents. He had never imagined his best student turning the lens around, holding it up to the republic itself, finding the flaws in the wheel.

"The wheel was flawed all along," she said softly to the dark street below. "I just thought the flaw was mine."

* * *

She did not go back to bed. She went back to the table, and she did something she had never once done in eleven years of service.

She picked up a pen and began, in her own neat courier's hand, to draft — not a report, not a delivery receipt, not a protest for filing in triplicate — but a plan.

If the commission was forged, then its authority was a costume anyone might wear. If the cipher it traveled in was genuine — and it was, the cipher was old and true, older than the general, older than the war — then the cipher did not belong to the liars. Instruments don't choose their players. The case had carried a lie; it could just as easily carry the truth. Or, her pen hesitated over the word and then committed to it, *a better lie — one told for love instead of power.*

She thought of Odile's forbidden broadsheet drying upstairs. Of Marta mapping escape routes in mapmaker's shorthand on the backs of free words. Of Tam, asleep, holding in his unfallen head more of the cipher than any codebook, reciting numbers like prayers. Of Ilan, who had spent his career counting the dead and had finally decided to count for the living.

They had everything a new letter needed. Everything except the one thing only she could supply: the authority. The signature. The dying voice of the republic, speaking one last time — and speaking *true*, whatever the ink's pedigree, because for once the words and the wish would match.

It would be forgery. She knew that. She had spent her whole life prosecuting the thing she now contemplated, and she let herself look at that squarely, no flinching, the way she'd looked at the wax.

Then she wrote beneath her notes, small and deliberate, the way one signs: *The republic is not the seal. The republic is the people who still believe it owes them truth. I am still its courier. I have simply finally been given honest orders.*

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, in the sleeping Workshop, the great press ticked as its iron cooled, patient as a heart.

And then — because the world has a sense of timing like a bad joke — she heard boots in the street outside. Measured. Courteous. Stopping at the door.

Through the window, in the gray before dawn, she watched a rider dismount beneath the Workshop's sign, smooth his coat, and knock — politely, patiently, the way powerful men knock when they are certain the door will open.

He wore the general's mark of safe conduct.

*Such parties as may present themselves,* the forged words said in her head, and Freya Sandoval, Courier First Class, freed woman, forger-to-be, closed her notebook, tucked the true-false commission into her coat, and went down to answer the door with her face arranged into eleven years of blameless service.

*Let them present themselves,* she thought, hand on the latch. *I have a few questions of my own.*
