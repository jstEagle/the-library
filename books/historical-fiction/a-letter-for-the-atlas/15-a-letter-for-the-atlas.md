## 15. A Letter for the Atlas

**Freya**

The lamp in Odile's workroom had a cracked chimney that made the flame stutter every few minutes, and Freya found she could not stop watching it. Each flicker looked like a small argument between the dark and the light, and each time the light won, but only just.

She had spread her commission across the composing stone an hour ago. The seal was broken now — properly broken, with a courier's knife, the way it should have been done on the first night if she had been braver or less obedient — and the decoded fragments lay beside it in Tam's careful hand: not written, of course, because Tam could not write, but recited to Ilan, who had copied them out in his census-taker's script, neat as gravestones.

"It's a strange thing," Freya said, "to spend your whole life learning to carry orders, and then discover the heaviest part was never the case."

Odile didn't look up from the type drawer. Her hands moved through the little metal compartments the way other people's hands moved through prayer. "It's a strange thing," she said, "to spend your whole life printing other people's lies and calling it craft. Sit down, Courier. You're making the room nervous."

Freya sat.

The plan had been made in pieces over three nights, the way all honest plans are made — reluctantly, in whispers, with everyone pretending at first that they were only talking. A letter in the true old cipher. The cipher the republic had actually used before the chancellery started cutting corners, before the forged signature, before the general's men learned to forge it too. A letter declaring the Atlas Workshop's plates state property of no one. Ordering their dispersal. To the people. All of it signed with the dying authority of a republic that had, in its last honest hour, perhaps thirty years ago, deserved such a letter.

It was forgery. It was treason. It was, Freya thought, watching the lamp argue with the dark, the first true order she had ever carried.

* * *

**Tam**

Tam liked the numbers best at night, when they came out of his head in the right order like sheep going through a gate.

The lady printer had asked him to say them slow, and he did, because saying them slow was harder than saying them fast and he liked doing hard things when people watched. The numbers sat in his head in rows, the way the census man's ledger pages must look, except Tam's rows were all in his skull where no soldier could burn them.

"Again," said the lady printer, whose name was Odile, which was a good name, like a door closing softly.

He said them again. Freya — who had a face like a locked box that somebody loved very much — wrote down the letters the numbers turned into. Tam didn't know how numbers became letters. He'd asked once and Freya had tried to explain, drawing little squares and arrows on scrap paper until Marta laughed and said, "You explain ciphers the way my husband explained tides, dear — accurately and uselessly."

But Tam knew what the letters were *for*, because Ilan had told him straight, the way Ilan always told him things straight, even bad ones. The letters were going to be a letter. The letter would tell everyone the maps and the plates belonged to nobody, which meant everybody, which meant — Tam worked this out himself, in the dark of the loft where he slept — it meant they belonged to him too. To a boy who couldn't read. That was the best kind of belonging. The kind you didn't have to pass a test for.

"Last row," Odile said.

Tam gave her the last row. It came out clean. He felt the small hot pride he always felt, and this time he let it show, because Freya looked up from her writing and smiled at him — really smiled, not the polite kind — and pride that gets smiled at is allowed to exist.

"That's all of it," Tam said. "All the pieces I heard through the case."

"You understand," Freya said slowly, "that what you memorized was the surrender order. And we're using its bones to build something else entirely."

Tam considered this. "Like when the butcher shop burned down," he said, "and the smith used the wall to build his new shed."

There was a silence. Then Marta, who had come in with a tray of bread and was pretending she wasn't listening, said, "Out of the mouths of babes and orphans," and Odile snorted, and Freya laughed in a way Tam had only heard twice before and both times had treasured it like a coin.

* * *

**Odile**

Fifty-one years Odile Ferrant had stood at a press, and in fifty-one years she had set type for coronations, capitulations, tax notices, war declarations, three separate constitutions, and one wedding announcement so full of lies that she had charged double out of spite. She knew type the way a priest knows scripture, and she knew this also: type does not care what it says. Type is the most honest material in the world and the least. It will declare peace or massacre with the same beautiful clarity. The sin or the grace lives entirely in the hands that set it.

Tonight her hands shook. Not from age. From the opposite of age.

"The old cipher has a flaw," she said, working as she talked, because her mouth and her fingers had long ago agreed to share the labor. "It's why they abandoned it. Every letter of the key appears in the body if the message runs long enough. Anyone patient can read it."

"Then anyone patient can read ours," Freya said.

"Exactly." Odile slid a line of type into the stick, upside-down and backward, letters facing the way letters face when they mean business. "We're not hiding this letter, Courier. We're publishing it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything I have left."

Marta had gone down to the cellar to fetch the second ream of broadsheet paper, and her footsteps on the stairs had a rhythm Odile had learned to read the way she read proofs: steady, steady, pause, steady. Grief walks like that. So does courage. They share a stride.

When Marta came up, she set the paper down and studied the composing stone, where the forged commission lay open next to the genuine seal matrix — the republic's own, borrowed from the dead ministry's stores by means Odile had decided not to ask about.

"You'll sign it 'by authority of the Provisional Secretariat,'" Marta said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'll sign it," Freya said, "the way a daughter signs a dead mother's name on a letter to keep the house from being sold. It's still a lie."

"It's still a signature," Marta said. "Ink doesn't grieve. Ink works." She touched the coffin-cart's blanket, still draped over the bench where she always kept it within reach. "I spent six months learning that."

Odile pulled the first proof. The impression came up sharp and black, the old cipher's dense blocks of figures marching across the page like an army that had changed sides. She held it to the lamp.

"Well," she said, and her voice did something embarrassing that she chose not to examine. "Look at that. Fifty-one years, and my finest work is a crime."

* * *

**Freya**

They finished near midnight, and then came the part Freya had been dreading, which was not the forging but the feeling.

The letter needed a signature. Not merely ink — authority. A courier's countersignature, the little constellation of marks she had been trained since girlhood to make, the marks that said *this document passed through honest hands.* Her hands had been honest. That was the unbearable joke of it. Everything else had been rotten, but her hands had stayed clean precisely so they could one day be used for this.

She dipped the pen.

"Say something," Marta suggested gently. "People always say something when they sign. My husband hummed."

"He's signing a forgery that commits high treason against a government that no longer exists," Odile said dryly, "on behalf of a secretariat that never existed. Perhaps a hymn is excessive."

Freya thought of what to say. She thought of the capital's final week, the ministries emptying like lungs. She thought of Ilan at the checkpoint asking travelers their allegiance instead of their age — *one question too many,* and how she had lied badly and been saved by it. She thought of the coffin with maps inside it, of a boy counting sheep-gates of numbers in the dark. She thought of the words she had decoded alone at night, the ones that had unmade her: *surrender the plates to the envoys of the General.*

"No," she said aloud, answering the room though no one had pressed her. "I won't say anything. I'll just do it properly."

She signed. The countersignature came out flawless, and she hated it and loved it, the way you hate and love your own handwriting on a letter to someone who has died.

"There," Odile said quietly. "The republic's last honest order. Issued forty years late and by the wrong government entirely."

"The timing," Freya said, blotting the page, "is the most republican thing about it."

Tam, who had fallen asleep sitting up against the paper reams sometime around the second proof, woke at the sound of laughter — hers, she realized; she was laughing, cracked and quiet — and asked blearily, "Did it work? Is it a real letter now?"

Freya looked at the page. Black cipher, white margins, a dead nation's seal, a living woman's hand.

"It's real," she said, "because we're going to act like it is. That's the only kind of real there's ever been."

* * *

**Tam**

They let Tam carry the letter to the drying rack, which was an honor, and he carried it flat on both palms like a bird that might startle.

While it dried he counted the hooks on the rack, because counting was what his mind did instead of sleeping, and he got to forty before the door opened downstairs — hard, fast footsteps, more than one person, and then Marta's voice going strange and flat in the way grown-up voices went flat when the news was bad.

Tam looked at Freya. Freya was already moving.

They came down together into the front room, where a mud-spattered rider — one of Marta's river-people, a boy hardly older than Tam — stood dripping by the stove with his cap wrung in his fists.

"They've taken him to the ford," the rider said. "The census man. The captain recognized him off the ledgers, and they've kept him under guard at the ford mill, and there's word posted—" He swallowed. "Word's gone out. Firing squad at dawn."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs the lamp with the cracked chimney guttered and steadied, argued and held.

Freya stood very still, and Tam watched her face do the thing locked boxes do when someone finally finds the key — nothing visible at all, and everything underneath.

"Dawn," she said.

"Dawn," said the rider.

And Tam, holding his count at forty, understood without being able to read a single word of it that the letter in his hands had become a race.
