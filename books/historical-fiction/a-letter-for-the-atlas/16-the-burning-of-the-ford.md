## 16. The Burning of the Ford

Marta Hale had spent thirty years making maps, and in all that time she had learned one truth above the others: a map is a promise that the world will hold still long enough for you to cross it.

The world had stopped holding still some months ago.

She heard the news before dawn, from a fishwife who came to the Workshop's side door selling eels and gossip in equal measure, and who had learned that Marta paid better for the second than the first.

"They've taken the census man," the fishwife said, hefting her basket of silver-scaled bodies. "The one they caught at the ford last week. The captain's been asking after him in every town since — you know the one, tall, talks like a tax form." She shrugged. "They're going to hang him at the ford at dawn tomorrow. Make an example. They always make examples at fords. Something about water and crowds."

Marta paid her for the eels, overpaid her for the gossip, and stood in the doorway for a long moment with the smell of river mud rising off the basket and into her lungs, thinking: *Ilan. Oh, Ilan, you brave, foolish, ledger-keeping man.*

Then she went inside, woke no one, and began to draw.

* * *

By the time the others stumbled down to the press room — Odile first, moving carefully around her bad hip; then Tam, hair like a startled crow's nest; then Freya, already dressed, already watchful, because Freya slept the way cats do, in shifts and with one ear open — Marta had three maps spread across the great flat table where the broadsheets were collated, weighted down at the corners with type cases and an ink stone and, for reasons known only to him, Tam's left boot.

"What's happened?" Freya said.

"Ilan is going to be executed," Marta said. "At the ford. Tomorrow at dawn."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the wind, and Tam flinched, and Odile put a hand on his shoulder without looking away from the maps.

"They took him days ago," Freya said. Her voice was level, but Marta, who had raised two sons and buried one husband and outlasted a war by being very good at reading people the way other people read weather, heard the crack running underneath it. "Why are we only hearing now?"

"Because the general's men don't announce their prisoners until they're ready to spend them," Marta said. "It keeps the price up." She pulled the candle closer. "Now. Sit down, all of you, because I have spent the night doing arithmetic with roads, and the answer is ugly, and I'd rather we were all sitting down when I say it."

They sat. Even Tam, though he sat on his boot, which Marta chose not to correct, because correcting it would mean admitting she'd noticed, and noticing things was not what she wanted on her face right now.

"Three routes out of this city once the letter is delivered," she said, tapping the first map. "You all know them; I drew them on the backs of forty broadsheets while you were all being noble and inky. Route one, north along the river road with the plates in the coffin-cart. Slow, but the cart looks like grief, and nobody searches grief. Route two, east through the marshes — fast if you know the paths, fatal if you don't, and I know the paths, so it's merely unpleasant. Route three, south over the ridge, which is the longest way around everything and therefore the safest, and takes four days."

"Good," Freya said. "We take—"

"All three require the whole night," Marta said, "and here is the part where you sit down properly, Freya, because I watched your knee start bouncing and it's going to distract everyone."

Freya sat down properly.

"The ford is two hours west," Marta said. "Dawn execution. To get there, halt it — and yes, Freya, I can see you're already composing your speech about courier's authority, save it, I believe you, I've believed you since the coffin — to get there, halt it, and get back with Ilan alive, costs us the whole night. Every hour of it. Which means—" she laid her palm flat on the maps, covering all three routes at once "—the plates don't move. Not tonight. Not before the general's men come for the Workshop, which they will do the morning after the execution, because executions are how men like that clear their throats."

"And if we move the plates," Odile said quietly, "we cannot reach the ford in time."

"And if we deliver the letter," Tam said slowly — and Marta marveled, as she did every day, at the boy, who could not read a word but could hear the shape of an argument the way other children heard songs, "then we choose between Ilan and the plates anyway."

"Yes," Marta said. "That's the arithmetic. One night. Three things worth saving. Pick two."

Silence. The good kind lasted about four seconds. Then it stopped being the good kind.

"We deliver the letter," Freya said immediately, "and we save Ilan. The plates—"

"The plates are the whole point of the letter!" Marta snapped. "A letter ordering the dispersal of plates that sit in ashes! Forgive me, but I didn't bury my husband's life's work under a corpse so we could abandon it the week it finally mattered!"

"Marta." Freya's voice went soft in that way that made Marta want to throw something. "He is going to *die.*"

"People die. Maps survive. That's rather the entire principle of my profession."

"Oh, that's—" Freya stood up. "That is the coldest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I was raised by ministry clerks."

"It's the truest thing anyone has ever said to you, which is why it feels cold. Truth does that. It's why I married a mapmaker instead of a poet."

"You *are* a mapmaker."

"I contain multitudes, and none of them are patient right now."

Tam had gone small and still in the corner, the way children go when the grown-ups' voices climb past some private threshold, and Odile saw it before either of the arguing women did.

"Ladies," the old printer said, in the voice she presumably used on apprentices who had just mixed lead into the ink, "you are shouting in front of my type."

Both women stopped. Marta took a breath that felt like swallowing a hinge.

"I'm sorry," she said. It cost her. "I'm sorry, Tam. And Freya. But someone at this table has to say the unforgivable thing out loud, and apparently tonight it's me. We cannot save all three. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a map of a country that doesn't exist, and I am the only licensed seller of fictional countries in this room."

Freya stared at the maps. Marta watched her do it — watched her look at the routes the way she'd once, on the road, looked at a burned village: with the particular horror of a person whose entire self was built on delivering things, confronting the fact that tonight she could only deliver one.

"There has to be another way," Freya said.

"There isn't."

"There's *always* another way. That's what the general's people believe, that force closes every gap. If we start believing there's only one road—"

"There are three roads," Marta said wearily. "I counted. Twice. I'm very good at counting."

"I know you are." Freya pressed both hands flat on the table. "And I know you drew these to help us. And I know you loved him too."

That landed somewhere Marta hadn't braced for. She looked down at her hands, ink-stained, widow's hands, hands that had sewn master maps into a coffin lining while telling herself it was practicality and knowing it was love.

"I did," she admitted. "I do. He asked me once, on the road, whether I thought paperwork could be honest. I told him no, and he laughed, and he said, 'Good, then I'll have to be the exception.' And now they're going to hang my exception off a bridge beam as a lesson to the countryside, and I am supposed to weigh him against paper." Her voice did something she hated. "I have been weighing things against paper my whole life, Freya. My husband weighed his maps above our safety and it got him killed, and I loved him for it, and I swore I would never be the one holding the scales."

"So what do we do?"

"I don't know. That's why I called the meeting. I draw the choices. I don't make them. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement."

* * *

They argued until the candle burned down to a thumb's width. Odile argued for the plates, in the flat, unanswerable tone of a woman who had spent fifty years watching ideas die and presses survive. Tam argued for Ilan — not eloquently, just stubbornly, saying "he taught me the nine-times trick" and "he carried me when my foot was bad" and "he counts us, Marta, he counts all of us, in his head, I've seen him do it, like we're his ledger" until Marta had to leave the room for a moment on the pretext of checking the door bar.

Freya argued for everything, which meant nothing, which meant she was drowning, and Marta recognized it because she herself had drowned exactly that way for a year after Aldous died — reaching for every option at once and thereby grasping none.

It was past midnight when the knock came at the side door. Three slow raps. The fishwife's rhythm.

Marta let her in. The woman was breathless, basketless, and gray-faced, and she grabbed Marta's sleeve with wet cold fingers.

"He escaped," she gasped. "The census man. Tonight — an hour ago — knocked out his guard with a chamber pot, they're saying, or a Bible, accounts differ, and ran. They've got dogs after him. He'll be caught by morning unless—" she caught her breath "—unless somebody knows the country better than the dogs do."

Marta turned around slowly. Freya was already on her feet. Tam was grinning like a lantern.

"Well," Marta said, and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time in hours, even as her mind was already laying new lines across the dark — the marsh path, the eel-cutter's landing, the ford at low water. "Isn't that just like a census taker. Refusing to stay counted."

"Can we reach him?" Freya asked.

Marta looked at her maps — all three routes, one night, and now a fourth line, a thin desperate thread through the reeds — and heard herself say, in the voice of a woman who had once promised never to hold the scales:

"Get the coffin-cart. We're going to need somewhere to hide him, and nothing hides a living man better than a box meant for a dead one."

She was already rolling up the maps when it struck her, with the cold clarity of a surveyor sighting a drop-off, that the plates were still sitting in the Workshop behind them — and that dawn was coming regardless.

"Someone," she said quietly, "stay with the press."
