## 17. Dawn at the Ford

Freya had rehearsed the walk a hundred times in the dark, and every rehearsal ended differently.

In some versions her hands shook. In others she spoke and no sound came out. In one particularly honest version, delivered somewhere around the third hour of a sleepless night, the captain at the ford simply laughed at her — laughed at the courier's sash over a printer's apron, at the letter sealed in wax that Odile had melted from candle stubs and Marta had pressed flat between the pages of an atlas until it looked older than it was — and put a bullet through Ilan Voss before Freya got past his first sentence.

But dawn did not care for rehearsals. Dawn came anyway, gray and cold, smelling of river water and woodsmoke, and Freya walked toward the ford because there was nothing else in the world to do that mattered.

The soldiers had strung lanterns along the near bank, and their light lay on the water in broken yellow coins. A crowd had gathered — not many, maybe forty souls from the ruined villages nearby, come to watch the census taker die, because when a country is falling apart people will travel a long way for any kind of certainty, even the certainty of an execution. They stood in silence. That was the worst part. No jeering, no weeping. Just the quiet patience of people who had learned that whatever happened next would happen whether they watched or not.

Ilan stood on the low stone platform the engineers used when measuring the ford's depth. His hands were bound in front of him, which was unnecessary — he was not going anywhere, and everyone knew it — but the general's men believed in procedure the way other men believed in God, and procedure said bind him. He looked smaller than Freya remembered. Or perhaps he simply looked like what he was: a thin, gray man who had spent twenty years writing down the names of the dead, and had finally been counted among them.

He saw her before the soldiers did. She saw him see her — saw the flicker of something that was not hope exactly, because Ilan Voss did not deal in hope, but was perhaps its poorer cousin, its ledger-entry cousin. *Item: one courier, arriving late. Item: reason unknown.*

Freya lifted her chin and kept walking.

The captain from the checkpoint stood at the platform's edge, arms crossed. He recognized her too. She watched recognition arrive in stages on his face: first *a woman*, then *a woman in a courier's sash*, then — with the slow unpleasant pleasure of a man finding money in an old coat — *the woman from the ford, with the coffin*.

"Courier," he said. "You're a long way from your route."

"I'm precisely on my route." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "And you're standing on it."

Behind her, back up the road where the mist still clung, Marta waited with the cart. Freya could not see it through the fog, and that was the point. The plan lived or died on what the fog concealed, and on whether a widow with a coffin and a boy who could not read could do, in ten minutes, what an army spent years learning: move something heavy past men with guns without being noticed.

She handed the captain the letter.

He took it the way soldiers took all documents, turning it over twice, frowning at the outer seal. It was good wax. Odile had spent an entire evening on it, cursing softly in three languages, pressing the republic's dying crest into it with a seal carved from a broken piece of type. To a captain's eye it would read as official. To a chancellery clerk's eye it might not survive a minute. But chancellery clerks were fifty miles east, and captains were here, and here was where the dawn was happening.

"Orders," the captain said. Not a question. A test.

"Orders," Freya agreed.

"For the prisoner?"

"For everything at this ford." She let a beat pass — Ilan had taught her that; paperwork spoke loudest in silences — and then, in the bored tone of a woman who had delivered a hundred such letters to a hundred such officers and found most of them wanting: "Read it, Captain. Or shall I read it to you? I know how long you've been awake. I can hear it."

His jaw tightened. He broke the seal.

The fog moved behind him. Somewhere out on the road, a wheel creaked — once, softly, the particular complaint of Marta Hale's coffin-cart under load, a sound Freya knew better than her own heartbeat by now. The captain did not turn. The soldiers did not turn. Forty villagers turned, all together, the way a field of grass turns when wind passes through it, and then turned back, having seen nothing worth seeing.

Freya held her breath and recited the letter in her head as the captain read it aloud, silently, with his lips moving.

*Ity order of the Chancellery of the Provisional Authority, all persons detained at the crossing known as Miller's Ford are remanded to the custody of the bearer, who carries full authority of transit and disposal. The prisoner Ilan Voss is to be released into the bearer's charge for deposition regarding census irregularities in the eastern districts. All plates, presses, and materials of state recovered in the region are likewise subject to the bearer's authority.*

It was beautiful. Tam had recited the cipher fragments for six nights running while Odile set the true text beneath them, and the boy had gotten every number right, and the numbers had become words, and the words had become this: a lie wearing the republic's uniform, signed with the hand of a government that no longer existed, ordering the release of a man whose only crime was asking one question too many.

"Deposition," the captain said slowly.

"The eastern districts burned before anyone could count them properly," Freya said. "There are eleven thousand unregistered dead, Captain. Someone has to account for them. The chancellery prefers it be the man who made the ledgers." She let that settle. Eleven thousand was a number she had invented on the third sleepless night, and it had the great virtue of being both plausible and horrifying enough that no man wanted to think about it too closely. "Unless you'd prefer to explain to the chancellery why their auditor died at a ford with his work unfinished."

The captain looked at the letter again. Looked at Ilan. Looked at the crowd of villagers, who were watching him now with the particular attention of people memorizing his face — and Freya saw him realize it, saw the exact moment he understood that however this morning went, these forty witnesses would carry it home to forty kitchens, and the story they told would have him in it.

Procedure, it turned out, had a cousin too. Its name was self-preservation.

"Cut him loose," the captain said.

*

Marta heard the order from thirty yards away, muffled by fog, and nearly sat down in the road with relief.

She didn't, of course. There was no sitting down today. Today the cart weighed four hundred pounds more than it had any right to, and every pound of the difference was engraved copper, wrapped in oilcloth and grave-linen and the false bottom of her husband's coffin, resting on top of the actual occupant — which was, at present, nothing but the maps' old hiding place and a good deal of straw, because they had buried the coffin empty months ago and Marta saw no reason to change the arrangement now. Aldous Hale had carried secrets in death as faithfully as he had in life. He could manage one more mile.

"Hold," Tam whispered beside her. He was walking the cart's near side, one small hand on the wheel, keeping pace, keeping count. He always kept count now — steps, breaths, soldiers, seconds — because counting was how he read the world, and today the world needed reading very carefully.

"I'm holding," Marta murmured.

"There's nine of them at the water. Two by the lanterns. One asleep against the post, I counted his breathing, he's asleep."

"You counted a sleeping man's breathing."

"He snores in fours," Tam said, with the mild dignity of a scholar citing sources. "One-two-three-snore. It's very regular."

Marta almost laughed, which would have been fatal, so she converted it into a cough and kept walking. This was the thing about the boy that nobody had planned for and everybody depended on: he noticed everything, filed everything, and reported it in the tone of a weather forecast. Somewhere in the last month he had stopped being a child they sheltered and become a member of staff. She suspected none of them would ever be able to say exactly when.

They came down onto the ford road through the thickest ribbon of fog, cart wheels muffled with rags, the horse — old Bess, borrowed, patient, deserving of a statue — moving at the unhurried amble of a beast hauling a coffin, because nothing on earth attracted less attention than grief. Two soldiers by the lanterns glanced up. Saw a widow. Saw a coffin. Saw a boy. Looked away. Men who had been awake all night guarding an execution had no appetite for looking at coffins; it felt like tempting something.

Only one stopped them. A young one, barely shaving, with the anxious eyes of a boy who had joined the general's army for bread and was only now doing the arithmetic on what else came with it.

"Papers?" he asked, and hated himself for it, Marta could tell. He looked at the coffin and then quickly away, the way young men look away from graves.

"Widow's pass," Marta said, and handed it over. It was genuine — the one document in her possession that had never lied about anything except the location of her heart. The soldier read it, though Marta doubted he could read much, and studied her face, and found there exactly what she showed him: a tired woman in black, eyes red-rimmed, hands folded on the cart's rail above her husband's box.

"My ma died in the spring," the soldier said suddenly, quietly, handing the pass back. "Fever. Nobody could get her to a proper burying ground."

"Then you know," Marta said gently, "that she wouldn't want me kept waiting in the cold."

"No, miss." He stepped aside. Then, as the cart rolled past, so low that only she and Tam could hear: "The ford road forks past the willow. Left goes to the marsh track. Soldiers don't use it. Mud's bad but it goes north."

Marta met his eyes. He flushed and stared at his boots.

"Thank you," she said. "When this is all over — if it's ever over — find the northern print shops. Ask for the broadsheet press. Tell them the widow sent you."

He nodded once, sharply, like a man accepting a commission, and went back to his lanterns, and Marta rolled on through the fog with her husband's maps and a republic's stolen future rattling gently behind her, and thought: *Aldous, you would not believe the company I keep now.* And then, because she knew him, she corrected it: *Yes, you would. You'd have fed them all by now.*

*

By the time Marta reached the far side of the ford, the sun had found a hole in the clouds and dropped one bar of gold across the water, and Freya was walking toward her leading a free man by the elbow.

Ilan Voss walked like a man who had not quite been told the news. His wrists were rubbed raw where the ropes had been. He looked at the cart, at the coffin, at Marta, at Tam perched on the cart's seat with his legs swinging, and finally at Freya.

"You came," he said.

"You sound surprised."

"I ran the probabilities," Ilan said. "I gave myself eleven percent."

"That's insulting," said Freya. "I gave us sixty."

"You would. You people with seals always overestimate yourselves." But his voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he stopped walking, and stood in the middle of the ford road with the gold light on the water behind him and the smoke of the burning city rising faint and far to the east, and pressed his bound-free hands briefly over his face, and let himself shake, once, the way a man shakes when a fever breaks.

Nobody said anything. Marta put a hand on his shoulder. Tam leaned over and patted his arm twice, solemnly, the way one pats a horse.

"All right," Ilan said finally, dropping his hands. His eyes were wet and he made no apology for it. "All right. What now? Where are the plates going?"

"North," Marta said. "Marsh track. A soldier drew me a map with his mouth."

"And the Workshop?"

Freya was quiet a moment. Behind them, at the ford, the captain was shouting his men into column — the execution cancelled, the crowd dispersing, the whole machinery of the morning grinding onward toward whatever the general pointed it at next. And beyond that, miles east, the Atlas Workshop stood with Odile inside it, and Odile had made certain arrangements of her own that she had described only once, calmly, over tea, and that Freya had agreed to because there had been no honest alternative.

"Odile knows what she's doing," Freya said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is today." Freya climbed up beside Tam and took the reins from him, and the boy surrendered them without complaint and immediately began counting the soldiers' columns instead. She looked east, toward the smoke, toward the building where she had arrived feeling safe and learned she had been carrying a leash. "She fired the kilns last night. By noon there'll be nothing there for them but ash and a story. Our story. The one we printed."

Ilan followed her gaze, and read the horizon the way he read everything — carefully, completely, missing nothing. "You're going to let her burn it."

"I'm going to let her choose it. There's a difference." Freya gathered the reins. Old Bess tossed her head and started forward, and the cart rolled north with its impossible cargo, and the fog burned off the fields in long white ribbons, and for half a mile nobody spoke at all.

Then Tam tugged Freya's sleeve.

"Yes?"

"Eleven percent," the boy said gravely. "That's what he gave us. We beat his numbers." He grinned, gap-toothed, entirely delighted. "I'm putting it in my head. Best number yet."

And Freya laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the capital, loud enough that the soldier at the willow fork looked up and smiled despite himself — and drove the cart north into a country with no government left to obey, carrying nothing sealed, toward the cellar where a screw and a lever and a blank page were waiting.

Behind them, far off in the east, the Atlas Workshop began, very quietly, to burn clean.
