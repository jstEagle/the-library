## 18. What the General Learned

Corvus Bellamy had been an envoy for eleven years, and in that time he had learned three things about power: that it preferred to be flattered rather than obeyed; that it never read past the second paragraph of anything; and that it could be bought — always, everywhere, by everyone.

He was beginning to suspect a fourth thing, and it troubled him more than the other three combined.

* * *

The trouble began at dawn on the morning after the execution that did not happen.

Bellamy had arrived at the ford with his clerk, two troopers, and a leather folio containing the general's terms, which were generous in the way that a snare is generous. He had spent the previous evening rehearsing his opening remarks — he was a man who believed that courtesy, properly applied, was worth a regiment — and he had risen early to review the courier's file one final time before the ceremony of surrender.

That was when he noticed the ink.

He noticed it because Corvus Bellamy had once, in his impoverished youth, worked for a notary in the river districts, and because a notary's apprentice learns things that ambassadors forget: how ink sits on paper, how it dries unevenly where a hand has paused, how a seal pressed twice leaves a ghost of itself at the edge like a man standing too long in one spot. The courier Freya Sandoval's commission bore the republic's chancellery seal, and it was a very good seal, and it was wrong.

Wrong how? He turned it under the lamp for an hour before he could say. Wrong in the pressure. The republic's chancellery used a press-seal, weighted and mechanical, that bit evenly into the wax like teeth. This seal had been pressed by hand — firmly, patiently, but by hand — and the wax had pooled a hair thicker at the southern rim, where a human wrist tires. A machine does not tire. A machine does not hesitate.

Someone had hesitated over this document. Someone had leaned all their weight into the seal and then, at the last instant, leaned a little harder still.

Bellamy sat back in his chair in the commandeered customs house and looked out at the ford, where the mist was lifting off the water, and thought: *Well. That is interesting.*

"Master Envoy?" His clerk, Pell, a young man with the anxious face of someone who had been told too many times that his handwriting would come to nothing, stood in the doorway with tea. "They're asking whether the courier will be receiving her commendation."

"She'll receive whatever I decide she receives," Bellamy said absently. "Tell them the ceremony is postponed. Weather."

It was, in fact, perfectly clear weather. But postponement was the envoy's oldest tool, and Bellamy reached for it the way another man might reach for bread.

* * *

By mid-morning he had assembled the facts, or what passed for facts in a country sliding out from under its own government.

Fact the first: the courier Sandoval had presented a commission bearing the republic's authority, ordering the Atlas Workshop's plates surrendered into the general's protective custody, and had done so with a straightness of manner that had convinced even him, and he was not easily convinced, having made his living for a decade on the principle that every straight manner concealed a crooked ledger.

Fact the second: the seal was forged, and forged well enough that only a former notary's apprentice would ever have caught it, which meant it had been forged by someone who understood that documents are not paper but weight — the accumulated gravity of everything they resemble.

Fact the third, which he found genuinely puzzling: the forgery had *worked*. The plates were gone. The coffin-cart had left the ford at dawn under his own soldiers' noses, carrying what the manifest described as burial goods, and Bellamy had watched it go, and had even — he remembered this with a small private wince — lifted his hat as it passed, because the widow driving it had looked tired and dignified and he was, despite everything, a courteous man.

He had waved goodbye to the treaty plates of the Republic of Vessany.

"Well," he said to the empty room, and then, because there was no one else to say it to, "Pell."

Pell appeared with the particular alacrity of a clerk who suspected he was about to be blamed for something. "Sir?"

"When we return to the city — and we are returning today — I want you to visit every print shop, stationer, ink merchant, and paper-seller within the walls. Quietly. Buy something at each so your visits have a shape. And note for me who is buying unusually large quantities of anything."

"Large quantities of what, sir?"

"That," said Bellamy, "is rather the question."

* * *

Pell came back at dusk looking as though he had seen a ghost, or worse, arithmetic.

"There are fourteen printing houses in the city, sir. Six are shuttered, four are doing funeral announcements and grain prices, and four—" He stopped, swallowed. "Four are printing the same thing. They don't know each other's doing it. Two of the masters haven't spoken in years over some dispute about kerning."

"The same thing being?"

"A broadsheet, sir." Pell laid a sheet on the desk, and Bellamy picked it up and felt his stomach perform a slow, professional somersault.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing that frightened him most, because Bellamy understood beauty the way a locksmith understands locks: as a mechanism with an intention behind it. The broadsheet carried no masthead, no printer's mark, no name at all. It carried the old cipher — the true old cipher, the pre-Republic diplomatic hand that half the ministries had forgotten and the other half pretended to — and beneath the cipher, in plain type set with a care that bordered on devotion, a single column of text:

*Be it known, by the authority vested in the Atlas Workshop of the Republic, that the plates, presses, and instruments of that Workshop are the property of no crown, no council, and no general; that they are held in trust for the people of this country, who may claim them at any door that prints honestly; and that any order to the contrary is void.*

Below that, instructions. Actual, usable, practical instructions — how to cut a block, how to mix ink from lamp-black and linseed, how to set a page by lamplight in a cellar. Below *that*, a map fragment, hand-drawn, showing three roads north.

"It's in a dozen hands already," Pell said miserably. "More than a dozen. The fishwives are reciting it at the market. There's a version sung to a hymn tune, sir, and I want to state for the record that it is *catchy*."

"How many copies?"

"We confiscated forty-seven. I believe that accounts for perhaps one percent."

Bellamy sat with the broadsheet in his hands for a long moment. Outside, the city lamps were coming on, one by one, in the ordinary way that cities light themselves when no one has yet told them to be afraid.

"Do you know what this is, Pell?"

"A forgery, sir?"

"No." Bellamy tapped the cipher. "This is a *letter*. A letter for the Atlas. Delivered. Do you see? They didn't steal the republic's authority. They *answered* it. Somewhere in that workshop there is a woman who took the worst instrument of our age — a sealed order, a cipher, a chain of command — and she wrote back."

* * *

The general's chancellery received reports by fast rider every third day, and Bellamy had three days to decide what his report would say.

He spent the first day walking the city without escort, which his troopers disapproved of and which he did anyway, because a man cannot understand a situation from inside a saddle. He visited the print shops himself. At the fourth one, a stooped master with ink-stained knuckles to the wrist offered him tea and, when Bellamy delicately inquired whether the man was aware that printing unauthorized material carried penalties, the master looked at him with an expression of such mild, unguarded pity that Bellamy felt like a tax collector lecturing the sea.

"Young man," the printer said — Bellamy was fifty-one, but he let it pass — "I printed obituaries for the ministry for thirty years. Names, dates, causes. 'Fever.' 'Accident.' 'Fever' again. Do you know how many of those fevers were fevers?" The old man poured the tea with a steady hand. "Neither do I. That was the point. Now go on and arrest me if you like. My daughter can run the press. She sets faster than I do anyway."

"I'm not going to arrest you," Bellamy said.

"Everyone says that first."

He did not arrest anyone. He went back to the customs house and sat up late with the forged commission and the genuine broadsheet side by side on his desk, and he performed the exercise that had kept him alive through three changes of government: he asked himself, coldly, *who profits?*

If he reported the forgery — if he reported that a courier had falsified the republic's seal, dispersed the plates, and ignited a printing movement now reproducing itself faster than soldiers could burn it — then what followed was predictable as weather. The general would descend on the city with fire and rope. The printers would die or flee and print from exile, and their broadsheet would gain the one thing it currently lacked, which was martyrs. The plates were already beyond reach, somewhere on the northern roads, in a coffin-cart driven by a widow who had looked tired and dignified. Burning the city would recover nothing except ash and a grievance.

And the general — Bellamy had served him for two years, and had studied him longer — the general did not actually want ash. The general wanted *legitimacy*, wanted it the way starving men want bread, and legitimacy could not be requisitioned from a population that was singing seditious broadsheets to hymn tunes. Every hanging would cost him ten printers' apprentices. Every burned workshop would mint a hundred new ones. You cannot bayonet a verb, Bellamy thought. It was a phrase he resolved to write down later, possibly in a memoir, possibly under a pseudonym.

Alternatively: he could report that the plates were destroyed.

* * *

On the second day he interviewed the survivors of the ford, one at a time, with tea and courtesy, because courtesy was cheaper than coercion and considerably more effective.

The captain who had memorized Ilan Voss's face at the checkpoint was furious and wanted the deserter's head, and Bellamy listened to him for twenty minutes before observing mildly that the deserter in question had walked free at dawn under the authority of a sealed commission, and that pursuing him would require declaring that commission invalid, which would require explaining to the chancellery how the envoy had failed to detect a forgery that a notary's apprentice had caught in an evening. The captain went quiet in a way that Bellamy found professionally satisfying.

The courier herself he did not interview. He requested an audience; word came back that she had gone north with the cart. He suspected this was true and also incomplete. He found, folded into the folio she had returned to him — the general's own terms, unaccepted, undamaged, returned with the neatness of a woman closing an account — a slip of paper in a firm, unhurried hand:

*You read seals. So you know the republic's own signature on my orders was false before I ever touched it. I did not break the chain. I only noticed where it was attached. — F.S.*

Bellamy read it four times. On the fourth reading he laughed — a short, startled sound, alone in the customs house at midnight, the laugh of a man meeting a colleague where he least expected one.

*She knew,* he thought. *She decoded it on the road and she came to the ford anyway, and she handed the general's own terms back to his own envoy, and she let me discover the forgery myself, because she judged — correctly, damn her — that I would rather bury the affair than admit it.* He had been managed. Skillfully, gently, with full respect for his vanity, by a woman whose entire strategy had apparently consisted of assuming he possessed a conscience and providing him with an exit ramp large enough to drive his career through.

It was the most elegant piece of diplomacy anyone had ever committed against him, and he included in that assessment his own marriage, which had ended along similar lines with less grace.

* * *

On the third day he wrote the report.

He wrote it slowly, in his best secretarial hand, the hand that had survived four ministries. The plates of the Atlas Workshop, he reported, together with the greater part of the Workshop's stores, had been destroyed by fire on the night preceding the general's envoys' arrival — an act of spite by the dying Workshop's master printer, who had perished in the blaze along with her life's work. A fragmentary inventory, recovered from the ashes, suggested nothing of value had been lost to the enemy. The courier Sandoval had discharged her commission under difficult circumstances and could be recommended for reassignment, though Bellamy noted she had since departed northward and could not presently be located. The matter of the deserter Voss was subsumed under the general amnesty the chancellery would shortly find it expedient to declare, given the, ah, evolving administrative situation. As for the broadsheets circulating in the city — here Bellamy paused, pen hovering, and then wrote with the serenity of a man stepping off a cliff into deep water — these were the work of scattered malcontents and would fade when the underlying grievance was addressed, which address the general's statesmanship would surely supply.

He sealed the report with the chancellery press — the real one, the machine that bit evenly, that did not tire, that did not hesitate — and he reflected, sealing it, that he had just done precisely what the courier had done: taken the machinery of authority and pressed it by hand, into wax that pooled a little thick where a human wrist chose to lean.

"Pell," he said. "Take this to the rider. Then come back. We're staying in the city another week."

"Another week, sir? May I ask why?"

Bellamy looked out the window. Across the square, in the window of a shuttered stationer's shop, someone had pasted a fresh copy of the broadsheet, and in the last light it glowed faintly, the way paper does, the way promises do.

"Because there are fourteen print shops in this city," Bellamy said, "and I intend to learn kerning."

Pell, wisely, did not ask.

* * *

The report reached the general's field headquarters on the ninth day. The general read the first two paragraphs — the plates destroyed, the Workshop burned, nothing of value lost — and set it aside with satisfaction, and gave orders that the occupation of the capital proceed gently, with an eye to winning the printers over, since printers, he observed, were useful people and better friends than enemies.

In the margin of the second page, where the general never read, Bellamy had written one line more, in the smallest hand he could manage without dishonesty:

*The plates are not destroyed. I know where they went, and so, soon, will everyone. Consider whether that is a loss, sir, or an inheritance. — C.B.*

Whether the general ever found the line, history does not record. What history does record — in a hundred handbills, forty hymnals, and eventually, in the north, in a book printed from a coffin-cart's cargo by a press built around a single salvaged screw — is that in the burning last week of the republic, the general's own envoy looked at the machinery he had served all his life, weighed it, and for the first time declined to purchase what it was selling.

Far to the north, in a borrowed cellar smelling of ink and lamp oil, a boy who could not read pulled a lever, and a page came out wet and shining, and asked what it said.

But that is another chapter, and Bellamy, pouring himself a celebratory glass of the customs house's confiscated wine, knew none of it yet — only that for the first time in eleven years, his hands smelled of honest work, and the seal on his conscience, though pressed by hand, had not hesitated at all.
