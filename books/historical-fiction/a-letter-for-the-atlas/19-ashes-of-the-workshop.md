## 19. Ashes of the Workshop

There is an art to burning a thing properly.

Most people think fire is chaos, but I have been a printer for fifty-one years, and I will tell you what I told every apprentice who ever inked a roller in my shop: nothing worth doing is done carelessly. You set your type with intention. You pull your lever with intention. And if you must burn down the only home you have ever had — well. You do that with intention too.

The general's men had given me until morning. They were courteous about it, the way a creditor is courteous, the way plague is punctual. A lieutenant with clean fingernails had stood in my doorway at dusk and said, *Mistress Ferrant, the Workshop passes into the custody of the Provisional Authority at first light,* and he had smiled as though he were doing me a kindness by naming it something other than what it was: theft.

I thanked him. I shut the door. And I went upstairs to decide how a building dies.

* * *

You would think, standing in a room full of everything you have made, that you would want to save it all. But grief is a sorting machine, if you let it work. It sorts ruthlessly. It asks one question over and over — *what was this, really?* — and most things cannot answer.

The treaty plates could not answer. They were brass and memory, and the treaties they printed were already dead; I had watched them die over years the way you watch a river silt up. The type cases could not answer — metal is metal, and someone else's hands would fill them again. The ledgers, the invoices, the forty years of receipts for paper and lamp oil and the small bribes that keep a business breathing: none of it could answer.

What answered was stranger. The apprentice stool with the wobbly leg where Tam sat counting under his breath. The burn mark on the composing stone where Odile-the-younger had set her sleeve on fire in her second week and learned, permanently, why printers wear wool. The window above the press where Marta had stood mapping escape routes on broadsheet backs, tapping her pencil against the glass like she was knocking on the future's door.

I walked through the whole building twice with a lantern and a can of oil, and I wept exactly once, briefly, when I found a proof sheet tucked behind the drying rack — the very first free broadsheet, misprinted, the headline crooked as a drunkard's fence. We had kept it because it was wrong. Because being wrong together, freely, had felt like the first honest thing any of us had done in decades.

I folded it into my coat. Some things you carry. That was Freya's catechism, not mine, but I had learned it from her without meaning to.

Then I went downstairs to tell the others what I had decided, which was the part I expected to be hard, and turned out to be the easiest conversation of my life.

* * *

"You're going to burn it," Marta said.

It was not a question. She had read my face the way she read terrain — quickly, and with an eye for where the ground fell away.

"I'm going to burn it," I agreed. "At dawn, after they've come for it. Let them stand in the doorway of their new prize and smell forty years of their own hypocrisy going up."

"Odile." Freya set down the cup she'd been holding. She had that courier stillness about her, the kind that looks like calm and is actually load-bearing. "You don't have to do this yourself."

"Yes," I said, "I do. That's rather the entire point."

They looked at each other, the three of them — Freya, Marta, Ilan, home again bruised and hoarse from the ford, alive in the way that makes people quiet. Even Tam had stopped counting. He sat on the wobbly stool with his chin on his knees, watching me with those eyes that took in numbers the way other children took in sweets.

"You built it," Ilan said at last. He understood endings better than any of us; he had spent his career recording them. "It should be yours to close."

That was it. No argument. Marta nodded once, briskly, the way she did when a route checked out. Freya reached across the table and put her hand flat on mine, and her hand was cold from the night air and steady as a press bed, and neither of us said anything sentimental because we were too old and too tired and it would have gotten in the way.

Tam raised his hand like he was in school.

"What?" I asked him.

"If you burn the letters," he said, "how will people know what they said?"

And there it was — the boy who couldn't read, asking the question that had kept me awake since the envoys first came. He wasn't asking about the plates. He was asking about the words. Whether a thing destroyed is a thing erased.

"They'll know," I told him, "because we already sent them out. Every broadsheet, every page Marta mapped, every copy that went out in a cart or a coat or a coffin—" I glanced at Marta, who permitted herself a thin smile at the coffin — "those are loose in the world now. Fire doesn't chase them. Fire just clears the ground so they can grow."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he brought to all arithmetic.

"So the building is just... the box the words came in."

"The box," I said, "and I have lived in it long enough to know the difference."

He nodded, satisfied, and went back to counting under his breath — primes, I think, or the cipher fragments he hoarded like a magpie hoards buttons. And I turned away before my face could do anything undignified, because apparently even a woman planning an arson needs a moment.

* * *

Dawn came gray and unhurried, as though it hadn't heard.

We assembled across the square, in the shadow of the chandler's warehouse, where the smoke wouldn't find us too soon. Freya stood beside me with the forged letter folded against her chest — she still carried it everywhere, out of habit or superstition or love, I never asked which. Marta had the coffin-cart loaded and the plates hidden beneath a false floor of mourning linens, positioned near the alley in case the fire gave us cover or complications. Ilan watched the street with a deserter's eyes, cataloguing every soldier the way he once catalogued the living.

And down the lane came the general's men, right on schedule, punctual as plague.

They were perhaps twenty. They marched to my door with the confidence of people collecting property, and their lieutenant knocked — knocked! — and waited, and knocked again, and I confess I savored every second of that waiting. Somewhere behind that door was a building that had obeyed every regulation for forty years and had decided, at the end, to be disobedient in the most thorough manner available.

"Mistress Ferrant?" the lieutenant called. "The transfer is scheduled. Open in the name of the Provisional Authority."

I struck the match.

I had soaked the floors the night before — oil in the paper stores, oil along the drying racks, oil in the stairwell where the flames would climb politely, floor to floor, like a reader moving through chapters. I had left the back wall damp. That sounds like mercy, but it was tactics: Marta had explained that a fire spreads fastest toward whatever you've failed to plan around, and I wanted the collapse to fall inward, onto the press, onto the plates' empty cradles, onto everything the general's clerks had already entered into their ledgers as *acquired.*

The match trembled slightly. I noted this with professional disapproval and lit it anyway.

The oil caught with a sound like a long exhale.

For a moment — just a moment — I almost ran inside. Fifty-one years. My husband's ghost in every corner, though he'd died before the republic finished dying and had mercifully missed the ending. My own young ghost, sleeve smoking, laughing at the burn mark. All of it going up in a light that was, I will maintain until my own dying day, beautiful. Not tragic. Beautiful. There is a difference, and the difference is consent. This fire had my signature on it.

"Mistress Ferrant!" The lieutenant had gone pale, which improved him considerably. "What — the building — *your building*—"

"It was never his," I called back, stepping out of the doorway as the heat pushed past me like a crowd leaving a theater. "That was rather the point of the exercise."

Smoke rolled out of the windows in fat black ropes. Somewhere inside, the great press groaned as its frame heated, and I felt the sound in my ribs like a farewell. Then the roof timbers caught, and the fire stood up, and the whole square filled with orange light at an hour when the sun hadn't yet managed anything similar, and I thought: *there. That's the last impression the Atlas Workshop will make. Not surrender. Illumination.*

A printer's joke. I allowed myself one dry laugh, which alarmed a nearby soldier considerably.

* * *

They did not arrest me. I had counted on that, too — you cannot prosecute a woman for burning what you claim was already yours, without admitting in open court whose it truly was. The lieutenant settled instead for shouting questions at my back while I crossed the square with the unhurried gait of a woman who has finally, at seventy-three, finished her work.

Freya met me at the corner. Her face was lit orange and her eyes were wet and furious and proud all at once, and she said nothing, because what was there to say that the fire hadn't already said louder?

But I had one more piece of business, and I had carried it wrapped in cloth inside my coat through all of the burning, because some things you carry, and some things you hand off.

"Freya." I unwrapped it. The master screw — the great iron screw that set the pressure of the press, that determined whether ink kissed paper or crushed it, that had turned under my hand ten thousand times and my husband's hand before that. It was warm from my body. It weighed what a promise weighs. "This comes out of the fire with us."

She looked at it and didn't reach for it. "Odile, I'm not a printer."

"No," I agreed. "You're worse. You're a founder."

"Odile—"

"A press is a screw, a bed, and someone stubborn enough to pull the lever. I have the screw. Marta has maps and a cart. Tam has numbers in his head that no fire can reach. Ilan—" I glanced at him; he was watching the smoke with the expression of a man doing sums about the future and finding, for the first time, a remainder he liked. "Ilan knows how many people there are and where they are, which is more than any government has ever managed. And you, girl, know how to make a lie that saves people. Do you imagine there is a printing house anywhere that doesn't run on exactly that?"

Freya closed her hands around the screw. I saw her fingers tighten on it the way they must have tightened, once, on a cipher-case — except this time the weight was hers by choice, and I think she felt the difference, because she exhaled like a woman setting down something heavy and picking up something true.

"North," she said. It wasn't a question either. Marta had drawn the route days ago, on the back of a broadsheet, naturally.

"North," I confirmed. "Where nobody's authority reaches and everybody's does. We'll need a cellar. Preferably one belonging to someone who owes me money — I kept excellent records."

Despite everything — the smoke, the soldiers, the ash beginning to sift down over the square like the world's saddest snowfall — Tam laughed. A real laugh, bright and startling, and Ilan laughed too, hoarse and surprised at himself, and even Marta's mouth betrayed her.

Behind us, half the roof came down in a fountain of sparks, and the general's men scattered backward cursing, and the light of my life's work painted all our faces gold.

I stood in the doorway of the square — doorways are wherever you decide to stand, I have concluded — and I watched it burn clean, and I felt no grief that I hadn't already spent, and no regret that survived inspection. An ending accepted on one's own terms. I recommend it to everyone, though the scheduling can be difficult.

Then Freya shifted the screw to one arm and offered me the other, formal as a courtier, and I took it, because a woman my age accepts good manners where she finds them.

"Which way?" she asked.

Marta was already climbing onto the cart, reins in hand, pointing north with her chin — past the smoke, past the soldiers, past the whole collapsing architecture of the world we'd been born into — toward a road that belonged to no one, and therefore to us.
