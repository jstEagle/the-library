## 20. The Long Way North

The first thing I learned about carrying nothing is that it weighs more than you'd think.

Not the absence itself — the absence was a marvel. For eleven years I had walked with a case against my ribs, and my body had grown around it like a tree around a fence post. On our second morning north, I reached for it out of habit and found only the strap of my pack, and for a moment I felt so light I was afraid I would simply drift off the road like ash off a fire.

Then Marta handed me the coffin.

"You're tallest," she said, as if that settled everything, which of course it did. "Lift from your knees, courier. You've hauled state secrets across a continent; you can haul a dead man's maps over a creek."

The coffin-cart had lost its horse at the ford — requisitioned by a militia that no longer existed by the following Tuesday, Ilan observed, so technically the animal was now free too — and so we took turns pulling it ourselves, five people and one box rolling north through a country that had stopped pretending to be governed. The roads were full of everyone else's belongings: a feather mattress abandoned in a ditch like something drowned, a church bell half-buried where someone had tried to hide it and given up, an entire apothecary's worth of bottles glinting in a hedgerow, each one carefully stoppered, because even in collapse nobody wants to waste good medicine.

"Look at all this," Tam said, walking backward in front of me so he could talk while facing the wreckage. He counted things when he was happy. He was counting bottles. "Two hundred and six. Somebody carried every single one of those."

"Somebody carried them and then put them down," Odile said. She walked with her hands folded behind her back like a woman inspecting her own funeral procession. "That's the whole history of the world, boy. Carrying, and putting down."

"I'm going to carry two hundred and six bottles," Tam said. "All the way north."

"You are not," said four voices at once.

* * *

We fell into a shape without anyone proposing it. Ilan walked point, because he had spent his career reading crowds and could tell you from a quarter mile whether the figures ahead were refugees or soldiers or just trees being optimistic. Marta held the rear, because she said she liked to see where we'd been, and because she had begun sketching the road on the backs of the broadsheets — not escape routes now, though old habits die slowly, but actual maps. Rivers. Hills. The little towns with their names written in her late husband's hand, copied from the master plates she still slept beside.

"He'd have hated this," she told me one evening, chin toward the burning horizon south of us, where the war was doing its slow work. "Corwin drew maps so people could find their way home. Now they're useful for leaving."

"They're useful either way," I said. "That's what a tool is."

She gave me a look — warm, wry, the look of a woman who has decided to forgive me for things I hadn't yet confessed to — and said nothing, which from Marta was a full paragraph.

Odile walked in the middle and complained. This was, we all understood, how she loved us. She complained about her boots (forty years old), her knees (older), the quality of northern bread (a rumor, since we had none), and the general condition of the republic's collapse ("slovenly — even ruin should be set properly"). She had come out of the ashes of her Workshop with singed eyebrows and a canvas bag containing the master press screw wrapped in three layers of oilcloth, and she guarded that screw the way other women guard infants. Some nights she took it out and polished it by firelight without saying anything, and we learned not to interrupt, the way you learn not to interrupt anyone at prayer.

And Tam walked wherever he wanted, usually in circles, counting.

It was on the fourth day that I noticed what he was counting this time.

He was walking beside me, hands in his pockets, mouthing numbers, and I caught the rhythm of them — seven, three, eleven, nine, four — and my stomach dropped through the road before my mind caught up and told it to climb back out.

"Tam." I kept my voice easy. Couriers learn easy voices the way sailors learn sea legs. "What are those numbers?"

He shrugged, pleased to be asked. "The letter. The real one. Yours." He tapped his temple. "I keep them so they don't fall out."

"The real—" I stopped walking. The column bumped into me like a cart hitting a stone. "Tam, that cipher is destroyed. It burned with the Workshop."

"No," he said, with the gentle patience of a child correcting a slow adult. "The paper burned. I've got the numbers." He frowned, working at it. "Numbers don't burn. Numbers aren't anywhere. That's why they're better than paper."

I looked back at Odile. She had heard. She was looking at the boy with an expression I had last seen on her face standing in her doorway while forty years of her life went up clean and bright behind her — grief and release, holding hands.

"Well," she said. "There's a thought."

"There's a *problem*," I said.

"There's always a problem," Odile said. "The question is whether it's a problem or a press. Keep walking, courier. You'll know which by the time we reach the river."

* * *

The country we walked through was nobody's, and everybody's, and it behaved accordingly.

At a crossroads shrine some anonymous hand had hung a sign: NO ALLEGIANCES PAST THIS POINT, and beneath it, smaller, in different ink, EXCEPT KINDNESS, WHICH IS COMPULSORY. Ilan read it aloud twice and then stood there with his jaw working like a man chewing on a sentence he couldn't swallow.

"That's a census question," he finally said. "Somebody made kindness compulsory. Somebody wrote it down."

"Is that bad?" Tam asked.

"It's the first law I've ever respected," Ilan said, and walked on fast, the way he did when feeling had gotten ahead of him and he needed his legs to catch up.

He was thinner than when I'd found him at the checkpoint, that gray man with his ledgers and his terrible question. The ford had taken more than his freedom; it had taken the belief that counting the fallen was a neutral act, and what grew in its place was stranger and better — a man who now counted forward instead of back. He kept a small book still, but the entries had changed. *Marta laughed today at her own joke, which is the only kind worth having. Tam ate four eggs. Odile admitted the boots were fine.* A census of the living, conducted daily, with no authority behind it but his own stubbornness.

I caught him writing once and he snapped the book shut like a boy caught with contraband.

"What does it say?" I asked.

"Nothing."

"You were a census taker for nineteen years. You have never once recorded 'nothing.'"

He looked at the road ahead, where Marta and Tam were arguing about whether a distant hill was a hill or a very committed cloud. "It says," he said, "that I used to ask people their allegiance. One question. And it ruined everything it touched." He turned the small book over in his hands. "So I'm practicing a different question. What can be carried. What must be put down. Who's hungry. Whether that hill is a hill." He glanced at me, quick and dry. "Whether the courier sleeps, or stands watch all night decoding ghosts in her head."

"I sleep," I lied.

"Mm," said Ilan Voss, and wrote something down.

* * *

On the sixth night we sheltered in a barn whose owner had fled weeks before, leaving behind — this being the season's miracle — half a sack of barley, a crock of rendered fat, and a cat of enormous self-regard who assessed our party, found it adequate, and installed himself in the coffin-cart atop Corwin Hale's master maps as though appointed to the position.

"Your husband is being sat upon by a cat," Odile informed Marta.

"Corwin would have loved him," Marta said, scratching its ears. "He always said the best maps get marked up. A map nobody touches is a map nobody uses." She fed the cat barley porridge, which it accepted as tribute.

We ate. Firelight. The particular silence of five people who have survived something together and are only beginning to notice they've become a household. Tam fell asleep mid-count, somewhere around sixty, his head on Marta's shoulder, and she didn't move for two hours rather than wake him, though I saw her arm go numb and saw her refuse to care.

And I sat with my back against the wall and my hands empty in my lap, and I waited for the panic.

Because here is what no one tells you about duty: it isn't only a weight. It's also a floor. For eleven years, whatever happened — the burnings, the checkpoints, the lies I told well and the ones I told badly — I had known exactly who I was. I was the woman with the case. Take away the case and you take away the answer, and I had discovered, sitting in that barn, that I didn't have another one ready.

Freya Sandoval, courier of a republic that no longer existed. Carrier of orders that had been forged. Keeper of a cipher that lived now only in the head of a child who couldn't read it.

"Stop it," Odile said, without looking up from the screw she was polishing.

"Stop what?"

"Standing at attention inside yourself. I can hear it. Forty years I listened to apprentices do it — the good ones, the ones who thought the work was the whole of them." She blew a fleck of ash off the screw. "You're not unemployed, girl. You're unassigned. There's a difference, and it's the width of the world."

"And what am I assigned to now?"

"How should I know? I'm a printer, not a prophet." But she smiled at the screw, at her own burned reflection in it. "Though I'll say this. In my trade, the worst thing that can happen to type isn't fire. Fire, you melt down and recast, and the letters come back the same. The worst thing is being left set in an old page forever. Locked in place. Saying the same thing until the wood rots around you." She looked up. "You've been distributed, Freya. Loose type goes in the case. And the case—"

"—gets composed into something new," I finished.

"Hm," said Odile Ferrant, satisfied, and put the screw away, and that was the entire conversation, and it saved my life a little.

* * *

We crossed the river at a ferryman's crossing on the ninth day, and the ferryman charged us nothing, and refused to explain why.

"Everyone refuses to explain anything anymore," Marta grumbled, settling the cart aboard. "Half the country's gone generous and the other half's gone honest, and nobody will own up to either."

"It's the broadsheets," Tam said.

We all looked at him.

He pointed downstream, embarrassed suddenly, the way he got whenever he noticed the adults noticing him. "In the towns. I've seen them. Nailed up. On doors, on wells." He counted on his fingers, which was how he quoted. "'The plates belong to no one. The Workshop's secrets are dispersed among the people.' That one. Our one." He shrugged. "People read it and then they're weird. Nice-weird."

The ferryman, poling us across, cleared his throat. "Saw that sheet myself," he offered, to the river generally. "Down in Millbrook. Thought it was nonsense at first — plates belonging to no one, what sort of order is that?" He grinned, gap-toothed. "Best sort. Nobody to arrest for obeying it."

Ilan and I exchanged a look over the boy's head.

Because here was the thing I had been circling for nine days without landing: the lie I had forged in the republic's dying name had escaped us. It was loose in the country, reprinting itself in a dozen hands, wearing its borrowed authority like a coat that fit better than the truth ever had. Somewhere a typesetter I'd never meet was setting those words again. Somewhere the general's men were reading them and grinding their teeth. And every copy was a small act of treason committed by strangers on behalf of an idea none of us owned.

Odile caught my expression and misread it perfectly, which was her gift.

"Guilt," she diagnosed. "Courier's guilt. You forged a document and it worked and now you feel like a counterfeiter."

"Aren't I?"

"Every printer alive is a counterfeiter," she said roundly. "We make copies of things and send them out to live without us. The only question — the *only* question, girl, write it on your heart — is whether the thing you multiplied was worth multiplying." She nodded downstream, toward whatever town came next. "Yours was. I set the type myself. I'd know."

* * *

That night, camped above the ferry landing, I took out the last thing I carried that was sealed.

Not orders — those were ash, along with the true ones. This was smaller: the outer wrapper of my original commission, the seal of the Republic Chancellery, wax cracked, ribbon frayed, the emblem pressed into it already softening at the edges like a face going vague in memory. I had kept it the way you keep a tooth that was pulled. Proof it had been in me. Proof it was out.

I turned it over in the firelight. The republic's eagle, wings spread, holding — I had never actually looked closely before, which tells you everything about how I served — a key in one talon and a chain in the other. Eleven years I'd worn that bird on my papers and never asked what it was holding the key *to*, or who held the other end of the chain.

"Tam," I said.

He looked up from the arithmetic he was doing in the dirt — teaching himself, in his way, to see numbers as shapes, since shapes were what reading was and nobody had told him otherwise.

"If I wanted to write something new — something true — in the old cipher. Could you still give me the numbers?"

His whole face lit like a window. "All of them? All hundred and twelve?"

"All of them."

"I *knew* they weren't nowhere," he said triumphantly, to the fire, to the cat, to the dark country lying open north of us. Then, quieter, leaning close, conspiratorial: "What's it going to say?"

And I found — this was the strange lightness again, arriving like weather — that I didn't know. No chancellery had drafted it. No minister had signed it. No general awaited its delivery with patient, courteous hands. For the first time in my adult life, the words were mine to choose, and the emptiness where the orders used to be wasn't empty at all. It was room.

"I don't know yet," I told him honestly. "But I think it starts with *we*."

Tam considered this with the gravity he brought to everything worth counting. "We," he repeated, testing the weight of it. Then he grinned, gap where a tooth was coming in. "Good. *They* was always such a small word anyway."

Across the fire, Odile was already unwrapping the screw. Marta had turned a broadsheet blank-side-up and was ruling lines across it, quick and sure, mapping something that had no name yet. Ilan had opened his little book. The cat sat on the coffin, tail curled, presiding.

North of us the road ran on through a country with no government and, increasingly, no need of one — just people, and ink, and morning coming up behind the hills like something printed fresh.

"First town's Halloway," Marta said, tapping her sketch. "Market day Saturday. Big square. Old guildhall with a cellar underneath."

Odile looked at the screw in her hands, and then at the rest of us, one by one, taking a count of her own.

"Well then," she said. "Someone find me a cellar."
