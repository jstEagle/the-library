## 21. First Impression

The cellar smelled of turnips, damp stone, and — by the third day — hope.

Tam had a system for smells now. He'd learned it on the road, because he couldn't read signs and couldn't read faces as fast as other people seemed to, so he'd taught himself the world through his nose instead. Turnips meant safety, mostly, because nobody hid in a turnip cellar unless they were trusted to be there. Damp stone meant north, which was where they were all going. And hope — well. Hope was ink and oil and hot metal, and it had followed them up from the ashes of the Atlas Workshop like a dog that had decided whose family it belonged to.

"Mind the screw," Odile said, for the eleventh time that morning.

"I am minding the screw," Tam said.

"You are standing *on* the screw."

He looked down. He was, in fact, standing on the screw — or near enough to it that his boot heel hovered over the great iron spiral like a heron deciding whether to commit to a fish. He stepped off with dignity. "I was guarding it."

"With your feet."

"That's how guards work," Tam said. "You put yourself between the thing and the danger." He gestured at the empty cellar around them, at the sacks of turnips, at Marta, who was down on her knees with a stub of charcoal, drawing lines on the flagstones. "There isn't any danger. So I'm doing an excellent job."

From somewhere above them came the muffled thump of their host — a widowed cooper named Bren who had taken one look at Odile's burned hands and asked no questions, which in the current country made him either very brave or very tired, and probably both — dropping another load of firewood onto the floor of his shop. Dust sifted down between the ceiling beams and settled on everything: on the press parts laid out in rows like soldiers awaiting inspection, on Marta's maps spread across two overturned barrels, on Ilan's ledger, which he still carried even though there was no one left to count.

Tam counted things anyway. It was easier than reading, and it kept his hands from shaking when he thought too hard about the ford, about the smoke, about Ilan walking out of it thin and gray and alive, alive, alive. Numbers didn't make him shake. Numbers were solid. You could hold a number in your head and it would stay exactly where you put it, which was more than you could say for towns or governments or fathers.

Four hundred and six. That was how many pieces the press had come apart into when they'd carried it out of the Workshop's burning basement, wrapped in sacking, in eleven trips. Tam had counted every one. He could recite them backward if anyone asked. Nobody ever asked, but that was fine. He knew they were true whether or not anybody said them out loud.

That was the thing about numbers nobody understood who could read. Readers thought words were the real thing and numbers were just words with fewer letters. But numbers were older than words. Numbers were what the world did underneath its talking.

"All right," Marta said, sitting back on her heels and surveying her chalk marks. She had drawn the press's footprint on the floor — the bed here, the frame there, the long lever arm sweeping out into the aisle like a ship's boom. "Odile. If we're wrong about the platen height, we'll crack the type."

"We won't be wrong," Odile said.

"We were wrong about the carriage rail yesterday."

"The carriage rail," Odile said, lowering herself onto a crate with the careful economy of a woman whose hands were still bandaged, "was sabotaged by damp. The platen is a matter of geometry, and geometry does not take sides." She looked over at Tam. "Boy. The number."

"One hundred and nine," Tam said instantly, because she'd asked this same question four times a day since the Workshop, and each time the answer was the same and each time it pleased her, and pleasing Odile Ferrant was one of the few reliable pleasures left in the world.

"One hundred and nine thousandths," Odile said to Marta, with the smugness of a queen producing a signed treaty. "He measured the platen face with a knotted cord while I was sleeping and remembered it better than my own notes."

"I remember everything I measure," Tam said. It wasn't bragging. It was just a fact about him, like being short or liking pickled things.

Marta smiled at him — her slow smile, the one that started at the corner of her mouth and took its time, like she was mapping it as it went. "Then you're the most dangerous man in this cellar," she said. "Governments have burned whole provinces over less than a boy with a good memory."

Tam considered this. "Good," he said. "They should be scared. I know where all the turnips are, too."

* * *

It took them two more days.

Two days of Ilan holding the heavy frame upright while Marta shimmed it level with wedges cut from a broken barrel stave. Two days of Odile directing operations from her crate like a general who had been promoted beyond her body's willingness to participate. Two days of Freya coming down the cellar stairs at odd hours with bread and cheese and questions she tried to make sound casual.

"How goes it?" she'd say.

"Slowly," Marta would say.

"Any word from the town?"

"Nothing worth printing," Ilan would say, and then everyone would laugh, because nothing was worth printing yet, because the press wasn't built, and also because everything was worth printing and they all knew it.

Tam watched Freya during those two days the way he watched weather. She was different since the Workshop burned. Lighter, she kept saying, like it was a good thing, like weight was only ever bad. But Tam had carried things before, and he knew that sometimes when you set a burden down your arms don't feel free — they feel confused. They keep reaching for the shape of the thing that isn't there anymore.

Freya reached a lot. For her cipher-case, which was ash now. For her commission papers, which were ash. For the little rituals of duty, checking seals, counting paces, marking distances — her hands would start the motions and then stop halfway, hovering, not sure what to do next, like birds whose flock had flown without them.

Except when she came down the stairs. Then her hands went still and settled, like they'd found something.

Tam didn't say anything about it. He was ten; he knew better than to say things like that out loud. But he noticed, and noticing was his job now. Somebody in this family had to keep track.

On the second evening, they set the type.

Tam was allowed to watch but not touch, which he accepted the way he accepted most unfairnesses — silently, and with a plan to get around it later. Odile sat close to the composing stick despite her bandages, because setting type by feel was the one thing in the world she would not delegate, not to Marta with her draftsman's fingers, not to Freya with her courier's precision. Her fingers walked the case the way Tam's memory walked number sequences: without looking, without hesitating, certain.

"Read it back to me," she said at last, and held the stick of assembled type toward Freya.

Freya read it. Tam watched her lips move and felt the old familiar wall go up between him and the page — that smooth gray surface where meaning lived behind glass, and everyone else had a key except him. He knew the shapes of some letters now. An A looked like a mountain. An S looked like a snake pretending to be a hook. Beyond that, it was all weather.

"What does it say?" he asked, because asking was allowed. Asking was always allowed. That was the rule they'd made on the road, the first week, after he'd gone quiet for two days rather than admit he couldn't read the notices nailed to a barn door. *Ask,* Freya had said. *Asking is how couriers survive. Asking is how everybody survives.* "What did you write?"

Freya looked at him across the candlelight, and something moved behind her eyes — that reaching-bird look again, settling.

"It says," she began, and then stopped, and started again, slower, the way people speak when they want the words to land whole. "*To all who can read this, and to all who cannot: the presses belong to no crown, no general, no republic, and no man. What is printed here is printed freely, and what is known may be spoken. This is the first letter of the Atlas, and it is addressed to everyone.*"

The cellar was quiet. Above them, Bren's firewood thumped once and went still, as if even the cooper upstairs wanted to hear it again.

"To everyone," Tam repeated.

"To everyone," Odile confirmed. "Every broadsheet we print carries it at the foot of the page. Let them try to arrest a sentence."

"They've arrested worse," Ilan said mildly, from his corner, and nobody laughed quite as easily as they might have a month ago, but somebody smiled, and that counted.

* * *

Morning came gray through the high window slit, and the press stood finished in the middle of the cellar floor, black and gleaming with fresh oil, casting a long shadow across Marta's chalk footprint — filling it exactly, every line, because Marta Hale did not draw maps that lied.

"There's one thing left," Odile said. She was standing beside the press with her hand resting on the great iron screw, and she was looking at Tam.

Tam looked behind himself. There was nobody there.

"Not me," Marta said quickly, holding up her charcoal-blackened hands. "My hands shake. Look at them."

"My authority is spent," Freya said, though her voice did something complicated on the word *authority*, like a door opening onto an empty room.

"And mine," said Ilan, "was never any use for anything but counting."

So it was Tam.

Odile beckoned him over. Up close, the press was enormous — taller than Ilan, wider than the coffin-cart, smelling of iron and linseed and the ghost of the Workshop's smoke. The lever arm rose above his head like a tree branch waiting for wind.

"Now listen," Odile said, and crouched down — it cost her something, he could see it cost her something, her knees cracked like green wood — until her eyes were level with his. "A press is not a weapon and it is not a toy. It is a promise. Every time someone pulls this lever, they are promising the person who reads what comes off it: *this is true, or if it is not true, it is honestly believed, and either way no one paid me to say it.* Do you understand?"

"No," Tam said honestly.

"Good," said Odile. "Neither do I, entirely. But you'll understand it someday, and when you do, you'll already have been keeping the promise for years, and that's the best way to learn anything." She straightened up with a wince. "Take the bar. Both hands. Pull smooth and hard, all the way through, and don't stop halfway no matter what it says about your arms."

Tam spat in both palms, which he had seen carters do, and gripped the lever. It was cold and slightly greasy and it hummed under his hands with all the stored patience of four hundred and six pieces.

Behind him, the whole family arranged itself without anyone arranging them: Marta with her arms folded and her head tilted, measuring the moment the way she measured everything. Ilan with the ledger tucked under one arm, watching with the expression of a man attending a birth. Freya closest of all, close enough that he could hear her breathing, steady and deliberate, the way she breathed when she was trying not to hope too loudly.

And Odile, hand on the screw, nodding once.

"Pull."

Tam pulled.

The lever swung down through its long arc and the press spoke — a deep, wooden, iron-bottomed sound, a sound like a door in a very large house closing forever on one room and opening onto all the others. The screw bit. The platen pressed. Somewhere inside the machine, wet ink met dry paper and became, in the space of a heartbeat, a fact.

Then the lever swung back up, light as anything, as if the press were handing it back and saying *again.*

Marta got there first. Of course Marta got there first; she moved like weather when she wanted to. She lifted the sheet from the tympan with two careful fingers and held it up to the window slit, and the gray morning light came through the paper from behind, and Tam saw it: dark letters, crisp and wet-shining, ranked across the page in neat battalions. His broadsheet. Their broadsheet. The first thing this press had ever said.

"It's crooked," Odile announced, squinting. "Half a degree off square. We'll fix the frisket."

"It's beautiful," Marta said.

"It's crooked *and* beautiful. That's how you know it's ours."

Tam stared at the page. The letters marched and wheeled in their mysterious orders, mountains and snakes and hooks and towers, and behind the glass of them, meanings went about their business without him. A year ago that would have closed him out completely, would have made him small and dumb in his own chest. But he had pulled the lever. Whatever those letters said, they existed because his arms had moved. He was in the page somewhere, the way the cooper's hands were in the barrels. You couldn't see the maker, but the making held.

"Freya," he said. His voice came out smaller than he meant it to, so he planted his feet and said it again, properly. "Freya. What does it say? All of it. Not just the part at the bottom. The whole first page."

She crouched down beside him — down to his level, like Odile had, like everyone in this strange family eventually did — and took the sheet from Marta, and held it where they could both see it, and read.

She read him the news of the ford, and the truth about the surrender order, written plainly where no seal could hide it. She read him the roads that were open and the roads that were watched, from Marta's maps set into type. She read him Odile's declaration, and a column of names — Ilan's census, reprinted, the dead given back their names in ink — and a notice at the bottom inviting any soul who could work a press, or carry type, or simply walk far enough, to come north.

Tam listened with his eyes shut, the way he memorized. When she finished, he opened them.

"Say the first line again."

"*To all who can read this, and to all who cannot—*"

"That's me," Tam said. "That line is me."

"Yes," Freya said. "Yes, it is. It was set with you in the room, and it will be printed in every copy, in every town, and every person who reads it will read past a sentence that belongs to you."

Tam thought about that. Four hundred and six pieces, one crooked sheet, five people in a turnip cellar — and somewhere out there, whole towns full of strangers who would hold this page in their hands and never know his name, but who would be touched by him anyway, the way the cooper's customers were touched by his barrels. Held, without seeing the maker. Held by the making.

He grinned. He couldn't help it. It started in his chest and came out crooked, like Odile's printing, and that seemed right.

"Pull it again," he said, and grabbed the lever. "We've got a lot of everyone to print for."

Freya laughed — really laughed, the unguarded kind — and Marta was already clearing space on the drying line, and Odile was saying *mind the margin, mind the margin,* and Ilan opened his ledger to a fresh page and wrote, at the top, the date, and beneath it, the beginning of a new count.

But outside the high window slit, faint on the morning air, came the sound Tam had learned on the road to dread: hooves. Several horses, moving slowly, the way men ride when they are in no hurry because they believe nothing ahead of them can hurt them.

Everyone stopped. The cellar went quiet, except for the press, which ticked softly as it cooled.

Freya rose, and her hand went — out of old habit — to her hip, where the cipher-case used to hang, and found nothing there, and closed on empty air.

"Through the back," Marta whispered, already rolling the first copies of the first page into the map tube. "Everyone. Now."

Tam picked up the master screw.
