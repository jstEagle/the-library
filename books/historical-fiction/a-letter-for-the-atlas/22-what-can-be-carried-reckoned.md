## 22. What Can Be Carried, Reckoned

The press had a rhythm to it, and after a year Freya had stopped thinking of it as work.

That was the strangest part, she often thought, inking the plate at dawn while the river mist came off the water in long gray ribbons. She had spent her whole life measuring effort against duty, and duty had always been heavy — the cipher-case with its brass clasps, the sealed commission against her ribs, the sense that every step she took was being weighed by someone she would never meet. The press was heavy too. The screw Odile had given her weighed more than any case of orders ever had. But it was a weight you chose, and it turned out that made all the difference in the world.

She pulled the lever. The page came out crisp, smelling of ink and linseed oil: the forty-third edition of *The Northern Lamp*, four columns, one of them devoted — as it had been for eleven straight weeks — to Ilan Voss's serialized accounting of what the census ledgers had actually recorded in the last year of the republic. He called it "What Was Counted." Marta had wanted to call it something more dramatic. Ilan had refused, gently but immovably, in the way he did everything.

"People will trust a dull title," he'd said. "They've had enough of dramatic ones."

He was right, as usual. Circulation had tripled since he began.

* * *

Freya was hanging the first sheets to dry when Tam came pounding down the cellar stairs two steps at a time, fourteen years old now and suddenly all elbows and appetite, waving a scrap of paper like a dispatch from a war zone.

"He's here," Tam said. "There's a courier at the gate."

Freya set down the inking roller. "A courier."

"With sealed orders." Tam's grin was enormous and entirely unhelpful. "Red wax. Official seal. He asked for you by name. *Madame Sandoval, printer.*" Tam said the title with relish, as though tasting it. "I told him we don't get many madames. Or seals."

"You told him — Tam, you can't just—"

"I also gave him tea," Tam added magnanimously. "Marta's rule. Anyone who waits at our gate gets tea, even soldiers, especially tax men. He looked like he might cry over it, actually. I think he's young."

Young. Freya felt something turn over in her chest — not dread, exactly, and not quite recognition either, though recognition was in there somewhere, folded into the feeling like a letter inside its cover. She wiped her hands on her apron, thought about not doing so, then did anyway because ink on a courier's commission seemed needlessly symbolic and she had developed, over the past year, a deep suspicion of symbols that arrived uninvited.

"I'll go up," she said.

"I'll watch the press," Tam said, and then, because he could not help himself: "What does this sheet say, by the way? The one you just pulled?"

Freya looked at him. A year ago the question would have caught her off guard; now it was simply their ritual, the same exchange every morning, and she loved it the way you love a door that always opens.

"It says," she told him, "that the bridge tolls at Harrow Ford were repealed last month, and that nobody in the provisional council will admit whose idea it was, and that Ilan has proof it was Marta who proposed it and then denied it so she wouldn't have to be thanked."

"That sounds right," Tam said solemnly. "She hates being thanked."

"She hates being *correct*, too, and yet."

* * *

The courier was indeed young — nineteen at most, with the particular posture of someone who had been taught to stand straight by people who no longer had the authority to teach anything. His boots were good but worn through at the heel. He stood at their gate holding his satchel the way Freya once held hers: close to the body, angled away from strangers, as if the papers inside were part of his skeleton.

He had a cup of Marta's tea going cold in his other hand. He looked, as Tam had predicted, like he might cry about it.

"Madame Sandoval?" he said, and then visibly lost his nerve, because whatever speech he'd rehearsed on the road had clearly assumed a printing house, not a converted mill with laundry strung between the presses and a coffin-cart — repainted twice, but still unmistakably a coffin-cart — parked out front like a garden ornament.

"This is the place," Freya said. "Come in out of the wind. You're from the southern provisional authority?"

"Yes, ma'am. From Chancellor Rell's office." He fumbled the satchel open. "I have sealed orders for the printer known to hold the Atlas plates. There's a — there's a requisition. The council requires the master plates for the old treaty documents be surrendered for archival purposes, and there's compensation offered, and—" He faltered under her gaze, which she hadn't meant to make stern but which apparently still worked. "Ma'am, may I ask — everyone south of the river says you're the one who carried the last letter of the republic. The real one. Is that true?"

Freya considered several answers. She had gotten better at answers over the years; the road had taught her that much, if nothing else.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Hollis, ma'am. Junior courier, third class."

"Well, Hollis, third class. Sit down. Finish your tea. Then give me your orders and we'll see what they're worth."

* * *

The seal, when she broke it — and she broke it without ceremony, without the little private reverence she once performed for wax, because she'd learned that reverence was a tool and tools belonged to whoever picked them up — contained precisely what she expected. Archival surrender. Compensation. A signature she recognized as belonging to a man she'd never met, affixed by a chancellery that had learned, from watching the old republic die, exactly which forms to imitate.

It was a good forgery. Not as good as theirs had been. Odile would have found three flaws in it; Tam, who couldn't read a word of it but had already glanced at the number sequences in the footer twice, could probably have recited its structure back to her by supper.

She sat across the kitchen table from Hollis and let the silence do some work. Marta's rule about tea extended to chairs, apparently; the boy sat like a man resting after a long swim.

"Tell me about your road up," Freya said.

Hollis blinked. "The — my route?"

"Where you slept. What you ate. What you saw."

"Oh. Well." He turned his cup. "There's fighting near the ford again. Not real fighting — posturing, mostly, two militias glaring at each other across the water. And there's a village, Brenner's Crossing, where they've started printing their own notices. Little handbills, grain prices, who's got seed wheat to trade. Nobody official makes them. An old woman does it on a press her son built out of a wine screw and fence posts." He said this with the baffled tone of a man reporting an anomaly, and then, unexpectedly, with something warmer underneath it: "Everyone there seemed — well. Happier than anywhere else on my route. I didn't understand why until just now, sitting here."

"No," Freya said. "You probably didn't."

"The chancellor says the plates are needed for the historical record. For stability."

"Mm."

"And I'm to wait for a receipt of transfer. Signed. It's — ma'am, forgive me, but I have to ask, because I've been carrying this question for six hundred miles and if I carry it home unanswered I think it'll rattle loose and drive me mad." He leaned forward, and for a moment the courier posture cracked and there was just a tired boy underneath, asking a question the way children ask them, plainly, because no one has yet taught him not to. "Is it true? What they say about the last night of the republic? That the letter you delivered wasn't the one you were given?"

Freya looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked past him, out the kitchen window, to the yard — where Ilan was showing Odile how the new compost beds should be turned, the two of them arguing companionably about nitrogen the way scholars argue about scripture; where Marta was re-inking the survey lines on a map of the northern valleys that half the settlements upriver now owned copies of; where Tam hung drying pages with the care of a man handling living things.

"I was given orders once," Freya said, "sealed in red wax, very official, signed by an authority I believed in with my whole life. I carried them across a burning country because carrying them was who I was. And when I finally opened them all the way — really opened them — I discovered the signature was false. The orders came from the man trying to destroy everything the orders claimed to protect. My duty had been someone else's leash the entire time, and I had worn it proudly."

Hollis had gone very still.

"So is it true?" Freya said. "Yes. And no. The letter I delivered that dawn was a lie, and it was the most honest document I ever signed. It declared the Atlas plates the property of no government and every person who could read them. It saved a condemned man's life, put a free press in a dozen towns within a season, and it was written by a dying printer, a boy who can't read, and a courier who had finally learned what questions to ask." She slid the broken-sealed orders back across the table toward him. "So before you take my answer home to your chancellor, junior courier Hollis, let me ask you something instead. It's the question a census taker once asked me at a border checkpoint, when I was pretending the old world still had rules, and it undid me more thoroughly than any soldier ever managed."

Hollis swallowed. "What question?"

"*Who do you serve?*"

The boy opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked down at his tea, at his satchel, at the orders lying on the table between them with their pretty forged seal.

"That's not fair," he said at last, quietly. "Nobody asks couriers that. We just carry."

"Somebody asked me," Freya said. "When it mattered. And I lied badly, and he saw it, and instead of reporting me he walked beside me for a hundred miles until I could answer honestly. So consider yourself reported, Hollis, third class. Consider this whole kitchen your checkpoint." She tapped the orders. "You have a choice, and it's the only kind that counts — the kind nobody assigned you. You can ride back with a receipt of surrender and a story about a difficult printer. Or you can stay the night, eat Marta's stew, learn how a press works, and ride home tomorrow with something else in your satchel."

"What else?"

"A receipt of refusal," Freya said. "Signed. With my real name, which is the same as my forged one, which is a longer story than you have time for tonight." She smiled, and found — not for the first time that year — that the smile cost nothing at all. "And a copy of our paper. Forty-three editions back, there's an article Ilan wrote about what happens to archives when the archivists stop serving the archived. I suspect your chancellor's office needs it more than the plates."

* * *

He stayed. Of course he stayed. Marta's stew was famous within thirty miles and notorious beyond that, and by the second bowl Hollis had confessed that he'd taught himself to read from confiscated pamphlets during the war, and by the third Odile had him at the press learning the difference between a forme and a frisket, correcting his grip with the patience of a woman who had once run the greatest printing house in the republic and now trained farm boys in a converted mill and considered it, by her own account, a promotion.

Freya stood in the doorway of the press room that evening and watched lamplight move across all of it — Odile's hands on the boy's shoulders, Marta arguing map margins with Ilan, Tam reciting the day's number sequences to himself as he banked the fire, each figure falling into place behind the last like a lock turning open.

A year ago, she had measured her life by what she could carry: orders, guilt, nothing else.

Now she reckoned the ledger differently. One press screw, heavier than any cipher-case, chosen freely. One map collection hidden in a coffin and brought out into the light. One ledger of the dead transformed into a record of the living. One boy who memorized codes because he could not read, teaching a new generation of readers why the codes mattered. One question — *who do you serve?* — passed hand to hand like a flame that burned without consuming anything but lies.

The letter she had carried across a continent was ash, and the lie she'd forged had traveled farther than any truth the old republic ever printed, and tomorrow morning a young courier would ride south carrying neither orders nor refusals but simply a copy of *The Northern Lamp*, folded once, addressed in Marta's precise cartographer's hand to a chancellor who had never once been asked whom he served.

Tam appeared at her elbow, wiping his hands on his apron, and followed her gaze around the warm room.

"What does tomorrow's page say?" he asked. It was their ritual, but tonight he asked it looking at her instead of the press.

Freya considered.

"Ask me again at dawn," she said. "By then, we'll know."
