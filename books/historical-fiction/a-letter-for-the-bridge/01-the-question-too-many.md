## 1. The Question Too Many

The rain had been coming down since morning in that thin, patient way English weather has of wearing a man down without ever raising its voice, and Silas Amberdale watched it from the kitchen window while the census man's bicycle leaned against his gatepost like a drunkard against a wall.

He had expected the visit. Everyone in the village had been expecting it for weeks — the notices pasted to the church door, the vicar's sermon about civic duty, Mrs. Pellow at the shop explaining to anyone who would listen that every soul in England was to be counted on this one night, April the second, nineteen hundred and eleven, as though the country were taking inventory of itself and finding the shelves in want of tidying. Silas had prepared accordingly. He had prepared the way he prepared for everything: carefully, quietly, with a lie folded flat and tucked where no one would think to look.

The knock came at four o'clock, which was earlier than he'd hoped and later than he'd feared.

Mr. Hobbes was not what Silas expected. He was young — twenty-five perhaps, with a clerk's soft hands and a moustache that looked borrowed rather than grown — but his eyes were another matter entirely. They were grey and unhurried and they moved over things the way a customs officer moves over luggage, noting weight, noting shape, noting what did not match the manifest.

"Amberdale," the man read from his book, standing on the doorstep without being invited in, rain beading on the brim of his hat. "Silas Amberdale. Cottage and garden, Mill Lane. Is that right?"

"It is."

"You're listed as head of household. Alone."

"I live alone."

Hobbes wrote something. Silas noticed that he wrote it slowly, and noticed further that he was the sort of man who wanted you to notice.

"Age?" Hobbes said, pencil poised.

"Forty-one."

The pencil moved. Then it stopped. Hobbes turned back two pages in his ledger, ran a finger down a column, and looked up with an expression of mild, almost apologetic interest — the expression of a man who is about to be very thorough and regrets nothing about it.

"That's curious," he said. "I have your discharge particulars here. Army Medical Department records are cross-referenced for pension purposes — I did six months clerking in Aldershot before this posting, so I know the forms." He tapped a paper clipped inside his ledger. "Discharged from South Africa, nineteen hundred and two. Queen's South Africa Medal, four clasps. Age at discharge: thirty-two."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"That would make you forty-one now," Hobbes continued pleasantly, "if my arithmetic holds. Which it does." He smiled. "But you see the trouble. Your stated age matches the papers exactly — which no honest liar manages — except that when I asked at the Rose and Crown last week, old Mr. Tredwell swears you told him you'd been a boy of nineteen at Ladysmith. Nineteen in ninety-nine would make you thirty-one now. Ten years adrift, Mr. Amberdale. Someone is wrong. Either the War Office or you."

"The War Office is rarely wrong," Silas said. "It simply declines to say so."

Something flickered across Hobbes's face — appreciation, possibly, though the customs-officer eyes did not soften. "And your occupation? You've written here—" he turned the page "—'gardener.' Just gardener. No 'jobbing,' no 'market,' no employer named. Most men write more than that."

"Gardener is sufficient."

"For the census, perhaps." Hobbes closed his ledger but did not move from the step. Rain dripped off the gatepost; somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, wheels hissing on wet stone. "You know, I've been doing this door-to-door for three weeks now. Forty-one houses in this parish. Most people can't wait to tell me their business. They inflate it, if anything. But you, Mr. Amberdale — you answer like a man reading from a card he keeps behind a loose brick." He tilted his head. "Do you keep cards behind loose bricks?"

"No," said Silas, who kept something considerably larger, and heavier, and considerably less lawful.

"Well." Hobbes replaced his hat, settled his satchel. "I'll copy the schedule fair tonight and post it to the district office tomorrow. If anything needs clarifying, they'll send for you. They're very keen on clarification this year. New rules." He paused at the gate, one hand on the bicycle, and looked back with that same mild grey attention. "Nurse Amberdale, isn't it? That's what they called you at the front. Tredwell says the wounded used to ask for you by name. Funny thing to call a gardener."

Then he was gone, pedalling through the rain toward the next house, and Silas stood in his doorway for a long time after, listening to the water run off the eaves, feeling the particular cold that comes not from weather but from the sense of a door having been left ajar somewhere in the walls of one's life.

* * *

He did not light the lamp until full dark. That was habit, not superstition — twelve years of habit — but tonight the darkness felt less like concealment and more like a held breath.

The cottage had two rooms downstairs and one above, and Silas had lived in all three of them with the economy of a man who had learned in field hospitals how little space a life actually requires. He built up the fire because the chimney drew well and the smoke could not be read. He ate bread and cheese standing at the window. And then, when the village lights had gone out one by one along Mill Lane — Mrs. Pellow's first, then the forge, then the vicarage, always the vicarage last, as though piety required illumination — he knelt before the dresser, worked loose the third floorboard, and lifted out the ledger.

It was not a grand thing. It had begun life as a stationer's day-book, marbled covers gone dull, spine cracked and mended twice with thread boiled soft from hospital linen. He carried it to the table and set it down the way one sets down something that might still be warm.

Twelve years ago, on the voyage home from Cape Town, he had started it. He remembered the exact hour — the ship rolling in the Bay of Biscay, the second-class saloon empty but for him, a nurse among officers' widows and returning colonels who never once asked him to sit at their table. He had been thinking about Private Ellis. He was always thinking about Private Ellis. Thomas Ellis, aged twenty-three, shot through the chest at Rietfontein, dead within the hour — and absent, utterly absent, from the regimental returns. Not listed as fallen. Not listed as anything. A man erased between one column of ink and the next, because the action had been mismanaged and someone senior needed the casualty list clean.

Ellis had been the first name. There were sixty-three more.

Sixty-four men whose deaths had been struck from the record or never entered at all — men who died of wounds recorded as recoveries, men killed in engagements logged as patrols, men whose names appeared on no memorial because their deaths inconvenienced a report, a pension, a career. Silas had been there for most of them. He had held their hands, or failed to hold them in time, and afterward he had written them down: name, rank, regiment, date, place, manner of death, and where he could manage it, the words they'd said, because a man's last sentence seemed to Silas a terrible thing to lose to somebody's tidy arithmetic.

He opened the ledger now and did not read it so much as let it look back at him.

The fire ticked. Rain worked at the glass. And Silas understood, sitting there in the small hours with his whole unlawful heart spread open on the table, that the comfort he had taken from secrecy had always been a thinner thing than he'd pretended. It was not peace. It was only the absence of discovery, and absence, he knew better than most, is not the same as presence. For twelve years he had kept these men alive in the only place they existed — these pages — and he had told himself that was enough. That invisibility was safety, and safety was enough.

But Hobbes's grey eyes had gone over him today the way a hand goes over a coat looking for what's sewn inside it, and Silas had felt, for the first time, how tired he was. Tired of answering like a man reading from a card. Tired of being gardener, of being nobody, of being the loose brick himself.

Somewhere in London there was a records office, and he knew — had known for months, from a clerk's careless letter, from the word *surplus* appearing on a form — that duplicates of the very returns he'd spent twelve years correcting were scheduled for destruction. Paper burned leaves ash. Ash leaves nothing.

He closed the ledger and sat with his hands upon it, and outside the rain went on falling, patient as ever, and far down the dark lane a light came on in the vicarage window again, briefly, as though someone there were also awake, also counting.

Sixty-four names. One night coming, sooner than he liked, when carrying them would mean running.

Silas blew out the candle, put the ledger back beneath the floorboard, and lay awake until dawn, rehearsing the weight of a coat.
