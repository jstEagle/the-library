## 2. What the Widow Knows

Edith Marchmont had learned to read the post before she read the letters.

Twelve years of widowhood taught a woman that envelopes carried weather. The thick cream stock with the crest of a London solicitor meant money, or the absence of it, dressed in courtesy. The thin grey paper from the regimental association meant another anniversary dinner she must attend and smile through. But the buff-coloured envelope, official, bearing the stamp of the War Office Records Office in Chancery Lane—that was new, and new things in Edith's world were almost never kind.

She sat in the morning room at Ashcombe House with the letter unopened on her knee for a full quarter of an hour. The fire had been laid but not lit; April was fickle, and Mrs. Tilly the housekeeper economised where she could, though neither of them would ever say so aloud. Through the long window Edith could see the stone bridge over the Avon at the bottom of the park—her husband's bridge, the village called it still, though Colonel Alistair Marchmont had been eleven years dead and the masonry had been paid for by public subscription and a generous grant he had secured by writing letters she now suspected he had not written alone.

She opened the letter at last.

*Madam,—It has come to the attention of this office that certain documentation pertaining to the late Colonel A. R. Marchmont, D.S.O., 2nd Battalion, Royal Wessex Regiment, may bear upon an inquiry currently in progress concerning irregularities in casualty returns for the South African campaign (1899–1902). We write to inform you that one S. Amberdale, formerly of the Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service, has been identified as a holder or custodian of private records relevant to said returns. This office will be in contact regarding the disposition of such materials.*

Edith read it three times. *S. Amberdale.* The name sat strangely in her mind, like a key that fit a lock she hadn't known her house possessed. She knew it—of course she knew it. Silas Amberdale had nursed at the base hospital at Wynberg while Alistair lay fevered with enteric; he had come home ahead of the colonel, and after, when the colonel's letters grew shorter and his hand less steady, it was Amberdale who had written to say the colonel was recovering, do not alarm yourself, madam. He had appeared once at Ashcombe after the funeral, standing at the edge of the churchyard crowd in a black coat gone green at the seams, and had left before the first spade of earth turned.

And now the Records Office wrote his name to her as though delivering a summons.

The word beneath all the official language was *irregularities*. Edith set the letter down on the small table beside her chair, aligned its edges with the blotter, and looked for a long time at nothing.

She was not a foolish woman. She had been married nine years to a man who came home from war decorated and diminished, and in those years she had learned to hear what lived underneath his sentences. When he said *the men were seen to*, she heard the pause before it. When he spoke of Paardeberg, he spoke of terrain and supply and never of the men themselves, as though they had been weather rather than people. Once—only once—he had woken at night calling a name that was not hers: *Prosser. Prosser, hold the line.* Josiah Prosser, his batman, listed among the dead of February 1900, buried where he fell, no grave to visit, no pension beyond a standard sum dispatched to a mother in Bristol who had written twice and then stopped.

Alistair had built the bridge the year before he died. He had stood at its railing in the rain and told Edith it was for the village, for the carts and the schoolchildren, and she had believed him then, and perhaps it was even true. But she had also watched him stand there as though waiting for someone who would never cross it.

Her livelihood—she did not flatter herself otherwise—rested on the honour. The D.S.O., the regimental dinners, the lectures she gave to the girls' school each Founder's Day about duty and sacrifice, the modest annuity from the memorial fund that kept Ashcombe's roof patched. Take away the clean story of Colonel Marchmont and what remained? A widow in a draughty house with a bridge nobody needed and a name that might curdle in the mouth of every family in the parish whose sons had served under him.

*Irregularities in casualty returns.*

She rose, crossed to the desk beneath the window, and unlocked the drawer where she kept the things that were not for display. Her own small archive: Alistair's campaign letters in their ribbon-tied bundle, the citation, two telegrams. And at the bottom, folded into quarters so many times the creases had begun to fray—a letter in a hand she had never identified, unsigned, received six months after the funeral.

*You were loved, madam, and lied to gently. Ask your husband's officers why the returns are short. Ask them who counted.*

She had asked no one. For twelve years she had kept the folded paper the way one keeps a stone in the shoe: proof, always present, that something was wrong with the ground she stood on—and never once taken out to be examined, because to examine it would be to act, and to act might cost her everything.

Now the Records Office had acted for her.

* * *

Silas Amberdale came to Ashcombe House on a Thursday, under a pretense so transparent that Edith nearly smiled when Mrs. Tilly announced it.

"He says he's come about the memorial fund, ma'am. Says there's a discrepancy in the subscriptions ledger."

"A discrepancy," Edith repeated. "How fashionable discrepancies have become this season."

He stood in the morning room looking exactly as she remembered him from the churchyard—careful, contained, a man assembled from economy, every movement costing precisely what it needed to and no more. His coat was better than his boots. His hands were a nurse's hands, scrubbed fine and scarred faintly at the wrists where buckles and splints had worked against the skin for years.

"Mrs. Marchmont." He inclined his head. "Thank you for receiving me."

"You've come about the fund."

"I've come about the fund," he agreed gravely, and she saw that he knew she knew, and that he had decided to let the pretense stand between them like a screen, behind which they might speak more honestly than they could face to face. It was, she thought, a very nursing thing to do. You did not look directly at the wound. You worked around it, cleanly.

"Tea?" she said.

"Thank you, no."

She poured one cup for herself anyway, for something to do with her hands. "I received a letter from Chancery Lane."

Something shifted in his face—not fear, exactly. A settling. "So did I. Eleven days ago."

"They name you."

"They name everyone eventually, Mrs. Marchmont. That is rather their profession." He stood by the cold fireplace, and she noticed he did not sit because she had not invited him to, though she should have. "I've come because you'll receive a second letter soon. They'll ask you whether your late husband kept private papers. Returns. Lists. Anything outside the official record."

"And if he did?"

"Then they'll ask you to surrender them. And if you don't—" he paused, choosing, "—they'll send someone who asks less politely."

Edith set down her cup. "Colonel Marchmont kept excellent papers. His correspondence was meticulous. The regimental historians have said so publicly."

"Yes," Silas said quietly. "Meticulous. That's rather the trouble with meticulous men, isn't it. They notice what's missing."

The silence stretched. Rain had begun against the window, soft and businesslike, and down at the bottom of the park the river took it without complaint.

"There was a man called Prosser," Edith said.

It was not a question, but she watched him receive it like a blow he had been braced for twelve years. His hands, folded before him, tightened very slightly.

"Josiah Prosser," he said. "Sergeant's stripes pending. Your husband's batman. He held the kopje line at—" and then he stopped himself, jaw working, and Edith understood suddenly, completely, that this man had carried the sentence unfinished inside him since 1900, and that finishing it here, in her morning room, over untouched tea, would change both their lives past retrieving.

"You know how he died," she said.

"I know how he wasn't recorded to have died."

"And the others?"

"The others," Silas said, "are the reason I'm here."

* * *

After he had gone—the pretense formally restored at the door, *the subscription ledger, madam, I shall send my corrections within the week*—Edith stood a long while at the window watching the road until the rain blurred it.

She went to the desk. She unlocked the drawer. She took out the folded letter, the stone from her shoe, and unfolded it at last, smoothing the creases flat with her palm as though ironing linen.

*Ask them who counted.*

Then she drew out a sheet of her own good paper, dipped her pen, and wrote to the Records Office:

*I have received your communication. Before I answer it, I require to know one thing: whether the materials in the custody of Mr. S. Amberdale concern the men only—or whether they concern my husband.*

She sealed it. And then she sat with it unposted on the desk before her, understanding perfectly well which answer would save her and which would ruin her, and knowing also—with the terrible clarity that had visited her once before, at a churchyard's edge, watching a man leave before the earth was turned—that she could no longer afford not to know.

In the drawer, beneath the citation and the ribbons, she found Alistair's old field notebook, the one she had never dared read. She opened it to the last used page.

Three names. Written in his failing hand, over and over, like penance: *Prosser. Prosser. Prosser.*

Outside, the rain thickened toward evening, and somewhere along the London road, she did not doubt, Mr. Amberdale was already packing.
