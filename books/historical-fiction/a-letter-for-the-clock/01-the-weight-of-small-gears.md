## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The bell over the door of Halloway & Sons rang at half past seven, and Zephyrine Oyelaran knew before he looked up that it would be trouble.

Not because of the hour — though the shop did not open until eight, and honest customers waited in the cold like everyone else — but because of the way the bell sounded. He had tuned that bell himself two winters past, filing a hair's breadth from its lip until it spoke in a clear B above middle C, bright enough to carry over the ticking of forty clocks without ever becoming shrill. He knew its voice the way a mother knows her children's footsteps on stairs. This morning it rang wrong: clipped short, half-swallowed, as though someone had caught it in their fist to silence it.

He set down his tweezers and the pallet stone he had been seating into a carriage watch no larger than a locket, and he looked up.

The man in the doorway wore the grey livery of the civic service, unadorned but for a brass button polished so faithfully it threw back the lamplight like a small false sun. His boots were wet with river mud, and he stood with the particular stillness of men whose business is delivering things that other people do not wish to receive.

"Shop's not open," called Mr. Halloway from the back room, where the smell of solder flux and strong tea mingled into the single scent Zephyrine thought of, always, as *home*.

"I'm not a customer." The man's voice was flat as slate. "I've a delivery for the house of Halloway. From the Observatory."

Zephyrine felt something shift in his chest, some small internal weight settling onto a new pivot, the way an escapement shifts when the mainspring is first wound.

The observatory stood on Mount Serel, above the city of Vellamare like a lantern held over dark water. Everyone in the river city knew it by sight; few had stood inside it. Its dome caught the morning light and threw it back in copper fire, and beneath it, they said, ticked the Master Clock — the great regulator against which every lesser clock in Vellamare was set, the heartbeat of the whole valley, the voice that told the docks when the tide would turn and told the guildhalls when the markets opened and told the churches when to call the faithful. Zephyrine had seen it once, at nine years old, from the deck of his father's ferry: a tower pale as bone against the sky, and he had pressed his palm flat against the rail and thought, with a child's absolute seriousness, *I will work on that someday.*

His father had laughed — kindly, but he had laughed. Ferry-men's sons did not work on the Master Clock. Ferry-men's sons worked ferries, or found trades, or went downriver like so many others, and Vellamare did not spend its fine mechanisms on them.

* * *

The messenger set the parcel on the counter between the watch cases and the tray of balance springs, and Zephyrine understood immediately why the bell had been silenced. The package was wrapped in rice paper — actual rice paper, imported, the kind used for letters between persons of consequence — and sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Into the wax, before it cooled, someone had pressed a signet: a stylized eye above a crescent, ringed in lettering too small to read at arm's length.

The governor's seal.

Zephyrine had seen it twice in his life, both times from across a square, on proclamations nailed to the customs-house door. Once it had announced a festival. Once it had announced a tax. It had never, in living memory, come to a clockmaker's shop on the Weft Row, sandwiched between a chandler and a shop that sold nothing but buttons.

"Mr. Halloway," Zephyrine said, and his voice came out steadier than he expected. "You'll want to see this."

There was a pause in the back room — the pause of a man deciding whether the world could be made to wait while he finished his tea — and then Edmund Halloway appeared in the doorway wiping his hands on his apron. He was sixty-two years old and built like the furniture he admired: squat, dark, made to last. Forty years of benchwork had bent him slightly forward at the shoulders, so that he seemed perpetually leaning into whatever stood before him, examining it, weighing it, finding its flaw.

He weighed the parcel now. He did not touch it.

"Who brought this?"

"I did," said the messenger. "From the office of the Keeper of the Observatory. By instruction of—" he glanced at the seal as if confirming what he already knew "—the Governor's court."

Halloway's jaw worked once, twice, the way it did when he was pricing a repair he didn't want to take. "And the message?"

"The clock has stopped."

Three words. The messenger said them the way a physician says *it's spreading*, and Zephyrine watched his master's face do something complicated and quick — surprise, then disbelief, then a flicker of something that looked almost like fear before pride clamped down over it like a case lid.

"Stopped," Halloway repeated. "The Master Clock doesn't stop. That's rather the point of it."

"It stopped eleven days ago," said the messenger. "At seventeen minutes past four in the afternoon. It has not run since."

Eleven days. Zephyrine turned the number over in his mind the way he'd turn a worn coin, feeling for its edges. Eleven days of silence from the tower on Mount Serel — and now he thought of it, the city *had* been quieter, hadn't it? The church bells of Saint Ferreol's had taken up the hours in the observatory's absence, but they were small bells, near bells, bells for one parish and not for a city. The harbor bells still rang the tide, because tides kept their own time and asked nothing of gears. But there was a hole in the sound of Vellamare. There was a place where the heartbeat should have been, and it had been silent for eleven days, and Zephyrine had noticed only now, only when it was named, which shamed him a little. A clockmaker who does not hear a missing clock is like a physician who does not notice a missing pulse.

"And they want us," Halloway said slowly, "to fix it."

"They want *someone* to fix it. The Governor's office extends its courtesy to the house of Halloway & Sons, being the foremost practitioners of the horological art in Vellamare." The messenger recited it like a man reading off the inside of his own eyelids. "There is a letter."

He left. The bell rang behind him — properly this time, full and clear, B above middle C — and the shop settled back around them, forty clocks ticking their forty small certainties, and the rice-paper parcel lying on the counter like a sleeping thing none of them wanted to wake.

* * *

Halloway did not open it at once. He carried it to the back room and set it on the bench beside the tea things, and then he stood looking at it for a long moment, and Zephyrine, pretending to sort screw drawers in the adjoining room, watched through the doorway and learned more from his master's back than most apprentices learn from a decade of lectures.

Edmund Halloway was afraid.

Not visibly. Not the way a journeyman flinches when a spring lets go under his pliers. It was smaller than that, and therefore worse: the way he squared the parcel to the edge of the bench, twice; the way he took up his tea, remembered it was cold, set it down again without drinking; the way his thumb moved over his knuckles, counting them, an old habit Zephyrine had seen only when the shop's accounts were bad.

Finally he broke the seal. The wax parted cleanly — good wax, expensive wax — and inside lay a single sheet of that same rice paper, covered in a clerk's immaculate hand, and beneath the writing, at the foot of the page, a second signature in ink so black it seemed cut into the paper rather than laid upon it.

Halloway read it standing. Then he sat down. Then he read it again, and Zephyrine saw his lips move slightly, and that frightened Zephyrine more than anything else, because Edmund Halloway read everything aloud to his apprentices as a matter of pedagogy — *listen, boy, this is how a gentleman phrases a complaint; mark the passive voice, it means he's angry* — and if he was reading silently, it was because the words were not meant for apprentices' ears.

"Master?" Zephyrine said. "Shall I put water on for fresh tea?"

It was a small kindness dressed as a chore, and Halloway knew it, and something in his shoulders eased by a degree. "Yes. And then you can stop lurking in my screw drawer, Zeph, and come look at this, because I find I don't understand it alone."

The letter, once Zephyrine had filled the kettle and stood at his master's shoulder, was brief. The Master Clock at the Observatory of Mount Serel had ceased operation on the fourteenth day of the present month. The Keeper, one Ambrose Pell, had been unable to restore it. The Governor, Aldous Renwick, desiring that the city be served by accurate time before the season turned and the bridge closed for winter, requested — the word *requested* was doing laborious work in that sentence, hauling a load it was never built to carry — the attendance of the foremost horological practitioner in Vellamare, at the earliest possible date. Expenses to be borne by the court. A reply was requested by nightfall.

"Foremost horological practitioner in Vellamare," Halloway said. He said it the way a man reads his own name on a debt notice. "That's me. There are six of us with shops on the Row and I'm the oldest, and oldest apparently means foremost."

"You *are* the best," Zephyrine said, and meant it.

"I'm the best at watches." Halloway tapped the letter. "Towers are a different animal. Big works want big hands and a taste for ladders and cold. And the Master Clock — Zeph, nobody outside the observatory has touched that movement in thirty years. Pell keeps his own keys, his own oil, his own secrets. If it's stopped and *he* can't mend it—"

"Then it wants a fresh eye."

"Then it wants a scapegoat." Halloway said it flatly, without drama, the way he stated facts about metal: brass expands in heat; steel holds an edge; governors eat clockmakers when their clocks fail. "Think, boy. Eleven days the city's been telling time by church bells and guesswork. Merchants screaming. The dockmasters screaming louder. Somebody important is embarrassed, and when somebody important is embarrassed, somebody expendable gets handed the bill. You know how many times in my life I've been summoned to a great house to mend a fine piece? Four. And do you know how many times the fault was mine? None. And do you know how many times I was blamed anyway?"

"...Two?"

"Two." Halloway smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "The Duchess of Marle's automaton harpsichord — a cracked comb, cast badly at the factory, nothing to do with me — and I spent three days in her anteroom while she decided whether to ruin my name. The Countess Vashti's astronomical regulator — dust in the train, ordinary dust, the kind that gets in everywhere — and she docked my fee and told her friends I was careless. Fine work goes wrong, Zeph, and when fine work goes wrong, the great ones do not blame the weather or the metal or their own neglect. They blame the hands. And the hands nearest to them are—" he gestured, taking in Zephyrine entirely, the rolled sleeves, the ink-stained fingers, the face that still carried its father's river-broad cheekbones "—hands like ours."

"We're not nobodies anymore, though," Zephyrine said quietly. "You built the cathedral turret clock. They gave you the commission in open competition."

"And it runs beautifully, and it cost me my savings when the oak for the case warped, and the chapterhouse paid me half and called the loss my tuition." Halloway pushed the letter away across the bench as if it might be contagious. "Quiet ambition is a fine thing, boy. I've watched you wind it like a spring these five years — careful, patient, always a quarter-turn at a time. But ambition in a small shop is safe. Ambition inside the Governor's court is a gear tooth in a machine that doesn't care if it grinds you."

Zephyrine looked at the letter. At the black signature. At the seal's blind waxen eye.

He thought about the tower.

* * *

He thought about it all that day, through the mending of a music box and the regulation of a marine chronometer that a ship's captain had sworn ran mad but which proved, on examination, merely lonely — its owner wound it irregularly and blamed the instrument, an old story. He thought about it while he ate bread and cheese standing at the side window, watching the light come gold-then-grey across the Weft Row. He thought about it while he trimmed the shop's regulator, the one true clock they owned, setting it against the church bell that was itself, these eleven days, guessing.

Ambition was not the whole of it. He would have liked to pretend otherwise, later, in the years when honesty became his trade — but that evening, standing in the lamplight among the ticking, what he felt was simple and enormous and slightly shameful: he wanted to see the movement. He wanted it the way other men wanted to see foreign countries. The Master Clock. The great escapement, the great train, the works that governed a city. Every apprentice dreamed of fine work, and here was the finest work in the valley asking, in rice paper and blood-colored wax, to be saved.

But underneath the wanting ran a colder thread, and the colder thread was his master's voice: *somebody expendable gets handed the bill.* Because Zephyrine knew about expendable. He was the son of a ferry-man who had gone downriver with a cargo and never come up again — lost to the spring flood when Zephyrine was twelve, no body, no grave, a line in the harbormaster's book and a widow's pension that stopped after two years on a technicality. He had come up the hill at fourteen with a tool roll his father had owned and a letter of introduction forged by his own hand in a fair imitation of a priest's script, and Edmund Halloway had looked at the forgery, looked at the boy, and said: *"You hold a file well. Show me tomorrow, and we'll say nothing about the letter."* He had never once mentioned it since. It was, Zephyrine suspected, the closest thing to love his master knew how to speak.

Apprentices were the cheapest thing in a clockmaker's inventory. Cheaper than brass. Brass could be melted down and sold.

The kettle whistled. Halloway waved it away impatiently, which meant he had decided something, and Zephyrine poured anyway and set the cup at his elbow, and waited.

"I'm sixty-two years old," Halloway said at last, to the tea rather than to Zephyrine. "My knees object to ladders. My lungs object to mountain air in November. And if I go up that mountain and fail, the house of Halloway & Sons fails with me — thirty years of reputation, gone in one winter, because the Governor needed someone to stand in front of the mob and be *foremost*. But if I refuse the Governor's courtesy—" he turned the phrase over with distaste "—refusals have longer memories than failures. Refusal follows a man. Failure, at least, stays where it happened."

"So you'll send someone."

"So I'll send someone." Halloway finally drank the tea, grimaced, drank again. "And the someone will be an apprentice, because an apprentice who fails is an apprentice who was insufficiently supervised, and the master apologizes, fines himself a fee, and lives. Whereas a master who fails is a master, full stop." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "That's the arithmetic of it, Zeph. Ugly arithmetic. I won't dress it up as anything else. If you go up that mountain and the clock defeats you, I will say publicly that you went beyond your training and I'll bear the censure, and it will cost me money and not my name. That's what the arrangement buys. That's what you're worth to me and what I'm worth to you, written plainly."

It was the most honest speech his master had ever made him, and Zephyrine loved him for it even as the cold thread tightened. *Expendable.* Said aloud, at least. Said aloud was worth something. Most masters never said it aloud; they simply sent the boy.

"And if I mend it?" Zephyrine asked.

Halloway looked at him for a long moment over the rim of the cup. Outside, the gaslamps were coming on along the Weft Row, one by one, little orange suns in the blue dusk, and somewhere below a barge horn moaned on the river, and above it all — silent, eleven days silent — hung the invisible tower on the invisible mountain.

"If you mend it," Halloway said slowly, "you'll be the apprentice who fixed the Master Clock. Every guildmaster in Vellamare will know your name by spring. And every guildmaster in Vellamare will also know exactly how much trouble you're worth to steal, and the Governor's court will know your face, and neither of those things is entirely a blessing. But yes. You'd have your name." He set down the cup. "You'll need to leave tonight or at first light. The bridge closes when the snows come, and the letter says before the season turns. Take your heavy roll. Take the good loupe, not the cracked one. And Zeph—"

"Yes, Master?"

"Whatever you find up there, however strange it looks, however it got that way — mend the clock first. Understand the rest afterward. Promise me. Curiosity is a fine quality in a craftsman and a fatal one in a witness."

It was an odd choice of word. *Witness.* Zephyrine noted it, the way he noted a burr on a pinion — small, sharp, out of place, worth remembering.

"I promise," he said, and began rolling his tools into the leather cloth, each piece into its pocket, the familiar ritual calming his hands while his mind ran ahead of him up the dark mountain road. Pliers, screwdrivers, the stake, the broaches, the oilers with their fine wire tips. The good loupe.

Outside, the first flakes of the season's snow came down through the lamplight, small and dry and early, and far upriver — though the Weft Row was too low to see it, and too loud with ticking to hear it — the observatory sat in the gathering dark with its stopped heart, keeping, for the moment, its secret.

The reply went back to the court by nightfall, as requested. It said: *The house of Halloway & Sons accepts the Governor's courtesy. Our apprentice, Zephyrine Oyelaran, will attend the Observatory at first light.*

It was signed, in his master's careful hand, with Zephyrine's own name written beneath it — the first time, but not the last, that his name would travel uphill toward the tower on Mount Serel.
