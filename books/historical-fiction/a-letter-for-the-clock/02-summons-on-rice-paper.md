## 2. Summons on Rice Paper

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a rice-paper envelope so thin the seal showed through it like a bruise under skin.

Zephyrine was at the bench when Halloway brought it up from the street, and he knew before his master spoke that something was wrong — not because of the envelope, though the governor's crest pressed into green wax would have been reason enough, but because of the way old Halloway held it. By one corner. The way a man holds something he has already decided not to touch again.

"Read it," Halloway said.

*To the master of the house of Halloway & Sons, Clockmakers, Vellamare.* The script was a clerk's script, precise and bloodless. *His Excellency Governor Aldous Renwick requires the services of a competent horologist at the Observatory of St. Erasmus, there to effect repairs upon the great clock, which has ceased to keep time. You will present yourself within three days. Lodging and victuals provided by the governor's office. By order of His Excellency's secretary.*

Below it, the seal. A river coiled around a tower — Renwick's device, stamped into every proclamation, every tax notice, every warrant that had ever crossed the counter of the shop.

"The observatory clock," Zephyrine said, and could not help it: the words came out with wonder in them, naked as a boy's. "The master clock. Father Ambrose Pell's clock."

"There is no such thing as *Father* Ambrose Pell anymore. He's Keeper Pell now, if he keeps anything." Halloway set the summons down on the bench between them, still by its corner, as though it might bite. "It stopped eleven days ago. You know what they're saying in the coffeehouses."

"They say it stopped at 4:17."

"They say more than that." Halloway wiped his hands on his apron, though his hands were already clean; it was what they did instead of wringing them. "They say nothing else in this city has run right since. That the tide came in late twice. That the ferries are arguing over their schedules like gulls over a fish head. Superstition, mostly. But the clock itself —" He shook his head. "I've never seen it. Nobody outside the observatory has seen it in thirty years. It's not a clock, boy. It's a cathedral with hands."

Zephyrine looked at the summons and felt the wonder swell in him again, warm as solder melting. The master clock of St. Erasmus. Nine feet of case, they said. A escapement the size of a dinner plate. Bells cast in the capital before the flood walls went up, tuned so that their strike carried from one bank of the Vel to the other. Every apprentice in Vellamare grew up on stories of it the way other children grew up on saints' lives. And here it was, summoned to him — well, summoned to Halloway, but Halloway was sixty-eight years old and had not climbed a stair without pausing since the winter of the chest fever.

"You'll go," Zephyrine said. Not asking. Hoping aloud.

"I will not."

The word fell between them and lay there.

"Master—"

"Sit down, Zephyrine."

He sat. The shop smelled of oil and brass filings and the cold coming off the canal through the ill-fitted window, the smell he had loved for six years now, the smell that meant *here, this place, this work*. Outside, a cart rattled past toward the bridge, and somewhere below a gull screamed its idiot joy.

Halloway lowered himself onto the stool across from him, joints cracking like small mechanisms engaging. For a long moment the old man said nothing. Then he turned the summons over with one finger, careful, and read the back of it, where no writing was, and turned it face-up again.

"When I was your age," Halloway said, "a man named Corvess had this shop. Before me. You never heard of him because there's nothing left to hear. He got a summons just like this one — different governor, same seal — to repair a clock for a man who owned half the salt trade. Fine work. The finest. And when the clock ran slow afterward — three minutes a week, an ordinary failure, the kind we fix before breakfast — the owner decided he'd been cheated. Decided it was deliberate. And Corvess, who had done nothing but honest work with honest hands, found out what it means to be an apprentice of the powerful." He looked up. "They took his tools first. Then his license. Then his shop, on a debt nobody could ever find the ledger for. He died in the debtor quarter two years later, and I buried him, and I have thought about that burial every day of my working life."

"That was thirty years ago. Governors change."

"Governors don't change. They rotate." Halloway tapped the seal with a fingernail, once, lightly, the way you tap a crystal to hear if it's cracked. "You know what happens to apprentices when fine work goes wrong? Nothing good. Apprentices are the parts you can replace. Masters are expensive. When a job for the powerful fails, someone must be blamed, and blame flows downhill like water. I am too old and too stubborn to be water. But you—" He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been tenderness or might have been arithmetic. "You are exactly the age Corvess was."

"So send me anyway," Zephyrine said quietly, "and stay here yourself."

"No."

"Then let me go alone."

The silence stretched. Down in the street, the lamplighter was making his rounds early, and the light through the window went amber, then gold, then the blue-gray of evening on water.

"It stopped at 4:17," Halloway said at last. "Eleven days. Do you know how hard it is to stop a clock of that quality at a chosen minute? Escapements don't fail at convenient times. Springs don't break to the second. Either it died naturally, which is possible after forty years even for a cathedral, or—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, as though the sentence were a tool he shouldn't have picked up. "Or somebody wanted it stopped. And either way, whoever goes up that hill walks into a question that has nothing to do with gears."

"Then I'll answer only the gear questions."

"You'll try." Halloway rose, groaning, and went to the cabinet where the good tools lived — not the display set, the *real* set, the ones with worn boxwood handles and blades honed until they whispered. He began laying them out on a leather roll with the economy of long habit: screwdrivers, pin-vice, the jeweler's loupe, tweezers fine enough to lift a hair spring, a burnisher, a small hammer with a brass face. "If you're going to be foolish, be foolish prepared. You'll take my roll. If you come back without it, don't come back at all."

Zephyrine stared at him. "You said you wouldn't let me go."

"I said I wouldn't go. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm old and you're not, and one of us should see the inside of that clock before we die." He rolled the tools with quick, fierce movements, cinched the leather ties. "But you listen to me, boy, and you listen all the way to the bottom. Up there, you fix the clock. You look at nothing else. You sign nothing except a receipt for your tools. You eat what they give you and drink less than they offer. And if anyone asks you what you saw inside that case — anyone, from the Keeper to the governor himself — you saw a broken clock and nothing more. Can your mouth do that?"

"Yes," Zephyrine said. And then, because lying deserved honesty somewhere: "I think so."

"Nobody thinks so. You do it or you drown." Halloway pushed the roll across the bench. His hands, for the first time Zephyrine could remember, were not quite steady. "Go home. Sleep. Take the morning packet boat. And Zephyrine —"

"Master?"

"Come back."

* * *

The packet boat left from Coal Wharf at seven, when the river fog was still deciding whether to be weather or simply atmosphere, and Zephyrine stood at the rail with his tool roll slung across his chest like a bandolier and watched Vellamare come apart into layers.

First the wharves dissolved, the cranes going soft at their edges. Then the warehouse roofs. Then the spire of the Guildhall, then the bridge itself — the causeway bridge, the great seasonal span that crossed the Vel above the falls and made the city two cities stitched together — until there was only the deck under his boots, the gray slide of the river, and the sound of the engine worrying the water into wake.

Upriver meant against the current, which meant slow, which meant hours to watch the banks go by.

The first hour was beautiful, and he let it be beautiful. Mist standing in the reeds like the ghosts of shepherds. Herons lifting off on wings like folded letters. A fisherman mending net on a pontoon, raising one hand as they passed without looking up, the way men greet boats they have greeted ten thousand times. Zephyrine felt the summons in his breast pocket — he had brought it, though Halloway told him to leave it, because it seemed to him the kind of paper you did not leave anywhere — and felt his own heartbeat keeping easy time beneath it. Six years of benchwork. Six years of hairsprings and pivot-polishing and the endless patient grammar of small gears. And now the great clock itself. He caught himself smiling at the mist like a fool and did not care.

The debtor quarter began where the tanneries ended.

He knew it was coming. Everyone in Vellamare knew where it began — you could smell the boundary before you saw it, the sweet-rot stink of the lime pits giving way to woodsmoke and wet wool and something underneath both, something human and unwashed and close-packed. But knowing did not prepare him for the windows.

That was the thing about the debtor quarter that no one said plainly: the windows were shuttered. All of them. Not broken shutters, not lazy ones — closed, latched, drawn tight as purses, on a bright morning, in houses where children surely lived. As though the whole quarter had agreed upon a single posture toward the world: *we are not looking at you; do not look at us.*

The packet boat slowed to take on a crate at a landing stage that served the quarter, and Zephyrine, standing at the rail, found himself close enough to see details. A woman filling a bucket from the river, her motion practiced to the point of sleepwalking. Three men on a stoop who were not talking, merely sitting, the way men sit when conversation costs more than silence. A child — a girl, perhaps seven — standing in a doorway between two shuttered windows, staring at the packet boat with an expression he took a full minute to understand, because it was not longing and not curiosity.

It was assessment. She was counting the passengers. He watched her do it — lips slightly moving — and when her eyes reached him, tool roll across his chest, fine coat, apprentice's calluses hidden under clean cuffs, something in her face closed like the shutters above her head, and she stepped backward into the dark of the doorway and was gone.

The engine coughed. The boat drew away from the stage.

"Debtors' landing," the mate said beside him, spitting over the rail with the indifference of ritual. "Used to be busier."

"Used to be?"

"Mm." The mate coiled rope and did not elaborate, and the elaborateness of his not-elaborating was itself a kind of speech.

Zephyrine turned back to the bank. The shuttered houses slid past, and among them, now that he was looking, other things slid past too: a wall with the pale rectangles where posters had been torn away rather than allowed to fade. A church with its bell gone from the tower — he could see the empty yoke, the beam where the rope would hang, cut off clean. A barge loaded with crates sitting low in the water, guarded by four men who were too well-dressed to be dockworkers and too bored to be anything but paid, and whose crates bore no markings at all, which in a city that marked everything marked them most of all.

Downriver, people said the ships went south with goods. Grain, timber, salt. Everyone said it. The saying was smooth with use.

But the girl had counted passengers, and the windows were shut, and the bell was gone from the church, and the crates had no names, and the fog was lifting now, and the observatory was visible ahead — St. Erasmus on its hill above the northern bend, white tower, black dome, the handless clock face catching the first true sunlight like a coin at the bottom of clear water.

Handless. He hadn't known that. From the river you could see that the great dial had no hands at all — or rather, that its hands hung, both of them, frozen at positions he was too far away to read but which he suddenly, unreasonably, needed to read. 4:17. Eleven days. The whole city telling time by a dead clock and pretending otherwise, the way the debtor quarter pretended otherwise, the way the mate spat over the rail and said nothing.

The wonder was still in him — that was the strange part, the part he would remember. It had not been replaced by unease. It had been *curdled* by it, the way milk curdles: still milk, still sweet-smelling at a distance, but changed in its nature, grainy, liable to separate without warning. He wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, to open that case and put his hands inside the cathedral of gears and make it live again. And underneath the wanting, laid over it like a second escapement running at a different rate, ticked the questions he had promised his master not to ask:

Who stops a clock at a chosen minute?

And what does a city become, the longer it tells time by a lie?

The packet boat rounded the bend. The tannery smell gave way to pine and cold stone, and the landing below St. Erasmus came into view — a single wooden stage, a winding path, and one figure waiting at the top of the path with a lantern lit against the last of the fog, though the sun was fully up.

Old, Zephyrine guessed, even at this distance. Old, and standing very straight, the way men stand when they have decided that bending is a luxury they can no longer afford.

Keeper Pell, waiting for the clockmaker who was coming to fix a broken clock.

Zephyrine tightened the strap of the tool roll across his chest, and climbed toward the shore.
