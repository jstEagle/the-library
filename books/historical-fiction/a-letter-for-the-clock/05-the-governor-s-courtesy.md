## 5. The Governor's Courtesy

The governor came at dusk, which was itself a kind of message.

Men of power in Vellamare arrived when they wished to be seen — midmorning for inspections, noon for ceremonies, their shadows short and honest beneath them. But Governor Aldous Renwick chose the hour when the light was failing, when the observatory's long windows had gone the color of pewter and the great clock's case stood half in darkness, so that a visitor might look at anything without seeming to look at it.

Zephyrine heard the carriage before he saw it: iron-shod wheels on the causeway stones, unhurried, the rhythm of a vehicle that had never once needed to hurry. He was elbow-deep in the striking train, a rag over his shoulder and grease to his second knuckle, and he had perhaps forty seconds to decide what an apprentice should be doing when the most powerful man in the river province walked through his door.

He decided on exactly what he had been doing. It seemed safest. A man caught pretending to work would be suspected of something; a man actually working could only be suspected of competence.

* * *

Keeper Pell met the carriage in the courtyard, and Zephyrine watched through the window as the old man bowed — not deeply, not obsequiously, but with the careful neutrality of a servant who had learned that enthusiasm invited scrutiny. Renwick descended without assistance. He was taller than Zephyrine expected, silver at the temples, dressed in grey wool so fine it made the dusk look shabby by comparison. He wore no visible chain of office. He did not need one. The two soldiers who remained with the carriage were chain enough.

"Keeper." The voice carried across the courtyard, warm as a hearth and about as safe to touch. "You've kept the stars waiting eleven days. I trust we haven't offended them past forgiveness."

"The sky forgives everything, Your Excellency," Pell said. "It's the city that keeps score."

Renwick laughed. It was a good laugh. Zephyrine would spend that evening learning how much labor went into making a laugh sound that good, and how much of the man behind it was labor too.

They came up the tower stair together, and Zephyrine arranged himself beside the clock case with his rag and his pliers, the picture of a craftsman interrupted. The lamplight caught Renwick first at the doorway — he paused there, deliberately, letting the room arrange itself around him — and then he crossed the floor with the easy stride of someone entering his own kitchen.

"So this is the patient." He laid a hand flat against the clock case, just above the escapement window, and Zephyrine felt something cold move down his spine — because the brass cylinder lay three inches beneath that palm, wrapped now in oilcloth in Zephyrine's tool roll, but the gesture had the quality of a man touching a wound he already knew the shape of. "Eleven days silent. Half the city sets its morning by this thing, you know. Bakers, boatmen, the harbor bells. When it stops, Vellamare holds its breath."

"Yes, Your Excellency," Pell said.

"And you are?" Renwick turned to Zephyrine with full attention, and Zephyrine understood at once why men lied badly in front of him. The attention was total, courteous, and utterly without mercy. It was the attention of a jeweler assessing a stone.

"Zephyrine Oyelaran, sir. Apprentice to Halloway & Sons, Clockmakers, Candle Street."

"Oyelaran." Renwick tasted the name. "A southern name. Your people keep time well?"

"We keep it as well as anyone can afford to, sir."

The laugh again, warmer this time, and Renwick's hand landed on Zephyrine's shoulder with a weight that was almost paternal. Almost. "I like him, Keeper. Honest hands." Then, to Zephyrine directly: "Can they fix it?"

"I believe so, Your Excellency. The fault is in the escapement region. Something lodged — a fragment of the case lining, most likely, come loose with the autumn damp. I've cleared it. The remainder is adjustment, cleaning, and patience."

It came out smooth. That was the remarkable part, the part Zephyrine would turn over later like a coin of suspicious mint: it came out smooth on the very first telling, no stumble, no tell, though every word of it was shaped around a hollow. *Something lodged.* True, in its way. A brass cylinder is a thing that lodges. *Come loose with the autumn damp.* False, and he knew it false, and the lie sat in his mouth like a well-machined part — fitted so precisely that even he could barely feel the seam.

He had never lied to power before. He discovered that he was good at it, and the discovery frightened him more than the governor did.

* * *

Renwick did not leave immediately. That was the performance of it — the courtesy, the lingering. He walked the length of the clock chamber with his hands clasped behind him, admiring the pendulum's slow arc, asking questions that revealed how much he already knew. Who had designed the striking train? (Vellamare's own Master Corvane, sixty years dead.) How many times did the hour bell speak at midnight? (Twelve, unless the correction cam engaged, in which case thirteen — a relic of an older calendar.) And how long, precisely, before the clock might be trusted again?

"A week," Zephyrine said. "Perhaps ten days, if I want the chimes true rather than merely loud."

"Ten days." Renwick nodded slowly, and looked out the long window at the darkening river, where the last barges were running downcurrent before the ice. "Then let us say before the season turns. The bridge closes soon — you know that? First snow seals the causeway, and nothing crosses until thaw. I should dislike to think of the city's heartbeat stopped all winter while her repairman sat comfortably trapped beside it." He smiled. It reached his eyes. That was the terrible thing about it; it genuinely reached his eyes. "You'll want to finish before then, I think. For your own sake as much as ours."

"Before the season turns," Zephyrine agreed. "Yes, sir."

"Splendid." Renwick turned back into the room, and for a moment his gaze drifted — casually, a man admiring craftsmanship — along the open tool roll on the bench, across the scattered plates and pinions, the small brass cylinder's empty cradle in the escapement where the obstruction had been. His eyes rested there perhaps two seconds. Perhaps less.

"You found the obstruction here?"

"In the escapement, sir. Wedged between the pallets."

"Mm. Curious place for case lining to wander." He said it lightly, a man making conversation, and moved on to admire the pendulum's suspension spring as though the thought had never existed. But Zephyrine had watched his father read weather in a customer's face for fifteen years, and he knew a barometer when he saw one. The question had been asked. The answer had been accepted. Whether it had been believed was a different instrument entirely.

At the door, Renwick paused. "Keeper. A word about provisions. My steward will see the observatory stocked against the winter — coal, flour, lamp oil. We look after our craftsmen." A beat, gentle as a closing drawer. "All of them."

He went down the stairs. The carriage ground away over the causeway stones, unhurried still, and the dusk closed behind it like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Pell did not speak for a long while. He stood at the window watching the carriage lamps shrink along the river road, two orange points swallowed at last by the bend below the debtor quarter, where the shuttered windows had been shuttered now for a season and showed no lamplight at all.

"He counted the tools," Pell said finally.

"What?"

"When he passed the bench. His eyes went left to right across the roll. He wasn't admiring your kit, boy — he was inventorying it." Pell turned. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had that morning, the way faces do after they have watched power pass close by. "That's how he does it. No threats. No questions a lawyer could object to. Just presence, and counting, and a smile that tells you he'll remember everything you didn't say. Eleven years I've kept this clock, and in eleven years he's visited four times. Every visit was a kindness. Every kindness was a leash."

Zephyrine wiped his hands slowly, the rag dragging grease across his knuckles. "He asked about the obstruction."

"I heard."

"It was a good answer."

"It was a quick answer," Pell said. "With him, those aren't always the same thing." He crossed to the clock case and stood looking into the escapement window, at the clean gap where the brass cylinder had spent eleven days strangling the heart of Vellamare's time. "You told him a week. Why not three? Get yourself gone before the snow."

"Because three days would have been a lie even he wouldn't buy," Zephyrine said. "And because—" He stopped. Because the ledger was real. Because the names in it were real. Because a clock that ran was a witness standing upright in the middle of the city, and he had not yet decided what it ought to say.

"Because?" Pell prompted.

"Because I said ten days, and I meant it. The work takes what it takes." He picked up his pliers again, needing the weight of them. "Your Excellency's steward may send whatever coal he likes, Keeper. I'll be here regardless. Before the season turns."

Pell looked at him for a long moment — the measuring look of a man deciding how much of a dangerous truth to hand to how young a pair of hands — and whatever he decided, he kept it behind his teeth. He banked the lamps instead, and the great clock's shadow folded over them both, patient as a held breath.

Outside, far downriver, the first flakes of the season's snow had begun to fall — soft, unhurried, each one a small white signature on the dark water, counting down toward the closing of the bridge.

And somewhere between the falling of one flake and the next, Zephyrine Oyelaran understood that he had just made his first promise to a liar: *before the season turns.*

He intended, for once, to keep a promise made to power — though not in the way power imagined.
