## 9. Keeper Pell's Confession

Ambrose Pell had not slept properly in eleven days, and he had stopped pretending to himself that he would.

He lay in his narrow bed above the observatory's north stair, listening to the building breathe. Old buildings breathed; he had learned that over forty years of keeping this one. The stones drank warmth by day and gave it back by night with a faint ticking of contraction, and in winter the wind found the weathervane's socket and played upon it like a reed. He knew every sound the observatory made the way a father knows the footsteps of his children. Which was why, on the eleventh night of silence from the master clock — eleven nights without the great escapement's heartbeat rising through the floorboards, eleven nights of a silence so total it felt like deafness — he lay awake and listened instead for the boy.

The apprentice was still below. Pell could hear him through two floors of timber and stone: the small industrious sounds of lamplight and cloth, the whisper of a file, once or twice the soft metallic *tick* of a part being set down on felt. Zephyrine Oyelaran worked the way water worked — patiently, without complaint, finding the shape of whatever contained him. Pell had watched him all evening through the doorway of the clock room, watched those dark hands move among the wheels with a reverence Pell remembered from his own youth, before reverence became routine and routine became fear.

*He is honest,* Pell thought, and then, as he had thought every hour for eleven days: *I hope to God he is honest, because I am too old and too frightened to be honest alone.*

He rose. His knees announced themselves as they always did, cracking like green wood, and he dressed in the dark by touch — the same touch that could set a pallet arbor blind, that could feel a bent tooth on a escape wheel through two thicknesses of glove. Forty years of keeping time had made his hands wiser than his eyes now were. He lit no lamp. He wanted, tonight, to be difficult to see.

The stairs down to the clock room were stone, worn into shallow smiles by a century of keepers' feet. Pell descended them slowly, one hand on the wall, and stood a long moment in the doorway watching the boy work.

The master clock filled the room the way a cathedral fills a nave. It had been built before either of their grandfathers drew breath — a great case of black walnut going soft with age at the base, its hood open now, its face a moon of white enamel above the city. Behind the enamel, visible through the opened back plate, the movement hung in its framework like a bronze skeleton: barrels and great wheels, the deadbeat escapement with its jeweled pallets, the nine-foot pendulum hanging motionless, dead as a hanged man. 4:17. The hands had not moved from 4:17 in eleven days, and every soul in Vellamare who looked up at the hill saw the same frozen minute and felt, without knowing why, a small cold hand close around the base of the spine. A city whose clock stops is a city holding its breath.

Zephyrine sat beneath it on a low stool, sleeves rolled, a loupe screwed into one eye, working at the escapement bridge with a burnisher no bigger than a matchstick. He did not hear Pell come in. Or perhaps he heard and chose not to show it; the boy was careful in ways that made Pell both hopeful and afraid.

"You've cleaned the third wheel twice," Pell said.

The boy did not startle — another point in his favor — but his shoulders changed, the way a man's do when a door opens behind him. "It wanted it," Zephyrine said. "There's verdigris in the pivot holes. Someone let damp into the case."

"Someone did." Pell came forward into the lamplight. "Someone let more than damp into it."

The burnisher stopped moving.

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was louder than the silence of the clock, because this one had a voice waiting inside it. Pell looked at the boy — really looked, the way he had failed to look at three other apprentices sent upriver before him, each turned away within a day with polite excuses about the state of the roads — and saw what he had hoped to see and dreaded to see: a young man deciding something.

"I know what you found," Pell said.

Zephyrine set down the burnisher with exaggerated care, aligning it parallel to the edge of the bench, the way apprentices are taught, the way Pell himself had taught boys thirty years gone. When he looked up, his face was composed, and it was the composure that frightened Pell most — the composure of someone who has already begun lying and found he is good at it.

"The escapement was fouled," Zephyrine said. "A brass cylinder, wedged deliberately between the pallets. I've never seen anything like it. It would have taken knowledge to place it there. Knowledge of the mechanism." A pause, delicate as a hairspring. "Keeper."

"And you took it out."

"I took it out."

"What did you do with it?"

Another pause. Longer. Outside, far below, the river muttered against the causeway pilings, and somewhere in the debtor quarter a dog began to bark and thought better of it. Zephyrine's eyes went — Pell watched them go, couldn't help watching — toward the tool roll at the end of the bench, the long leather wallet with its rows of slots, where a cylinder of brass the length of a forearm might lie flat among the files and be invisible unless you knew to unroll it.

Pell sighed, and the sigh seemed to empty him of something he had been carrying for eleven days, some ballast that had kept him upright and was now, suddenly, no longer needed or no longer bearable.

"Don't," he said quietly. "Don't lie to me, boy. I've watched you lie to Governor Renwick already — yes, I was in the room, I'm always in the room, nobody sees an old keeper any more than they see a chair — and you lied well, better than I could have, and it curdled my stomach to watch it because you should not lie well yet, you're too young to lie well. But don't lie to me. Not me. Not here."

The lamplight moved. The pendulum hung dead. And Zephyrine Oyelaran looked at him for a long moment with those steady dark eyes, and then reached out and laid two fingers on the tool roll, and said nothing at all — which was, Pell understood, its own kind of confession, and a more dignified one than words.

"Good," Pell said. His voice cracked on the word. He cleared his throat. "Good. Then sit down. No — stay standing if you like, but stop working. What I have to say will take longer than a pivot polish, and I'd rather you heard it with clean ears."

* * *

He told it badly at first, the way a man tells anything he has rehearsed too many times alone. He paced. He touched things — the case, the great wheel's rim, the cold brass of the seconds dial — because his hands needed occupation or they would shake visibly, and he was ashamed enough of the shaking he could not prevent in his voice.

"I have kept this clock for forty-one years," he began. "My father kept it before me, and his mother before him — she was the first keeper, when they built the tower, which is a story nobody believes and I'll not waste your time on. In forty-one years it has stopped four times. Twice for frost in the oiling. Once when lightning struck the vane and the surge jumped down the rod and fused a contact — a week's work, that was, and the whole city talked of nothing else for a month. And once," he said, "once on purpose. By my own hand."

Zephyrine had gone very still.

"There is a hatch," Pell said, "in the base of the case, behind the weight channel. Concealed — not cleverly, but sufficiently. My grandmother built it. Keepers' business goes in it: tide tables, correspondence from the capital guild, the private ledger of the observatory itself. For forty-one years it held nothing but dust and my grandmother's old letters, which I confess I read, one dull winter, and which were mostly complaints about her husband." He smiled briefly, terribly. "Eleven days ago it held a brass cylinder full of names."

"You put it there," Zephyrine said. Not a question. His voice had dropped into something flat and careful, the voice of a man walking across ice, testing each step before committing his weight. "You wedged it in the escapement yourself."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Why?" Pell laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle like a dropped bell. "Because I am a coward, boy. That is the whole of the answer and none of the answer. Because I found out what those papers said and I could not carry it and I could not destroy it and I could not give it to anyone who might believe me — so I gave it to the clock. To the only thing in Vellamare that cannot be bribed." He stopped pacing. He stood beneath the dead pendulum, a small old man in a shabby coat, dwarfed by the machinery above him, and his voice came down almost to a whisper. "Do you know what happens in this city when a debt goes unpaid? Truly unpaid? Do you know what happens to the families in the quarter you passed on your way upriver — you passed through it, I know you did, everyone does, the road allows no other way?"

Zephyrine said, slowly: "The windows were shuttered. Whole streets of them. As if the houses were closed for winter."

"They are closed for winter. They are closed for every season now." Pell's hands had found each other and gripped. "Three years ago the governor's court established the Relief Office. Fine name. Debtors who cannot pay are given a choice — a generous choice, they call it, merciful — indenture downriver, at the ironworks and the cane lands, their bonds sold to cover what they owe. Their families may follow when the debts clear." His mouth twisted. "The debts never clear. The interest compounds against their wages at rates no guild would print. And the bonds — the paper itself, the proof of what these people are worth, of how much blood was paid for them — the bonds pass through the court's hands. Through the governor's court. Through Magistrate Renwick's office and his clerks and his captains, and every bond that passes leaves a residue behind it, a little grease on the wheel, a percentage, a consideration. They are selling people, apprentice. And skimming the price."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river struck the pilings again, patient, endless.

"How do you know?" Zephyrine asked. "Truly know. Names and sums — you said names and sums—"

"Because I copied them." Pell said it fast now, the words tumbling like water from a breached weir, forty-one years of careful quiet collapsing all at once. "Eleven days ago a clerk came up with the quarterly returns to be filed in the archive — the observatory holds the city's sealed records, boy, has for a century, nobody thinks about us, nobody ever thinks about us, that is our entire value — and he left the strongbox unlocked while he took brandy with the under-warden. And I had been suspicious for a year. Suspicious of the shipments I saw from my windows. Suspicious of the shuttered streets. Suspicious of the way certain names vanished from the tax rolls without death certificates. So I opened the box, and I read, and then—" his voice broke, steadied— "and then I sat down at this very bench, at this very stool where you are sitting, and by lamplight, until dawn, I copied every page. Every name. Every date of indenture. Every sum received and every sum recorded, and the difference between them, which is the size of the theft. Two hundred and six souls in three years. Two hundred and six names, and beside each name a number, and the numbers do not lie, because numbers are the one language in which men cannot hide what they have done."

His breath went out of him. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, and when he took it away they were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it.

"Then I hid the original where no one would look for it — inside the clock itself, in the weight channel, wrapped in oilcloth — and I took my copy and rolled it tight and wedged it in the escapement. Between the pallets. Where it would jam the train and stop the clock." He gestured upward, at the frozen hands, the white face, the minute that had lasted eleven days. "Four-seventeen. I chose the time myself. The seventeenth minute. There were seventeen souls on the first shipment, the one they thought nobody counted."

Zephyrine had risen from the stool without seeming to decide to. He stood with his hand resting on the tool roll, on the copy hidden inside it, and Pell watched understanding arrive in him like weather crossing a valley — first the wind, then the shadow, then the rain.

"You wanted the clock repaired," Zephyrine said.

"I wanted *you*," Pell said. "Or someone like you. Three others came. I turned them away — told them the fault was beyond their skill, that the master must come himself, knowing the master would not come, knowing Halloway is too proud and too comfortable and too frightened of the governor's seal to cross the river in the turning season. I sent them home and I waited and I prayed, boy, I prayed like a heathen, prayed to gears and springs and the God who watches old men, that the fourth summons would find someone who could read a cipher. Because the ledger is written in cipher, isn't it. You've seen. Column after column of figures that mean figures, and figures that mean names, and figures that mean dates — I wrote it in the old customs code, my grandmother's code, from her letters, because I dared not write plain words. Plain words hang men. Figures only puzzle them."

The room was very quiet. Then Zephyrine reached into the tool roll, and drew it open along its seams with the deliberation of a man unveiling a finished work, and lifted out the brass cylinder, and set it on the bench between them with a click that sounded, in the silence, as loud as the striking of the hour.

"It's beautiful work," Zephyrine said quietly. "Whoever cut the threads on this cap knew what they were doing." He looked up. "You."

"My grandfather taught lock-making before he taught clocks. It skips a generation sometimes." Pell looked at the cylinder — his own cowardice, his own hope, lying gleaming under the lamp — and felt the old fear rise in him again, the fear that had walked with him for eleven days like a second shadow. "Now you know everything. Now you are in it, as deep as I am, deeper, because you are young and they will believe anything of the young. So I will tell you the last thing, the thing I have been carrying up these stairs and down again for two days trying to find the courage to say, and then you can take your tools and go home and tell no one, and I will understand. I will understand, Zephyrine Oyelaran, because I have been exactly as afraid as you are now, every hour of every day since I turned that first wheel."

"Tell me," the boy said.

"The ship," Pell said. "The *Meridian*. She lies at the winter dock below the cliff road, and she sails on the morning tide — eight days from now, if the weather holds, nine if it doesn't. She carries the last of the bond papers. The originals. The court means to send them downriver to the capital for 'consolidation,' which is a word that means burning. When the *Meridian* clears the headland, there is no evidence. There is no crime. There are two hundred and six names that no one living can prove ever existed, and shutters on the windows of a quarter that will stay shuttered forever." His voice dropped. "Everything — the copies, the cylinder, my testimony, whatever my testimony is worth from a mad old keeper — everything dies with that tide. Unless the city knows. Unless the truth gets off this hill and into the streets before the morning tide takes the last of it away."

* * *

For a long time after that, neither of them spoke.

Pell sank onto the stool at last — his own stool, forty-one years shaped to him — and watched the boy. Zephyrine had picked up the cylinder again and was turning it in his fingers, not reading it now but weighing it, his thumb moving over the engraved cap in slow circles, and Pell recognized the gesture because he had made it himself a hundred times over eleven sleepless nights. A craftsman's gesture. The gesture of a man measuring whether a thing can be borne.

"The bridge closes soon," Zephyrine said finally. His voice was strange, level, distant, a voice speaking from inside a calculation. "First snow seals the causeway. Everyone knows it. The governor ordered the clock repaired *before the season turns* — those were his words to me. Before the season turns." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible and young. "He wasn't talking about winter, Keeper. He was talking about witnesses. Once the snow falls, whoever is on this hill stays on this hill until spring. You. Me. Anyone he chooses to trap here with his crime."

"Yes," Pell said simply. "That is why I had to tell you tonight. Tomorrow the snows may come. Tonight you might still go down. Take the cylinder, take the ferry, take your life, and hate me forever for handing it to you — but go knowing. A man should choose his chains with his eyes open."

"And if I don't go?"

Pell had prepared answers for that question. He had prepared them lying awake in the dark above the north stair, had polished them like pivots, had fitted them together into speeches meant to persuade, to inspire, to shame. None of them came. What came instead was the truth, naked and trembling, and he gave it as it was.

"Then I will hand you the rest of it," he said, "the cipher key, the archive, everything I have, everything I am — and we will be two men on a hill with a stopped clock, trying to shout louder than a government." He laughed again, softly, and this time the laugh was almost real, almost warm, the laugh of a man setting down a weight he has carried so long he has forgotten his own posture without it. "Desperate hope, boy. That's all I have to give you. It burns going down. I won't pretend otherwise. I've burned my hands on it for eleven days and they haven't healed and they never will, whether we win or lose."

Zephyrine looked down at the cylinder in his hands. Outside, high above the river, the first clouds of the season had begun to assemble themselves over the western hills — Pell could see them through the tall window, gray and slow and heavy with what they carried — and the lamplight flickered once in a draft that smelled of iron and coming snow.

"Eight days," Zephyrine said. "Nine, if the weather turns." He was looking at the dead clock now, up at the frozen hands, at the great silent skeleton of wheels, and something was moving in his face that Pell had not seen there before — something that looked, God help him, like the beginning of a plan. "A clock that strikes true can tell the hour. But a clock is a machine for making patterns out of time, Keeper. And a pattern..." He turned the cylinder over once more, and set it down, gently, precisely, aligned parallel to the bench's edge. "A pattern can be read."

Pell's heart, which had been beating like a small frightened animal for eleven days, went very still.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying," Zephyrine Oyelaran said, reaching for the lamp to turn its flame higher against the gathering dark, "that you should show me the cipher key. All of it. And then show me the under-works of this tower — every floor, every gear, every bell. Because if that ship sails with those bonds aboard, it will not be for lack of noise on this hill."

Through the tall window, far below, the river ran black and patient toward the sea, and the first flakes of the season's snow came down out of the western dark like something being decided.
