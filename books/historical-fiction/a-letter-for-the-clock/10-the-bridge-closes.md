## 10. The Bridge Closes

The snow came in the night, the way bad news comes — quietly, and then all at once.

Zephyrine woke to a silence so complete it seemed to press against his ears. He had slept badly in the keeper's spare room, on a bed too short for him, beneath a quilt that smelled of camphor and old wool, and for a moment he could not account for the silence at all. The observatory was never silent. Even with the master clock stopped these eleven days, there were other voices: the tick of Pell's regulator clocks down the corridor, the wind worrying at the tower's copper roof, the river muttering below the cliff like an old man talking to himself in his sleep.

Now there was nothing. Nothing, except a light like hammered tin coming through the shutters, and the particular hush that Zephyrine knew from childhood — the hush of a world freshly buried.

He dressed in the dark, fingers finding buttons by habit, and went to the window. When he lifted the shutter, the cold came through the gap and bit his knuckles, and he understood, before he saw anything, what the snow had done.

The causeway bridge was gone.

Not gone — he corrected himself, pressing his forehead to the freezing glass — *hidden*. Two miles of timber trestle and iron span that stitched the observatory rock to the Vellamare shore still existed somewhere beneath the white, but the snow lay on it thick and seamless, drifted in long smooth curves where the wind had combed it across the planking, and at the far end, where the causeway met the toll road, the whole structure simply dissolved into the general white of the world. No figures moved along it. No cart tracks scored its surface. It looked less like a road than like a sentence someone had begun and then thought better of.

Below, the river had gone the color of gunmetal flecked with ash. Ice was forming along the margins in pale lace, and out in the channel the water moved dark and deliberate, carrying its cargo of floes down toward the sea, toward the docks, toward wherever the *Meridian* lay taking on whatever she took on.

*The morning tide,* Pell had said, three nights ago, pressing the brass cylinder into Zephyrine's hands as though handing him a coal straight from the fire. *She sails on the morning tide, boy, and everything she carries goes with her.*

Zephyrine stood at the window a long time, watching the snow come down again — fine, dry, indifferent snow, filling in what little the wind had left bare — and did the arithmetic that clockmakers do without meaning to. Eleven days the clock had been stopped. Nine days, perhaps eight now, until the tide that mattered. And between him and the city: two miles of bridge under two feet of snow, closing by the hour.

* * *

Pell was already in the clock room when Zephyrine climbed the tower stairs, standing before the stopped mechanism with his hands clasped behind his back like a man attending a funeral. He had lit only one lamp, and its glow made an island of warmth in the vast cold chamber, gilding the great wheel rims, the hanging weights, the pendulum suspended mid-swing forever at the end of some stroke it would never finish. The whole machine hung frozen in the attitude of a dancer caught between steps.

"You've seen the bridge," Pell said. It wasn't a question. The old man's eyes were red-rimmed; he had not, Zephyrine suspected, slept much either.

"The snow's sealed it."

"Sealed it." Pell tried the words out as though tasting something gone off. "That's the polite term. The harbor office will call it *closed for the season* by noon, you'll see. They always do. First snowfall, and the causeway's closed until thaw. Four months, if we're lucky. Five, if we're not." He turned to look at Zephyrine, and in the lamplight his face seemed older than it had a week ago, the lines around his mouth cut deeper, as if the confession had cost him flesh as well as sleep. "You understand what that means."

Zephyrine understood several things it meant, and said none of them aloud. It meant no more supplies up from the city except what could be rowed across in calm water, and the water would not be calm now. It meant no word traveling faster than a boat or a bird. It meant that whoever was in this building when the snow fell was in it until spring.

It meant he was trapped in a house with the crime, and — he was almost certain — with at least one of its authors.

"I'll want to get a message downriver," he said carefully. "To my master. Halloway will expect word that I've arrived safely, that the work is proceeding."

"Work *is* proceeding," Pell said. "Isn't it?"

"It is." That much was true. By day he worked at the clock honestly enough — cleaning the grime of eleven idle days from the escapement, checking every pivot, truing the bent suspension spring he'd found and set right. A clockmaker who could not show progress was a clockmaker who would be sent away, and being sent away was impossible now anyway, which made the pretense both easier and stranger. "But the letter—"

"There's a sledge," Pell said. "And there was a ferryman, Tam Osgood, who ran the crossing below the point. But look at that water." He nodded toward the black windows, toward the river beyond. "No man rows that channel in November for a letter. And no sledge crosses a bridge nobody's cleared." He rubbed his face with both hands. "The letter can wait. Letters can all wait. That's the one mercy of winter — it makes waiting respectable."

Zephyrine looked at the stopped clock, at the pendulum hanging in its unfinished gesture, and felt the trap close another notch. Everything waited now. Everything except the *Meridian*, which waited for nothing but the tide.

* * *

They came down to the common room for the midday meal and found they were five.

Governor Renwick's party had grown by one overnight: a clerk named Fenn, small and neat and gray as a ledger page, who had arrived — so the governor's man Harrow explained, in the tone men use for explanations that explain nothing — *with the last crossing yesterday evening*. Last crossing. As if someone had known the season was turning and made sure to be inside the walls before it turned. Zephyrine watched Fenn ladle soup with precise, economical motions and wondered how many hours the man had been in the building while Zephyrine slept with a stolen ledger's cipher-work folded into the lining of his coat.

Five of them now at the long table under the mullioned windows: Pell, silent and hollow-eyed at one end; Zephyrine near him; and down at the other end, arranged around the governor like satellites, Harrow the aide, Fenn the clerk, and Magistrate Ilse Verany — *former* magistrate now, stripped of her office, permitted here at the observatory over the winter in what Renwick had called, with exquisite courtesy, *a retreat from public life*. Exile dressed in kindness. Zephyrine had not yet found the courage to speak to her alone since learning what the cylinder held, since reading — in the coded columns he was slowly, painfully deciphering — entries that touched, however obliquely, on the debts of a brother whose name she had once begged him never to speak of.

Ilse ate little. She sat with her back very straight and her hands quiet on the tablecloth, and she watched the snow through the window with an expression Zephyrine had seen once before, years ago, in his master's workshop, on the face of a woman watching a debt collector count out her brother's obligations coin by coin. The expression of someone measuring a distance they may have to cross on foot.

"The roads will be impassable until March," Renwick was saying, cheerfully, to no one and everyone. He was a handsome man who wore his fifty years lightly, silver at the temples, a laugh kept ready in his pocket like a watch. "Which suits me admirably. No petitions, no deputations, no committees. Just work, good food, and honest company. Mr. Oyelaran — how fares our patient?" He turned the charm on Zephyrine full-force, and Zephyrine felt the familiar cold slide into his stomach, the cold he had learned to smile through these past days.

"She responds well, Your Excellency. The stoppage was fouling in the escapement — grit, most likely, carried up on the wind. I've cleaned the train and trued the suspension. With the striking work remaining, I'd estimate a fortnight to full service."

"A fortnight." Renwick spread his hands as if receiving a gift. "Then we shall have the new year rung in properly after all. You see how provident the weather is? Snow seals us in with our work, and when the bridges open—" he smiled around the table— "everything will be in order. Order above all, gentlemen. Above comfort, above convenience. Above friendship, even." His gaze traveled, mild as milk, across Ilse's bowed head, and moved on.

Fenn had stopped eating. He was looking at Zephyrine with an attention so complete it had a texture to it, like a fingertip laid lightly on the pulse. Not hostile. Worse than hostile: curious. Zephyrine returned his glance for exactly as long as courtesy required and then looked down at his soup and thought, with a strange floating clarity, *I am having lunch with the people who sold forty-one human beings downriver, and the soup is good, and the bread is warm, and nobody is screaming.* The banality of it was the most frightening thing he had encountered yet — more frightening than the ledger, which at least had the decency to be monstrous on paper.

* * *

In the afternoon the snow stopped and the sky cleared to a hard blue, and Zephyrine went out onto the gallery walk that circled the tower's base, ostensibly to inspect the exterior dial. The wind off the river had teeth in it, but the cold was honest, and he needed honesty after lunch.

From the gallery he could see the whole sweep of the closed world. The causeway running white and featureless to the vanished shore. The river, half-locked, its ice sheets grinding slowly against each other with a sound like far-off furniture being moved. And downstream, just visible past the point, the masts and spars of the winter fleet riding at anchor in the lee of the breakwater — among them, if Pell's information held, the *Meridian*, black-hulled, patient, waiting for her tide and her crates.

Two miles of bridge. Four months of winter. Nine days of tide.

He heard footsteps on the gallery behind him and knew, before turning, who it would be. There was a way Ilse Verany walked, even exiled, even stripped of office — heel-to-toe, measured, a magistrate's walk learned in courtrooms and never unlearned.

"Mr. Oyelaran." Her breath smoked in the cold. She stopped beside him at the rail, gloved hands resting on the stone, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the ice ground and shifted. "I wanted to say — I am glad you are here. This place, this winter... it helps to know one honest person is in the building."

The word landed in Zephyrine like a splinter driven deep. *Honest.* He kept his eyes on the river and managed, "That's generous, Magistrate."

"Not anymore, thank God. Only Verany. Or Ilse, if you're feeling brave." She smiled a little, and then the smile faded into something wearier and truer. "Do you know what I think about, out here? I think about how a bridge closes. Not the engineering of it. The *fact* of it. One morning there's a road, and by nightfall there isn't, and everyone on the wrong side of the snow is simply... elsewhere. Whatever happens in this building until thaw happens without witnesses." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were sharp and dark and searching. "It makes a person consider whom they're sealed in with, doesn't it? And what those people might be doing while the world isn't looking."

Zephyrine's heart was beating hard enough that he feared she could hear it over the ice. Every answer he might give seemed to lead somewhere dangerous. "I consider mostly gears," he said at last, and was relieved to hear his voice come out steady, almost light. "Gears and pivots and how to make a dead clock live again. It's narrow work, but it keeps a man out of trouble."

"Does it?" Something flickered across her face — disappointment, or doubt, or perhaps only the wind. "My father used to say the narrowest doors open onto the largest rooms. Be careful, Zephyrine." She said his name deliberately, the first time she had used it since arriving, and it went through him like a key through a lock. "This mountain keeps secrets badly. Things buried here have a way of ringing out."

She left him then, her measured footsteps fading along the gallery, and Zephyrine stayed at the rail until his fingers ached, looking down at the black hull riding at anchor in the distance, and understood at last the full architecture of his imprisonment.

The bridge was closed. The river was closing. In nine days the tide would lift the *Meridian* and carry the last of the evidence — the bonds, the sums, the proof — downriver and out of reach forever, unless he could make a stopped clock speak.

And the only door out of this place was the same door the crime intended to use.

He turned back toward the tower, toward the lamplight and the frozen pendulum, and began climbing the stairs to work.
