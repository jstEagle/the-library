## 12. Pell Disappears

Zephyrine woke to a smell that did not belong to the observatory.

It came through the gap beneath his door like an intruder — woodsmoke, yes, but wrong for morning, too acrid, threaded with something sweeter and fouler underneath, the smell of burned wool and burned paper and something else he could not name and did not want to. For a moment, half-dreaming, he thought of the soldering iron left hot on his bench, and he was up before his mind finished the thought, fumbling a coat over his nightshirt, wrenching the door open onto a corridor full of grey light.

The corridor was empty. The cold poured in from the gallery windows at its end, where dawn was only a rumor behind the river fog, and the smell hung in the air with nowhere to go.

He stood listening. The observatory had a language of sounds he had learned over these weeks without noticing he was learning it: the tick and drag of the great clock above, still stopped at 4:17 though he had long since freed the escapement of its sabotage and set the time train running silent as a held breath; the settling groans of the tower in the wind off the water; the scrape of Keeper Ambrose Pell's slippers on the stair at whatever hour the old man pleased, because it was his observatory, his kingdom of brass and starlight, and he rose when he rose and slept when he slept.

There were no slippers now. There was no sound of Pell anywhere in the building, and Zephyrine understood, in the way one understands a stopped clock — all at once, and then forever — that the silence itself was the alarm.

"Pell?" His voice went down the stairwell and came back to him flattened, unanswered.

He dressed badly, fingers stupid with cold, and went down.

* * *

Pell's quarters opened off the under-gallery, past the archive room with its ledgers of star positions and tide tables, down a short passage the old man had called, with some pride, "my hermitage." Zephyrine had been inside twice: once to return a borrowed loupe, once to sit while Pell brewed tea so strong it could have stood a spoon in it and talked, obliquely and endlessly, about the patience required to keep anything true — a clock, a record, a promise — across years.

The door stood open. Beyond it, the room was black.

Not dark — black. Charred. Someone had built a fire inside the little chamber and fed it everything Pell owned. The narrow bed was a slab of wet ash; the mattress springs stood up out of it like the ribs of something small and starved. The bookshelf had collapsed inward, its spines fused into a single carbonized block that crumbled when Zephyrine touched it, shedding flakes that drifted around him like filthy snow. The window had been shuttered before the fire was lit — he saw that immediately, saw it with the part of his mind that would always see such things now — because the glass was intact but smoked brown on the inside, and the burn pattern climbed the walls evenly instead of reaching for fresh air. A contained fire. A deliberate one. Whoever had done this had known enough about fire to keep it obedient, and had wanted the room destroyed but not the tower.

On the floor near the threshold lay the tea kettle, knocked from its hook, dented where a boot had caught it. Beside it, half-melted into the flagstones, a brass thimble-sized cup. Zephyrine crouched and did not touch either. He found himself cataloguing, the way he catalogued damage in a movement: here the violence, here the haste, here the thing that had been interrupted. There was no blood that he could find. No sign of struggle beyond the kettle. The fire had been set by someone calm.

*He hid the ledger,* Zephyrine thought, and the thought arrived with its twin, inseparable: *and I let him carry it alone.*

He had taken the cylinder from the escapement eleven days ago. He had read what was inside it by lamplight until the cipher broke open and the numbers became people, and he had told himself that knowledge was a burden he carried alone so that no one else would have to. But Pell had carried it first. Pell had wedged the truth into the heart of the governor's own clock, in the one place in Vellamare where stopping time would be noticed by everyone, and had waited — months, perhaps a year — for someone honest to come and fix it. And when that someone came, Pell had pressed the whole burning weight of it into Zephyrine's hands like a coal, saying *you're young, they'll not watch you*, as if handing it over would save them both.

Now the old man was gone, and the coal had burned through its bearer after all.

Zephyrine knelt in the ash and felt the guilt arrive not as a sharp thing but as a slow flooding, cold at the ankles, rising. He had meant to go back to Pell. He had told himself, two nights ago, lying awake: *tomorrow I will ask him how he got the cylinder past the guards who searched the clock every quarter-day; tomorrow I will ask who else knows.* Tomorrow. And tomorrow had come smelling of smoke.

He stayed there a long time, kneeling in another man's ruined life, while the fog outside turned slowly from grey to the color of pewter, and somewhere below, muffled by stone, the river moved against the pilings with its patient, indifferent sound.

* * *

They came for him mid-morning — two soldiers and Mr. Fenn, the clerk whose notebook had begun appearing at the edges of Zephyrine's days like a third shadow. Fenn wore his politeness the way other men wore armor, fitted close and gleaming, and today it seemed freshly polished.

"Keeper Pell is absent," Fenn said, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "Governor Renwick wishes you to hear the circumstances from him directly. He's asked that you attend."

"Attend where?"

"The lower hall. He's gathered the household." Fenn's eyes went past Zephyrine's shoulder to the scorched doorway, and something flickered there — satisfaction, quickly sheathed — before the polite mask resumed. "You may want to bring your coat. The hall holds the cold poorly."

Zephyrine brought his tool roll instead, out of habit or defiance, and Fenn noted it. He actually watched the man note it: the small motion of the pencil, the tilt of the notebook. Every object on my body, Zephyrine thought, is being entered into evidence against some future hour. He made himself walk steadily down the stairs with the roll under his arm anyway, because a craftsman does not go unarmed into a strange mechanism, even if his only weapons are awls and files.

The lower hall of the observatory was a vaulted room intended, Pell had once said, for astronomers' suppers and the governor's occasional visits, though it had hosted neither in years. Today it held nearly everyone who lived or worked within the walls: the two under-keepers, the cook and her boy, four servants, six soldiers, Ilse Verany standing apart by the tall windows with her arms folded and her face arranged into the blankness of a woman who has learned that her expressions are read as testimony. And at the head of the room, beside the dead hearth, Governor Aldous Renwick.

Renwick did not look like a man whose keeper had fled in the night with stolen funds. He looked rested. He wore dove-grey, and his hands were clasped loosely at his waist, and when Zephyrine entered he turned his head with the smooth economy of a well-made gear taking up its load — no jerk, no lost motion, everything meshed.

"Master Oyelaran." He used the honorific generously, wrongly; Zephyrine was an apprentice and both of them knew it, and the error was a kind of leash, flattery tied to obligation. "Thank you for coming. I'm sorry to summon you from your work. I know how you clockmen hate interruption." A smile, warm as lamplight. "Though I suppose the clock is running again, thanks to you. The city owes you its mornings back."

"It runs quiet yet," Zephyrine said. "I've not reconnected the striking train. It keeps time, but it doesn't tell anyone."

"No. It doesn't tell anyone." Renwick let that stand a moment longer than comfort allowed, then sighed, and his shoulders performed sorrow with the same precision as his smile performed warmth. Zephyrine watched it happen and marveled, distantly, at the craftsmanship of it — this man was a mechanism too, just built for a different purpose. "I have distressing news. Keeper Pell has absconded."

Murmurs. The cook put her hand over her mouth. One of the under-keepers — Hobb, the younger one, who adored Pell with the dogged devotion of the unremarkable for the remarkable — made a small wounded noise and then went very still, as if ashamed of it.

"Auditing the observatory accounts last evening," Renwick continued, "we discovered irregularities going back several years. Sums drawn against the maintenance fund. Disbursements recorded to suppliers who do not exist. Old men grow strange, and stranger still when they think themselves unwatched." He spread his hands. "Last night, evidently, he learned the audit was coming. He burned his quarters to destroy the records of his thefts — we found the remains of account papers in the ash — and slipped out before the watch could be raised. Whether he's gone downriver or into the hills, we don't yet know. There will be a warrant. There will be a reward." He paused, and his gaze traveled the room, touching each face like a finger testing a wire for current. "I tell you this myself because I will not have rumor doing the work of fact. Keeper Pell was trusted, and he betrayed that trust, and I am angry about it — personally angry — because I liked the old man. That is all."

That is all. As if the whole of a human life could be weighed, sentenced, and dismissed in three sentences delivered beside a cold hearth.

Zephyrine stood among the servants with his tool roll under his arm and did the arithmetic of the lie, because he could not help it; it was what his mind did now, unbidden, the way it counted chimes. *Account papers in the ash.* Pell had kept no accounts in those rooms. He had kept books on stars and tides, a tin of tea, a carved wooden bird Zephyrine had once admired and been given permission to hold. Whatever papers had burned there had been burned for one reason only: to make the story fit the ashes, the way a counterfeit case is fitted around stolen works. Which meant the story had been written before the fire was lit. Which meant—

Which meant Pell had not fled. Or if he had fled, he had been *made* to flee — flushed, named, condemned in absentia within hours, before any honest search could begin. You do not build the gallows before you catch the thief unless you already know the thief cannot speak for himself.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder: *they found out he told me.* Or feared it. Either way, the message of the burned room was addressed to more than one reader. It said: *this is what becomes of keepers.*

Beside the window, Ilse had not moved. But she was looking at Zephyrine now, and her face was still blank, and her eyes were not. They were asking a question with the directness of a summons: *do you believe this?* And Zephyrine, bound hand and foot by a promise three winters old, gave her the only answer he could bear to give, which was no answer at all — a slow blink, gaze dropping to the floor, the craftsman's studied neutrality that said *I am only here to repair what I am paid to repair.*

He hated himself for it. He felt her receive his silence and file it, and he felt something between them cool by a degree, like metal withdrawing from flame.

* * *

Afterward — after Renwick had dismissed them with a benediction about diligence and order, after the servants dispersed in murmuring knots, after the soldiers took up new positions at the stairhead and the gallery doors, positions that had nothing to do with catching a fugitive and everything to do with watching the people who remained — Zephyrine climbed back to the under-gallery and stood in the doorway of the burned room a second time, because he could not stay away, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Fenn was already there.

The clerk stood in the middle of the ash with his notebook open, making his small entries, and he did not startle at Zephyrine's appearance, which meant he had heard him coming and permitted it. "Tragic," Fenn said, without looking up. "A whole life, reduced to this in an hour. Fire is efficient that way. So are certain other instruments." He closed the notebook. "You knew him best of us, I think. The two of you spent evenings together. Talking."

"We talked about clocks."

"Mm." Fenn smiled, and let the smile linger past all natural duration, until it stopped being an expression and became a wall. "Old men talk about more than clocks, Master Oyelaran. They talk about the past. About regrets. Sometimes, if they're frightened, they talk about insurance — arrangements made against hard times, secrets entrusted to younger men." He tapped the notebook lightly against his palm, twice, like a physician sounding a chest. "If he ever gave you anything to keep. Papers. Objects. A confidence of any kind. You'd want to bring it forward. The governor is reasonable with those who come forward, and less reasonable afterward. History is quite consistent on the point."

"I repair clocks, Mr. Fenn. He showed me a clock. That's the whole of my estate."

"Of course." Fenn stepped past him through the doorway, close enough that Zephyrine smelled ink and bay rum and, faintly, woodsmoke — had the clerk been here since dawn? had he watched the fire, or set it, or merely arrived to harvest it? — and paused. "One more thing. The striking train. You said it doesn't yet tell anyone. When will it?"

"Soon. It must be re-timed bell by bell. Chimes are tuned like instruments; rush them and the whole city hears a wrong note for a hundred years."

"Yes." Fenn regarded him with mild, bottomless attention. "We wouldn't want the city hearing anything wrong."

He went. His footsteps descended the stairs at an unhurried pace, each one distinct, a metronome counting down something Zephyrine could not yet read.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon because his hands demanded it and because idle hands, in a house full of watchers, looked like guilty hands. He sat at his bench in the workshop gallery with the clock's removed parts laid out on baize before him — the great wheels, the pallets, the pendulum's suspension spring — cleaning, adjusting, doing genuine and necessary work, while his mind ran on a separate train altogether.

They had taken Pell's name and made it mean *thief*. They had burned his life and called it evidence. Within a week, in the markets and taverns downriver, Ambrose Pell would be a cautionary tale, told with wine and laughter: the old keeper who stole from the governor's own roof. And the ledger — the real ledger, the cylinder of names and sums that Zephyrine had hidden weeks ago inside the hollow counterweight of the striking-train barrel, wrapped in waxed cloth like a relic — would stay buried, and the families it named would stay vanished, and the Meridian would sail on the morning tide whenever the season allowed, carrying the final bonds away into the world's indifferent commerce.

Unless.

The thought arrived the way his best thoughts always arrived — sideways, while the hands were busy, uninvited and complete. He was holding the small hammer of the striking train, the one that fell upon the great bell to mark the hours, and he was thinking: *a bell is a voice.* He was thinking: *the clock stands higher than every roof in Vellamare, and its voice carries from the Custom House to the debtor quarter, over water and stone, into every room with a window.* He was thinking: *they can burn a man's quarters. They can burn his name. But they cannot unsay what a bell has said to ten thousand ears at once.*

His hands went still on the bench.

Below, the fog was lifting off the river, and the docks were visible for the first time in days — the masts, the crates, the small dark figures of the guard walking their rounds along the quay, patient as ticks. Somewhere down there was a ship waiting for the season's turn, and somewhere in the tower above him was a mechanism built to announce only hours, and between the two lay a distance he could measure exactly, in gears and teeth and days, because measuring impossible distances was the trade he had chosen and the trade, he understood suddenly, had chosen him back.

They had burned Pell's rooms to bury his message.

Very well. Zephyrine picked up the hammer, turned it in the lamplight, and began, quietly, to think about bells.

Above his head, through floors of timber and stone, the master clock ticked on in its silence — keeping perfect time, telling no one, waiting, as all good mechanisms wait, for the hand that would give it back its voice.
