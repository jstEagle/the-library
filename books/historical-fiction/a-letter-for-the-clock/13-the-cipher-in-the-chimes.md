## 13. The Cipher in the Chimes

The lamplight did something strange to the striking train. By day, when Zephyrine worked on the going train — the great wheelwork that turned the hands and kept the observatory's time honest — the mechanism felt like a patient animal, breathing its slow breath of weights and escapement. But at night, alone in the clock room with the lamp turned low and the city below him guttering down to embers, the striking train became something else entirely.

It became a voice.

He had noticed it first as a craftsman notices everything: accidentally, while measuring. He had been tracing the path of a blow from the lifting lever to the hammer, counting the wheels between, and he'd caught himself mouthing the numbers aloud. *One. Two. Three.* Fourteen blows for the fourteen hours of the shortening day. And then his mouth had stopped, because a thought had arrived that was so large it seemed to fill the whole tower, pressing against the stone like water against a dam.

The chimes were not music. They were numbers. And numbers could be made to say anything.

* * *

He did not sleep well after that. He lay in the narrow keeper's cot that Pell had vacated — Pell's own bed, which Zephyrine still thought of as borrowed even though the old man had not returned to claim it — and listened to the master clock tick above him through two floors of timber, each beat arriving faint and enormous, like the pulse of something buried alive beneath the observatory.

*One. Two. Three.*

In the morning he climbed the ladder before the fire in the little stove had warmed the room, and he stood in front of the striking train with his arms folded like a man confronting a rival, and he began, properly this time, to think.

Here is what a clock's striking train is, if you strip away the reverence. It is a counter. A wheel called the count wheel has notches cut into its rim — one notch for one blow, two notches close together for two blows, and so on around the circle until twelve. As the hour wheel turns, a pin lifts the warning lever; the train unlocks; the hammer falls as many times as the notches allow; and then the count wheel locks again and waits, dumb and obedient, for the next hour to come and give it orders.

Twelve positions. Twelve possible utterances.

Zephyrine took out his notebook — the cheap one, the one Fenn never saw, kept sewn into the lining of his coat where the tool roll rode against his ribs — and he wrote at the top of a fresh page, in letters small enough to be mistaken for nothing: *12.*

Then he sat down on his stool and looked at that number for a long time.

Because twelve was not enough. He knew that already, in his bones, before any arithmetic confirmed it. The ledger held sixty-one names. Sixty-one names, each with dates, each with sums, each with the cold little code-numbers that marked which bond, which shipment, which pocket the money had vanished into. You could not say sixty-one names in twelve syllables.

But you did not have to say them all at once. That was the thing. That was the beautiful, terrible, reckless thing.

The clock struck every hour. Day and night, without ceasing, whether anyone wished it or no. And every citizen of Vellamare lived inside earshot of those chimes — the fishwives on the shingle, the clerks in the customs house, the debtors' quarter where the shuttered windows stared out over the river like closed eyes. Eleven days the clock had been silent, and the whole city had felt the silence the way a body feels a missing tooth. They *listened* for it. They set their bread to rise by it, their meetings by it, their griefs by it.

If the clock spoke differently — if it spoke in a pattern no hour had ever spoken — they would listen harder.

* * *

He spent the morning doing genuine work, because Fenn would arrive at nine, and because the work steadied him the way prayer was said to steady other men. He cleaned and oiled the second wheel of the going train. He trued a bent pallet stone with a touch so fine it left no mark. His hands moved and his mind ran elsewhere, down a road he had never let himself walk before, and by the time he heard Fenn's polite knock on the tower door his notebook page had grown a thicket of figures.

Fenn came in with the snow on his shoulders and his notebook already open, and he looked at the striking train with the flat professional interest of a man appraising furniture.

"You've turned your attention to the chimes," Fenn said.

"The governor wants the clock repaired," Zephyrine said. "All of it."

"Of course." Fenn wrote something. Zephyrine watched him write it and felt the old cold crawl up the back of his neck — the feeling of being copied down, reduced to ink in someone else's hand. But there was a new element in the feeling now, a strange double exposure. Because Fenn was writing down the truth. Zephyrine really was working on the striking train. The best lie he could tell Mr. Fenn was simply to do exactly what he said he was doing, and let the man's own diligence bury him.

"The count wheel is worn," Zephyrine said, and lifted it from its arbor to show him. It was true enough; eleven days of deliberate jamming had scored the notches. "I'll need to recut several of these."

"A long job?"

"A careful one." He met Fenn's eyes and gave him the small, tired smile of an apprentice who thinks only of hours and wages. "Craftsmanship can't be hurried, sir. My master says a rushed gear is a broken gear wearing its best clothes."

Something flickered behind Fenn's politeness — amusement, perhaps, or appetite — and he noted that down too, whatever it was, and went away down the stairs with his footsteps precise as a metronome.

Zephyrine stood listening until the outer door closed. Then he sat back down and opened the notebook to the page headed *12*, and crossed it out, and wrote instead:

*Not 12 sounds. 12 sounds, but how many times each?*

And under that, the arithmetic bloomed.

* * *

Here is what he found, working through the afternoon while the light went copper and then grey behind the east windows. If you freed the striking train from the tyranny of the hour — if you let the count wheel turn through many revolutions instead of locking after one — then a single striking sequence could be made arbitrarily long. Not twelve blows. Fifty. A hundred. Five hundred, if the weight could bear it and the mechanism did not tear itself apart with the effort.

Five hundred blows was more than enough to spell a name.

He took the cipher from the ledger — the cipher he had cracked by lamplight in chapter upon sleepless chapter of the last fortnight, the substitution scheme built on the letters of Vellamare's own river-alphabet, each letter a number between one and twenty-six — and he laid it beside the striking train in his mind like two halves of a broken mold.

A letter was a number. A number was a count of blows. One blow, one blow, three blows — pause — two blows, five blows, one blow — pause. *A-B-C. Pause. B-E-A.* The hammer knew nothing of letters. The hammer only knew falling. But falls could be counted, and counts could be read, and reading was the one thing Vellamare's dockworkers and fishwives and debtors did together every evening, huddled over the broadsheets in taverns, spelling out the shipping news letter by letter for whoever hadn't learned.

They would hear the clock strike forty times, and pause, and strike five, and pause, and strike one. And someone — some clerk, some schoolmistress, some clever child with chalk — would write it down. *XL-V-I.* And someone else would say, *that's not an hour,* and someone else would say, *no, wait —* 

And then the whole city would be holding the same thread, pulling it, and the thread would run through every name in the ledger, every date, every sum, straight into the governor's court.

Zephyrine put his head in his hands and laughed once, a single dry bark that startled him in the empty room.

It was mad. He saw that clearly. It was the plan of a boy who had spent too long among gears and too little among soldiers. To rebuild the striking train — not repair it, *rebuild* it, add wheels, re-cut the count wheel entirely, devise a pausing mechanism that existed nowhere in any book he had studied — that was months of work for a guild shop with a full bench. He had nights. He had stolen hours. He had Fenn's notebook logging his every movement and Renwick's suspicion coiled somewhere below the tower like weather coming.

But madness, he thought, looking at the great silent mechanism with its brass arms folded across the stopped face of the dial — madness had a certain economy. No one guarded against a clock. No one feared a hammer. The whole apparatus of the state, with its guards and its ledgers and its ships and its burned quarters, was built on the assumption that power speaks and everyone else listens.

He was going to make the listening thing speak.

* * *

That night he did not sleep either. He climbed to the clock room with the lamp and a stub of pencil and he measured the striking train the way a physician measures a body: every arbor, every wheel, every tooth, every drop of the hammer's fall. He drew it all into the notebook, front elevation and side, and then, on the facing pages, he began to draw what it might become.

The problems arranged themselves like obstacles on a dark stair, and he took them one at a time.

First: duration. A sequence long enough to spell even a single name — say, fifteen letters, with pauses between — meant upward of two hundred blows. Two hundred blows required either a prodigious driving weight or a separate source of power. He solved it on paper with a secondary weight, a modest one, hung on its own line inside the case, wound independently. The observatory's weights descended through the full height of the tower; there was room for one more line, hidden behind the bellows that muffled the hour strike. He sketched it, erased it, sketched it smaller.

Second: the pauses. This was the heart of it, the invention that did not exist. The clock must strike, stop, strike again, stop — and the stops must themselves be meaningful, must mark the boundaries of letters. Silence was punctuation. He worried at it for two hours, pacing the small circumference of the room, until he thought of the warning piece — the little fan fly that governed the speed of the strike — and realized the answer was not to stop the train but to *detain* it. A detent, tripped by pins on an added wheel, that held the train locked for a fixed interval — four seconds, six, whatever the ear needed — and then released it. A wheel of pins, like a barrel in a musical box. He had seen a musical box once, in a shop window in the capital district, behind glass, priced beyond a year of his wages. Its pinned cylinder had turned and turned and played its little tune to no one.

He was going to build a musical box the size of a cathedral and make it testify.

Third — and here his pencil actually trembled — secrecy. Every part had to come from somewhere. Brass stock, wire, pin stock, a spare weight. Halloway & Sons' tool roll held much but not all of it. And Fenn logged his movements; the stores below were inventoried; Renwick had doubled the dock guard since the ledger cylinder vanished from his strongbox, and suspicion sat on the observatory like frost.

He would have to make the parts himself, from raw metal, in the dead hours, and hide them where a search would not look. He looked around the clock room slowly, cataloguing it as a searcher would catalogue it, and his eyes settled on the great bellows that softened the hour strike — leather lungs big as coffins, hollow, unexamined, part of the furniture of the machine. Inside a bellows, who would look? Inside the counterweights, cast around a hollow core? Inside the very clock, in the spaces between the wheels where only a maker's eye would know there was room?

His work would hide inside the work. The cover story and the crime would share one body.

* * *

Toward dawn he allowed himself the indulgence he had been refusing all night: he worked out the opening sequence in full.

The ledger's first entry. He knew it by heart now; it had been the key that broke the cipher open, the name that had made him sit down on Pell's floor as though the boards had gone soft. He converted it letter by letter into the river-alphabet numbers. Then he converted the numbers into blows. Then he counted the blows, and the pauses, and the seconds, and he arrived at a total running time for the first name alone.

Eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes of hammer-fall in the dark, ringing down off the tower and out across the frozen roofs of Vellamare, and the city would wake, and the city would listen, and the city would begin — clumsily, half-asleep, chalk on doorposts and slate on knees — to write down what its own clock was saying.

Sixty-one names. At eleven minutes apiece, with the pauses and the sums and the dates — he did the division twice because the first answer frightened him — it came to better than two full nights of continuous striking. Two nights. The Meridian sailed on the morning tide in nine days. The arithmetic fit, barely, the way a bearing fits an arbor: precisely, with no slack at all, and ruin if it ran dry.

He blew out the lamp as the sky behind the east windows turned the color of pewter, and he stood at the tall window watching the city he was about to betray into wakefulness. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys, thin and straight in the windless cold. Downriver, invisible beyond the bend, the Meridian sat in her ice collar with her hold waiting for paper. Somewhere in the debtor quarter a shutter would open soon, and a woman would step out into the blue morning to fetch water, not knowing that in nine days the sound of her own clock was going to say her husband's name to the whole world.

*Let it be enough,* he thought. *Let the sound carry. Let someone understand.*

And then, because he was a craftsman and could not help himself, he thought: *and the fourth wheel will need a lighter pinion, or the train will labor on the long sequences — I'll cut it from that scrap of hard brass, the piece wrapped in oilcloth under the third floorboard—*

He caught himself smiling in the dark glass, a thin fierce smile he didn't recognize as his own.

Below him, faint through the floor, he heard the tower door: the knock pattern, always the same, two and one. Fenn, early today, with his notebook and his snow-damp shoulders and his appetite dressed up as duty.

Zephyrine closed the notebook, sewed it back into its lining against his ribs, and went down to meet the day — to clean wheels, to true stones, to be the honest apprentice in the honest tower, while under the floorboards and inside the bellows and behind his own steady face the reckless, beautiful machinery of the truth waited to be born.

He paused at the top of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and listened — not to Fenn, but to the silence of the stopped clock above him, the silence he had lived inside for eleven days.

*Hold your tongue a little longer,* he told it. *You're about to learn to speak.*
