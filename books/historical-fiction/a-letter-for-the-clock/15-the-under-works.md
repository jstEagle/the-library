## 15. The Under-Works

The door to the under-works was where Pell had always said it was: behind the weight-shaft, past the third landing, a panel painted the same gray as the stone so that only a clockmaker's eye would catch the seam. Zephyrine had known it existed for eleven days now — he had counted his days at the observatory the way he counted everything, in escapements and pendulum strokes — but he had never opened it. There was no reason to. The under-works were Pell's country, the keeper's private labyrinth of grease and shadow where the great clock's roots went down into the rock like the roots of some iron tree.

Now there was a reason. Now there was an empty bed with the blankets still folded, and a scorch mark on the floorboards of the keeper's quarters shaped like nothing so much as a kneeling man, and a governor downstairs announcing to anyone who would listen that old Ambrose Pell had fled with stolen funds and was to be treated as a criminal if found.

Zephyrine did not believe it. He had spent three nights turning the question over the way he turned a stubborn screw, and every angle told him the same thing. A man who flees does not fold his blankets. A man who steals does not leave behind the one thing worth stealing — the ledger cylinder was still hidden where Zephyrine had moved it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the hollow of the hour-weight, warm as a heart against all that cold brass. And a man who spends thirty years keeping time for a city does not simply stop.

No. Someone had taken Pell, or someone had driven him out, and either way the fire in his quarters had been meant as punctuation. *Here ends the keeper.* The governor wanted a period at the end of that sentence, and Zephyrine intended to find out whether the sentence was finished at all.

He waited until the fourth bell after midnight. Fenn slept — or pretended to sleep — in the chamber two doors down, and Zephyrine had learned the rhythm of the clerk's rest the way he learned any mechanism: by listening. Fenn turned over twice between the second bell and the third. He rose once, always once, to relieve himself, and his boots creaked on the third stair going down, never the second. Tonight the creak had come on schedule, and now the silence below was thick and complete.

Zephyrine took the lamp with the shuttered flame, the one he'd rigged from a biscuit tin and a scrap of mica, and he went down.

* * *

The panel opened onto air that smelled of machine oil and stone dust and something older underneath — the deep mineral breath of the hill itself, cold and mineral-sweet, like licking a coin. The stairs were cut into the living rock, narrow, each step worn into a shallow dish by a century of the keeper's feet. Pell had walked these stairs perhaps a hundred thousand times. Zephyrine tried to imagine that — one man's feet, wearing grooves into stone — and could not, quite. It was like trying to imagine the number of teeth in all the gears of all the clocks in Vellamare.

The lamp threw his shadow huge against the walls, a giant hunched over a doll's staircase. He made himself go slowly. The under-works were not a place for hurry. He remembered Pell saying that, back when Pell had been a voice instead of an absence: *You don't rush down here, boy. The clock doesn't know you're in a mood. She only knows whether you're careful.*

The first gallery opened around him and he stopped, as he always stopped, undone despite everything by the sheer fact of it.

The clock went down farther than most churches went up. That was the secret of the observatory tower that no visitor ever guessed, standing in the lantern room craning up at the beautiful works — that the half of the mechanism anyone admired was only the crown of the thing, the visible flourish. Below, filling galleries carved from the hillside like the crypts of some engine-cathedral, hung the weights. Three of them, each taller than a man, each cast from a single pour of lead and iron, hanging on cables thick as Zephyrine's wrist. They descended through the rock on their slow weekly journey, driving everything above them by nothing more than their patient falling, and when they reached the bottom a winch — hand-cranked, sweat-labor, Pell's own arms twice a week — drew them back up. The whole city's time, Zephyrine thought, was powered by one old man's shoulders. Nobody in Vellamare knew that either.

The weights hung motionless tonight. Of course they did; the clock stood stopped at 4:17, had stood there since the ledger went into the escapement, and Zephyrine had not yet dared restart it. Every hour it stood still was an hour the conspirators' almanacs stayed wrong, small comfort though that was. But standing beneath the motionless weights was worse than standing beneath moving ones. Moving weights felt like weather. Still ones felt like held breath.

"Pell?" he whispered, and hated how small it sounded, how the stone took his voice and gave back nothing.

He went deeper.

The second gallery was the striking train's cellar — the great barrels where the chime cable wound and unwound, the massive fly fans that kept the hammer-stroke from tearing the works apart. Here the smell of oil was stronger, fresher. He noticed that before he understood it. Old oil goes sour, goes sticky; this oil smelled recently poured. And there — on the edge of the fly fan's housing, where no workman would have reason to touch — a smear of something dark. He touched it with his fingertip and rubbed. Blood, dried to the color of rust. Not much. A few drops. Enough.

His pulse picked up its pace, which was an irony he noted distantly, professionally, the way he noted everything now. Two weeks ago he had been an apprentice who noticed things because noticing was how you avoided a master's wrath. Now he noticed things because noticing was the difference between lives.

"Pell." Louder this time, but still a whisper, pitched under the silence rather than through it. "It's Zephyrine. From Halloway's. I've come alone."

Nothing. The rock breathed its cold around him. Somewhere far above, faint as memory, the tower creaked as frost worked at the joints of the stonework.

He pressed on, down a ladder-run of iron rungs set into the wall, into the lowest gallery — the one even Pell rarely visited, the root-cellar of the clock where the counterweight for the dome's shutters hung in its own shaft, and the drainage channel ran, and the year's meltwater dripped somewhere in the dark with a patience like a second, slower clock. *Drip. Drip. Drip.* Water keeping time. Everything kept time, if you listened long enough.

The lamp found footprints in the dust of the drainage shelf.

They were old — days old, blurred at the edges where dust had drifted back over them — but they were boot prints, and they went along the shelf toward the shutter-weight shaft and did not come back. Zephyrine's chest went tight. He followed them with the lamp held low, walking on the balls of his feet without deciding to, his body remembering years of sneaking down shop stairs past Master Halloway's lighted window.

At the shaft's mouth, wedged into the angle where the stone wall met the timber framing, he saw the blanket first. Gray wool, army-issue, folded — no, not folded, arranged, propped. Then the shape inside it resolved, and his heart went off its train entirely.

"Ambrose."

The old man's eyes opened. They took a moment to find him, swimming up from wherever they'd been, and then they fixed on him with an expression Zephyrine would carry the rest of his life: not surprise, but a kind of fierce, exhausted confirmation, as if Pell had been holding a gear-train in place with his bare hands for four days and here, finally, was someone to take the weight.

"You took your time," Pell rasped.

* * *

He was hurt worse than he let on, which was very much like him.

Zephyrine knelt in the dripping dark and got the blanket open and catalogued him the way he'd catalogue a damaged movement: left ankle swollen to twice its size, the boot cut away, the skin gone the purple-black of over-heated tempering oil. Ribs bound with a strip torn from a shirt, badly, by his own hand. A burn along the outside of the right forearm, blistered and weeping — from the fire, then. From setting his own quarters alight, or from escaping through it, Zephyrine couldn't yet tell. His hands, though — his hands were whole, and clean, and steady, and Zephyrine felt something in himself unknot at the sight of them. A clockmaker's hands were the man. As long as those hands lived, Ambrose Pell lived.

"Who did this?" Zephyrine asked, already working as he asked it — unwinding his own scarf, folding it, packing it around the ankle with snow brought down in a flask from the parapet, packed tight. Cold first. Elevation later. His master's voice ran the liturgy in his head: *Cold for swelling, tight for support, and never, ever tell the patient everything you fear.*

"Two men came the night after your dinner," Pell said. His voice was gravel and wind. "Renwick's men — I knew the big one, he was a dock guard last winter before they transferred him up here. They said the governor wanted a word about irregularities in the accounts. I said fine, I'd come in the morning. They said the governor wanted the word now." His mouth twisted. "I've spent forty years learning what men mean underneath what they say, boy. That meant the word was going to happen in an alley, and the accounts were going to be settled permanently. So I went out the coal chute while they hammered on the front door, and I came down here, and I burned my own rooms behind me."

"Why burn them?"

"Because a missing man is hunted. A dead man is grieved and forgotten." Pell coughed, a dry tearing sound, and gripped Zephyrine's shoulder until it passed. "Fire leaves bones, or the idea of bones. Let them think I'm ash. Let them spend their looking on the river." He looked at Zephyrine then, really looked, lamp-light and shadow moving on his face. "You didn't tell them. About the ledger."

"No."

"They asked you?"

"Fenn asks everything. That's his whole employment. He hasn't asked that, though — he doesn't know it exists. As far as Renwick knows, the clock stopped because clocks stop." Zephyrine tightened the scarf-wrap and Pell hissed through his teeth. "I'm sorry. It has to be tight or it's nothing."

"Tight is honest," Pell said. "Go on."

"I moved it. The cylinder. It's inside the hour-weight, wrapped in oilcloth. Nothing short of disassembling the weight will find it, and nobody disassembles a weight that isn't broken." He paused. "Pell. Everyone thinks you're dead. Ilse thinks you're dead. I watched her hear the news and I have never seen a face do what hers did. If you're going to stay dead, we should decide that deliberately. And if you're not—"

"I'm not done," Pell said. Something came up in his voice that hadn't been there before, a banked heat. "I didn't hide down here to die in a hole like a rat. I hid down here because down here is where the clock keeps its secrets, and I had one more secret to keep until I found a man to give it to." His hand closed on Zephyrine's wrist — dry, strong, trembling slightly with pain or age or both. "I found him. You're the man. You've been the man since you climbed the stairs with your tool roll and looked at my stopped clock like it was a person that had been silenced."

Zephyrine knelt back on his heels. The water dripped its slow count. Above them, through a hundred feet of rock and masonry, the great clock hung stopped at 4:17, and somewhere beyond it Vellamare slept under its first true snow, and somewhere beyond that the Meridian sat frozen at her moorings with crates of bond papers in her hold, waiting for the tide that would carry the last of the evidence away forever.

"Then tell me what you hid," Zephyrine said.

"The striking work." Pell's eyes had gone bright in the lamplight, fever-bright maybe, or maybe just the brightness of a man who has carried something too long and can finally set it down. "Everyone thinks the chimes are music. They are, but they're also arithmetic — twelve bells in sequence, weighted, the intervals set by the pin drum. Any pattern the drum can be cut to play, the clock will play, and she'll play it loud enough to wake the river." He fumbled at his shirt, wincing, and drew out a folded paper gone soft with handling, stained with oil and sweat. "Every name in the ledger. Every date, every sum. Cut to a cipher — mine, simple enough that a schoolmaster could read it, published it in the almanac twenty years ago and everyone's forgotten. If the drum plays it, the whole city hears the court's crimes ring out over the rooftops, night after night, and no one can un-ring a bell."

Zephyrine took the paper. His hands were steady. Later, in private, they would shake; here, under the old man's eyes, they were steady, because hands remember their training even when the world comes apart.

"It can be done," he said slowly, and heard the plan ignite in him even as he spoke it, reckless and enormous. "Not on the original drum — Renwick would hear the difference the first night. But a second train. Hidden. Built into the case behind the bellows, driven off the same great wheel, connected only when—" He stopped. Recalculated. "It'll take parts I don't have. Brass stock, wire, pin rod. I can salvage from the dead movements in the workshop, but Fenn logs every tool I touch."

"Then don't touch tools he sees." Pell's grin was sudden and wolfish and made him look forty years younger. "Boy, you are sitting in the best-supplied junkshop in Vellamare. Forty years of replaced parts, worn drums, retired hammers — it's all down here, in the galleries, in the crates along the north wall. My hoard. Every keeper hoards; it's the one vice of the trade." He tapped the paper in Zephyrine's hand. "And you'll need dark hours to work. Tell Fenn you're tempering mainsprings. Night work reads as devotion to a clerk like that. He'll write it in his little book as evidence of your diligence, and it will be — just not his kind."

Zephyrine almost laughed. It startled him, the laugh, rising out of all that fear like a bubble through oil. Then it died, because the next thought arrived, the one that had been waiting for him since he'd knelt in the dust:

"They'll come looking down here eventually. When the search moves from the river to the rock. Fenn watches everything — sooner or later he'll notice the coal chute hinge, or find the panel seam, or simply follow me one night." He met Pell's eyes. "If they find you, they finish what the alley started. And if they find *me* down here with you, then the ledger, the plan, all of it dies in the same hour."

"Then don't get found."

"That's not a plan, Ambrose."

"It's the only plan there's ever been," Pell said. "Everything else is craft." He shifted against the stone, and the pain crossed his face like a cloud, and when it passed he was quiet a moment, looking up the black throat of the weight-shaft toward the distant, invisible sky. "You should know what you're carrying now. Before you decide anything else. You came here to fix a clock. Instead you're fixing to spend your life on this — yours, and the girl Verany's, and mine, such as it is. Two lives hang off your escapement now, boy. Maybe three, counting hers, counting whatever she decides to be when she finds out what you know. That's a heavier load than any weight in this hill. I want you to feel it before you lift it."

Zephyrine sat back in the dripping dark and felt it. Felt it settle across his shoulders the way the weights settled onto their cables — not crushing, not yet, but present, constant, pulling everything downward toward the single point where all the force of a mechanism must pass. The pallet. The escape wheel. The one small place where the entire system lived or died.

Ilse. He hadn't lied to Pell about watching her face when the news came — the way she had stood very still in the doorway, snow melting on her coat, and said *he deserved better than to die running,* and the stillness of her had frightened him more than any storm. She thought the old man was ash on the river wind. She was spending her grief like a woman with nothing left to spend. And Zephyrine was sitting on the truth of it, hoarding it the way keepers hoard, because a promise made in peacetime had teeth in peacetime and fangs now.

But a promise, he thought, looking down at Pell's ruined ankle, at the blood-smear on the fly fan, at the soft-worn paper full of names — a promise was a mechanism too. And mechanisms could be re-cut. Re-pinned. Made to serve a truth they were never built for.

"I feel it," he said aloud. "I'll carry it."

"Good." Pell closed his eyes. Within moments his breathing lengthened into sleep, the sleep of the utterly spent, and Zephyrine tucked the blanket higher around him and sat a while longer in the drip and the dark, memorizing the route home.

He was halfway up the iron rungs when he heard it — faint, filtered through a hundred feet of stone, but unmistakable to a man who had spent two weeks learning the building's every voice.

Boots. On the third stair going down.

Fenn was awake. Fenn was coming.
