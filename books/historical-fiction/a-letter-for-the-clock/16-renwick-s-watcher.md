## 16. Renwick's Watcher

The summons came to Mr. Fenn at half past six in the morning, delivered by a boy who smelled of the governor's kitchens — warm bread and candle tallow — and who would not meet his eyes.

Fenn dressed carefully. He always dressed carefully; it was a form of armor, and he had learned early that in Vellamare a man's cuffs were read before his face. Grey coat, grey waistcoat, boots polished to a dull humility that suggested diligence rather than ambition. He wound his pocket watch, checked it against the church bell of Saint Ferrol's, found it four seconds slow, and corrected it before he permitted himself breakfast.

His name was Aldous Fenn, though no one used the Aldous. He was thirty-one years old, a second clerk in the Office of Accounts, which meant he spent his days copying sums that other men had already summed, into ledgers that other men had already signed. He was good at it. He was very good at being invisible in rooms where visibility was dangerous, and very good at being visible when invisibility would cost him. These were not contradictions. They were the same skill, practiced from both ends.

He reported to the governor's residence by seven. Renwick received him in the small study off the west gallery, the one with the maps of the river district, and did not offer him a chair.

"You've heard," the governor said, "that there is trouble at the observatory."

"Only what the town says, sir. That the clock stopped."

"The clock stopped eleven days ago." Renwick stood with his back to the window, so that his face was in shadow and the river behind him burned with morning light. It was, Fenn thought, a posture the man had rehearsed. Everything about Renwick was rehearsed; that was what made him dangerous rather than merely powerful. Power that improvises can be surprised. Power that rehearses cannot. "A clockmaker's apprentice has been sent up to mend it. An apprentice, Fenn. Out of all the guilds of this city, the governor's own observatory, and they send me a boy with a tool roll."

"Perhaps the master feared the journey," Fenn offered. "The causeway road is poor."

"Perhaps." Renwick turned, and the light caught the lower half of his face, leaving the eyes unreadable — an effect Fenn suspected was also rehearsed. "Something was taken from my strongbox last week. A document. I won't describe it. I will say only that if it has surfaced anywhere in this city, it will surface through that observatory, because that tower is now the only place in Vellamare where a man can hide something from me and believe it hidden."

Fenn said nothing. He had learned that silence invited more than speech did, and that the men above him mistook his listening for agreement, which suited him fine.

"I'm assigning you to assist him," Renwick went on. "You'll carry tools, hold lamps, take notes on the work for the Office of Accounts — the repair will be billed to the city, and the city requires documentation." A thin smile. "Documentation is your gift, I'm told. Your ledgers are models of thoroughness."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me yet. You'll lodge at the observatory until the season turns or the bridge closes, whichever comes first. You'll observe everything. The keeper — Pell — is old and strange, and strange old men hoard things. There is a magistrate lodging there as well. Ilse Verany." He said the name the way a man says the name of a disease he has survived. "She is stripped of office, but she has not been stripped of her habits, and her habit is asking questions. If she asks you any, you will answer them honestly, stupidly, and completely, like a clerk who has never had a thought that wasn't written down first."

"And if she asks about the strongbox?"

Renwick looked at him for a long moment, and Fenn felt the particular pleasure-terror of being weighed by a man who weighed everything and discarded most. "Then you will look confused," the governor said, "and you will be believed, because you are going to be genuinely confused, because you genuinely do not know what was in it." He returned to his desk. "That is your protection, Fenn. Ignorance. Guard it. A man who knows too much cannot be innocent, and innocence is the only uniform you'll be issued."

* * *

The causeway road climbed north out of the city in long switchbacks, the river falling away below in sheets of grey-green light. Fenn rode up in a hired cart with his single valise, a writing case strapped to his knee out of habit, and watched Vellamare shrink behind him: the tiled roofs, the smoke standing straight in the cold air, the masts along the quay like a field of winter reeds. Somewhere down there were the debtor quarters, shuttered since autumn, their windows dark in the way windows are dark when no one has paid for glass to replace what broke.

He had copied the shipping manifests for those quarters once. Two years ago, a routine task, forty pages of names and bonded sums, each name followed by the notation *transported — debt service*. He had not thought about them then. He thought about them now, on the climbing road, without knowing why, and put the thought away the way he put away everything: neatly, dated, filed.

The observatory rose out of the hill's shoulder like a stone flower, white dome and black tower, its weathervane turning slowly in a wind that smelled of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Fenn paid the cartman, stood a moment in the yard letting the cold settle into him, and composed his face into what his mother had called his *good clerk's expression* — attentive, harmless, faintly tired.

Keeper Pell opened the door before he knocked.

The old man was smaller than Fenn expected, and quicker. His eyes went over Fenn the way a pawnbroker goes over a watch: coat, hands, boots, the strap of the writing case, the ink stain on the right cuff. "The governor sends a clerk," Pell said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"To assist with the repair and document costs for the Office of Accounts." Fenn produced the letter of assignment. "I'll try to be no trouble at all."

"No trouble at all," Pell repeated, tasting it. "That's what the tide says when it comes in." But he stepped aside, and Fenn entered, and the door closed behind him with a sound like a verdict withheld.

* * *

He met the apprentice within the hour, in the great chamber of the clock tower, and understood immediately why Renwick had been uneasy.

The room was enormous — a vertical cathedral of gears — and at its heart the master clock stood open like a dissected animal, its brass viscera exposed to the lamplight, and kneeling inside it, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, was a young man with dark skin and ink-stained fingers who did not stop working when Fenn entered. He acknowledged him with a nod, no more, and continued fitting something small and silver into a cluster of wheels that ticked around him like rain on a roof.

Zephyrine Oyelaran. Fenn knew the name already — Renwick's people kept files even on apprentices, especially perhaps on apprentices — and had expected someone cowed. What he found instead was a stillness. The young man moved through the mechanism with the unhurried certainty of a surgeon, and when at last he withdrew his hands, wiped them, and turned, his gaze crossed Fenn's face the way light crosses a dial: evenly, registering everything, committing to nothing.

"You're the governor's man," Zephyrine said.

"Mr. Fenn. Second clerk, Office of Accounts." He bowed slightly. "I'm to hold tools, keep records of parts consumed, and stay out of the way. In roughly that order."

Something flickered at the corner of the apprentice's mouth — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Parts consumed," he echoed. "You make it sound like a funeral."

"In my experience most things are, eventually. Clocks included."

"Mm." Zephyrine picked up a lamp and held it toward the open movement, and for a moment his whole face changed — lit from within by whatever it was he saw in there. "Come here, then, since you're to document. Tell me what you see."

Fenn stepped closer, wary of a trap, and looked. Gears upon gears, wheels and pinions, levers like sleeping animals. "I see a striking train," he said carefully, "and a going train, and a great deal of brass."

"You see a machine that has kept time for this city for sixty years without losing a minute a month. Every tooth in every wheel was cut by hand. And eleven days ago it stopped as if it had decided to." Zephyrine set the lamp down. "Your notes should say that. Not *stopped due to wear*. Stopped. Deliberately. Whoever wrote your reports for the next few weeks should write the truth, Mr. Fenn, even the parts that inconvenience people."

There it was, then — the first edge under the politeness. Fenn inclined his head. "I only record what I'm told," he said, in his best stupid-clerk voice, and watched the apprentice file *that* away too, behind those level eyes.

They measured each other for three seconds longer than courtesy required. Then Zephyrine turned back to the works, and Fenn opened his writing case, and the logging began.

* * *

It became a discipline within two days, and disciplines comforted him.

At seven each morning Fenn took his position at the side table Pell had grudgingly cleared, and began. *Day 3. Apprentice opened barrel housing, 8:10. Inspected mainspring, declared sound, 8:40. Removed count wheel for cleaning, 9:15.* He noted the oil used — two measures, saffron-colored, smelling of lavender and metal. He noted the rags, counted and recounted. He noted that the apprentice sang sometimes, low and tuneless, songs in a language Fenn didn't know, and stopped singing whenever footsteps approached the stair. He noted that Magistrate Verany came up twice daily and spoke with Zephyrine in voices too low to carry, and that when she left, the apprentice worked faster, as if her visits were wind in his sails. He noted that Keeper Pell slept badly and walked the gallery at night, and that on the third night Pell stood for eleven minutes — Fenn timed it — before the door of the clock chamber, not entering, just standing, like a man outside a confessional.

None of it meant anything yet. That was the point. Meaning was a thing you grew from observations the way a crystal grows from solution — slowly, invisibly, until one day the shape was simply there and undeniable. Fenn had built three careers' worth of such crystals in the Office of Accounts. Two men owed their ruin to his patience. He felt no pride in this and no shame either; it was simply his craft, as gear-cutting was the apprentice's, and he practiced it with the same devotion to tolerance and finish.

What unsettled him — and he logged this too, privately, in the coded margin shorthand only he could read — was how well the apprentice let him log.

Most men under surveillance chafed. They performed for the watcher or hid from him, and both performances were data. Oyelaran did neither. He worked. He explained things when asked, generously, precisely, holding up wheels to the light and naming their functions as if Fenn were a student rather than a spy. He answered questions about the guild, about wages, about his master Halloway's failing business, with a frankness that seemed artless and probably wasn't. Once, when Fenn remarked that the escapement was beautiful, the apprentice had looked at him for a long moment and said, "Yes. It is the part that decides when truth gets told. Everything else just stores the power." And then had gone back to his filing, humming, leaving Fenn to sit with the sentence like a stone in his shoe.

On the fifth day, Fenn wrote in his private margin: *He knows what I am. Unclear whether it matters to him.*

Below it, after consideration: *Assume it matters.*

* * *

The snows came early that year, and with them the news Fenn had been sent to await: the causeway bridge closed. A rider labored up through the flurries on the sixth evening with dispatches and a copy of the closure order, and Fenn read it twice by lamplight — *passage suspended until thaw, by order of the Harbor Commission* — and felt the observatory contract around him like a fist closing.

Trapped now. All of them. The keeper, the magistrate, the apprentice, himself, and whatever it was that Renwick feared enough to post a clerk in a clock tower. Outside, the wind came off the river with teeth in it, and the shutters spoke in their sleep, and somewhere below in the under-works the great weights descended inch by patient inch into the dark, storing power, as the apprentice had said. Everything else just stores the power.

Fenn sat late over his ledger, fair-copying the day's entries in his immaculate hand, and allowed himself the small luxury of thinking about what he would do with what he found — if he found anything. Promotion, certainly. Transfer to the capital, possibly. A life spent nearer the fire, which was where warmth was, and where men got burned. He was honest with himself about the risk; honesty with oneself was the one accounting he never fudged. Renwick devoured instruments when they dulled. Fenn intended never to dull.

A sound made him lift his head.

Footsteps overhead — in the clock chamber, where no one should be at half past midnight. Soft, deliberate, moving from the case toward the rear wall, where the old bellows and the weight shafts lived. Then silence. Then, after a while, the footsteps again, returning, and a door eased shut somewhere with a care that itself made noise to anyone listening for it.

Fenn sat quite still in the cold room, pen lifted, and listened to the building breathe.

In the morning he would note it, of course. *Apprentice active in chamber, 12:30–1:00 approx., purpose unknown.* But he found, logging it in advance in his mind, that he lingered over the entry — turned it over, examined its teeth, held it up to some inner light. Because there were two ways to write a report, and he had known that since he was nineteen years old and had chosen the wrong way exactly once and paid for it in ways he did not intend to catalogue tonight.

He could report that the apprentice moved strangely at night.

Or he could wait, and watch, and find out *why* — and then decide whose account the knowledge belonged to.

Fenn dipped his pen, wrote the neutral version, blotted it neatly, and blew out his lamp. Above him, through two floors of stone and timber, he could just hear it: the tick of the great clock's temporary works, keeping its imperfect time, waiting — like everyone in this tower, like the city below, like the river freezing toward its own decision — for something to strike.

He fell asleep composing tomorrow's questions. He would ask the apprentice about mainsprings. About tempering. About why fine steel was worked hot and cooled slow, and what happened to metal that was forced before its time.

And he would watch the young man's hands while he answered.
