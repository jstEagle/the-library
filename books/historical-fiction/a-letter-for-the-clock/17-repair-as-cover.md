## 17. Repair as Cover

The clock, Zephyrine learned, did not forgive.

It forgave nothing and it forgot nothing, and every morning he climbed the ninety-one steps into the great case with his tool roll under his arm and gave it another day of honest work — honest because it had to be, because Mr. Fenn sat below with his notebook open like a second mouth, because the governor's men came twice a week to look at the movement through their little brass lorgnette of suspicion. If the master clock had gone back together too quickly, too perfectly, they would have asked what else he'd found time to do. So he worked the way water works: patiently, in the dark, wearing down stone.

By day he was the diligent apprentice. He cleaned the going train tooth by tooth with a brush dipped in benzine. He re-bushed the worn pivot holes in the great wheel, cutting each brass bushing by hand on the lathe Pell had left behind — *Pell*, whose name was now a stain on the observatory's books, *the thief Pell, who fled with stolen funds* — and pressing them home with a staking set until the wheels spun true. He adjusted the escapement's drop so that each pallet caught its tooth with a sound like a held breath released. He polished the pendulum rod until it threw back his own face, thin and hollowed, a stranger's face, a face that hadn't slept properly in eleven days.

And every night, after Fenn went down the hill to his lodging in the keeper's old quarters — *my* quarters now, Zephyrine thought, though he slept in the workshop among the shavings rather than in any bed that man might visit — Zephyrine lit a lamp behind the bellows curtain and made treason out of brass.

* * *

The idea had come to him the way most true things come: not as inspiration but as inevitability, arriving fully formed at three in the morning when his hands were shaking from exhaustion and his mind had stopped pretending to govern itself.

A striking train was only this: a source of power, a counting mechanism, a release. The great bell above him already existed; the hammer already existed. What was missing was a second brain — a train that would count not hours but letters, that would strike the ledger's cipher into the midnight air in a pattern no citizen of Vellamare had ever heard, and that some of them, enough of them, would understand.

So he built one.

He could not buy parts. He could not order parts. Every file, every wheel blank, every length of steel wire that passed through Fenn's notebook had to be explainable — *mainspring tempering, sir, the old springs are soft as cheese* — and so Zephyrine became the most scrupulous bookkeeper of small metal in the history of Halloway & Sons. He drew up false repair lists in his careful apprentice hand and left them where they'd be found. He genuinely tempered mainsprings, dozens of them, filling the workshop with the smell of hot oil and scorched steel so that the smell itself would become alibi.

And then, at night, he stole from the clock.

Not really stealing — repurposing, he told himself, and knew the distinction was thinner than a pallet spring. The observatory was full of dead mechanisms: obsolete regulators in the cellar, a broken orrery in the archive, a century of retired parts shelved in drawers labeled in Keeper Pell's cramped, upright hand. From these he harvested wheels. A pinion here, a lantern pinion there, a count-wheel blank he filed down and re-cut tooth by tooth over four nights, checking each tooth against the cipher table he kept written in mirror-script inside the crown of his hat.

The cipher itself was simple once you understood its grammar — Ilse's phrase, *the numbers are people*, had taught him how to read it. Each name in the ledger resolved to a number of strikes; each number of strikes, separated by pauses of fixed length, spelled a date; each date anchored a sum. It was arithmetic disguised as music. Anyone who had the key — and he would give the key away freely, chalked on walls, tucked into fishmongers' scales, whispered into the ear of anyone who could carry it — could stand in the market square at midnight and hear the city's stolen names counted out on the great bell, one stroke per soul, until the whole river town knew exactly what its governor had sold and for how much.

But first he had to build it. And building it meant hiding it.

* * *

He hid the wheels inside the weights.

This was the joke of it, the bitter private joke that kept him upright through the fourth week: the governor's own clock weights, the great canisters of lead that drove the going train down their wires into the pit, were hollow. They had always been hollow — lead cast around an iron core, and between core and shell a void of perhaps two inches, running the full height, invisible, inaccessible, unless one unsoldered the base cap and looked.

Zephyrine unsoldered the base cap of the third weight on a Tuesday night, working by feel more than sight because the lamp had to stay hooded, and found the void clean and dry and waiting. Into it, wrapped in wool waste and waxed cloth, went the count-wheel. The escape wheel went into the second weight. The delicate stuff — the pallets, the pins, the collection of tiny levers that would make the release sequence speak — went into the bellows of the tower's defunct organ attachment, a relic of some nineteenth-century ambition, its leather lungs cracked and silent for forty years. No one opened bellows. No one opened weights. Things inside machines were assumed to belong there, the same way sins inside respectable men were assumed to be virtues.

Every morning before dawn he re-soldered the caps with a low flame, wiped the solder bright, and lowered the weights back onto their wires carrying their secret cargo like pregnant mules. Every morning Fenn arrived at eight, punctual as guilt, and consulted his notebook, and asked his questions.

"How goes the great work, Master Oyelaran?"

"Slowly, Mr. Fenn. Fine work is slow."

"Of course. And the night work?"

"The tempering, you mean." Zephyrine would hold up a mainspring, blue-gray, beautiful, real. "Steel remembers everything done to it. You have to be patient or it remembers wrong."

Fenn would smile his long smile and write something down, and Zephyrine would go back to his bench and think: *so do I.*

* * *

Exhaustion became a country he lived in. He learned its geography: the gray hour when his hands began to lie to him, missing the third finger's reach for a pin vise; the deeper hour when the lathe seemed to breathe; the deepest hour, near dawn, when he caught himself asleep standing up with a burnisher still in his grip and woke with the specific terror of a man who has dreamed of dropping something fragile. He ate standing. He slept in snatches of ninety minutes, timed like a striking train, because longer than that and the dream came — the dream where the bell struck thirteen and kept striking, names without end, and he stood in the tower unable to make it stop, unable to make it start, both failures at once.

Twice he nearly ruined everything through sheer fatigue. The first time, he almost soldered a weight cap with the count-wheel still sitting on the bench beside him, and caught it only because his eye snagged on its shadow — the wrong shadow, a shadow with teeth. He stood for a full minute afterward with his heart slamming, looking at the small brass wheel, thinking: *this is what I am now. A man who hides a list of the disappeared inside a machine that tells time.* He could not decide if it was blasphemy or devotion. Perhaps the clock didn't care. Clocks, unlike gods, accepted any worship offered honestly.

The second time was worse. Fenn appeared in the workshop doorway at half past two in the morning — ostensibly fetching a memorandum left in the tower, ostensibly — and found Zephyrine at the lathe, and for three endless seconds the lamplight lay full on the work: a wheel blank, half-filed, with a tooth pattern that matched no gear in any clock ever built in Vellamare.

Zephyrine did not flinch. That was the training of the past weeks speaking — the hands remember, the face forgets. He yawned, enormously, theatrically, and said, "Forgive the hour, Mr. Fenn. This spring refused to cooperate all day. Steel's contrary at night."

Fenn looked at the blank. Looked at him. Wrote something.

"A devoted apprentice," Fenn said at last, "is a treasure to his master."

"And a nuisance to himself," said Zephyrine, and laughed, and the laugh came out steady, and Fenn went away down the stairs, and Zephyrine sat down slowly on his stool and discovered that his shirt was soaked through and the room was cold.

That night he moved the cipher table out of the hat crown and committed it wholly to memory, and burned the paper in the forge, and watched the numbers curl and go black — the sums, the dates, the names beneath the names — and felt the ash settle on his sleeves like snow that wouldn't melt.

* * *

Ilse came on Thursdays.

She came openly, which was its own kind of cover — the disgraced magistrate visiting the observatory to consult the archive on her own defense, a thing she was technically permitted and practically despised for. She brought bread. She never brought questions in front of Fenn. They had built a grammar of glances the way he had built a grammar of strokes: a pause of fixed length meaning *nothing new*; two taps of her ring against her teacup meaning *someone watched us just now*; the particular way she said "good day" when it wasn't one.

On the fourth Thursday, she stayed late, helping him inventory the archive — legitimate work, tedious work, the kind of work that made watchers bored — and while Fenn was below checking the dock schedules, she stood beside him at the bench where the half-finished pallets lay under a rag, and said quietly, without looking at them:

"You're building something."

"I'm repairing something."

"Zephyrine." His name in her mouth still carried the weight of three years ago, the rain, the promise, the shape of a debt neither of them had ever named aloud. "I have read every document in this room. I know what the Meridian carries. What I don't know—" and here her voice did the smallest thing, a fracture no wider than a hairline in a pivot, "—is why you won't let me help carry it."

He looked at the pallets under the rag. He thought about telling her. The words were right there, loaded like a mainspring, needing only release.

"Not yet," he said. "Soon. I swear it on the clock."

"That," said Ilse Verany, gathering her coat, "is precisely what frightens me. You've started swearing oaths to machinery." And she went down the stairs, and the door closed, and Zephyrine stood alone in the lamplight with the truth under a rag and the exhaustion lying over him like river fog.

* * *

Near the end of the month, on a night of hard frost when the tower timbers ticked and groaned like a ship, he finished the last component: the release lever, a single piece of steel filed from an old saw blade over six evenings, its cam surface ground and polished until it would trip at the precise moment the going train's midnight lock disengaged. He held it up to the hooded lamp and turned it, watching light run along the bevel, and felt something that was not pride and not fear but the strange flat calm of a craftsman at the end of a long job — the moment when the object exists and the maker briefly doesn't.

He hid it inside the bellows with the rest. He re-soldered the final weight cap. He banked the forge, trimmed the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark workshop listening to the master clock tick overhead, steady, indifferent, keeping perfect time above the machinery of its own indictment.

Nine days, the tide tables said. Nine days until the Meridian sailed.

He blew out the lamp. In the blackness, the frost ticked, and somewhere below, faint as a pulse through stone, he heard the tower door open and close — footsteps crossing the workshop floor toward the stairs, unhurried, familiar.

Someone was climbing.
