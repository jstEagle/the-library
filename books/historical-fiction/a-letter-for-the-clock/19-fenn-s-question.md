## 19. Fenn's Question

Mr. Aldous Fenn had been keeping notebooks since he was nine years old, and in nineteen years he had learned one thing above all others: that people lied most fluently with their hands.

Words were cheap. Words could be rehearsed in the dark of a bedroom or muttered into a washbasin until they ran smooth as river stones. But hands — hands went on working while the mouth performed, and the hands always knew what the man knew. A clerk who feared dismissal folded his papers too many times. A wife who no longer loved her husband smoothed her apron where his name would have rested. A governor who had grown rich on other men's bodies tapped one finger against his signet ring, twice, when a question displeased him — a small metronome of irritation that only a patient observer would ever count.

Fenn counted. It was why Governor Renwick kept him, though neither of them ever said so aloud. Officially, Mr. Fenn was a second clerk attached to the observatory commission, sent upriver to "assist with documentation" during the clock's repair. Unofficially, he was a pair of eyes with a salary, and he was very good at being unofficial. He had a talent for unremarkableness — a face like an unpaid bill, a voice like damp weather, a manner so deferential that servants forgot to stop talking around him. He wore grey because grey was invisible, and he carried his notebook everywhere because a notebook was the least threatening object in the world. Everyone wrote things down. Only the guilty worried about who read them later.

He had filled forty pages since arriving at the observatory, and thirty-eight of them concerned the apprentice.

* * *

The boy's name was Zephyrine Oyelaran, and on paper he was nothing: seventeen years old, apprenticed to Halloway & Sons, no family of consequence, no money, no history worth the ink. But paper lied, too. Fenn had watched him now for six days, and everything about the boy was slightly, persistently wrong.

He worked like a man twice his age — that was not suspicious in itself; fine trades aged their practitioners early. What was suspicious was the *shape* of his days. By daylight he repaired the master clock with a diligence so complete it looked almost devotional: cleaning pivots, truing the escapement, adjusting the pendulum's rating nut by fractions no ordinary eye could follow. He was good. He was, Fenn suspected privately, better than good — there was a rightness to his hands that made the great mechanism seem less like machinery than like something being healed.

But at night — at night, the boy did not sleep.

Fenn had noticed it the second evening, quite by accident. He had risen past midnight with a sour stomach and stood at his window to breathe the cold air, and below him, across the courtyard, lamplight burned in the workshop under the tower. He had told himself it was Pell, the old keeper, unable to sleep in his own house. But Pell's window had been dark, and when Fenn leaned out further, careful as a heron, he had seen the shadow of a young man bent over a bench, and heard — faintly, threading through the frozen air — the small music of metal being worked. Not filed. *Cut.* A jeweler's saw, perhaps, or a piercing frame, the sound of brass being persuaded into shapes it did not naturally hold.

On the third night, the same. The fourth, the same, though later — past two o'clock — and longer. On the fifth night the lamp moved: from the bench to the floor beside the great weights, then back again, and once, for nearly an hour, it did not move at all, which meant someone was lying still in the dark doing either nothing or something.

A diligent apprentice might work late to finish a repair. A diligent apprentice did not work *in secret*. The master clock's case stood open all day; whatever the boy did after midnight, he did not want it seen, and he did not want it heard, and he arranged his nights around both wants with the discipline of a conspirator.

Fenn wrote this down in his neat, upright hand, and then he sat for a long while looking at the words, feeling the peculiar cold pleasure that was the nearest thing he had to joy. Somewhere beneath the observatory, in the dark, an honest craftsman was building a lie out of brass. And Mr. Aldous Fenn intended to find out what shape it was.

* * *

He chose the morning carefully.

Renwick had departed for Vellamare two days prior, leaving instructions that the clock be finished "before the season turns" — an order delivered with that smiling menace the governor wore like a dress uniform. Ilse Verany, the disgraced magistrate who lodged in the east room and watched everyone with the flat patience of a cat at a mousehole, had gone down to the archive before dawn, as she did most mornings now, hunting through old manifests with the desperation of a woman digging for her own name. Pell was still limping from his fall — the official story, the fall, though Fenn had noted the burns on the keeper's doorframe and drawn his own quiet conclusions — and Pell kept to his quarters until midmorning.

Which left the workshop, the apprentice, and the snow.

Fenn dressed slowly, chose a notebook with fresh pages, and practiced his expression in the spotted mirror: open, friendly, faintly stupid. Curiosity without threat. He had found that the best interrogations never felt like interrogations; they felt like conversation between two bored men waiting out bad weather. You asked about the work itself — craftsmen loved to talk about their work; it was the one subject on which vanity overcame caution — and you let them teach you, and you listened with your whole body, and somewhere in the middle of their lecture they forgot you were listening at all. Then you went away and compared what they'd said against what you'd seen, and the gaps between the two were where the truth lived.

The gap this morning was a mainspring.

* * *

The workshop smelled of oil and cold iron and the ghost of wood shavings. Zephyrine was already at the bench when Fenn came in, sleeves rolled, a loupe screwed into his eye, working over a coil of steel no larger than a bird's nest. The single lamp — the night lamp, Fenn noted; the same lamp, still warm — stood pushed to the corner as though it had always lived there.

"Good morning," Fenn said, cheerful as porridge. "Cold enough to crack the river."

"Mm." The boy didn't look up. His hands moved among the coils of the spring with a delicacy that seemed almost tender, testing tension, easing a kink.

Fenn helped himself to the stool at the end of the bench — not too close; closeness was pressure, and pressure closed mouths — and produced his notebook with a small flourish, as if taking notes were simply what clerks did with their hands. "I've been meaning to ask you," he said. "About the mainsprings. I confess I don't understand them at all. All that power coiled up inside a ribbon of steel thinner than my finger. How does it not simply tear itself apart?"

That worked, as it usually did. Something in the boy's shoulders loosened a fraction — the universal relaxation of a man invited to explain his own excellence. He drew the loupe from his eye and held up the spring to the window light, where it threw a faint blue shimmer.

"Tempering," he said. "That's the whole secret. You can forge a spring perfect to the ten-thousandth and it will die in a week if the temper's wrong. Too soft and it sets — takes a memory of being wound and never fully unwinds again. Too hard and it snaps." He turned the steel, and Fenn marked how his thumb found a certain spot on the coil, an unconscious gesture, a habit. "You cook it in the flame and then you quench it, and then you cook it again, gentler, at low heat, until it turns this color—" he tilted it toward Fenn, "—straw. Pale straw for springs. Blue for balance wheels. Every shade is a temperature, and every temperature is a different temper of the soul of the metal."

"The soul of the metal," Fenn repeated, writing it down, delighted. "You talk about it as if it were alive."

"Everything in a clock is alive, if you mean it has a temper." The boy said it lightly, but his eyes went, just for half a heartbeat, toward the stair that led up to the great case — toward the stopped heart of the observatory — and Fenn wrote that glance down too, inwardly, in the notebook behind his eyes. "Steel remembers what you do to it. Heat it, beat it, quench it — it holds all of it, quietly, forever, and gives it back when you least expect. That's why you can't rush it. That's why—"

He stopped. Just barely. A word swallowed whole.

"Why?" Fenn asked pleasantly.

"Why some jobs take as long as they take," Zephyrine said, and turned back to the bench.

There it was. The first knot.

Fenn let the silence sit a moment, unhurried, then leaned forward with the eagerness of genuine ignorance. "And the tempering — is that slow work? Is that what keeps you at your bench so late? I've seen your lamp burning past midnight, you know. I'd have thought even steel needed its sleep." He laughed at his own small joke, warm and harmless.

The boy's hands paused over the spring. It lasted perhaps two seconds — long enough for Fenn to count three full beats of his own patient heart — and then resumed. When Zephyrine spoke, his voice was easy, almost amused. "You've been watching my lamp, Mr. Fenn? I'm flattered."

"Hard to miss it. I'm a light sleeper, and the courtyard's bright with snow."

"Then you know how short the days are." The apprentice set the spring down and finally looked at him directly, and Fenn was struck, as he was every time, by the composure in the young face — not arrogance, nothing so crude, but a settled quality, like water that has decided which way to run. "Tempering can't be done in daylight hours alone. Not if the job matters. The metal has to cool at its own pace, and you can't leave a spring half-annealed overnight in the cold — the temperature swings ruin it. So you stay with it. You heat, you quench, you draw the temper at the fire, and you watch the color come. Straw. Then brown. Then blue. If you look away at the wrong moment—" he snapped his fingers, softly, "—you start again with new steel and you've wasted a week."

"That sounds lonely work."

"It's honest work," the boy said. "It doesn't mind company, but it doesn't need it."

Another knot. Two knots, close together now, and Fenn felt the line go taut.

Because the answer was *good*. That was the maddening thing. He had gone down to the workshop armed with questions and found the boy had answers pre-built, smooth-fitted, load-bearing — answers about cooling curves and color changes that Fenn could not fault, though he understood metallurgy only as a gentleman understood farming. Either the apprentice was telling the truth, or the apprentice had prepared a truth-shaped thing and rehearsed it until it rang true. And Fenn had listened to enough rehearsed testimony in Renwick's antechambers to know that rehearsed truth rang truest of all.

"Still," Fenn said, folding his hands atop the notebook, "tempering alone doesn't account for the sounds. I heard sawing last night. Or filing. Metal being cut — that thin singing a piercing frame makes. Unless your springs arrive needing surgery?"

Something crossed the boy's face then, quick as a fish under ice. Not fear — Fenn would have given much for fear; fear was a confession. It was closer to *recalculation*, the flicker of a mind checking its ledgers. And then the composure returned, seamless, and Zephyrine smiled — actually smiled, a real one, tired at the edges.

"You have good ears, Mr. Fenn. That was the spring again. When you draw a temper you sometimes have to correct the coil afterward — ease a section that's pulled tight, open one that's closed. You cut nothing. You persuade." He picked up a small tool from the bench, showed it: a slender frame with a blade fine as a grass stem. "This. A piercing saw. For brasswork mostly — decorative work, dial work. On steel this fine it only whispers." He demonstrated, drawing the blade once across a scrap of offcut, and it made exactly the sound Fenn had heard through his window at two in the morning: a high, threadlike singing.

Except. Except that Fenn had heard that sound run on for minutes at a stretch, steady as rain, and a correction to a single spring took seconds. And except that the boy had reached for that particular tool a half-beat too quickly, the way a man reaches for a lie he has already built a shelf for.

Fenn smiled back. He smiled for a long time — longer than politeness required, longer than comfort allowed, watching the boy watch him smile, and he let it stretch until the workshop air itself seemed to tighten around them like wire. He wanted the boy to feel it: not the accusation, which could be denied, but the *attention*, which could not. *I see your lamp,* the smile said. *I hear your whispering saw. I am counting your nights.*

"You're very good at explaining things," Fenn said at last, closing the notebook. "Your master must be proud. Halloway & Sons, isn't it? The shop by the Salt Gate?" He stood, brushing non-existent dust from his knee. "I'll write down what you've taught me. About the tempers. The governor does like his reports thorough — and he does so hate surprises."

For the first time, the apprentice's composure showed a seam. Small — a fractional stillness, a blink held a heartbeat past natural — but Fenn had spent nineteen years reading hands and faces, and seams were his trade. *Surprises.* The word landed. Fenn watched it land.

"He'll get no surprises from me," Zephyrine said quietly.

"No," Fenn agreed pleasantly. "I'm sure he won't. Good day, and thank you for the lesson."

At the door he paused, as if struck by an afterthought, and turned. "One more thing. Purely clerical. Your lamp — the oil comes out of the common stores. I log the stores." He let that sit in the cold air between them like a coin placed on a table. "You burn more than the rest of the observatory together. I'll note it as tempering, shall I? Unless you'd rather I write the truth, whatever it happens to be."

"I'd rather you write tempering," the boy said, "since that's what it is."

"Of course," said Fenn, and meant: *then you won't mind if I check.*

* * *

He climbed back to his room through the falling snow and sat at his desk without lighting a lamp, letting the grey noon light do its grey work on the page. He wrote for an hour. *Subject works nights. Explains nights as tempering — technically plausible, insufficient. Sound of sawing consistent with brass-cutting, not spring-correction. Subject fluent under questioning; fluency suggests preparation. Subject reacted to mention of reports/surprises. Oil consumption logged: 3× household average. Recommend continued observation. Recommend inspection of workshop contents during subject's absence.*

He read it back twice, and then, because he was thorough, he did what he always did with a nearly finished page: he tried to argue himself out of it.

Perhaps the boy truly was tempering springs. Perhaps the extra cutting was dial work, decoration, a private commission. Perhaps the long smiles and the ready answers belonged to nothing darker than a clever young man protecting his craft secrets from a clerk he rightly distrusted. Fenn had been wrong before — twice, memorably, and both errors had cost him a month of humiliation and a season of Renwick's silken contempt. He knew the shape of his own mistakes: he found patterns the way starving men found bread, eagerly and sometimes in gravel.

But.

Thirty-eight pages. The stopped clock that had been stopped *deliberately*, by a hand that knew mechanisms — that fact alone sat in Fenn's stomach like a swallowed key. Pell's burned door. Verany's daily excavation of the archives. The Meridian loading bond crates at the frozen docks under guard, sailing on the morning tide, carrying whatever it carried out of all their lives forever. Everything in this place was a knot, and the boy's lamp burned at the center of the tangle every night, steady as a signal.

And there was one more thing, the smallest thing, the thing Fenn saved for last because it troubled him most. Last night — the sixth night, the worst of the wind — the lamp had not appeared at all until nearly three, and then it had shown not in the workshop but *above*: a moving light behind the tall windows of the tower itself, climbing, pausing, climbing, somewhere in the under-works where the great weights hung in their shafts like organs in a body. No tempering was done in the under-works. No spring lived up there. Whatever the apprentice had been doing in the dark belly of the clock, it had nothing to do with steel's temper and everything to do with the clock's.

Fenn dipped his pen and added a final line, in his smallest, neatest script:

*Subject's true work is in the tower. Find out what he builds there before the tide takes the question away.*

Below the window, far downriver, the Meridian's riding lamps glimmered through the snowfall — patient, anchored, waiting. Nine days, the harbor-master's table said. Nine days of tide left in the world.

Fenn blotted the page, squared it with the others, and began, with the contentment of a man tightening a knot he alone could feel, to draft his letter to the governor.
