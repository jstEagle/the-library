## 22. Sabotage Suspected

Governor Aldous Renwick woke before dawn, as he always did, to the sound of the river breathing against the pilings below Government House. For twenty-two years that sound had been a lullaby of commerce — crates knocking hulls, ropes creaking through blocks, the low chorus of men who moved other men's fortunes on his word. This morning it sounded like something else. Like water finding its way into a cellar.

He lay still beneath the linen and let the unease name itself.

The strongbox.

It was a small thing, the thought, no larger than a coin of doubt, but coins have a way of rolling into cracks and multiplying there in the dark. He had opened the box last night — he could not afterward say why; habit, perhaps, or some animal instinct beneath the habit — and had run his thumb along the row of ledgers bound in grey calf, the manifests, the letters of credit, the little empire of paper by which Vellamare's debts became its disappearances. All present. All in order. Except—

Except the cylinder.

He had told himself it was misfiled. He had told himself Fenn had taken it for copying. He had told himself three or four reasonable things while the candle guttered and his own reflection looked back at him from the polished lid with an expression he did not care for: the expression of a man who already knows.

Now, in the grey hour, he stopped telling himself anything at all, and rang for his valet.

* * *

The strongbox sat in the inner study, behind two doors and a screen of carved cedar, because Renwick believed — had always believed — that power was mostly architecture. You built rooms within rooms, and men learned their size by the number of thresholds they were permitted to cross. He knelt before it now himself, refusing help, and turned the dial with fingers that were steadier than he felt.

Inside, the papers lay in their appointed ranks like soldiers at inspection. Grey ledgers. Blue manifests. The oilskin packet of bond certificates awaiting the *Meridian*. And the space where the brass cylinder had rested for eleven years, a space shaped exactly like itself, lined in velvet gone pale where the metal had pressed it — a negative image, a wound in the fabric of the arrangement.

Empty.

He sat back on his heels. The fire had not yet been lit; the room was cold enough that his breath showed faintly, and for a moment he watched it dissipate and thought: *that is what authority is, when the agreement withdraws.* Vapor. Shaped briefly like something solid, then nothing.

"Mr. Fenn," he said, without raising his voice, because he had never needed to raise his voice. The house was built to carry him.

Fenn arrived as he always did, already dressed, notebook in hand, spectacles catching the early light so that his eyes were invisible behind two small white flames. Renwick had chosen him years ago precisely for that quality — a man whose face gave away nothing because everything about him was given over to recording.

"The cylinder," Renwick said. "From my strongbox. When did you last see it?"

Fenn did not consult his notes, which was itself an answer; a man who consulted them would be a man who might not know. "Eleven days ago, sir. When you placed it there yourself, after the observatory clock was found stopped."

"And since?"

"I have entered the room twice at your instruction — to fetch the manifests for the dock schedule. I touched nothing else."

"You're certain."

"I am certain that I touched nothing else." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat on a table. "I cannot speak for others. There are four servants with access to this corridor. The night watchman makes his rounds at eleven and three. And Mrs. Halloway's boy came Tuesday with the invoice for the clock parts — he waited in the outer study, but doors were open."

Renwick rose. His knees complained; he ignored them the way he ignored all small rebellions of the body, on the principle that attention encouraged them.

"Eleven days," he said softly. "The clock stops. Eleven days later the cylinder is gone. Do you find that arithmetic restful, Mr. Fenn?"

"No, sir."

"Neither do I."

He crossed to the window. Below, the river lay under its first skin of ice at the margins, the channel still dark and open in the middle where the current ran — the *Meridian*'s road out, narrowing day by day. Nine days until the morning tide she was contracted for. After that, the ice would close the river entirely, and everything in that strongbox — everything now missing from it — would be stranded here, in a city full of eyes, until spring thawed both the water and whatever patience the capital's creditors possessed.

Someone had taken the ledger. Someone had taken eleven years of names and sums and dates, the whole grammar of the arrangement, and walked it out through two doors and a cedar screen.

Or someone had never put it back at all.

That was the thought he kept turning over, the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath. Because there was another possibility, colder than theft: that Keeper Pell — that soft-eyed old religious functionary of gears and pendulums — had never surrendered the only copy. That the cylinder in the strongbox had been a duplicate all along, a decoy Renwick himself had been shown and had accepted because accepting it was easier than admitting the old man had outmaneuvered him. That somewhere in that tower of wheels and weights, above a city that told its hours by Renwick's sufferance, the true account of every soul sold downriver sat waiting to be read.

He pressed his palm flat against the cold glass.

"Double the guard at the docks," he said. "Every crate for the *Meridian* is to be opened, listed, and sealed in my presence or Captain Durell's — no one else's. Nothing boards that ship that isn't on my manifest."

"Yes, sir."

"And the observatory." He heard his own voice go quiet, which those who knew him understood to be the most dangerous register he owned. "I want a man inside it. Not a soldier — soldiers make people careful. A clerk. Someone with a notebook and a patient manner. The clockmaker's apprentice has been up there eleven days on a repair that should have taken three."

"He may simply be slow, sir. Apprentices usually are."

"Apprentices are slow," Renwick agreed. "Clocks are not. That clock was stopped deliberately once already — we know this because the thing that stopped it is missing from my strongbox. So we will assume the man who winds it now is either a fool or a courier, and we will treat him as the second, because fools are cheap and couriers are not." He turned from the window. "You'll go yourself, Mr. Fenn. Offer your assistance. Be useful. Be pleasant. Write down everything — how long he works, when he sleeps, what he carries in, what he carries out. If he asks why you're watching him—"

"He won't, sir."

"No. They rarely do. Guilt is polite; it thanks you for your interest."

* * *

By midmorning the snow had begun again, fine and dry as sifted flour, and Renwick stood at the tall window of the council chamber while the city whitened beneath him and the walls, metaphorically speaking, drew inward.

He had summoned them — Verany's old colleagues, the magistrates who had learned to look at documents without reading them — and they sat around the table in a silence he found instructive. Not loyal silence. Calculating silence. He knew the difference the way a farmer knows weather by the ache in old bones. Loyal silence waits for instructions. Calculating silence is measuring the distance to the door.

"Gentlemen," he began, "a matter of housekeeping."

They inclined their heads. Magistrate Corvane, fat and pink and always slightly damp-looking, arranged his hands like a man posing for a portrait of innocence. Young Delacroix studied his own cufflinks. None of them would meet the empty chair at the end of the table — Ilse Verany's chair, which had not yet been removed, because removing it would be an admission that her removal meant something.

"A document has gone missing from my custody," Renwick said. "A record of municipal debt transactions. Its absence is inconvenient rather than alarming, and I mention it only so that if any of you are offered — let us say — unusual paper by unusual hands, you will know where it came from and return it promptly." He smiled, and watched the smile do its work: not warmth but weather, a front moving through. "There will be no inquiry. There will be no scandal. There will simply be a restoration, and everyone involved in restoring it will be remembered generously. Everyone who obstructs it—" he let the sentence stand unfinished, a door left open onto a cold room, "—will also be remembered."

Corvane cleared his throat. "Governor, if I might — the timing. First the Verany affair, now this. People talk. The market women talk, the dockhands talk—"

"People always talk, Corvane. It is what they do instead of thinking." He moved along the window, and their heads turned with him, a tableful of flowers following a sun. "What matters is not what they say but whether what they say can attach to anything. Paper attaches. Names attach. Dates attach. As long as the talking stays loose — air, rumor, complaint — it is merely the sound a city makes. But if someone gives it structure—" he tapped the tabletop once, a single clear note, "—then it becomes evidence, and evidence requires response, and response is expensive for everyone. Including you gentlemen. Especially you."

Delacroix spoke without looking up from his cufflinks. "And the ship? The *Meridian* sails on the tide after next."

"The *Meridian* sails when I say she sails, with what I say she carries, guarded by twice the men I planned yesterday." Renwick paused at the end of the table, nearest the empty chair, and permitted himself to look at it. Ilse Verany had sat there for six years and asked questions for the last eight months of it, each one small and reasonable and corrosive as meltwater, until the whole arrangement had begun to groan in its joints. She was stripped of office now, disgraced, declared an enemy of the state — and still her chair commanded more attention than any occupied one in the room. Power, he reflected, was not only architecture. It was haunting. Rooms stayed shaped around the people who had challenged them.

"If anyone approaches any of you," he said, "with documents, with questions, with appeals to conscience or to fear — you will bring it to me before you breathe a word to anyone else. Not after. Before. Am I understood?"

Murmurs of assent, variously pitched. He read them the way he read everything: Corvane would comply out of appetite, Delacroix out of terror, and the two quiet ones at the far end out of something he chose, for his own comfort, to call prudence.

He dismissed them and stood alone in the chamber as the snow thickened against the glass, erasing the river, the docks, the far tower — erasing the city down to a few near shapes, as though the world were being withdrawn from him one layer at a time.

* * *

In the afternoon he went down to the docks himself.

This was against the advice of everyone, and he did it precisely for that reason. A governor who inspected his own cargo was a governor who could not be lied to about his own cargo; a governor who stayed in his study was a governor who learned of rot from reports, and reports were written by the same men doing the rotting. Besides, he wanted to feel the cold. Fear, properly administered, was clarifying, and he wished to be very clear over the next nine days.

The *Meridian* lay at her berth like a sleeping animal, black hull furred with frost, her masts bare against a sky the color of dirty wool. The doubled guard stood along the quay — thirty men now, bayonets fixed, breath rising in columns — and the crates destined for her hold sat in rows under tarpaulins, each one chalked, sealed, and manifest-listed in Fenn's immaculate hand.

Captain Durell met him at the gangway, stamping his boots, a weathered man with a beard like frozen tar. "Governor. We're ready on your word. Tide after next, weather willing."

"Weather is never willing, Captain. It is only occasionally bribed." Renwick walked the line of crates, touching none of them, letting the guards watch him not touch them. "These go aboard under your eye and Lieutenant Marsh's, jointly, and neither of you signs without the other. The hold is locked in both your keeping. And Captain—" he turned, and lowered his voice to the register he used for gifts and threats, having never seen the need to distinguish them, "—if this ship leaves this berth carrying anything not on my list, or fails to leave on the tide I name, your commission will become a memory and your name a cautionary tale. Do you find that acceptable?"

Durell held his gaze for a moment longer than a subordinate strictly should — a sailor's frankness, salt-cured — and then nodded. "Acceptable, Governor. Though I'll say plain: ice don't negotiate. If the river closes early, no guard on this quay puts her out."

"Then pray the river keeps its agreements better than my colleagues do."

He walked back along the quay slowly, past the fish stalls shuttered for winter, past the debtor quarter's edge where the windows had been dark for months — dark windows, empty bunks, names struck from rent rolls with a neatness that had once pleased him and now read like handwriting on a wall. A woman was sweeping snow from her doorstep. She stopped as he passed and did not curtsey, and did not look away either, and something in the flatness of her gaze — neither defiance nor submission, just *witness*, patient and unhurried — lodged under his skin like a splinter too deep to dig out.

They know something, he thought. Or they sense it, the way animals sense weather. The city was holding its breath, and a city's breath could be held only so long.

Back at Government House, Fenn was waiting in the study with his notebook and a cup of tea gone cold beside him.

"Well?"

"The apprentice, sir. Zephyrine Oyelaran. Twenty-two years old, apprenticed six years to Halloway & Sons. Sent upriver alone because the master refused the summons — illness, he claimed, though the shop traded normally within the week." Fenn turned a page. "He works days on the clock's going train, genuine work — Pell confirms the escapement was damaged. But he also works nights. Two, sometimes three hours past midnight. He requests lamp oil beyond his allotment. And last Thursday he sent down to the ironmonger for brass stock — rod and sheet — invoiced to the observatory account, described as 'general repair.'"

Renwick stood at the fire, warming his hands, saying nothing. Brass. Rod and sheet. Enough, if a man were clever, to machine almost anything a clock could want.

"Pell?" he asked.

"Keeps to himself. Old. Afraid of something — he startles at footsteps. He has not left the observatory since the bridge closed." A pause. "And the magistrate Verany has taken rooms there, sir. In the keeper's wing. Since the snows began."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, a door closed, and the sound traveled up through the house's bones like a pulse.

So. The disgraced magistrate, the frightened keeper, and the apprentice who worked by night with brass — all gathered in a tower that had already stopped once on purpose, at the exact hour the cylinder vanished from a strongbox behind two doors and a cedar screen. It was nothing, of course. It was coincidence wearing the costume of conspiracy, as coincidences often did in anxious minds. Any magistrate in the republic would laugh the pattern out of court.

But Renwick had spent twenty-two years learning that courts were not where power lived or died. Power lived in whether the dockworkers loaded the crates, whether the soldiers obeyed the order to burn a barricade, whether the city believed the story you told it at breakfast. And patterns, laughed out of courts, had a way of returning through windows at midnight.

"Mr. Fenn," he said, "you will take up residence at the observatory tomorrow. Effective immediately."

"Yes, sir."

"And you will find out what that boy is building."

Fenn bowed his head, and behind the two small flames of his spectacles, something moved — quick and unreadable, gone before it could be named — and Renwick, who missed very little even now, filed it away in the growing ledger of things he would examine when there was time.

Outside, the snow went on falling, filling the ruts in the causeway road, sealing the bridge, closing the ring. Nine days to the tide. The walls were drawing in, and Aldous Renwick told himself, as he poured the cold tea away and reached for the bottle instead, that it was the conspirators who were trapped.

He did not quite succeed in believing it, and the clock in the corner — a small, honest, unimportant clock — ticked on toward evening regardless of what any of them intended.
