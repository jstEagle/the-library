## 23. Pell's Sacrifice

Ambrose Pell had kept the observatory for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above the rest: a clock does not forgive. It only records. Every hesitation, every lie, every hour stolen and spent — the great mechanism above his head held it all in its brass memory, patient as God, impartial as tide. He had hidden a ledger inside that memory because he could no longer carry it inside his own, and now the carrying had passed to a boy with steady hands, and Pell's part, he understood, was nearly finished.

He felt it the way an old clockmaker feels a worn pivot: not by looking, but by listening. The tower had begun to sound wrong to him. The footsteps on the stair came too often. Mr. Fenn's boots had a particular rhythm — unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a man who counted things — and lately that rhythm climbed the spiral more frequently than any errand required.

* * *

The ninth night after Pell's flight from his burning quarters, he lay in the under-works among the weight channels, wrapped in a sailcloth blanket that smelled of hemp and cold stone, and listened to the building breathe. Above him, Zephyrine worked at the second train — Pell could tell by the sound. The boy filed differently when he was doing forbidden work: shorter strokes, breath held between them, as though the metal might inform on him if he made a noise. It was beautiful, that caution. It was also, Pell knew, doomed. Fenn's notebook grew fat as a harvest mouse before winter, and notebooks like that were never kept for their own sake.

Pell's ribs ached where the beam had caught him during his escape from the fire. His left hand still trembled when he tried fine work — the burns across two fingers had healed crooked, and the physician Renwick permitted him (a courtesy that was really a leash) had splinted them badly out of spite or haste. He could no longer hold a graver. He could barely hold a spoon. But a man did not need hands for what he had decided upon.

He had been turning the decision over for three nights, the way one turns a recalcitrant screw, testing whether it would seat. Each night it seated deeper. The mathematics of it were simple enough for even a magistrate to follow: Fenn was closing on the boy. When Fenn closed, Renwick would come, and everything Zephyrine had machined in the dark — nine nights of hidden genius folded into bellows and counterweights — would be seized, melted, forgotten, along with the boy himself and the Verany woman besides. Unless something pulled Fenn away. Unless something pulled all of them away, loudly, unmistakably, toward another door.

There was only one other door in the observatory that rang loud enough.

* * *

In the morning he climbed, slowly, painfully, to the old keeper's gallery — the narrow iron balcony bolted inside the belfry chamber, sixty feet below the great bell itself but close enough that its voice arrived through the bones rather than the ears. He had rung the alarm from this gallery perhaps six times in thirty-one years: twice for fire, once for flood, three times for drills that Renwick's predecessor had insisted upon and Renwick had let lapse. The mechanism was a simple one — a hand crank engaging the bell's yoke directly, bypassing the clock train entirely, so that the alarm could sound even when the master clock stood dead. He had built that bypass himself, years ago, and had always thought of it as the most honest piece of work in the tower: a voice that worked even when time itself failed.

Now it would be his own voice, spent all at once.

He waited through the day. He watched from the gallery's slats as Zephyrine descended at dusk to eat, as Ilse Verany took her turn at the archive window pretending to read manifests she had long since memorized, as Fenn's lamp burned in the lower scriptorium with its patient yellow patience. At half past eight the boy went back up to the under-works. Good. Whatever came next must not catch Zephyrine in the open.

At a quarter past eleven, Pell heard the front door far below admit someone new — heard the particular deference of the sentries' greeting that they reserved for their captain — and knew the arithmetic had finished itself. They were coming tonight. Of course they were coming tonight. Renwick had lost his ledger cylinder; a man who has lost his ledger counts everyone twice a day until he finds it or burns the counting-house down around it.

Pell stood. His knees announced their objections and he overruled them, as he had done for seventy years. He crossed the gallery to the crank housing, set his burned hand flat against the cold iron, and allowed himself one minute of looking — at the bell's dark shoulder looming above him like the flank of a sleeping animal, at the vent slit beyond it where the city showed through in its winter scattering of lights. Vellamare. Eleven thousand souls asleep beneath this roof's shadow, none of them knowing what had been done in their names, in ledgers, in sums. He had served this city badly for a long time by serving its clock well. Tonight the accounts would begin to balance.

"Forgive me the noise," he said aloud, to the bell, because an old keeper apologizes to his instruments. "It isn't your fault what I'm about to ask of you."

Then he turned the crank.

* * *

The first stroke went through him like a ship's collision. The whole tower became a struck thing — iron singing in the walls, dust sifting gold through the lamplight, the floor trembling under his ruined feet — and the sound poured out through the louvres into the frozen dark, enormous, ancient, furious. Fire. Fire in the observatory. That was what the alarm meant, and every soul within earshot would know it, and every soul within earshot included the garrison barracks by the docks, the watch house on Cordwainer Row, and — this was the point, this was the whole of the point — Mr. Fenn at his lamp below, who would have to choose between his notebook and his duty, and whose duty, whatever else could be said of him, was real to him. Pell had watched the man's face for days. Fenn was a spy, but he was a believing spy, and believing men run toward alarms.

Pell cranked on. Stroke, and stroke, and stroke. His shoulders screamed. His burned fingers slipped on the handle and he regripped with his forearm, hauling with his whole body, an old man wrestling a giant awake. Below he heard doors bang, boots, shouting — Fenn's voice rising in command, sentries clattering into the yard, the captain's whistle splitting the night. Let them come to the wrong fire. Let them search the empty east range while a boy in the west under-works sat motionless beside a hidden machine, holding his breath, being nothing, being nobody.

Stroke. Stroke. He was weeping without noticing it, the tears freezing at his jaw, and he laughed at himself even as he cranked, because he had imagined this moment so often as defeat and here it was, in truth, the finest work of his life. A diversion, the strategists would call it, dismissively. But what was a clock's whole purpose if not a diversion? What was any bell, anywhere, ever, but a machine for making strangers care about a danger that was not yet theirs?

The door to the belfry stair burst open below him.

He did not stop. He saw, between strokes, the lantern light climbing, and he gave the crank everything his arms had left, dragging the bell into a frenzy, filling the tower so completely with iron thunder that whoever climbed those stairs would climb inside the noise, deafened, disoriented —

Fenn emerged onto the gallery with a drawn pistol and four sentries behind him, and the sight of Pell — small, scorched, half-blind old Pell, alone at the crank — stopped them all as though the bell's voice had finally knocked the sense out of the air.

For a moment nobody moved. The bell swung above them all, decaying slowly toward silence, each dying stroke softer than the last, until the chamber settled into that ringing hush that follows great sound, where one's own blood is audible.

"You," Fenn said.

"Me." Pell released the crank. His hands would not uncurl from it; he had to peel them off finger by finger, and the burned ones left skin behind. He turned and faced the lanterns, blinking, arranging his face into the mild confusion he had practiced in the dark — the doddering keeper, the old man overtaken by terror or senility, take your pick, gentlemen. "Is it the east range? Is the archive alight? I smelled smoke — I was certain I smelled smoke—"

"There is no smoke." Fenn's voice was very quiet, which was worse than shouting. The pistol did not waver. "You are supposed to be hiding, Keeper. Or fled, according to the governor's proclamation. Instead you are here, ringing a false alarm, on this particular night, of all nights."

"False?" Pell let his voice crack with theatrical outrage, an old man's performance, and saw it land — saw the sentries glance at each other, uncertain whether they were arresting a traitor or rescuing a lunatic. Either reading bought hours. Both readings bought hours. "Thirty-one years I have kept this bell, sir, and never once has it rung falsely. Ask the clock. The clock remembers everything."

Something flickered across Fenn's face — that terrible quick intelligence, the notebook-mind working — and Pell understood with a lurch that he had said one word too many. *Ask the clock.* Fenn's eyes went upward, involuntarily, toward the great case above the belfry, toward the works, toward the weights and bellows where nine nights of forbidden metal slept.

But then the sentries moved, and there was no more time for thinking on either side.

They took his arms. He did not resist; resistance would make them search the tower thoroughly, and surrender made them merely escort him out, eager to be rid of the embarrassment. This, too, was in the arithmetic. He went down the spiral stairs between them, his bad hip catching on every third step, and he used the pain gladly, crying out, stumbling, making the descent slow and public and noisy, so that every man in the party looked at the old man falling and none of them looked up.

At the landing where the under-works passage branched off into darkness, Pell allowed himself one glance — a single sideways flick, the kind of glance a keeper gives a clock face, casual, habitual — toward the black mouth of the passage where, forty feet in, behind a false wall of weight channel, a young man sat with his hand over his own mouth, not breathing.

Zephyrine. *Boy,* Pell thought, with a tenderness that surprised him by its size, *I hid a ledger in a clock because I trusted clocks more than men. Don't you dare prove me wrong.*

Then they were past, and out, and the cold of the yard hit his face like open water.

* * *

They marched him across the causeway road to the guardhouse, through snow that squealed under the sentries' boots, and Pell held his head high because a condemned man's posture is the last property he owns outright. Word ran ahead of them — the alarm had waked half the river district, and faces appeared at shuttered windows as the little procession passed, pale rectangles of curiosity in the dark. Pell looked at each one. *Remember the bell,* he wanted to tell them. *Remember that it rang, and ask why.* But he said nothing. The bell had already said it. Everything now depended on whether Vellamare had ears or merely windows.

At the guardhouse door, Fenn stopped the column and leaned close, and for the first time all night spoke without an audience to perform for.

"The clock," Fenn said softly. "You told me to ask the clock. Why?"

Pell studied the young man's face — the clever, tired, honest-adjacent face of a man who kept meticulous records of other people's crimes in order to avoid keeping any record of his own — and felt the strange pity that the old feel for the ambitious.

"Because," Pell said, "it is the only witness in this city that has never been paid."

Fenn held his gaze for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes a page turned; Pell watched it happen and could not tell which way the page fell.

"Take him in," Fenn said to the sentries, and turned back toward the observatory, toward the tower, toward the dark — and Pell, being shoved through the guardhouse door into the yellow light and the smell of wet wool and ink, understood that he had perhaps bought not hours after all.

Perhaps he had bought only minutes, and a question that would not stop turning in that notebook mind like a gear with no escapement to govern it.

They put him in the cell at the end of the corridor, the one with the high barred window facing the river. Through it, as they locked the door, Pell could see the observatory tower standing black against the stars, and — faintly, impossibly, beautifully — he could see a thread of candlelight moving in the under-works vent, low near the ground, where no lawful light had any business being.

The boy was working. The boy was still working.

Pell lowered himself onto the plank bed, laid his ruined hands palm-up on his knees like a man done with tools at last, and began, silently, to count seconds — because a keeper counts, because counting is prayer, because somewhere above him a hidden train was nine parts finished and needed only the time that he, Ambrose Pell, had just purchased with the whole of his remaining coin.

Down the corridor, boots approached. Keys sounded. The governor, he would wager, wished to thank him personally.

Pell smiled at the barred square of sky, and waited for the door to open.
