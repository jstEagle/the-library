## 24. Interrogation Below

They came for him before the kettle sang.

Two soldiers this time — not the polite clerk with his notebook, but men in grey wool with river-mud on their boots and the particular blankness of faces that have been told not to think. The taller one held out a folded paper bearing the governor's seal, and Zephyrine saw that his own hands were steady as he took it, which surprised him, because inside his chest something was beating like a fly against glass.

*You are requested below. Immediately.*

Below meant down — past the observatory's ground floor, past the guardroom they had established in what had once been Pell's map room, down a stair he had never noticed in eleven years of visiting the tower, into the undercroft where the observatory kept its foundations: great stone piers sunk into the river rock, the roots of the mountain it stood upon. He had heard of such rooms. Every tall building has a cellar it does not advertise.

The clock above him ticked through the floorboards as they walked him down. He counted the beats without meaning to. It was a habit older than his apprenticeship — Halloway had beaten it into him the way other fathers beat manners into sons: *know where the time is, boy, even when you cannot see it.* Four minutes past nine by the feel of it. The second train lay finished in its hiding place among the bellows, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for midnight two days hence. Pell sat in a cell somewhere below him, having spent himself like a struck match to buy those two days.

Zephyrine could not save him. He knew that already, walking down. What he could do was make sure the old man's coin bought exactly what it cost.

The stair turned twice and opened into a low chamber lit by three lamps and a brazier. The brazier was unnecessary — the undercroft held the earth's own warmth, a deep mineral heat that rose from the rock — but Renwick liked fire near him; Zephyrine had noticed that at dinner. A man who keeps fire close is a man who imagines cold things coming for him.

Governor Aldous Renwick sat alone at a table too small for the room, and the smallness was deliberate. Zephyrine understood furniture the way he understood gear trains, and everything in this room had been arranged the way one arranges an examination bench: the single chair facing the table, set back far enough that its occupant must lean forward to be heard; the lamps placed so their light fell full on the chair and left the governor's face in a soft amber half-dark; the brazier positioned so that anyone in the chair would feel its heat on one side of his body and not the other — a small asymmetry, a constant low-grade wrongness, the kind of thing that wears a man down over hours without his ever knowing why.

Zephyrine had spent his life studying mechanisms designed by clever men to do quiet work. It was the first time he had been seated inside one.

"Master Oyelaran." Renwick did not look up from the papers before him. "Sit."

He sat. The chair was hard oak, unupholstered, another detail noted and filed. He let his tool roll rest across his knees — he had brought it without being asked, because a craftsman summoned about a broken clock brings his tools, and innocence is mostly a matter of doing the obvious thing at the obvious moment.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"The alarm bell, sir." His voice came out level. Good. "I heard it ring in the night. I understand Keeper Pell rang it."

"You understand a great deal, it seems." Renwick turned a page. "You understand what he rang it *for?*"

"No, sir. I was asleep. When I woke, the guards were already in the stairwell, and Mr. Fenn told me to stay in my room."

"Fenn tells everyone to stay in their rooms. It's his whole philosophy." Renwick looked up then, and Zephyrine made himself meet the look — not boldly, not defiantly, but with the mild, slightly tired patience of a workingman called away from his bench. Boldness would be interesting. Interesting was dangerous. He wanted to be the least interesting object in this room, duller than the table, duller than the lamp brackets.

Renwick studied him. There was no other word for it. The man studied people the way Zephyrine studied movements — looking for the irregularity, the tooth that didn't mesh, the beat that came early or late. And Zephyrine felt the study happening, felt it move over his face and hands like a caliper, and understood with cold clarity that the only defense was to *be* what he appeared to be: an apprentice, twenty-three years old, tired, frightened in the ordinary way any man is frightened when soldiers come at dawn, and thinking chiefly of his wages and his master's shop and whether he'd be home before the bridge closed.

Fortunately, all of that was true. That was the strange mercy of it. He did not have to invent an innocent man. He only had to keep the guilty one folded very small behind the innocent one, the way a mainspring lies coiled inside its barrel — enormous force, held in check by nothing but good craftsmanship.

"Tell me," Renwick said, "about the clock."

"The master clock, sir?"

"Don't play the fool, it doesn't suit you. Yes, the master clock. You've had it apart how many times now?"

"Twice fully. More times partially." He said it simply, a tradesman reciting a job list. "The escapement needed re-fitting. The striking train had wear on the third wheel. The going barrel was gummy with old grease — someone had used tallow instead of clock oil, years ago, and it had gone acid."

"Tallow." Renwick's mouth curved faintly. "And who would have done that, I wonder. Some previous keeper cutting corners?"

"Pell keeps a clean house, sir. My guess is whoever serviced it before him. The work dates back thirty years or more, by the corrosion."

"Mm." Renwick turned another page. Zephyrine watched the pages and did not try to read them upside down, because trying not to read them would show in his eyes, and eyes were the one part of a mechanism he could not disassemble and clean. "Eleven days it stood stopped. Four-seventeen, day and night, while the whole city went about its business asking the wrong questions of the wrong hour. Then you arrive, and within a week it runs again. You're a gifted young man."

"My master says I'm adequate."

"Your master," Renwick said, "is a coward who wouldn't come upriver when the governor's seal asked him personally. Adequate men don't get summoned to observatories. Adequate men don't lie awake listening to alarm bells." He leaned forward into the lamplight at last, and Zephyrine saw the performance end and the attention begin — the real Renwick, the one beneath the charm, with eyes like a man reading fine print. "So let us speak plainly, since plainly seems to frighten you less than politely. Last night, an old man rang my alarm bell at two in the morning for no reason anyone can determine, and in the confusion of ringing it, contrived to be found hiding in a place he should not have been able to reach, carrying — or having recently carried, we're still establishing which — certain papers. Papers that belonged to me. And this morning I find myself wondering: what did the keeper of this clock hope to accomplish? Who did he expect to hear that bell and come running? No one comes running in winter. The bridge is shut. We are a sealed jar, Master Oyelaran, all of us floating on the river like a cork in a bottle. Whatever message he thought he was sending had nowhere to go."

"Unless," Zephyrine said carefully, "he wasn't sending a message. Old men get confused. He might have thought he heard something."

"What, in the clock?"

"In anything. He lives alone with a machine, sir. Machines talk if you listen long enough."

Something flickered across Renwick's face — amusement, perhaps, or the memory of amusement — and was put away. "You're fond of him."

"He's kind to me. He shows me the star charts. He makes terrible tea." Each true. Each a small brick in the wall. "I'll be sorry if he's punished badly. But I don't see what it has to do with me, sir, and I'd like to get back to the clock, because the fourth wheel wants attention before the frost gets into the case, and if the frost gets into the case—"

"Yes, yes." Renwick waved a hand, and for a moment the gesture was pure exasperated aristocrat, and Zephyrine thought: *there, that's the seam in him, the vanity, the boredom with things that don't glitter.* "The clock. Everyone's obsessed with the clock. Do you know what I think, Oyelaran? I think clocks are the most dishonest machines ever built. They pretend the world is orderly. Tick, tock, every second identical, forever. But the world isn't like that. The world is weather and appetite and men doing desperate things at two in the morning. A clock is a beautiful lie we agree to believe so we can meet our trains." He smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You believe in your machine, though. I can see it. You look at it the way priests look at relics."

"I look at it the way a man looks at his livelihood, sir."

"And what else do you look at?" The question dropped lightly, almost idly, the way a fisherman drops a line. "In this tower. These last weeks. Anything strike you as — irregular?"

Here it was. The whole night balanced on the next four seconds, and Zephyrine let them pass at the speed of a craftsman considering a question rather than a conspirator surviving one. He scratched his jaw. He glanced toward the brazier. He said:

"The damp in the west wall. If it gets into the weight chutes it'll gall the cables. I mentioned it to Mr. Fenn."

"I'm sure you did. Fenn mentions everything to me." Renwick sat back. "Damp. Cables. Tallow. Is there anything in this tower you *haven't* catalogued, apprentice?"

"Only what's above the ceiling, sir. Nobody's been up there since the dome was sealed. Not even Pell — he says the ladder rotted out before his time."

This was true, and it was also a gift, and Zephyrine hated himself a little for how smoothly he gave it: because the space above the ceiling was where he had *not* hidden anything, precisely so that he could offer it. Give the searcher a room and he stops searching walls. Fenn would climb up there tomorrow with his notebook and find dust and pigeons and the architectural honesty of an empty attic, and while he climbed, the bellows in the striking room would go on holding their secret a little longer.

Renwick regarded him for a long moment. Then he gathered his papers, squared their edges with a fingertip — a precise man, a man who squared edges — and said, almost gently:

"There's a ship leaving on the morning tide. Day after tomorrow, weather permitting. You may have seen her loading."

"The Meridian, sir. I've seen her masts from the tower."

"She carries government freight. Bonded freight. It's sensitive material, and sensitive material attracts thieves, and thieves attract rumors, and rumors in a river city are worse than cholera." He stood, and the interview was visibly ending, and Zephyrine rose with it, keeping his body a half-beat behind the governor's the way a well-made follower wheel keeps its distance from the driver. "If you hear any rumors, Master Oyelaran — any whisper, any suggestion that the freight aboard that ship is anything other than what the manifests say it is — you will bring that rumor to me. Not to Fenn. To me. Directly. Do you understand why?"

"Because rumors grow in the dark, sir."

"Because," Renwick said, taking the lamp from the table and lifting it so that its light fell full and sudden on Zephyrine's face, close enough that Zephyrine could smell the hot brass of it, "a man who brings me a rumor proves he hears rumors. And a man who hears no rumors at all, in a tower where an old man just threw away his freedom ringing bells in the night—" the light did not waver, nor did the voice, "—is either a saint or a liar, and I have met very few saints who could repair a striking train."

The silence held for three seconds. Zephyrine counted them off against the ticking that came faintly down through the stone — the master clock overhead, keeping its honest time above the dishonesty of men — and he let his expression do what an honest confused face does: nothing graceful.

"I'll listen, sir," he said. "That's most of the trade anyway. Listening to what a mechanism tells you."

"See that you extend the courtesy to me." Renwick turned toward the stair, then paused, his back half-turned, and spoke without looking around, casually, terribly: "One more thing. Your hands. Show me."

"...Sir?"

"Your hands. Hold them out."

Zephyrine held them out. Palms up, fingers relaxed, the way a surgeon presents instruments. The lamplight found the calluses, the oil stains in the creases, the small silver burn scar on the right thumb from a soldering iron at fifteen. The hands did not shake. He had known they would not shake — he had willed them not to shake, had gone down into the marrow and taken hold of the trembling there and squeezed it still, the way you squeeze a spring into its housing — but knowing did not lessen the cost, and he felt the cost now, a fine hot wire of strain drawn through him from scalp to sole.

Renwick looked at the hands for a long time. Longer than courtesy. Longer than sense. Looking, Zephyrine understood, for the tremor that would tell him everything, because Renwick was a man who believed that the body always confessed eventually, that truth leaked out of people the way oil leaks from a bad seal — slowly, invisibly, until one morning you find the stain.

Let him look, Zephyrine thought. Let him look all night. The stain is on the inside, Governor. I am the finest work my master ever turned out, and I have been finishing myself for weeks.

"They'll do," Renwick said at last. "Go back to your clock, apprentice. Make it run true. Of everything in this tower, it's the only thing whose honesty I trust — and even that," he added, climbing the first stair, his voice dropping into the dark of the undercroft like a stone into a well, "only because it hasn't yet learned to lie."

* * *

They let him walk up alone. The soldiers peeled away at the guardroom door, and Fenn was there, of course, sitting with his notebook and a cup of tea gone cold beside him, and he looked up with his pleasant grey face and said, "All finished? He can be thorough. He means well, in his way."

"Does he," Zephyrine said, noncommittal, moving toward the stairs that led up into the clock's body, wanting only to be inside the movement, inside the ticking, where the world made sense.

"He asked me once if I loved my work." Fenn turned a page, not looking at him. "I said yes. He said that was the most frightening answer I could have given. Because a man who loves his work will do anything to keep doing it." Now he looked up, and smiled his long polite smile. "Do you love yours, Master Oyelaran?"

"Ask my master," Zephyrine said. "He says I'm adequate."

He climbed. The stair wound up through the tower's throat, past the weight chutes and the cable galleries, past the room where Pell's tea things still sat on the shelf, unwashed, waiting for a hand that was currently in a cell two floors below the river's high-water mark. Zephyrine stopped there, in the dark, and pressed his palm flat against the cold stone, and let the shaking come — all of it, the whole night's ration of it, pouring out through his arm into the wall until his teeth ached with the effort of silence.

Above him, the clock ticked. Below him, the river moved under ice. Somewhere between them, in a bellows among the bellows, the second train waited with its cargo of names, two nights from midnight.

And on the desk in the guardroom behind him, he had seen it as he passed — laid square-edged and neat, the way Renwick squared his papers — a new manifest for the Meridian, fresh-inked, with a column of corrections in the governor's own hand.

The tide tables had been changed. The ship would sail a day early.

One night. He had one night, and a clock to teach to speak.
