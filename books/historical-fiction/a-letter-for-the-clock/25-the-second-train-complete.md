## 25. The Second Train Complete

The last pinion went in at half past ten.

Zephyrine held it on the tip of his smallest driver, breath stopped, and lowered it into the heart of the bellows where he had spent eleven nights building a machine that did not officially exist. The brass caught what little light the shuttered lantern allowed and gave it back warm, like a coin held too long in a fist. He seated it against the wheel below, felt the mesh take — that soft kiss of tooth finding gap — and let his lungs go.

It was done. Or as done as anything was ever done, because a clockmaker knew better than to say *finished* aloud; finished invited the universe to find the flaw. He said nothing. He wiped his palms on his apron, which was already more oil than linen, and sat back on his heels in the dark of the under-works and listened.

Above him, through three floors of timber and stone, the great clock ticked. Below him, the river ground its ice against the pilings. Between them, folded into the bellows chamber among the genuine bellows — the ones that fed air to nothing anymore, relics of some keeper's abandoned scheme to blow dust from the dials — hung his second striking train, coiled and patient, carrying forty-one names in its teeth.

He had built it out of the clock's own leavings. A spare escape wheel salvaged from the repair bench Pell had kept for thirty years. Wire drawn down on the little lathe during hours when Fenn was at supper. Weights poured from lead scavenged off the roof flashing, hidden inside hollowed counterweight casings that no one would question because they looked exactly like themselves. Every part of it was honest metal doing dishonest work. Every part of it was Vellamare in miniature.

*One night,* he thought. *The ship sails tomorrow's tide now.*

His hands, which had not shaken all evening, began to shake now that there was nothing left for them to do. That was the way of it. Fear always waited until the work was over to present its bill.

He climbed down through the tower's throat, past the going train's slow respiration, past the weights descending in their shafts like men being lowered into a mine, and came out into the clock room where the master case stood taller than two men and older than the city charter. The pendulum swung its grave arc behind the glass. 11:41. The hands said so, and for once Zephyrine believed them, because he had spent the whole winter learning every lie this mechanism could tell and correcting each one.

There was one more thing to do tonight, and it could only be done at midnight.

* * *

He had prepared the test the way Pell had taught him to prepare everything: so thoroughly that the doing became almost an afterthought. Rags — he had stolen them honestly enough, torn from his own shirt hem over weeks so no single theft would be noticed — were already packed around the strike bells in the belfry above, wound tight and tied with waxed cord. A muffled test. The chimes would speak, but only to him, only in felt vibration rather than sound, the way a deaf man reads speech in another's throat. If Fenn stood outside in the cold listening, as Zephyrine was nearly certain he did on nights when sleep failed him, he would hear nothing but wind and the ordinary quarter-hour.

Zephyrine carried the lantern up the winding stair, and the stair carried him up through the smell of cold iron and pigeon lime, and at the top the belfry opened around him like the inside of a bell itself — curved shadow, curved silence. The great hour bell hung overhead, big as a drowned thing. The smaller chime bells, eight of them, ranked along the north wall, each wrapped now in his rag-and-cord muffling, looking absurdly like bandaged patients.

He checked each knot. Then he checked them again, because the whole plan lived or died on knots.

At five minutes to midnight he took up his position on the frame between the bells, laid his bare palm flat against the crown of the lowest chime bell, and waited. His breath made small ghosts in the lantern light. Through the floor he could feel the clock thinking — the escapement letting time through in equal payments, tick, tick, tick — and beneath that, deeper, the second train he had grafted into the bellows chamber three floors down, its own weight hanging ready on its own line, waiting for the connection he had rigged to the midnight warning lever.

If his arithmetic was right — and he had checked it fourteen times, and then twice more after waking from dreams of having miscounted — then at midnight, instead of the plain twelve strokes, the muffled chimes would ring out the ledger's opening sequence. The cipher was musical by design; Pell had built it that way years ago, a code keyed to intervals, note-pairs standing for letters, rests for word-breaks. Forty-one names. Dates. Sums. The first verse of a long indictment, broadcast to anyone who knew how to listen.

Nobody in Vellamare knew yet. But Ilse would learn. Ilse, who had grown up in a house where her mother played psalms by ear and corrected her daughter's singing with a ruler's patience — Ilse would hear twelve wrong notes and understand that they were not wrong at all.

And after her, others. There were others who'd known Pell's old system — he'd taught it to choirboys once, decades back, a keeper's idle kindness. Some of those boys were dockworkers now. Some were clerks. Some were dead, Zephyrine supposed, or sold downriver, their names sitting in the very sequence the bells would spell.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The minute hand crawled toward the top of the dial three floors below him, and he could do nothing now but feel.

Midnight arrived the way tides arrive — not as an event but as a change in what was already happening.

The warning wheel lifted. Somewhere far beneath him he heard — felt — the click of the connection engaging, his borrowed linkage taking up its slack, and then the second train ran free for the first time, its weights paying out down their concealed lines, its wheels turning wheels turning wheels, and the first muffled stroke came up through the bell crown into his palm like a heartbeat arriving from underground.

*Thum.*

A low interval. *Re*, if you were generous about pitch, though the rag-wrapped bell had no true pitch left, only intention. First letter of the first name: **M**. Marta Okonjo, washerwoman, bond sold 4 March, sum eleven pounds.

*Thum-thum.* Two strokes, close together. **A**.

*Thum.* Rest. Word complete. *Ma—*

Zephyrine kept his hand on the bell and counted, and wept without deciding to, tears running warm down into his collar in the freezing belfry, because it was working. It was working. Eleven nights of lying to a spy, of filing brass by candle stub, of eating standing up with files still in his pockets — and the machine spoke. Not beautifully; the muffled tones were shapeless, a stuttering pulse rather than music. But the intervals were true. The grammar was intact. The clock was saying *Marta* to the night, and the night was holding still to listen.

Stroke by stroke the sequence unwound. He followed it with his lips moving, silent, the way he'd seen Ilse read difficult documents — *Marta Okonjo. Tobias Wren. Adaeze Nwosu. Four March. Seven March. Eleven pounds. Nine pounds. Fourteen* — and each name landed in his palm as vibration and rose up his arm and settled somewhere behind his sternum, where they would stay, he suspected, for the rest of his life.

Then the last rest, and the train ran down into silence, and the belfry was just a cold room full of bandaged bells again.

Zephyrine stood in the ringing absence for a long moment. His hand was numb where it lay against the metal. He noticed distantly that he was smiling, that his face ached with it, that this was triumph, this exact feeling — and that even as he felt it, something under the triumph was already tallying costs, the way his master used to tally stock at year's end, unsmiling, by lamplight.

Because the sequence had taken four minutes and eleven seconds. At real volume, unmuffled, at midnight, every soul in Vellamare within a mile of the river would hear it. And Renwick slept six hundred paces from the observatory door.

* * *

He descended slowly, letting his legs remember the stairs, and paused at the clock room to verify the main mechanism had suffered nothing from its strange child's first utterance. The going train ticked on, indifferent. The hands stood at 12:09. He watched the pendulum make nine full arcs before he trusted it.

In the guardroom corridor he blew out his lantern and moved by memory, hugging the wall where the boards were quietest.

Fenn's door stood open.

Not wide. A hand's width of darkness, no lamp burning inside. But Mr. Fenn was a man who closed doors the way other men signed documents — completely, deliberately, every time. Zephyrine had logged the habit across a winter of logging everything else. An open door from Fenn meant either carelessness or invitation, and Fenn had never once in three months been careless.

Zephyrine stood in the dark and considered the geometry of the night. The test was done. Nothing remained upstairs but rags and silence. If Fenn waited beyond that door with his notebook and his gentle terrible patience, then whatever happened next happened now, and Zephyrine would have to improvise — and improvisation, he had learned, was simply honesty wearing the wrong clothes.

But the dark behind the door held no breathing he could hear. And after a hundred heartbeats he heard, faintly, from the direction of the water stair, the scrape of a boot on stone, receding. Fenn, out walking. Fenn, checking the ice. Fenn, perhaps, keeping his own midnight appointments.

Zephyrine passed the doorway without looking at it, which cost more discipline than the entire winter of secret machining, and gained the shelter of his own narrow room. He barred the door — a formality, they'd removed the bar's brackets weeks ago "for safety" — and sat down on the cot and put his face in his hands.

Triumph, and then the bill.

He itemized it, because itemizing was the only prayer he knew. One: the test had proven the train, but proving was not performing. Tomorrow — tonight, now — he would have to connect the final linkage in daylight hours, under Fenn's eye, in movements small enough to pass for maintenance. Two: the tide tables had changed. The Meridian would load at dawn and sail on the morning tide, which meant the bonds would leave Vellamare hours after his chimes spoke — unless the chimes worked, unless people listened, unless someone acted. Three:

Three was Ilse.

She had found things. He knew she had found things, because she had stopped asking him questions gently and started asking them precisely, and precision from Ilse Verany was the sound of a trap being built around someone she loved. She had seen cipher fragments — he didn't know how, didn't know what — and she had spent three days watching him the way he watched mechanisms, looking for the point of engagement, the place where pressure applied would produce motion.

And he had promised her. Three years ago, in her brother's parlor smelling of camphor and rain, with her hand still warm from saving Halloway & Sons from ruin: *whatever you learn of my family's debts, keep it behind your teeth, and I will never ask you to break it.* A promise made in peacetime, cheap as promises are in peacetime, and it had compounded interest ever since until it weighed more than any weight in any shaft.

Tomorrow night the bells would say what he had never dared say to her directly.

Or — no. Not tomorrow night. Tonight. The tide had moved; everything had moved. Midnight was twenty-two hours away, and between here and midnight stood a governor with fresh ink on his manifests, a clerk with an open door, and a woman in the east guest room who deserved the truth from his mouth before she heard it from a bell.

*Give her the truth first,* he had told himself weeks ago, in the first flush of the plan, *let her choose. That is how you keep a promise — by breaking it open in front of the person it binds.*

It had sounded noble then. Sitting on the cot with his hands shaking again, it sounded like what it was: the hardest work remaining, harder than any gear.

He rose. He took off his apron, folded it over the rail — oil side in, so the smell wouldn't travel — and stood a moment in the dark listening to the house of clocks breathe around him.

Then he opened his door and went to find Magistrate Ilse Verany, stripped of office, declared enemy of the state, awake — he was sure she was awake; she was always awake at the hour when truth got told — and offered her, three floors above a sleeping conspirator's court, the choice he had carried for her since peacetime.

Behind him, in the belfry, eight bells hung in their bandages, holding forty-one names against morning.

Below him, the river moved its ice, counting down the tide.
