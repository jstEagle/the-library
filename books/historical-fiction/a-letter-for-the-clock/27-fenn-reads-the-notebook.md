## 27. Fenn Reads the Notebook

The rain had come in off the river around dusk and settled in for the night, a fine gray insistence against the observatory windows that made the whole tower sound like it was breathing. Mr. Aldous Fenn sat in the small room assigned to him — a former chart room, by the smell of dried ink and old salt in the plaster — with his notebook open before him and a single lamp turned low enough to be thrifty, high enough to read by.

He had been at Vellamare for nineteen days now. He knew this because his notebook told him so. The notebook told him everything. It was the one honest companion he had ever kept: it never flattered him, never forgot, never forgave him anything he wrote down.

*Day 1.* Arrival. Assigned quarters on the third level of the observatory. Subject: Zephyrine Oyelaran, apprentice clockmaker, age approximately twenty-four, employed by Halloway & Sons. Note: hands steady. Note: eyes move too much for someone with nothing to hide.

He had written that last line on the first evening, mostly to have something to write. Governors did not send clerks across half a province to record nothing, and Fenn had long ago learned that a report began as an atmosphere before it ever became a fact. You described the weather of a man until the man emerged from it.

But nineteen days was a long time to describe weather, and lately the notebook had begun to say things he had not intended it to say.

He turned back through the pages. His handwriting was small and even, the hand of a man who had been educated above his station and resented neither the education nor the station, only the distance between them. He had been a copying clerk in the governor's registry for eleven years. He could transcribe a shipping manifest faster than any man in the building. He could detect a falsified column of figures at forty paces, which was how he had first come to Governor Renwick's attention, though Renwick had never said so aloud. Power in Vellamare did not announce its observations. It simply arranged them.

Fenn poured himself two fingers of the cheap brandy he permitted himself — one finger on ordinary nights, two when the numbers were wrong — and began the work he had been avoiding for three days.

The work was simple in principle. He had pages of observations: times, sounds, movements. What he needed was a pattern, and patterns required him to stop pretending he wasn't looking for one.

*Day 4.* Oyelaran requests additional lamp oil. States purpose: study of escapement theory. Oil consumed suggests 5+ hours beyond stated repair schedule.

*Day 7.* Heard filing, very fine, from the clock chamber between midnight and three. Next morning, Oyelaran's right thumbnail split. Filed brass under nails is visible if one looks. (I looked.)

*Day 9.* Oyelaran descends to under-works twice in one night. Carries nothing down. Carries something up. Shape consistent with bellows section, approx. length of a forearm.

*Day 11.* Requests steel stock — "for pallet replacement." Quantity requested exceeds pallet replacement by a factor I would estimate at four.

*Day 13.* Muffled striking heard at 2 a.m., wrapped in what sounded like sacking or wool. Eleven blows. Then a pause. Then three more, spaced unevenly. Not a test of an existing train. A test of a new one.

Fenn set down the pen and rubbed his eyes until colors bloomed.

There it was, laid out in his own neat hand like a confession he hadn't known he was writing. The night work. The excess steel. The bellows-shaped bundle carried up empty-handed and returned full. The muffled chimes that struck eleven and then three more, unevenly, as though learning their steps.

He thought of the ledger cylinder. Everyone at the observatory had heard the story by now, worn smooth with repetition: the master clock stopped eleven days by a slipped part; the apprentice found the fault; the fault was removed and locked in the governor's strongbox. And then, nine days ago, the story had grown a second chapter — the strongbox opened, the cylinder gone, the governor's face when they told him, which Fenn had seen only briefly but would remember the rest of his life, if his life contained many things worth remembering. Renwick had looked like a man who had reached into his own coat pocket and found another man's hand there.

A striking train. Fenn turned the phrase over. Every clock had one — the mechanism that lifted the hammer to the bell. The great master clock had its own, vast and venerable, and Oyelaran had been repairing it honestly by day; Fenn had watched him do it, had logged every honest hour, because honesty in daylight was exactly the kind of thing that made a man trustworthy after dark.

And at night, the apprentice was building a second one.

For what? A clock already struck. Unless the second train was not meant to strike hours.

Fenn was not a clever man in the way clever men in novels were clever; he was a thorough man, which was rarer and less comfortable. Thoroughness meant following a thought past the point where a sensible man would have stopped. So he followed it. The chimes rang out over Vellamare at every hour; the city set its life by them, boats and bakers and bells answering across the water. If the striking train were altered — if the pattern of blows were changed —

He stopped. He actually put both palms flat on the desk, as though steadying himself against a swell.

Eleven blows. Then three. The ledger, Keeper Pell had said, when Pell still gave tours of his own guilt — the ledger recorded names and dates and sums. Eleven names on the first page? Three entries for the eleventh of some month? Fenn didn't know the cipher. He didn't need to know it. That was the terrible elegance of the thing: he didn't need to break the code to know a code existed. A clockmaker working nights to build a hidden striking train, in a tower whose chimes reached every ear in Vellamare, while a coded ledger of the governor's crimes lay missing — these facts did not require interpretation. They required only arithmetic, and arithmetic had no loyalty.

Fenn sat very still and listened to the rain.

Here was the difficulty, and he had known it would arrive eventually, the way winter arrives: you can see the ice forming under the ferry landing weeks before anyone says the word. He was the informer. That was his office, his salary, his reason for sleeping under this roof among people who believed he was a minor functionary sent to inventory lamp oil. His whole value to Renwick lay in what he noticed and reported. And he had noticed. The evidence assembled itself more neatly than any manifest he had ever copied.

So why was his pen not moving?

He picked it up. Dipped it. Held it over the page, over the waiting blank space where *Day 19* should begin, where the conclusion should go, tidy and damning: *Oyelaran constructing unauthorized striking train. Recommend immediate search of clock case, weights, and bellows. Recommend detention of subject.*

The ink gathered at the nib and fell, one round black drop, onto the paper — in the wrong place, between lines, a blot like a period at the end of nothing.

Fenn looked at it for a long time.

He thought about the morning he had found the keeper's quarters burned. He had stood in the wet ash smell of that corridor with his notebook open, recording scorch patterns, and he had felt — he had not written this, he had refused to write this — he had felt the notebook become heavier than paper. Because the official account said Pell had fled with stolen funds, and Fenn's own observations said Pell was seventy years old, half-lame, and had owned nothing worth stealing except books, which were still on their shelves. The official account was a fiction, and Fenn had transcribed it anyway, in his small even hand, because that was the job. Transcribe. Report. Do not be the thing reported upon.

He had spent eleven years being the man who watched. He had told himself it was clean work, watching — that the knife stayed in other men's hands and he merely measured the cuts. But standing here, with the truth about Oyelaran sitting complete and undeniable on his desk, Fenn understood suddenly that there was no measuring without cutting, that his notebook was itself a blade, and that for nineteen days he had been sharpening it against an innocent man — or against a guilty one, and that was precisely the question, and the question was not his to answer, and yet he was the only one positioned to answer it, and either way someone would bleed.

If he reported, and Oyelaran was innocent of treason and guilty only of some private grief-work in brass, then Fenn delivered him to a governor who had already shown what he did with inconvenient keepers. If he withheld the report, and Oyelaran's midnight train was aimed at something worse than chimes — at sabotage, at the weights, at the governor himself — then whatever fell would fall on Vellamare, and some portion of it would fall on the clerk who saw it coming and said nothing.

And beneath both calculations, quieter than either, a third thing stirred that he did not want to name. He named it anyway, alone, into the rain-noise, because he was a thorough man even with himself.

It was the way Oyelaran worked. Fenn had watched craftsmen all his life from the outside — the registry had been full of men grinding through documents like oxen at a mill — and he had never once seen anyone love the labor. But the apprentice loved it. Even exhausted, even hunted, even with his thumbnail split and the fear walking behind him like a dog, Oyelaran touched the gears the way a priest touches a book. Whatever the boy was building, he was building it as an act of devotion. Devotion to what, Fenn could not say. Justice, possibly. Or memory. Or the dead.

You cannot inform on devotion, Fenn thought. You can only betray it, and be changed by the betrayal, and carry the change.

He blew out the lamp, which he had never done while working, and sat in the dark and let the rain speak for a while.

When he relit it, he did the only thing his nature allowed: he finished the entry. Not the recommendation. Just the facts, stripped of conclusion, accurate to the grain:

*Day 19.* Night work continues. Second striking train assessed near completion. Purpose unknown. No report issued pending further observation.

Then, below it, in handwriting slightly less even than the rest — the only uneven line in nineteen days of pages — he added:

*Note to self: find out what the chimes are for.*

* * *

He went up the tower stair before dawn, on the pretext of checking the oil stores in the clock chamber, and found the door unlatched, which it never was.

Inside, the great clock filled the dark with its patient breathing — the slow swing of the pendulum, the tick that was really a thousand ticks braided together. Oyelaran stood at the open case with his sleeves rolled, a lamp hooked above him, a gear the size of a dinner plate cradled in both hands like a sleeping animal. He looked up, and for a moment neither man moved, and Fenn understood that they were both doing the same arithmetic: what does the other one know, and what will he do with it.

"Couldn't sleep," Fenn said. "Thought I'd check the oil levels."

"They're fine," Oyelaran said. "I topped them myself."

"Of course you did." Fenn looked at the gear. "Escapement wheel?"

"Pallet assembly. For the hour train."

"The hour train," Fenn repeated mildly. "Working nights again?"

"When the clock permits." The boy's voice was perfectly level. His eyes, which moved too much, moved now to the notebook in Fenn's hand and then away, deliberately, the way a man looks away from a thing he refuses to fear.

They stood in the tick and tock, two liars keeping each other company, and Fenn felt the strange vertigo of realizing he liked the boy — liked his competence, his stubbornness, the split thumbnail and the steady hands — and that liking him made the notebook heavier still.

"Mind the cold," Fenn said finally. "The river ice is thickening. Another week and even the Meridian won't move."

Something crossed Oyelaran's face then — quick, involuntary, a fish turning under still water — and Fenn caught it, and logged it, silently, in the place where his notebook ended and his conscience began.

* * *

Governor Renwick received him at nine, in the long room at the foot of the tower where the maps of the province hung like trophies. The fire was lit too early in the season and too hot for comfort; Renwick liked rooms warm the way he liked everything, past the point of sense. He was pouring wine when Fenn entered, and did not offer any.

"Well?" Renwick said. "Nineteen days, Mr. Fenn. My patience is a mechanism too, and it has a mainspring."

Fenn had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike him. He had walked down from the tower with his notebook under his arm and discovered that the words he intended to say and the words he believed had parted company somewhere on the stairs.

"The repairs are genuine," he said. "The clock will keep true time within the week."

"I didn't ask about the clock."

"No," Fenn said. "You asked about the clockmaker."

Renwick set down the decanter with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a small lock turning. "You have a manner this morning, Mr. Fenn. I don't care for it. Out with it."

And here was the moment. Nineteen days of observation sat in Fenn's mouth, fully formed, ready to save him: *the night work, the stolen steel, the second train, the muffled chimes, the boy is building your ruin inside your own clock.* Say it, and he was safe — promoted, perhaps, certainly trusted, certainly complicit forever in whatever Renwick did next with the information, which Fenn's own observations suggested would involve rope, or the river, or both.

He heard himself say instead: "There are irregularities in the workshop accounts. Steel ordered beyond requirement. Lamp oil drawn at night. Nothing conclusive. I wanted to examine the requisition ledgers against the guild price lists before troubling you."

A silence. Renwick studied him the way he studied everything — like a mechanism whose loyalty might be slipping a tooth.

"Nothing conclusive," the governor repeated.

"Not yet."

"Mr. Fenn." Renwick came around the desk slowly, wineglass in hand, and his voice dropped into the register he used for confidences that were also threats. "Do you know why I chose you? Eleven years in my registry, and I chose you because you once flagged a falsified grain manifest that four inspectors had signed off on. You see what others sign past. It is your entire value. So when you stand in my room and tell me you've found irregularities and nothing conclusive, I am obliged to wonder whether the clerk has gone soft, or the clerk has gone bought, or the clerk has gone clever — and of the three, Mr. Fenn, clever is the one I shoot."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, faint through stone, the master clock ticked on, keeping its honest time over the head of the dishonest city.

"The requisitions will be examined," Fenn said. "Thoroughly. You'll have my findings."

"See that I have them soon." Renwick drank, watching him over the rim. "And Mr. Fenn — the Meridian sails on the morning tide, storm or no storm, day after tomorrow. After that, none of this matters. Make sure your findings arrive before the tide does."

Fenn bowed, and left, and stood a moment in the corridor with the cold of the stone seeping through his boots, feeling the shape of what he had just done — not reporting, not protecting, merely delaying, buying hours for a boy he had no business believing in, spending the coin of his own safety at a rate he could not sustain.

He went back up the tower stairs, took out his notebook, and wrote, beneath the note to self:

*Findings due before the morning tide. Two days.*

And then, after a long look at the rain-gray light in the window, he added the line he had been circling for nineteen days, the one that made the notebook tremble in his hand like a living thing:

*I know what the chimes are for. God forgive me — I think I've known since Day 13. The question is no longer what Oyelaran is building. The question is whether I am the kind of man who informs on justice, or the kind who watches it hang.*

Below the tower, at the frozen docks, the guards on the Meridian doubled their watch, and far upriver the ice spoke to itself in the dark, groaning like a door deciding whether to open.
