## 30. The Tower Siege

The door was oak, two inches thick, and it would not hold past dawn.

Zephyrine knew this the way he knew the weight of a mainspring — not by argument but by long acquaintance. He had carried that door's twin up the tower stair as a boy of fourteen, when Halloway & Sons won the contract for the observatory refit and old Halloway himself had stood at the bottom shouting measurements while the apprentices sweated. Oak swelled with damp and shrank with cold, but against an axe swung by a man who did not care whether the wood survived, two inches was a conversation, not an answer.

He wedged the last of Pell's furniture against it anyway — a bookshelf, a writing desk, the iron stove they'd wrestled from the keeper's quarters — because a conversation could be prolonged, and every minute was a gear-tooth of the plan.

Above him, through three floors of stone and timber, the master clock ticked on. He could feel it in his boots more than hear it: that patient, enormous pulse, eleven hundred pounds of driven brass counting toward midnight. Counting toward the chimes.

*Not yet*, he told it silently, the way one speaks to a movement mid-assembly. *Hold your beat. We have work to do.*

Ilse Verany came down the stair with her arms full of candles, her hair coming loose from its knot, and in the guttering light she looked — for the first time since he'd known her — like a woman running out of authority to borrow. The magistrate's coat she'd claimed was too big in the shoulders. She wore borrowed power the way she wore everything: straight-backed, defiantly, daring anyone to notice the fit.

"They've stopped hammering," she said.

"They haven't stopped." Zephyrine tilted his head. Beneath the silence there was the scrape of something heavy being dragged across the flagstones of the gatehouse below — a ram, or a beam off some cart. "They're changing tools."

Pell sat on the third step from the bottom, where Zephyrine had deposited him after cutting the old man free of the storeroom lockup. His wrists were ringed red where the irons had bitten; his left eye was swollen nearly shut from Fenn's enthusiasm, and his breathing had the wet, ragged hitch of cracked ribs. But his hands were steady in his lap, folded like a man at prayer, and when he spoke his voice was dry and even.

"Renwick will not storm us all at once," Pell said. "That's not how he does anything. He'll knock politely for hours first. He'll send someone to negotiate. He'll wait for us to tire, to doubt each other, to make mistakes." A thin smile moved under the bruising. "I watched him do it to the harbor board for six years. Patience is the whole of his armory."

"Then we spend the night being patient back," Ilse said. She set the candles down and began sorting them by length with quick, economical movements — a magistrate triaging evidence, Zephyrine thought, even now.

"No." Zephyrine heard his own voice come out flat and certain, and was briefly surprised by it. "We don't have patience. We have until midnight, and then we have however long the chimes take to say what they have to say, and then—" He stopped, because *and then* was a country none of them had mapped. "Everything before midnight is just keeping the door closed. Everything after is out of our hands."

* * *

They worked.

There was nothing else to do with fear, Zephyrine had learned long ago at Halloway's bench — nothing else that helped. Fear was energy, and energy unspent went sour in the blood. His master used to say a frightened apprentice should be given a file and told to make ten thousand strokes exactly even, and by stroke six thousand he'd forget what frightened him, and by ten thousand his hands would know something his head didn't yet believe: that the world could be met one precise motion at a time.

So they fortified.

They carried up every barrel of lamp oil in the stores and rolled them behind the barricade — not to burn, Zephyrine insisted over Ilse's glance, but to fill space, dead weight that couldn't be shifted quietly. They hauled water in the great rain cistern buckets, enough for a day, enough for burns if it came to fire. Pell, moving slowly, wincing, directed them to a trapdoor above the bell chamber he'd half forgotten existed, a maintenance crawl from his predecessor's era, and they cleared its rusted bolt and confirmed it opened onto the roof lead — a way out that was also no way out, four stories onto frozen slate, but Ilse made them confirm it twice anyway.

Zephyrine, meanwhile, did what he was made for.

He climbed into the clock's works with a lantern clenched in his teeth and went through the second striking train joint by joint, wheel by wheel, the way a surgeon goes over sutures. This was the machine nobody living had seen but him — forty days of night work hidden inside bellows and counterweights, a counterfeit of the clock's own voice, built to speak a language of strokes. Nine strikes, pause. Three strikes, pause. Seven. The opening sequence of the ledger, translated into the only broadcast Vellamare had ever possessed: the master clock's voice over the rooftops.

His fingers found what they were looking for on the fourth wheel — a burr of solder where he'd been rushed, weeks ago, working by feel in the dark with Fenn's footsteps creaking overhead. Small. Almost nothing. The kind of flaw that would let itself be forgiven nine hundred times out of a thousand.

But the chimes would strike perhaps four hundred times tonight, and a clock does not forgive; it repeats. One skipped tooth at the wrong hour and the sequence stammered, and a stammered code was worse than silence, because it would sound like madness instead of testimony.

He reflowed the solder with a spirit lamp cupped in his palm, holding his breath until the joint cooled, and lay back in the tangle of brass and let out a long slow breath at the dark underside of the bell chamber.

*Hands remember their training,* old Halloway used to say. *Trust them farther than you trust yourself.*

Below, faint through the floor, the dragging scrape stopped. Then came the first polite knock — three measured blows of something heavy, spaced like a caller at a door.

* * *

"He wants to talk," Ilse said, when the knocking paused and a voice floated up the tower shaft, smooth as oiled leather. It was Fenn's voice, pitched carefully to carry without shouting. "Keeper Pell! Magistrate Verany! The governor extends his compliments and requests a parley. No harm need come to anyone. He has questions only."

"Questions," Pell repeated softly. From his seat on the stairs he had barely moved in an hour, conserving himself like a spring wound down to its last turns. "In my experience, Mr. Fenn's questions arrive before the answers are decided."

"You should answer him," Zephyrine said. "Silence tells him we're weak. Let him hear voices — let him count three."

Ilse looked at him a moment. Something passed between them that had taken thirty chapters of their lives to build — the promise, the broken open promise, the alliance built in its place — and she nodded once and went to the arrow-slit window that overlooked the causeway side.

"This is Magistrate Verany," she called, and her voice rang off the stone with the old courtroom authority, undiminished by the oversized coat. "State your business."

"Magistrate!" Fenn sounded genuinely delighted. "What a pleasure. The governor wonders — respectfully — what you imagine you're accomplishing. The tower holds no treasure. The keeper is a fugitive thief. The apprentice is a boy in over his head. And you, madam, are an enemy of the state harboring both. Open the door, surrender the ledger if you have it, and I am authorized to offer you passage on the morning ship. Any port you like."

Zephyrine's blood went cold at *the morning ship*. So Renwick knew about the tide table too. Of course he did. The Meridian was his ship, his bonds, his escape route if the night went badly — and every hour the tower held out was an hour closer to the ice closing the river and sealing all of them, evidence and conspirators together, into one frozen city.

"The governor," Ilse called back pleasantly, "may present his questions to me in the market square, under oath, before the guildmasters. Tell him the docket opens at dawn."

A pause. When Fenn spoke again, the silk was thinner. "You misunderstand your position."

"I held court in this city for nine years, Mr. Fenn. I understand everyone's position better than they do. Good evening."

She stepped back from the slit. In the candlelight her hands, Zephyrine noticed, were trembling very slightly — the tremor of a drawn bow, held at full draw.

"That was well done," Pell murmured.

"It was theater."

"All courage is. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

The real assault began at half past eleven.

No parley preceded it — only the sudden deep boom of the ram taking the door in earnest, a sound like a giant knuckle rapping the ribs of the building, felt in the floorboards and in the teeth. Dust sifted from the joists. Somewhere below, men shouted in rhythm.

Zephyrine was already climbing.

He had planned this hour for forty nights, and now that it arrived it was smaller and colder than his plans had made it — just a boy again in the dark guts of a machine, lantern hooked to a nail, sleeves pinned back, the second train's release lever under his right hand and the escapement's brake under his left. Below him the door boomed again, and a splinter-crack ran through the sound of it, and Ilse's voice came up the stair shaft, steady as a metronome:

"Ten minutes past eleven. Door holds."

He checked the connection between trains one final time. The linkage was beautiful — he allowed himself one honest craftsman's second to admire it — a clutch of pawl and detent that would let the counterfeit train take control of the great hammer at the first stroke of twelve, speak its coded sequence across perhaps twenty minutes, and then disengage, leaving the clock innocent, keeping ordinary time, while the city below woke to names and dates and sums spelled out in bronze.

One chance. If the chimes began and Renwick's men reached the bell chamber before the sequence finished, they'd cut the hammer line and the message would die half-spoken — worse than never begun, a rumor instead of a record.

Which meant the door needed to hold twenty minutes past midnight. Which meant—

"Ilse." He leaned out over the rail. "When it starts, they'll stop battering. They'll panic — try the stairs. You and Pell can't hold the stair."

"We don't have to hold it," she said, and he saw it land in her face too, the arithmetic they'd agreed on and never said aloud. "We have to make them believe the cost of the stair is higher than the reward. There's oil at the turn. And there's you, with a clock full of iron weights and a trapdoor."

"And there's the truth," Pell added from below, "which travels faster than soldiers once it starts."

Another boom. Another crack, longer this time. The desk behind the door groaned and shifted an inch, its legs shrieking on stone.

Zephyrine looked up into the dark where the bells hung, unseen, patient, ready to be made to speak. He thought of his master's bench, of ten thousand even strokes of a file, of the lesson underneath the lesson: *you cannot control the outcome, only the quality of the work.* He thought of the debtor quarter's shuttered windows, of the families whose names he'd spent forty nights learning to spell in strokes of a hammer.

His hands, he found, had stopped shaking somewhere around the tenth boom. They hung at his sides warm and ready and absolutely steady, waiting for midnight the way a pendulum waits for gravity.

"Ilse," he said. "How long?"

She had no watch — hers had gone with her office — but Pell had given her his, and she held it up to the candle.

"Eleven fifty-two," she said. "Eight minutes."

Below, the ram drew back for another blow, and Zephyrine put his hand on the escapement brake and waited for the twelfth hour with the steadiest heart he had ever owned and the fastest-beating pulse of his life — listening, beneath the thunder of the door, for the great clock's first stroke of midnight to rise through the tower like water rising in a lock.

The door cracked. The clock gathered itself.

Midnight came.
