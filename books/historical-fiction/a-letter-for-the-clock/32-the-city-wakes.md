## 32. The City Wakes

In the tower, Zephyrine Oyelaran had done all that hands could do.

The second train hung inside the great case like a secret organ grown behind the visible one — pinions and levers he had filed by night, hidden in bellows and hollowed weights for nine days, connected now by three brass links that trembled under his fingers as the minute hand crawled its last distance to twelve. Below him, through two floors of oak and stone, axes struck the barricaded door in a rhythm he refused to let his heart keep. Thud. Thud. The wood splintered somewhere deep. Ilse stood at the landing with a boat-hook in her hands like a halberd, and Pell sat against the wall cradling his bandaged ribs, and none of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the clock would not say better.

Zephyrine's lamp burned low. He did not need it. His fingers knew every tooth of every wheel he had cut, the way a blind man knows his own teeth. At four minutes to midnight he wiped his palms on his apron, though they were not wet — they were dry as paper, dry as the ledger pages locked against his chest — and he watched the escapement tick, and thought: *Catch. Please catch.*

The eleventh stroke of ten had come hours ago, ordinary, honest. Now the twelfth hour approached carrying something no clock in Vellamare had ever carried.

One minute. Thirty seconds. The door below boomed again, closer, angrier.

*Catch,* he thought, and let go of everything but that.

* * *

Across the river, in the debtor quarter where half the shuttered windows had not opened in a year, Maren Kostel woke without knowing why.

She was a washerwoman, fifty-one years old, with hands gone soft as wet cloth from thirty years of other people's water. Her husband Tomas had been taken downriver fourteen months ago on a Tuesday, sold against a debt of eleven crowns that the court clerk swore had grown to forty, and she had stopped sleeping properly sometime around the following spring. So she was awake at midnight anyway, sitting up in her cold bed, when the observatory clock began to strike.

Everyone in Vellamare knew the master clock's voice. It struck the hours with twelve slow bronze notes, and the quarters with pairs, and on feast days it played a little descending figure the children called *the stairs*. Maren had heard it ten thousand times. She knew it the way she knew rain.

This was not that.

The first note fell alone. Then a pause — long, deliberate, a silence shaped like a held breath. Then two notes close together. Pause. Three. Pause. One. It was counting, she thought dully. Like a ledger being read aloud. Like someone reading names off a list in a language made only of numbers, and the numbers went on, and on, past anything an hour should hold, and the pattern repeated — she was sure it repeated — the same phrase twice, then changed, then again, patient as water dripping into a bucket.

Maren got up. She went to the window and pushed open shutters that hadn't moved since autumn, and the cold poured over her like riverwater, and across the black city she saw what made her knees go strange: lights. Candles kindling window by window down the hill, along the quay, up in the merchant streets. Doors opening. Figures in nightclothes standing in doorways with their heads tipped back toward the tower, listening.

Old Sefa from the ground floor was already out in the lane in her shawl, one hand pressed flat against her breastbone. "It's spelling something," she said when Maren joined her. "My father was a signalman on the river boats. Long-short, long-short — it's a code. It's *counting*, child."

"Counting what?" Maren asked, though some animal part of her already knew, the way you know weather before it breaks.

And then the phrase came round again — the same sequence, unmistakable now — and Sefa said, in a small voice, "That's a number. That's a sum. Eleven crowns." She turned to Maren, eyes wide and wet. "Eleven crowns. That was your Tomas's debt. Everyone in this quarter knew it. Eleven crowns."

The clock struck on. And Maren Kostel stood barefoot in the frozen lane with her arms wrapped around herself, listening to her husband's name spoken in bronze, and began, without deciding to, to weep.

* * *

At the frozen docks, the night foreman Bram Ostergaard heard it too, and understood it faster than anyone, because he had spent twenty years reading tide tables and cargo manifests and he knew what a column of figures sounded like.

He stood beneath the loading crane with his lantern raised, and the Meridian lay dark beside him, crates of bond papers lashed under canvas in her hold, six guards stamping their feet along her rail. The clock's strange arithmetic rolled down the hill and across the ice-glazed water and Bram counted along with it out of pure habit — he couldn't help himself — and when the sequence cycled a third time he felt the bottom drop out of his stomach.

"That's the bond sums," he said aloud, to no one. "Those are the *bond sums*."

He had loaded those crates himself, three nights ago, under orders signed by men whose signatures he was paid not to look at too hard. He had told himself the usual things a man tells himself: that the papers were tax records, that curiosity was a luxury for people without daughters. Now the whole sky seemed full of the numbers he had carried in the dark, broadcast from the highest point of the city to every ear that could hear, and there was no telling himself anything anymore.

The guards heard it too. He watched them stop stamping. Watched them look at each other. One of them — young, red-nosed, a farm boy by his boots — climbed halfway down the gangplank and stood there with his hand on the rail, listening, his face turning slowly toward the debtor quarter, where the lights were multiplying like a fever.

"Foreman," the boy called. "What is that? What's it saying?"

Bram put out his lantern with his thumb. In the sudden dark he found he could answer honestly for the first time in months.

"It's saying," he said, "what's in the hold."

* * *

In the governor's residence on the hill, Aldous Renwick woke to the sound of his own crime being read to the city.

He was at the window within a minute, dressing-gown thrown on badly, one hand gripping the frame hard enough to blanch the knuckles. Below him Vellamare was lighting itself candle by candle, street by street, a contagion of flame spreading outward from the river quarter — and above it all the clock tolled its alien arithmetic, tireless, exact, repeating with the patience of a thing that could not be argued with or bribed or appointed away.

"Fenn." His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later; he would remember being proud of that. "Fenn, wake up."

But Fenn's rooms were empty, and Renwick stood alone with the sound pouring in through the glass, and for the first time in thirty years of public life he felt the specific vertigo of a man who has stepped onto a floor that is no longer there. Power, he had always believed, was architecture — load-bearing agreements, walls of mutual interest, a roof of shared silence. He had built his carefully. He could hear it, tonight, coming down.

Because he understood the code. Of course he understood it; he had written half of it, or caused it to be written. The phrases cycling through the midnight air were entries from a ledger that had been in his strongbox until eight days ago. Names rendered as counts. Dates as intervals. Sums — God, the sums, repeated over and over so that no listener could miss them, so that every widow and washerwoman in the river quarter could match a number to a face.

Somewhere below, faintly, he could hear his own household stirring. A maid's footsteps in the corridor, quick and frightened — or was it quick and *running*? Toward the stairs, or away?

Renwick rang the bell pull. No one came.

He rang it again, harder, and the little silver handle came away in his hand, broken at the stem, and he stood holding it like a man holding the last tooth of a gear that has stripped, and outside the window the clock went on striking, and striking, and striking.

* * *

Mr. Fenn, meanwhile, was walking very fast down the hill with his notebook in his coat pocket, and his thoughts were in ruins.

He had known for two days — he had worked it out cross-referencing his own meticulous notes, the night filings, the missing striking-train parts, the rags muffled around the bell — and he had gone to Renwick with it, and Renwick had sent soldiers to batter a door instead of asking the obvious next question, which was: *what will the train actually strike?* Fenn was a careful man. Careful men are destroyed by the questions they don't ask, and he had not asked, because some part of him — the part that kept the notebook, that wrote everything down, that had begun weeks ago writing things down *against* his employer without admitting it — had perhaps wanted the answer to remain unwritten a little longer.

Now it was written across the entire sky in sound.

He stopped on the bridge road, midway between the hill and the docks, and took out the notebook, and stood in the falling snow reading his own entries by the light of the burning city's candles. Every tool movement logged. Every night hour accounted. And threaded among them, because he had never been able to help himself, the other record: the manifests he'd copied, the guard rotations, the crate tallies of the Meridian. Two ledgers in one book. He saw now that they had always been two ledgers, and that he had been waiting, all these weeks, for someone to force him to choose which one he was.

The clock struck on. Down at the docks, faintly, he could hear shouting — not alarm now, something else, voices multiplying.

Fenn closed the notebook. Then, after a moment, he opened it to the first page, tore it free, and dropped it into the snow — no. No, he didn't. He was a careful man. He folded it instead, and buttoned it inside his coat, over his heart, where evidence goes when a man has decided which side of it he stands on.

Then he turned uphill, toward the tower, walking fast through the snow, toward whatever was left to be on the right side of.

* * *

Inside the tower, the door was going.

Ilse Verany heard the hinges surrender one at a time, and she planted the boat-hook against the stairwell floor and thought, with a strange clarity, of her brother's debts — eleven crowns once, before the court grew them like mold — and of Zephyrine's young face three years ago in the shop on Cordwainer Lane, promising her silence as if silence were a gift he could afford. She had collected that promise like rent ever since, and tonight she finally understood what it had cost him to pay it, and she was ashamed, and the shame made her stand straighter.

Beside her, Pell pushed himself up the wall, ribs and all, and picked up the iron poker from the dead hearth.

"They'll be through in a minute," he said.

"Yes," said Ilse.

"And when they're through—"

"Listen," said Ilse.

Because the clock had stopped counting.

The silence lasted three seconds — the longest three seconds of any of their lives — and then the great bell above them swung into a figure none of them had programmed, a rising run of strokes, five ascending notes like a stairway climbed, and Pell began to laugh, a cracked, delighted, disbelieving laugh that hurt his ribs and continued anyway.

"The repeat signal," he gasped. "The boy built in a *repeat* — it'll cycle the whole ledger until dawn — Ilse, do you hear it? It's going to start *again*. Anyone who missed the beginning—"

The door burst inward.

* * *

They came up the stairs four abreast, soldiers with axes and lanterns, and found at the top of the spiral not a fortress but a tableau: an old keeper with a poker, a disgraced magistrate with a boat-hook, and a slight apprentice in a leather apron standing calmly at the open case of the master clock, both hands still resting on the frame, as if the machine were a piano mid-phrase and he its only faithful audience.

"Stop it," said the sergeant. "Stop the clock. Governor's order."

Zephyrine did not move. Above them all the bell rolled out its bronze arithmetic, patient, inexorable, beginning the ledger again from the first name.

"You can arrest me," Zephyrine said. His voice was quiet and it carried anyway, the way fine mechanisms carry motion through small parts. "You can break my hands. But I'd have to stop it, and stopping it means opening the case, and opening the case means finding the governor's ledger wedged in the escapement where his own keeper hid it — entry by entry, sergeant, matching what's ringing over your head right now. Are those the orders? Truly? Read them to me slowly."

The sergeant looked at the paper in his fist. Outside, across the city, the lights were still kindling. From down by the river came a new sound, growing, rolling up through the snow: hundreds of voices, unarmored and unarmed and entirely unwilling, converging on the docks where the Meridian waited to be loaded.

The soldiers looked at one another. The youngest one lowered his axe first.

Above them the clock struck on, and on, and would not stop until morning — and down in the lanes of Vellamare, in kitchens and doorways and frozen squares, the whole city read aloud together what had been done to it, name by name, date by date, crown by crown, while at the foot of the hill the dockworkers of the Meridian began, quietly and then not quietly at all, to refuse.

And Aldous Renwick, watching from his darkened window, gave the order that would end him.
