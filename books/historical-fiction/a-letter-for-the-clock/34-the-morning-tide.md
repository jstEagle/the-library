## 34. The Morning Tide

Zephyrine did not sleep. There had been no hour in which sleep was possible — the tower above him still ticking through its altered heart, the depositions still arriving below in the market square, and somewhere on the river the Meridian riding at her moorings with nothing in her hold but ballast and shame. So he sat instead at the long workbench in the keeper's room with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold hours ago, watching the window turn from black to iron to the color of oyster shell.

The storm had spent itself sometime before midnight. What remained of it was a fine drifting haze of snow-melt off the water, so that when he finally climbed the stairs to the clock gallery and looked out through the eastern lancet, the whole river seemed to be exhaling — mist rising off the current in slow ribbons, the far bank a smudge of gray willows, the sky behind it going pale as vellum.

Below, the city was awake in a way he had never seen it awake. Not the waking of ordinary mornings, which was a gradual thing, a tide of lamplighters and bakers' boys and the first carts on the cobbles. This was a waking like a body coming out of a fever — sudden, disoriented, unable to return to what it had been before. Lights burned in windows that should have been dark. Figures stood on balconies in dressing gowns, looking down toward the docks. Somewhere a bell that was not his bell was ringing — the harbor chapel, perhaps, or the guildhall, calling people to witness.

They had decoded it. All night they had been decoding it.

He thought about that: three hundred names, forty-one dates, sums tallied to the last copper, struck out over the rooftops of Vellamare in a pattern no citizen had ever heard, each sequence repeated four times because four times was what the mathematics allowed between the hours, and then again at dawn if the train held. He had built it to be heard once by one man in one room — a ledger read aloud in brass. Instead the whole city had sat up in the dark with pencils and paper and listened to their own griefs spelled out in chimes.

*Oyelaran*, someone would know now. The name of the apprentice who broke the clock open. He found he could not decide whether that frightened him. Perhaps both things were true at once, the way both weights hung on either end of a single cord.

* * *

He went down.

The observatory's front steps were slick with wet snow, and the causeway road below them was already crowded — not with soldiers, not anymore, but with citizens walking down toward the water in ones and twos and then in dozens, carrying lanterns against the last of the dark, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who have decided something overnight and will not be argued out of it by daylight.

At the foot of the hill, where the road met the dock road, Zephyrine stopped and simply looked.

The Meridian lay along the quay like a patient animal, her rigging furred with rime, her deck lamps still burning yellow against the graying sky. She was a coastal brig, broad-beamed, built for cargo, and even from this distance he could see that she sat wrong in the water — too high, too light. A ship loaded for the capital should draw six feet at least. She drew hardly more than her empty draft. Any dockhand could read it at a glance. *She sails with nothing in her.*

And around her, on the quay, the dockworkers were standing in a line.

Not working. Standing. Maybe two hundred of them, maybe more, in wool caps and oilskins, arms folded against the cold, arranged along the length of the berth in a silent picket that began at the forward bollard and ran all the way past the stern line into the fog. They were not shouting. They were not holding signs. Vellamare's dockworkers had learned generations ago that signs gave soldiers something to burn. They were simply *present*, in the way that a wall is present, in the way that a closed door is present.

Aboard ship, a handful of crew moved aimlessly about the deck. On the quay behind the line of workers, a squad of Renwick's soldiers stood in a loose cluster near the warehouse door, their officer talking rapidly with the harbormaster, whose gestures — Zephyrine watched them for a moment and translated — were those of a man explaining tide tables to someone who does not want to hear them.

The morning tide would turn at seven minutes past six. He knew this the way he knew the escapement's beat, because Pell had made him memorize the harbor tables years' worth back when Pell had still believed the boy might stay. The Meridian could sail until roughly half past nine before the channel shoaled. After that she waited a full day, and after that the ice.

It was twenty minutes past five.

* * *

Ilse found him there, or perhaps she had come down beside him without his noticing; exhaustion makes ghosts of companions. She stood with her hands buried in the sleeves of her coat, her breath smoking, her face turned toward the ship. She looked as though she had slept less than he had, which he would not have believed possible an hour ago.

"They came all night," she said. Her voice was rough. "To the guildhall. We set up tables in the reading room and the corridor and eventually on the stairs. Clerks from three different offices volunteered to take dictation. By two o'clock we had run out of sworn statements forms and Master Quillon — the old notary, you remember him — he began writing them out by hand, one page at a time, and signing his seal to each." She paused. "There are four hundred and eleven. I counted them at dawn because I couldn't stop counting."

Zephyrine said nothing. There was nothing in him equal to speech yet. He watched the light come up along the water.

"An old woman," Ilse went on, quieter now, "brought a shoebox of her son's letters. Fisherman, taken for debt two winters ago. She'd kept every letter he sent from downriver until the letters stopped. She wouldn't leave until someone wrote down every date. She made us get the dates exactly right." Ilse's jaw worked. "I swore her statement myself. I am no longer a magistrate. I have no authority. It means nothing, legally. She thanked me anyway."

"The clock is still striking," Zephyrine said. "Every quarter hour. The full opening sequence. It'll hold through the morning if the temperature stays steady."

"I heard it at midnight from my window." Something moved through her voice then that he could not name — awe braided with grief, or grief wearing awe's coat. "Do you know what it sounded like? From across the city?" She shook her head before he could answer. "It sounded like the city counting its dead out loud. And no one could make it stop, and no one wanted to."

* * *

By six, the crowd on the quay had grown past counting.

They came down every street that led to the water — from the debtor quarter, shuttered for a season and now flung open, windows lit, doors propped with stones; from the tanners' lanes; from the merchant rows, where men Zephyrine recognized as bond-holders and factors walked stiffly among the laborers with the uncomfortable faces of people arriving late to their own rescue. Someone had brought braziers. Someone else had brought bread, and loaves were being passed hand to hand down the length of the picket line without comment, the way supplies move along a siege.

On the Meridian's deck, Captain Aldous Voss — Zephyrine knew the name from the manifests, had spelled it himself in cipher — stood at the rail with his arms on the wood, looking down at the crowd with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he does not like.

The soldiers had not dispersed. That was the thing Zephyrine kept returning to, watching them from the fringe of the crowd with his collar up and his tool roll conspicuously absent, trying to look like a bystander, a clerk, anyone. There were perhaps thirty of them, under a lieutenant named Corhane whom Zephyrine had seen at the governor's dinner, seated two places from Fenn. Thirty soldiers, two hundred dockworkers, four hundred citizens and rising. The mathematics were not subtle. Even the soldiers could do them. And yet they stood, because standing was what they were paid for, and no order had come telling them to do anything else.

No order had come because the orders came from the governor's house, and Zephyrine had heard — the news moved through a crowd faster than any courier — that Governor Renwick had not been seen since midnight. His house blazed with light on its hill across the river. Carriages had come and gone. But the man himself had not appeared, had not spoken, had not countermanded anything or commanded anything, and his absence was itself a kind of confession, legible to everyone on the waterfront: power, confronted with its own arithmetic, calculating.

"He won't burn the ship," Ilse said. She had followed his gaze to the soldiers. "I kept asking myself all night whether he would. Whether a man like that, cornered—" She stopped. Started again. "But look at them. Look at the line. If he gives the order to clear that quay by force, every soldier knows his name will be in tomorrow's deposition too. Power is only ever other people agreeing to obey. You taught me that phrase yourself, or the clock did, one of the two. And this morning nobody agrees."

"You said that better than I ever did," Zephyrine murmured, and was surprised to find his mouth almost bending toward something like a smile.

* * *

At seven minutes past six the tide turned.

He felt it rather than saw it — no, that was fancy; he saw it, in the slow reversal of the drift beneath the pier pilings, in the way the fog began to lean downstream instead of up. The water began its patient pull toward the sea, taking with it the last of the storm's wreckage, a few planks, a drowned crate, the season itself.

The harbormaster walked the length of the quay to the Meridian's gangway. The lieutenant fell in beside him. There was a conversation at the foot of the plank — Zephyrine was close enough now, having drifted forward with the crowd's slow gravity, to see the harbormaster's face, which was the face of a man delivering a verdict he has rehearsed. Then the harbormaster produced a document, unfolded it, and held it out.

Captain Voss came down to read it.

What it said Zephyrine never learned in words, but he learned it in every other language: the captain read it twice, looked up at the crowd, looked down the length of his own empty hold through the open hatch, and then turned and gave an order to his mate in a voice pitched to carry no further than the deck. The mate relayed it. The bosun relayed it further. And the Meridian's crew, with the unhurried competence of sailors everywhere, began to make ready to sail.

Not to load. To sail. Empty.

The crowd understood it all at once, the way crowds understand things — a ripple running the length of the quay, a sound halfway between a sigh and a cheer, quickly suppressed, because no one quite trusted the moment enough yet to celebrate in it. The dockworkers did not break their line. They simply stepped aside, segment by segment, opening a lane from the gangway to the channel like a gate swinging wide.

But the ship did not sail. Not yet. Because that was the moment the second procession arrived.

* * *

They came down Harbor Street four abreast, carrying the depositions.

Ilse had organized it, of course — Zephyrine realized that only afterward, seeing the order in it, the way he saw the order in a gear train once it was assembled and turning. Four hundred and eleven sworn statements, bound in twine in bundles of fifty, carried openly on trays like bread at a feast, borne by clerks in ink-stained cuffs and notaries with their chains of office worn defiantly over plain coats and, at the head of the column, old Master Quillon himself, ninety if he was a day, walking with two canes and refusing both offered arms.

The column reached the gangway. Quillon mounted it — slowly, terribly slowly, the whole quay silent enough that Zephyrine could hear the old man's canes knocking on the boards, knock, knock, knock, like a small clock keeping time — and presented the documents to Captain Voss on the deck of his own ship.

"We are told," Quillon said, in a voice like dry paper, "that you sail for the capital on the morning tide. Good. These go with you." He lifted the top bundle so the crowd could see it. "Four hundred and eleven sworn testimonies concerning the sale of persons out of this port. Witnessed, sealed, and dated. You will deliver them to the High Assizes, and you will tell them, when they ask why the papers smell of salt, that they were carried by a ship that left here empty, because the people of Vellamare declined to load anything else aboard her."

The cheer that went up then could not be suppressed. It rolled off the water and came back off the cliffs doubled.

Captain Voss took the bundles. Zephyrine watched the man weigh them in his hands — actual weight, a few pounds of paper and wax — and watched something shift in the sailor's face, some final calculation resolving. Voss looked at the crowd, at the empty hold, at the harbor mouth where the fog was tearing open onto a hard blue morning, and then he put one hand flat on the stack of depositions as though taking an oath upon them.

"We sail on the tide," he called down. "Anyone who wants their name on these papers has until the topsails are sheeted home."

They came running. That was the part Zephyrine had not imagined, could not have imagined: that after everything, there would be more. Citizens pressing up the gangway with last statements scrawled on whatever paper they owned, a woman with a child's drawing of a man on a boat, an old debtors'-quarter priest with a list of the missing copied out in a fair round hand. Clerks took them at the rail. The bundles thickened.

Zephyrine stood at the foot of the gangway with the crowd surging past him on both sides and let it happen. He had done his part; the clock had done its part; the rest belonged to them. But he could not leave. He stayed and watched every bundle go aboard, and he counted, because he could not stop counting either, and when the last citizen came back down the plank he had lost track somewhere past five hundred.

* * *

The topsails went up at half past eight.

They rose white against the clearing sky, and the crowd hushed again, that same untrusting hush, and then the lines came off — forward spring first, then stern, the deckhands coiling and stowing with the smooth economy of men doing the one work that had never lied to them — and the Meridian leaned away from the quay and caught the ebb.

She went downriver slowly, as empty ships do, high-sided and buoyant, her wake a thin seam of foam in the gray-gold water. The crowd along the quay turned to follow her, hundreds of heads moving together like a field bending under wind. No one spoke. The only sounds were the water, the gulls, the creak of the pier, and — faint, from up the hill behind him, unmistakable to a man who had built it — the master clock beginning the quarter-hour strike, the ledger's opening sequence rolling out over the rooftops one more time, following the ship down the channel like a blessing or a summons.

*Fourteen. Seven. Three. Nine.*

Names. Dates. Sums. People.

Zephyrine watched until the Meridian passed the harbor forts and her hull shrank to a dark line on the brightening water, and then until the line thinned, and then until there was only the sea and the fog burning off it, and the ship was gone — carrying, for the first time in the whole history of that route, nothing but the truth.

Beside him, Ilse exhaled, long and shaking, as though she had been holding her breath since November.

"It's done," she said. "Whatever happens now — the assizes, the trials, all of it, months of it, years of it — the evidence is beyond his reach. It's on the water. It's *gone*." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and exhausted. "You understand what you did? Not the clock. Anyone might have—no. No one else could have done the clock. But you gave her the choice. Before any of this. In peacetime, when it cost you nothing to keep quiet, and again in the tower, when it cost you everything. You gave me the choice, and I—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "I keep thinking about what I nearly chose."

"But you didn't," Zephyrine said. "The rest is arithmetic."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound with no real laughter in it, and every bit of real relief. "Arithmetic," she repeated. "You and your numbers."

Behind them, up the hill, the clock finished its sequence and settled back into its plain, honest tick, and Zephyrine turned toward it, and that was when he saw them coming down the causeway road.

Six riders, moving fast, mud to the fetlocks, with the governor's colors at their front — but riding easy, weapons slung, no warrant shouted ahead of them. At their head rode a thin, neat figure in a clerk's gray coat that Zephyrine knew too well, and Mr. Fenn had a notebook in one hand and, oddly, his hat in the other, held to his chest like a man approaching a church.

Ilse saw them a heartbeat later. Her whole body changed. "Fenn," she breathed. "Alone. Unarmed. Coming to *us*." She looked at Zephyrine, and he saw the question form in her face just as it formed in his own: what does an informer want at dawn, on the day the ship sailed, with his patron's house dark behind him?

The riders drew rein at the foot of the observatory steps. Fenn dismounted, bowed — actually bowed, low and formal — and opened his notebook.

"If you please," he said, in his mild, terrible, courteous voice, "there is something in these pages that the assizes will need. And there is nowhere left to bring it — except to the people the clock woke."
