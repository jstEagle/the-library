## 38. Pell's Retirement

The winter had taught Ilse Verany how much a body could endure and still call itself alive. She had learned it the way one learns any hard arithmetic — not all at once, but in increments: the cold that seeped through the tower's stone and settled in her knuckles; the hunger that stopped being pain and became a kind of quiet; the sound of axes on oak below, each blow a question she had answered already with her feet on the stairs. Now, in the thaw-light of late March, she found she did not know what to do with a body that was merely comfortable. Comfort felt like a language she had spoken as a girl and half forgotten.

She sat in Pell's quarters — her quarters now, though she had not agreed to that yet — with the window open an inch, letting in the smell of the river unbinding itself from ice. Below, the causeway bridge stood black and dripping against the bright water, its timbers swollen with the season's first meltwater. Workmen were already out on it, small figures in brown coats, prying at the snow-fences, sweeping the ice from the planking. In three days, perhaps four, the first cart would cross.

In three days, perhaps four, she would cross with it, going the other way — upriver, toward the capital, toward a courtroom where men in grey wigs would ask her to say what she knew.

She had spent the morning writing it down anyway, because Ambrose Pell had told her once that a keeper's habit was to write things before they were needed. *The clock stopped on the ninth of October,* she wrote. *It was stopped by hand.* The ink moved well in this light. That was something. The ink moving well was something.

* * *

Pell came up from the under-works at noon, as he had come every day since they had carried him down from the alarm bell's platform with his ribs stove in and his left hand ruined. He climbed the ladder badly now, one rung at a time, pausing to breathe, and Ilse had learned not to offer her arm because he would only refuse it, and refusing cost him more than climbing did.

He lowered himself into his chair by the stove like a man docking a boat — slowly, with attention to the angles — and looked at his bandaged hand where it lay in his lap. Fenn's men had done that. No; Fenn himself, or so the interrogation transcripts said, in that flat clerk's hand that made violence sound like bookkeeping. Fenn had surrendered his notebooks in exchange for his pardon, and somewhere in the capital those notebooks were being read aloud into a record, and somewhere in Vellamare a man who had broken an old keeper's hand with a truncheon was walking free because he had been useful in his treachery.

Ilse had opinions about that. She kept them folded away for the trial, where they would do more damage.

"Pell," she said. "You should eat."

"I should do a great many things." He smiled — the same dry smile he'd had the night he confessed to her and Zephyrine both, handing them the ledger like a coal out of his own burning. Age had hollowed him further since; the winter had taken flesh off him the river took banks. But the eyes were unchanged: pale, patient, and lit from underneath by whatever stubborn mineral keeps old men upright past all reason. "I should have hidden that ledger better. I should have trusted Zephyrine sooner. I should have let you sleep last night instead of talking until the candle drowned."

"You talked," Ilse said. "I listened. There's a difference."

"There is." He turned his good hand palm-up on the armrest, an old man's gesture of surrender to the room. "Ilse Verany. Sit down properly. I have business with you, and I haven't the strength to conduct it standing."

She sat. Outside the window, a gull rode the wind above the bridge, wings fixed, going nowhere beautifully.

"I am seventy-one years old," Pell said. "My hand will never hold a winding key again — Doctor Ammeret says so, and Ammeret is honest, which in a doctor is nearly as rare as in a magistrate. My ribs knit crooked. I cough in the damp, and this tower is nothing but damp wearing stone as a disguise." He said it without self-pity, the way he read tide tables. Facts, arranged. "The observatory needs a keeper. Not next spring. Not after your trial. Now. A master clock does not forgive neglect; it takes revenge slowly, which is worse than taking it quickly."

"You're retiring."

"I am being retired," he corrected. "By my own body, over my objection. It happens to every keeper eventually — the mechanism wears out before the works it tends. We are clocks too, Magistrate. We simply keep poorer time." He looked at her then, directly, and she understood with a lurch that the business was not what she had assumed. "I'm not asking you. I haven't the authority to ask anyone. What I have is the naming, and the naming is done. I named Zephyrine Oyelaran acting keeper this morning, in writing, witnessed by Ammeret and the harbormaster's clerk. He doesn't know yet. I wanted to tell you first, because you'll want to argue, and I'd rather argue with someone whose arguments I've already lost once."

Ilse sat back. "Zephyrine is an apprentice."

"He was an apprentice. He reworked a striking train inside a besieged tower with a spy in the shop and an axe at the door, and the chimes spelled out thirty-one names across a sleeping city without dropping a single beat." Pell's voice roughened on the last words, and he let it. "The guild can make him a journeyman by summer if they have any sense, and if they don't, I'll buy him his freedom papers myself and be damned to Halloway & Sons. The boy kept time honestly when everyone else in this city was lying with their mouths full. That's the whole of what a keeper is."

"And me?" she asked, though she heard the answer coming the way one hears weather.

"You," said Pell, "are leaving on the bridge."

* * *

They found Zephyrine in the clock room, of course. Where else. He was up the short ladder at the movement, sleeves rolled, a lamp hooked on the frame casting his shadow enormous across the case, and he was doing the thing Ilse had watched him do a hundred times that winter without ever tiring of watching it: listening with his hands. Two fingers resting on the great wheel, eyes closed, reading the vibration the way a blind man reads a page.

"Your keeper has news," Pell called up.

Zephyrine came down fast, wiping his hands on his rag, and Ilse saw again what the winter had made of him — the softness gone out of his face, replaced by something planed and true, like wood worked past its grain. Seventeen months ago he had been a boy who blushed when she spoke to him. Then he had been a boy holding a secret between them like a drawn blade. Now he was neither. He was a man who had broadcast a governor's crimes across the sky in bell-metal and slept afterward, she suspected, like a stone.

"He's not my keeper," Zephyrine said.

"He is now." Pell handed him the folded paper. "Acting keeper, effective today, pending the guild's ratification, which I will bully out of them by letter. Read it later. For now there are two things. First: the third wheel in the striking train has a worn tooth — you'll have noticed the hesitation on the half-hour. Replace it from the stores, the spare is wrapped in oilcloth behind the weight shelf, and for pity's sake check the oil before you seat it, the last apprentice who didn't—" a pause, and something flickered through the old man's face, grief arriving punctually at its appointed hour, "—the last apprentice is beyond correction. Second thing."

Zephyrine waited. Ilse watched him wait — the stillness of it, hands loose at his sides, giving the moment its full weight. He had learned that from the clock, she thought. Or the clock had always been in him, and the winter had merely wound it tight enough to show.

"The Meridian sails on the morning tide in four days," Pell said. "Empty, thank God, but sailing. And the bridge opens the day after, and on that bridge goes the Magistrate Verany, to the capital, to testify against Aldous Renwick and eleven others. Which means that from the day after tomorrow, this tower holds exactly one keeper and no magistrates and no spies and no soldiers, and the loudest thing in it will be the truth, ticking." His voice caught on the plainness of it. "I wanted us three to hear it said aloud while we're still three. After this week we won't ever be three again. That's all. That's the second thing."

Nobody spoke. Above them, muffled by floors of timber and stone, the master clock ticked on — the sound Ilse had come to think of as the city's pulse, except that pulses didn't lie, and for eleven years this one had been forced to. It didn't lie anymore. She had checked. Every hour, she checked.

Zephyrine looked at the paper in his hand without unfolding it. "I accept," he said, "on one condition. You stay in the north room until the thaw is done and your ribs are set straight. Acting keeper's orders can wait on the real keeper's health."

"That's not how—"

"It is now. I read the paper. It says I have authority over the maintenance of the observatory." A ghost of a smile. "You're maintenance."

Pell stared at him, and then laughed — a rusty, painful, genuine sound, one hand pressed to his crooked ribs as though holding coins that might spill. "God help Vellamare," he said. "They gave the tower to the one apprentice in the river country who argues back."

* * *

That evening Ilse packed.

It took less time than she expected. A winter lived in a clock tower compresses down to very little: two dresses, the grey coat, a brush, a bundle of documents tied in oilcloth against the damp, and the notebook — hers, kept through the siege in defiance of every instinct of safety, because a magistrate stripped of office learns that the only court that never adjourns is the written page. She packed her seal too, the obsolete one, Renwick having declared it void the autumn he declared *her* void. She packed it deliberately. In the capital she intended to use it, void or not, until someone with the authority to stop her actually tried.

At the bottom of her trunk, wrapped in a stocking, was the thing she had not decided about: a single sheet of paper, three years old, in Zephyrine's careful apprentice hand. *I, Zephyrine Oyelaran, promise silence concerning the debts of Tomas Verany, this debt being discharged…* A foolish document. A beautiful one. She had carried it through everything — through her own disgrace, through the siege, through the terrible night in February when she had demanded the truth from him and he had given it to her *first*, before the city, before the soldiers, honoring a promise by breaking it open in her hands and letting her choose.

She held it over the stove a long time.

The fire took it grudgingly, the way fires take promises — curling at the edges, browning, then blooming into a brief clean flame that smelled of iron-gall ink. She watched it go without sadness. Promises had cost enough. What she and the boy had now was sturdier than a promise: it was a shared act, witnessed by a whole city, and acts did not need keeping. They only needed telling.

A knock. She knew the knock — two fingers only, light, a craftsman's knock, apologetic about interrupting whatever the wood was thinking.

"Come in, Keeper."

"Don't call me that yet." Zephyrine entered, and she saw he had dressed with care — the good shirt, the collar actually buttoned — and was carrying something wrapped in flannel the size of a loaf of bread. "Pell's asleep. Ammeret dosed him. So it's just us, and I wanted—" He stopped, restarted, and Ilse realized with a strange tenderness that the boy who could lie smoothly to a governor still could not find easy words with her. Some things survived even a revolution. "I wanted to give you this before you go. Because on the bridge, going upriver, you'll be passing everything. The debtor quarter. The docks. The whole geography of it. And I thought you shouldn't pass empty-handed."

He unwrapped the flannel.

Inside lay a small clock. Not a toy — a true clock, though small as a hymnal, its case of dark river-walnut polished to a low shine, its dial enamel white with black Roman numerals and no ornament whatsoever. She turned it over. On the back, engraved in letters fine as frost: *V.*

"You made this?"

"In the nights. Before—" he gestured vaguely, meaning the siege, the axes, all of it, "—and some after. It keeps good time. I regulated it against the master for six days running; it lost four seconds total." He hesitated. "There's no second train in it. No cipher. No tricks. It just strikes the hours, plainly, the way a clock ought. I thought—" and here he looked down at his own hands, which had done such work and carried such weight, "—I thought you'd earned a piece of time that asks nothing of you."

Ilse took it. It was heavier than it looked, the way honest things usually are, and warm from the flannel, and when she tilted it the escapement ticked against her palms like a small stubborn heart.

"Zephyrine." She made herself say the rest plainly, because they had earned plainness too. "When I come back from the capital — and I mean to come back; I intend to walk into that guildhall of yours and see you sworn in with my own eyes — I won't be a magistrate. Probably ever. Whatever they decide about my reinstatement, I'll carry the stain of that year regardless. Men like Renwick make sure the people who catch them stay marked."

"Good," Zephyrine said.

She blinked. "Good?"

"A marked magistrate is a useful magistrate. Everyone knows which way you lean." He said it lightly, but underneath she heard the steel that had held a door shut against soldiers. "Besides. The city remembers who stood in the tower. That memory doesn't stain. It seasons."

She laughed — really laughed, for the first time since autumn, a short startled sound that fogged in the cold air of the room — and set the little clock on the sill where the last of the daylight touched it. "Then I'll take that as testimony, witness Oyelaran, and enter it into the record."

"Objection. Hearsay."

"Sustained anyway. I'm still technically a magistrate until the capital says otherwise, and I intend to abuse that fact thoroughly on the journey."

* * *

The fourth morning came grey and silver, the river running high and loud with melt, and the bridge stood open.

Ilse saw it from the tower window before dawn: lanterns moving along the causeway, the first carts, a knot of people simply standing at the near end looking across as though the far bank were a foreign country. Four months sealed. Children born in December who had never seen the other shore. She watched a dog cross at a dead run, ecstatic, skidding on the wet planks, and something in her chest unlocked that had been locked since the first snowfall.

The Meridian went out on the morning tide with the ebb, riding low and light, empty of bonds at last. From the tower gallery they watched her turn downstream, sails filling, growing small against the bright water — and Ilse found she could not hate the ship anymore. Ships only carried what people put in them. For a hundred years this one had carried people sold like cargo; yesterday it had carried nothing; someday, if the world improved, it would carry only what ships were meant to carry. Progress, measured in cargoes. She would settle for convictions.

Her trunk went down on a dockhand's barrow. Her documents went inside her coat, against her body, where no wet or theft or accident would reach them. At the foot of the tower stair, Pell waited in his chair — Ammeret had carried him down, over his furious objections, so that he could see her off — and he took her hand with his one good one and held it longer than manners required.

"Magistrate," he said. "Go make the law remember what it's for."

"And you, Keeper. Go keep."

Zephyrine walked her as far as the bridgehead, carrying her smallest bag, and there they stopped, because some distances are meant to be crossed alone. The crowd flowed around them. Somewhere a ferryman was shouting fares. The air smelled of thaw and tar and the green hint of things not yet grown.

"No promises," Ilse said.

"No promises," he agreed. "Promises have cost enough."

"But write to me. Care of the Guild of Scriveners in the capital — they'll forward anything; I bribed the under-clerk with the story of the siege, and he's seventeen and thinks you're a legend."

"I'm not a legend."

"You rang a city awake with stolen names. Write anyway." She took the bag from his shoulder and held his hand a moment — just his hand, scarred at the knuckles now, steady as a pendulum. "And mind the third wheel. It hesitates."

"It won't by tonight."

She believed him. That was the strangest gift of the whole terrible year: she believed him completely, the way one believes a clock, and she crossed onto the bridge with that belief ticking in her breast pocket beside her seal.

Halfway across she stopped, as everyone stops halfway across a long bridge, and looked back. The observatory stood white-capped against the pale sky, its dial catching the first true sun of spring. And beneath it, faint with distance but unmistakable, the master clock began to strike the hour — ten strokes, even and unhurried and utterly plain, each one falling on the river like a stone dropped into clear water.

No cipher. No code. Just the time, told honestly, to anyone who cared to hear.

Ilse Verany stood on the opening bridge and listened to all ten strokes, and then she turned upriver toward the capital, toward the wigs and the witnesses and the long reckoning, and did not look back again.

Behind her, in the tower, a new keeper climbed the stairs to check a wheel.
