## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The coach from Valcourt took eleven hours, and Marguerite spent nine of them memorizing it.

This was habit, not anxiety. Habit was the whole of their religion: the mile markers counted, the fords noted, the places where the road narrowed enough that two men with muskets could stop a carriage and be done with it before the driver finished his oath. They sat in the corner seat with a leather satchel on their knees and the posture of a man who had been traveling since dawn and resented every minute of it, which had the great advantage of being true. Nobody bothers a tired clerk. Tired clerks are furniture.

In the satchel, beneath a customs manifest and a shirt gone soft at the collar, lay fourteen sheets of paper covered in what appeared to be a merchant's dull correspondence about hemp prices. Fourteen letters in diplomatic cipher, each one worth more than the coach, the horses, the driver, and Marguerite's life priced together at market rates. The Republic of Valcourt was dying by inches on its northern border, and its government had responded the way dying governments always do: with paperwork.

*You will proceed to Saint-Aubin,* the orders had read, in the handler's cramped hand. *You will present yourself as a clerk attached to the provincial survey office. You will await further instructions.*

Further instructions. Marguerite had been awaiting further instructions for six years. It was rather like waiting for rain in a country where it never rained; you kept your coat oiled anyway.

The town announced itself first as a smell—woodsmoke and river mud and, underneath, the green bite of orchards—and then as a smudge of red roofs folded into a bend of the Aubin like coins spilled from a pocket. From the ridge road it looked, Marguerite thought, like the sort of place that had never once made history and was proud of it. A church spire. A bridge. A market square with a fountain in the middle that some generation of practical ancestors had put there so the women would have an excuse to stand and talk.

They catalogued it automatically, the way other people breathed.

*One stone bridge over the Aubin, wide enough for carts, guarded by nothing but a toll house with a sleeping dog. A second crossing upstream—a ford, if the summer had been dry; the reeds along the bank said yes. Three roads out: north to Valcourt, which was no longer an exit but a mouth; south toward the frontier and the customs house; east through the hills, a track really, passable for a mule or a determined man and not for an army, which meant it was the best road of the three.* The church had a squat tower, good for a lookout or a bell-ringer, and a graveyard whose wall backed onto a tannery, which meant any hole dug in the churchyard's far corner would smell of hides and attract no questions. The market square had four ways in. Every town has four ways in if you look long enough.

Marguerite looked long enough. Then they closed the ledger of it in their mind and let the coach rattle down into the ordinary noise of Saint-Aubin, because a clerk arriving at dusk does not stare at bridges. A clerk arrives, is tired, wants soup.

* * *

The inn was called the Compass Rose, which struck Marguerite as either a joke or an act of faith, since the sign hanging over the door showed a compass rose whose points had all been repainted at different times and now disagreed about where north was. Underneath it, in flaking gilt: GOOD BEDS. HONEST RATES.

The honest rate turned out to be twenty sous a night including supper, and the good bed turned out to exist, which already put the establishment ahead of three inns Marguerite could name in Valcourt. The common room was low-ceilinged and warm, half full of farmers drinking slowly, a carter arguing amiably with nobody, and two garrison soldiers from the Republic's border company playing cards with the concentrated dishonesty of men who were losing. A fire. A cat asleep in a bread basket, unchallenged. The smell of onion stew that suggested someone in the kitchen understood that onions are a form of mercy.

Behind the bar stood a woman in her forties with flour to her elbows and an expression that took inventory of everything and committed none of it to memory where anyone could see her doing it. She had the kind of face that is called plain until you notice the eyes, and then you wonder who decided.

"Clerk?" she said, reading the survey-office story off Marguerite before it was fully out of their mouth. "For the province?"

"Attached to the provincial survey," Marguerite confirmed, and produced papers that would survive a casual inspection by God himself and a close one by most lieutenants. "Roads and boundaries. I'll want a room for—some weeks, likely."

"Some weeks." She said it without inflection, which was itself information. Inns near borders learn not to ask how long. "Supper's included. Stew tonight. There's wine if you're the type, and watered wine if you're the type who thinks he isn't."

"Watered," said Marguerite, who was precisely that type, and watched something almost like approval move behind her eyes.

She poured it herself and set down bread that was still warm, which meant she'd pulled the evening loaf early on purpose, which meant she'd seen them come up the road an hour ago and decided how to receive them before they'd opened their mouth. Marguerite filed this away under *the innkeeper is smarter than she lets you think* and then, catching themselves doing it, felt the small familiar ache that came whenever competence in another person registered as anything other than useful.

"Vasquez," they said. "Clerk Vasquez."

"Ferrand. Odile Ferrand. My daughter Sylvie waits tables, my late husband built the stairs you'll climb, and if you hear hammering after midnight that's Tomas from the forge, who believes his wife doesn't notice when he's out. Everyone here believes something untrue about everyone else. It keeps the peace." She wiped the counter in front of Marguerite with a cloth, though it was clean. "Eat your stew while it's hot. Cold stew is just a rumor of stew."

It was excellent stew. Marguerite ate it the way they ate everything, thoroughly and without visible pleasure, and hated themselves a little for the discipline involved.

* * *

After supper they walked.

A clerk may walk. Clerks walk everywhere; it is practically their trade. So Marguerite strolled the perimeter of Saint-Aubin in the blue hour, hands clasped behind their back, admiring fences, and did with their feet what other spies needed maps to do.

The bridge first. Stone, five arches, the middle one showing old flood repairs. The toll house held one ancient keeper and the aforementioned dog, now awake and professionally disinterested. A cart could be searched there in four minutes flat, and often was, judging by the straw scattered on the planks and the bored scratch-marks where soldiers had tested knives. *Checkpoint, informal. One man. One dog. Dog is the greater threat; dogs don't take bribes.*

Then south along the river path, past laundry bleaching on hedges—white sheets like flags of surrender nobody had bothered to raise—and past the tannery, confirming the graveyard theory, and out to where the orchards began. Apple trees in rows going feral at the edges. Somewhere a girl was singing to herself, tunelessly, the way children sing when they believe the world consists entirely of themselves and apples.

Upstream, the ford. Knee-deep, gravel-bottomed, marked only by a leaning post and the flattened grass of generations of lovers and smugglers who had found the same shortcut. Marguerite stood on the bank and let the river talk to itself for a moment. The water was loud and indifferent and completely honest, which made it the only thing in Saint-Aubin they could trust outright.

They thought, not for the first time, about how they loved places.

It was a real love, that was the strange part. Not feigned, not performed. Marguerite loved towns the way travelers love railway platforms—not the journey's beginning or end, but the suspended moment between, where you belong nowhere and are therefore safe from belonging anywhere. Six years of this. Six years of learning a town's exits before its streets' names, of knowing exactly how many steps it took from any bed to any window, of loving Saint-Aubin already, wholly, in the space of one evening, with the specific tenderness one feels for things one has already decided to leave.

The alternative was unpacking. Unpacking meant a trunk. A trunk meant a future. A future meant names on lists beside yours, and Marguerite had learned at nineteen what happened to names that shared a page with theirs.

So: platforms. Briefly, warmly, without ever setting down more than one bag.

The church bell rang nine, slightly flat. Marguerite noted the pitch—it would do for counting time at night, if it came to that—and walked back through lanes that smelled of cut grass and banked fires, past windows glowing amber, past a blacksmith's forge where a broad-shouldered man was indeed hammering something after hours with tremendous concentration and no discernible purpose. Past the inn again, pausing at the corner just long enough to see, through the common-room window, Odile Ferrand banking the fire and her daughter—must be the daughter, thirteen or so, all elbows and dark hair—sweeping the floor with the aggressive thoroughness of someone performing labor loudly enough to be excused from a different chore.

Ordinary. The whole scene was aggressively, radiantly ordinary. Marguerite stood in the dark outside the window of a lit room full of strangers and felt the pull of it like a current.

*Don't,* they told themselves, in the tone of long practice. *You've done this forty times. You know how it ends. It ends with you on a coach at dawn, loving this town the way you love a platform, and it ends fine, and everyone lives longer for never having known your face.*

They went in. The daughter looked up from her sweeping.

"You're the clerk," she said, with the devastating accuracy of children. "You walk around the town at night counting things."

Marguerite kept their face pleasant and empty, the face they wore for customs officers and priests and mothers. "Counting things?"

"Sides of the square. Windows on the church. How many geese." She leaned on her broom. "Père Anselme says surveyors count geese."

"Père Anselme," said Marguerite carefully, "is clearly a scholar."

"He's old and he talks to birds." She resumed sweeping. "There's honey cake left if you want it. Maman says clerks never eat enough, she can tell by the wrists."

From the kitchen doorway, without turning from her pans, Odile Ferrand said: "Sylvie. Leave the man his wrists."

And Marguerite Vasquez—who had crossed three frontiers with cipher sewn into a coat lining, who had lied fluently in four languages to bishops and sergeants, whose pulse did not rise at checkpoint inspections or midnight knocks—stood in a warm inn holding a plate of honey cake pressed on them by a child, and experienced the single most dangerous sensation of their professional career.

They wanted to come back tomorrow.

Not for the mission. For the cake. For the fire and the flat bell and the argumentative cat and the woman who judged health by wrists. For one hour of being a person in a room instead of a shape passing through one.

They ate the cake standing up, thanked nobody too warmly, went up the stairs the dead husband had built, and lay in the good bed listening to the river and the last carters leaving the square.

Tomorrow, they reminded the ceiling, there would be instructions to wait for, exits to refine, a town to memorize and release. Eleven days, perhaps twelve, and then a coach south, and then a new town, and the whole sweet ache repeated.

Below, faintly, Sylvie was still sweeping, and singing to herself, tuneless as the girl in the orchard, as though the world consisted entirely of herself and brooms.

Marguerite fell asleep cataloguing the sound, which was the closest thing to unpacking they had done in six years—and knew, even as sleep took them, that somewhere in Valcourt a government that had already forgotten them was writing the next letter that would change everything.

They dreamed, for the first time in years, of staying.
