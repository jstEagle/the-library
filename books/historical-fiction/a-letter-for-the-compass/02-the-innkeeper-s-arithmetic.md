## 2. The Innkeeper's Arithmetic

The inn was called the Compass Rose, which struck Marguerite as either an accident of geography or a joke the town played on itself—a building named for pointing the way home, standing in a place where nobody had ever wanted to stop longer than a night.

It sat at the crook of the road where the north highway bent toward the river square, two stories of grey stone with a slate roof patched in three different colours, like a coat mended by someone who refused to throw it away. A sign swung above the door on iron brackets, and the paint had faded until the compass points were more suggestion than instruction.

Marguerite had catalogued forty-one buildings between the bridge and this door. They had noted which had back stairs, which had cellars with ground-level windows, which had roofs that sloped close enough to the neighboring roof to make a crossing possible. It was not paranoia. It was arithmetic. A clerk arriving in a strange town counted things; that was what clerks did.

What they had not counted on was the smell.

Bread, and under the bread, onions softened in fat, and under the onions something herbed and dark that pulled at the stomach like a hand. Marguerite stood in the doorway a moment longer than a person simply passing through would stand, and hated themselves for the pause, and stepped inside.

* * *

The common room held six people: two drovers asleep over their cups, a tanner mending a strap by the fire, and an old man playing solitaire with a deck so worn the kings looked like beggars. Behind the counter, drying cups with a cloth that had once been white, stood a woman built like the inn itself—broad, grey-slated, patched but sound.

She looked up. She looked at Marguerite's travelling cloak, worn at the hem. At the satchel carried high on the shoulder, the way clerks carried documents rather than clothes. At the boots—good boots, too good for a junior clerk's salary, but scuffed deliberately at the toes, which was the detail that always gave the game away. People who dressed down remembered the coat and forgot the feet.

"You'll be the clerk from the ministry," she said. Not a question.

"Marguerite Vasquez." The alias came out smooth as poured water. "Sent ahead to organize the garrison's requisition paperwork. I'm told you have rooms."

"I'm told a lot of things." The woman set down the cup. "Odile Ferrand. Room and board is twelve sous the week if you're staying, thirty if you're pretending to stay. Supper's extra, except tonight, when it isn't, because there's stew left over and stew is worse on the second day and I refuse to serve it twice."

Marguerite opened their mouth to negotiate—it was habit, negotiation was a way of being disliked slightly, which was safer than being liked—and found no words available. "I... don't understand the difference in rates."

"Neither does anyone, until they do." Odile Ferrand turned and called up the stairwell, her voice carrying with the ease of long practice, "Sylvie! Come take the gentleman's bag. Gently. It looks like it holds his whole opinion of himself."

A clatter above. Footsteps. And then a girl came down the stairs sideways-fast, the way children descend when they have been forbidden to slide the banister and are negotiating with the prohibition. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and dark hair escaping its braid, with her mother's same level brown eyes deployed at twice the speed.

"Are you really from the ministry?" she said, before she had finished arriving.

"Sylvie," said Odile, in the tone of a woman ringing a small bell inside a large cathedral.

"What? He's *from* somewhere. You can't just not ask people where they're from, that's how you end up feeding spies."

"That's exactly how you end up feeding spies," Odile agreed placidly, "at your own table, out of your own pot. Bag. Stairs. Third room on the left, the one with the window that doesn't rattle."

Marguerite watched the girl heft the satchel—she staggered theatrically under its weight, though it could not have been more than ten pounds—and thought, with the cold part of their mind, the part that kept ledgers of a different kind: *the daughter talks too much. The daughter will be a liability.* And then a different thought arrived uninvited, soft-footed as a cat: *she has her mother's laugh waiting behind her teeth.*

They had not heard a laugh yet. But it was waiting. Marguerite could tell, the way one can tell weather from a horizon.

* * *

Supper was served at the long table by the fire because, Odile explained, the corner tables were for people who wanted to be alone and Marguerite had not paid for solitude.

"Is solitude expensive here?" Marguerite asked.

"Everything's expensive here. That's what happens when you live next to a border. The war makes everything cost more—flour, nails, news." She ladled stew into a bowl without asking whether they wanted any. It was not the kind of house where wanting was consulted. "You'll want bread. Don't argue, the loaf went stale today and I need it eaten."

The stew was, in fact, better than most meals Marguerite had eaten in the capital, where restaurants measured their worth in candlelight rather than content. There was lamb in it, or something that had once been ambitious about being lamb, and beans, and rosemary, and a depth that spoke of a pot kept going across days like a banked fire.

"Where's it from?" Marguerite asked. "The recipe."

"His mother." Odile said it the way people say *the church* or *the river*—a fact of landscape. "My late husband's mother. A terrible woman. Suspicious of everyone, kept a knife in her boot until the day she died, and made the best stew in three parishes." She wiped the table in a slow arc. "I've known clerks who couldn't eat like that."

Marguerite paused with the spoon halfway home.

"Like what?"

"Like the food might be taken away mid-bite. Like it's evidence." Odile did not look at them. She was watching the fire with the particular attention of a woman who had spent twenty years keeping one alive. "Clerks from the capital eat fast because they're important and busy. You're eating like the bowl owes you nothing and might still take everything back."

The fire ticked. Across the room, the old man laid down a losing card with the sigh of a philosopher.

"The roads have been bad," Marguerite said. "One eats when one can."

"Mm," said Odile Ferrand, and let it go—which was itself so unusual that Marguerite spent a full minute examining it, turning it over like a coin of uncertain minting. In their experience, people who noticed things either pressed or fled. This woman had done neither. She had simply placed the observation on the table between them, like a dish neither of them would acknowledge was there, and continued eating around it.

* * *

The daughter reappeared during the cheese course, ostensibly to fetch more bread, actually to sit down two seats away and begin an interrogation disguised as conversation.

"Do clerks get holidays?"

"Sometimes."

"Have you ever seen the sea?"

"Yes."

"Was it blue or grey? Because Papa said it was grey and Mama says he only ever looked at it on bad days on purpose, to prove a point."

"Grey," Marguerite admitted, "on every day I saw it. Though I may have been proving a point myself."

Sylvie considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Do you have brothers or sisters?"

"No."

"Nobody at all? Nobody writes to you?"

The question landed in a place Marguerite kept swept and locked, and it rattled the door handle before finding nothing to grip. "No one writes."

"Not even wrong addresses? Mama says half the letters in the world go to people who've moved, and that's practically the same as writing to nobody, so somebody must write to you even accidentally—" She stopped. Her mother's hand had come to rest on her shoulder, light as a falling leaf, and Sylvie read whatever was written there and stood up, muttering, "Everyone in this house treats questions like they're knives," and went off to bed with tremendous, audible dignity.

"She asks everyone those questions," Odile said, into the quiet. "The blacksmith. The priest. The drovers, who answered all of them and then some. Don't flatter yourself that you were investigated specially."

"I wasn't flattering myself."

"No," Odile agreed, gathering bowls. "But you were bracing for it, and I'd have liked to know why, except I find that asking people why they brace usually makes them brace harder. So." She stacked the bowls with a click like a full stop. "Your room's made up. Water in the pitcher. If you hear knocking in the night it's the shutters—the third one has never once stayed latched and I've stopped apologizing for it."

She paused at the foot of the stairs, cloth over her shoulder, firelight making copper of the grey in her hair.

"For what it's worth," she said, "whatever it is you're organizing paperwork for—I hope it keeps you fed. That's all the arithmetic I do anymore. Whether the people at my table ate."

And she went up to bed, leaving Marguerite alone with the fire, the cheese, and the unfamiliar sensation of having been seen completely and charged nothing for it.

* * *

Upstairs, in the room with the rattling third shutter, Marguerite performed the nightly liturgy. Satchel inspected. Cipher letters verified beneath the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, untouched since the capital. Window assessed—second floor, drainpipe to the left, stable roof below, a drop survivable if one rolled. Boots positioned for a silent exit. Coat over the chair, ready to be shrugged into in four seconds.

It took eleven minutes. It had taken eleven minutes every night for six years, in eleven towns and three countries. It was not fear. It was discipline. Fear was a guest; this was furniture.

Marguerite sat on the edge of the narrow bed—the mattress smelled faintly of lavender, which meant someone had thought about the comfort of strangers, which was a thought Marguerite had never once had about anyone—and reviewed the day the way a merchant reviews accounts.

*The innkeeper noticed the boots,* they wrote mentally. *Noted. She said nothing. Either she doesn't care, or she's holding the information. Determine which. The daughter is curious beyond safety—limit contact. The room has two exits if the shutter counts. The stew was excellent. Irrelevant. Strike that.*

But the entry would not strike. It sat there in the ledger of the mind, stubborn as ink: *the stew was excellent*, followed immediately by the image of Odile Ferrand saying *whether the people at my table ate*, and Marguerite recognized, with the weary clarity of long experience, the first symptoms of a disease they had contracted before and always survived.

The disease was called *almost belonging*. It presented as warmth in the chest. Its danger lay in the fact that it made a courier slow down. Made them unpack. Made them learn the name of the third shutter and the history of the stew. Made them, in the final and fatal stage, into someone whose disappearance would leave a mark—and marks were how people got found, and found was how people got arrested, and arrested was how—

Marguerite closed that ledger firmly.

There had been a family once, early on. A misdelivered letter. Names spoken aloud in a market where they shouldn't have been. But that was another chapter, filed in a drawer Marguerite did not open on the first night in a new town. Opening it required a darkness deeper than this one, and a reason.

The reason arrived sooner than expected.

Because as Marguerite reached to trim the candle, they saw it: tucked into the frame of the mirror above the washstand, a scrap of paper, folded once. A note, in a round, unhurried hand:

*Third shutter sticks from the OUTSIDE. If you leave through the window, push it shut after you — I won't have the cold getting in on my account. — O.F.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*There's more stew if you're hungry in the night. No charge. No questions either. The two are related.*

Marguerite sat very still.

In six years, no one had ever noticed them leaving through a window, because no one had ever noticed them at all—that had been the entire architecture of their life, load-bearing wall of it. Invisibility was the trade. Silence was the currency. You gave up being seen and in exchange you got to keep breathing.

And this woman had looked straight through the disguise in a single evening, priced it correctly, and responded not with blackmail or alarm but with *a note about the draft.*

Warmth offered without conditions. Marguerite turned the note over as if the reverse might contain the trick, the invoice, the hook. Blank. Just the lavender smell of the paper and the ghost of a thumbprint near the fold.

They should burn it. Burning was procedure; procedure was survival.

Instead, Marguerite folded it along its existing crease and put it inside the coat pocket—the coat that hung ready for flight, the coat that now contained one item that was not escape equipment. One small, folded piece of evidence that somewhere in the world, a stranger had looked at them and asked for nothing.

Outside, the third shutter knocked against its latch, patient as a pulse.

And far off to the north, past the dark hills, past the sleeping garrison, on a road Marguerite had been watching since dusk—so faint it might have been thunder deciding whether to become weather—came the first drums.
