## 4. Drums on the North Road

The first sign was the birds.

Marguerite noticed them before anyone in Saint-Aubin did—starlings lifting off the north fields in a single dark sheet, wheeling once, and settling again further east, as though the whole sky had flinched. They had spent enough years reading landscapes the way other people read letters to know that birds did not move like that for weather. Birds moved like that for something walking beneath them.

They were at the market square, ostensibly comparing the price of lamp oil for the inn, actually counting the garrison's wagons. There had been eleven two days ago. This morning there were seven, and three of those were being loaded with crates that clinked in a way Marguerite's ear recognized as *documents*, not dishes.

"Well," said the oil merchant, squinting north with his hand shading his eyes, "that's a lot of geese."

"That's a lot of geese," Marguerite agreed, and paid him, and walked home slowly so as not to look like someone who had just seen the horizon change its mind.

* * *

By noon, the outriders were visible from the church tower, which meant they were visible from Odile Ferrand's kitchen window, which meant that by the time Marguerite arrived back at the inn, the entire town knew and had arranged itself into the traditional postures of a town about to be requisitioned: denial, gossip, prayer, and hoarding.

"The Vaneau boy says he counted forty horses," Sylvie reported breathlessly, meeting Marguerite at the door as though she'd been posted there. She probably had. "Forty! And Captain Roux has ordered the garrison to strike the colors at dusk."

"Has he," said Marguerite.

"And Madame Cheval says the prefect's clerk left this morning with two trunks and his mistress and didn't even pay his tab at the bakery, which is very like him, Madame Cheval says he's been a coward since he was in swaddling—"

"Sylvie." Odile's voice came from the kitchen, calm as a level surface. "If you're going to report the news, report it standing still. You're wearing a groove in my floor."

Sylvie stopped, vibrating. Marguerite slipped past her into the kitchen, where Odile was doing something violent and methodical to a cabbage.

"You've heard, then," Marguerite said.

"I've heard." The knife came down. "Captain Roux came by personally an hour ago. Asked if I could feed twelve officers tonight and twenty dragoons tomorrow morning. I told him I could feed whoever could pay, which is more charity than most of us are feeling at the moment." Another stroke of the knife. "You'll want to know that he also asked after you. The clerk from the survey office. He wanted to know when you'd be leaving south."

Marguerite kept their face in the pleasant, unremarkable shape it lived in. "And what did you tell him?"

"I told him your business wasn't finished." Odile looked up, and there was something in her eyes that Marguerite did not enjoy—a directness, a concern with nowhere to hide. "Was that wrong?"

"No," Marguerite said, which was true in the way that most useful lies are built on true foundations. Their business was not finished. Their business, in fact, had just become a great deal less finished than they would have liked.

Because there had been no word from Vasseur. Four days now since the last dead-drop letter: *Hold position. Await final instructions.* Four days of holding position in a town whose garrison was packing its wagons, while the final instructions presumably sat somewhere in a dispatch case traveling at the speed of a tired horse in the opposite direction.

Marguerite climbed to their room under the eaves, took out the little tin of sewing supplies that contained no sewing supplies, and read the letter again for the dozenth time. *Hold position. Await final instructions.* Vasseur's hand, cramped and precise. No contingency. No extraction. No *if nothing comes by such-and-such a date, do thus.*

Just: wait. In Saint-Aubin. Which would be behind imperial lines within the week.

"Ah, Vasseur," Marguerite said softly to the ceiling beams. "You absolute monument of bureaucratic confidence."

The beams offered no reassurance. Beams rarely did.

* * *

That evening the burning began.

It started discreetly—the prefecture chimney smoking heavier than usual, a smell of scorch on the evening air that the town pretended not to notice—and then became less discreet, because discretion is expensive and panic is free. By full dark there were three fires going in the official quarter, and clerks were carrying armloads of paper into the flames with the grim industry of people who had spent years filing things and were now un-filing them forever.

Marguerite stood in the inn's doorway with a cup of Odile's cider, watching the sparks climb, and made their own quiet inventory. The registry ledger sat locked in the town records office with one ancient clerk as its guard. The garrison's cipher books would go into a fire or a wagon by dawn. The courier routes through the valley—*their* routes, drawn in their own careful shorthand—were copied in a dispatch box in Captain Roux's quarters, and Captain Roux was leaving.

And Marguerite's orders said *wait*.

"It's like watching a man burn his own house down around a guest," said Tomas the blacksmith, who had appeared beside them with his own cup and the easy silence of a man who didn't need conversation to keep company. He nodded at the fires. "Ten years of tax rolls. Every debt in the valley. Up in smoke."

"Somebody thought it mattered."

"Somebody always does." He drank. "Doesn't mean it mattered."

Marguerite glanced at him. Tomas was a broad, unhurried man with burn scars on one forearm and the kind of face that made strangers confess things on trains. Marguerite had spent two weeks carefully knowing nothing about him. Now, watching the papers curl in the firelight, they felt the strange weight of all that deliberate ignorance, like a coat grown suddenly too heavy.

"Your passes," Marguerite said, before they could stop themselves. "For hauling ore south. Are they good through the frontier, or just the valley?"

Tomas turned his head and looked at them for a long moment. Not suspiciously—that was the unsettling part. Thoughtfully, the way a man looks at a lock he's deciding whether to pick or break.

"Frontier," he said finally. "Good till the first of the month. Why?"

"No reason. A clerk likes to know where the roads go."

"Mm," said Tomas, who clearly did not believe this for a moment, and mercifully let it stand. That was the thing about Saint-Aubin, Marguerite was beginning to understand. People here saw everything and filed it under *none of my business*, which was either admirable community values or a catastrophic security failure, depending on which side of the ledger one stood.

From inside the inn came Sylvie's voice, rising over the supper crowd: "—and Papa says the last time soldiers came through, Grand-mère hid the good brandy in the church and the priest drank it himself, which is why we don't leave brandy with priests anymore—"

"Sylvie," said Odile, from somewhere deeper in, "the soup."

"—I'm *going* to the soup—"

Marguerite smiled into their cider before remembering not to. It was becoming a problem, that smile. It kept arriving without authorization.

* * *

At midnight, the drop had an answer for them.

Marguerite went out through the kitchen garden on the pretext of sleeplessness, which was close enough to the truth, and knelt by the loose stone at the wall's corner with fingers that were steadier than they deserved to be. The packet was small. Vasseur's seal. Marguerite carried it back up to their room, lit the candle stub low, and opened it with the particular care one reserves for documents that might be bad news.

It was bad news.

*Vasquez. Hold position. Final instructions delayed—courier line compromised west of Marres. Do not attempt south road independently; your cover is registered with the liaison office and unsanctioned movement will flag you. Await next drop, five days. Maintain clerk identity. Trust the local arrangements only as far as necessary.—V.*

Marguerite read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had been interrupted by a laugh that escaped before permission was granted—a short, bitter sound, quickly strangled.

Five days. The garrison would be gone in two. The imperial column would arrive behind the outriders within three. And Vasseur wanted them to sit in a requisitioned inn, maintaining a cover identity that would be worth precisely nothing the moment an imperial officer asked to see working papers, and *trust the local arrangements only as far as necessary*—which was Vasseur's way of saying: *don't get attached, don't get helped, don't be known.*

Too late, Vasseur. Six days too late, if one counted from the stew.

Marguerite fed the letter to the candle and watched the words curl. *Do not attempt the south road independently.* Of course. Because independence was the sin. A courier alone was an asset; a courier with allies was a liability, a leak, a chain of custody with human links. Marguerite had absorbed that doctrine so thoroughly it had become a personality, and here it was again, arriving five days late to a town falling down around them, instructing them to be invisible in a place where the innkeeper already knew, the blacksmith suspected, and the daughter—

The daughter asked too many questions. The daughter had opinions about everything and a memory like flypaper. The daughter was, by every professional measure, the single greatest threat to Marguerite's continued existence in Saint-Aubin.

And when Marguerite imagined the imperial column coming up the north road, the image their mind produced, unprompted, was of Sylvie's face in the window. That was new. That was unwelcome. That was exactly what Vasseur's doctrine existed to prevent.

"Maintain clerk identity," Marguerite murmured to the ash. "Yes, sir. Right away, sir."

Outside, the last of the official fires had burned down to orange embers, and the smell of ten years' paperwork hung over the town like rain that wouldn't fall.

* * *

Two mornings later, the drums came.

Not metaphorically—actually. Imperial cavalry did not sneak into towns; they announced themselves, because terror traveled better with percussion. Marguerite woke to the sound rolling down the north road, deep and unhurried, and lay still on the narrow bed listening to it approach the way one listens to a storm deciding whether to arrive.

Downstairs, the inn was silent in the wrong way. Not empty—the whole town seemed to have packed itself indoors—but hushed, the hush of a held breath. Odile stood at the window with a dish towel over her shoulder, motionless. Sylvie was pressed against the glass beside her, being quiet with tremendous effort, which was somehow louder than talking.

"They're stopping at the square," Sylvie whispered.

"I can hear that, love."

"There's so many horses."

"I can hear that too."

Marguerite came and stood at the window's edge, out of the sightline, and looked. The column filled the north road end to end—dragoons in grey-green, bayonets catching a flat morning light, supply wagons behind them, and at the head an officer on a chestnut horse who sat with the patient arrogance of a man collecting property rather than conquering it. Which, in fairness, was what he was doing. The Republic had signed away this valley in a room far from here; this officer was merely the delivery service.

"They're not looting," Tomas observed from the doorway, arms folded. "Yet."

"They won't loot," Marguerite said. "Looting is disorderly. They'll requisition. There's a difference."

"What difference?" Sylvie asked.

"Looting takes your things angrily," said Odile, turning back to her kitchen. "Requisitioning takes them politely, with receipts you can't cash. Come away from that window, both of you. If we're going to be occupied, we'll be occupied with breakfast on the table. Nothing says *this house is ordinary* like soup at the usual hour."

She said it lightly, but Marguerite caught the steel underneath, and caught too the glance Odile sent them—quick, appraising, gone again. *You especially,* that glance said. *Sit down. Eat soup. Be nobody.*

It should have been a relief, being managed by someone else's competence. Instead it lodged under Marguerite's ribs like a splinter, because Odile Ferrand had just added *protecting the boarder* to her morning list between bread and soup, and she hadn't asked whether Marguerite wanted protecting, and the terrible truth was that Marguerite did, and the more terrible truth was that wanting it made them slower, and slowness in the coming weeks would be measured in consequences.

Through the window, across the square, the town clerk was unlocking the records office door for a group of imperial staff officers. Marguerite watched them go in. Watched the door stay open a long time.

The registry ledger was in that building. Every name in the valley. Every classification. And somewhere in its courier index, in a neat administrative hand, an alias belonging to a clerk from the survey office who had orders to wait five days for instructions that might never come.

"Soup's on," said Odile.

Marguerite sat down at the table with the family, under the eyes of an occupying army, and ate.

* * *

That afternoon brought the evacuation's ugly tail-end. Captain Roux's rear guard rode out at four o'clock, and with them went the last pretense that Saint-Aubin was still anybody's but the empire's. A handful of officials left with the column—men who had spent the week burning paper and were now burning rubber on the road south, so to speak. The rest stayed, because staying is what people do: they have ovens and graves and children in churchyards, and armies do not understand any of these currencies.

Marguerite spent the evening doing what they did best: walking slowly and seeing everything.

The bridge checkpoint was being established already—two wagons, a plank barrier, six men with a brazier. A sergeant with a ledger of his own. At the customs house on the south road, lanterns burned late; new stamps, new seals, new rules being written in real time. And at the abbey above the town, lights moved in the guest wing, which was interesting, because the abbey had claimed for years that the guest wing was unsafe, uninhabitable, closed for repairs. Apparently repairs concluded promptly when soldiers needed billeting.

Everyone was choosing sides, or rather, everyone was discovering that the sides had chosen them.

Back at the inn, the common room was fuller than Marguerite had ever seen it—not with travelers, but with neighbors. People came to sit near other people when the world tilted; it was older than politics. Odile moved among the tables pouring thin wine and thick reassurance. Tomas sat in the corner mending a harness that did not need mending. Père Anselme was by the fire, telling a story about the last occupation that was apparently funnier the second time around, judging by the laughter.

And Sylvie was clearing dishes, and paused beside Marguerite's table just long enough to say, in a voice pitched carefully under the room's noise:

"You're leaving, aren't you? Before they finish taking the town."

Marguerite looked up. The girl's face was composed, almost stern, in the way of someone fifteen who has decided to practice being thirty.

"Clerks travel when their work travels," Marguerite said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best kind."

Sylvie set down the plates, and for a moment her eyes were not fifteen at all. "My mother says everyone in this room is going to need everybody else in this room, soon. She says it like it's arithmetic." A half-shrug. "I just think you should know you're in the sum."

She went off toward the kitchen before Marguerite could construct anything adequate, which was fortunate, because nothing adequate existed.

Upstairs, Marguerite checked the drop stone one last time—empty; of course empty; day two of five—and stood a while at the dark window, looking north along the road the drums had come by, then south along the road the instructions would have to come from.

Wait for a drop. Trust no one. Be no one.

Below, through the floorboards, the sound of the household settling: Odile banking the fire, Tomas's boots on the stair, Sylvie's laugh at something unheard, bright and brief and utterly uncoded.

Five days, Vasseur had written.

Marguerite had a bad feeling, growing by the hour, that the ledger in the records office would not wait nearly so long—and that neither, whatever Vasseur believed, would the names inside it.
