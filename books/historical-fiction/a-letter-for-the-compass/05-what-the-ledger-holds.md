## 5. What the Ledger Holds

The requisition began, as all disasters in Saint-Aubin seemed to begin, with a knock at the inn's door that was really more of a kick.

Marguerite was in the kitchen pretending to inventory Odile's wine stores—a task they had invented for themselves two days earlier because a clerk with nothing to do attracted less attention than a clerk with something to hide—and so they heard everything: the kick, the scrape of hobnails on the flagstones, and Odile's voice rising in that particular register she kept for officials, warm as fresh bread but with an iron core you could crack a tooth on.

"Gentlemen. You'll wipe your boots or you'll stand outside, and I don't care whose uniform that is."

A pause. Then, muffled, sheepish: "Apologies, madame."

Marguerite set down the wine ledger—Odile's real one, which was more honestly kept than anything the Republic had ever produced—and moved to the kitchen doorway where they could see the front room without being seen from it. Old habit. They had a whole repertoire of doorways by now, catalogued like exits: the kitchen arch, the stair shadow, the gap behind the coat pegs where a person of moderate thinness could vanish entirely.

Three imperial officers stood in the entryway, actually wiping their boots on the mat, which told Marguerite something important about Saint-Aubin's new administration before anyone said a word. Armies that kicked doors did not apologize to innkeepers. Armies that apologized were planning to be here a long time, and wanted the town quiet about it.

The one in front held a folded paper bearing a wax seal the color of dried blood.

"Madame Ferrand. By order of General Vasseur, commanding the Southern Occupation District, this town is hereby placed under requisition governance. All municipal records are to be surrendered to the office of the intendant by evening bell." He said it the way men read weather reports. "The registry ledger especially."

Something shifted in Marguerite's chest—not fear exactly, not yet. More the sensation of a floorboard giving slightly underfoot. The registry ledger. Every soul in Saint-Aubin, entered in a clerk's careful hand: names, trades, households, births, deaths, debts, allegiances noted where known. The town's memory, bound in calfskin, sitting in the town clerk's office not forty paces from where they stood.

"Records," Odile repeated, in the tone of a woman being asked to surrender her soup pot. "You've come for *paper*."

"The Republic burned what it could when its garrison left," the officer said. "What remains, we keep."

"Well," said Odile, "I hope your general enjoys reading about drainage assessments," and Marguerite had to turn back to the wine shelf and pretend very hard to count bottles, because if they had been visible just then their face would have done something dangerous.

Sylvie appeared at the top of the stairs, seventeen years old and constitutionally incapable of letting a scene happen without her in it. "Are they arresting someone?"

"Sylvie," Odile said, without turning around, in the voice mothers use to mean *go upstairs*, and Sylvie, who could read that voice fluently even though she pretended otherwise, went—but slowly, backwards, taking in every detail of the officers' uniforms with the frank appetite of a girl who intended to describe all of it later at length.

The officers left. The door closed. Odile came into the kitchen, took down a bottle of the good cider without asking anyone's permission, and poured three cups, because she had already counted who was in the house.

"They want the ledger," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question, but saying it aloud made it heavier.

"Everyone wants everyone's paper these days." Odile drank. "My husband used to say a man's whole life fits in a drawer, and whoever holds the drawer holds the man. I thought he was being gloomy. He was being *accurate*."

Marguerite wrapped both hands around the cup and did not drink. They were doing arithmetic—the kind they were good at, the kind that kept people alive. A ledger in imperial hands was a list. A list was a schedule. And somewhere between those two words lay a road south out of Saint-Aubin that would soon be closed to certain names on it.

They needed to see that ledger.

Which meant, some treacherous part of them noted, that they needed to go back to the town offices they'd spent three days carefully avoiding, in the company of the family they'd spent eleven days carefully not belonging to, and do it soon.

"I should report to the requisition office," Marguerite said, setting down the cup. "Clerks will be wanted for the record transfers. It's expected."

"Of course it is," Odile said, and looked at them for a moment longer than the sentence required—that look of hers, the one that saw straight through the clerk's coat to whatever was underneath, and filed it away without comment, the way she filed away everything, including, Marguerite suspected, the exact number of times Marguerite had lied to her, which currently stood at a figure Odile found either amusing or pitiable, possibly both.

"Take the east lane," she added. "The soldiers are looting Broussard's bakery on the square and you don't want to be asked to help carry anything."

* * *

Saint-Aubin under requisition governance looked, in its first six hours, almost exactly like Saint-Aubin under the Republic, which Marguerite found more unsettling than any amount of marching boots. Flags changed; paperwork did not. The same clerks sat at the same desks in the same municipal office, now with imperial armbands over their Republican ink stains, transferring the town's memory from one master's drawer to another with the weary competence of people who had learned that regimes come and go but ledgers are forever.

Marguerite presented themselves as Clerk Vasquez, seconded from the prefecture, available for record work. The imperial sergeant in charge—a broad, pink-faced man named Hessler who smelled of pipe smoke and had the eyes of someone who found his work neither interesting nor uninteresting—glanced at their papers, stamped them, and pointed at a table.

"You. Registry. Copy the household entries into the classification book. Names stay in the original order. Don't skip."

And there it was.

The ledger lay open on the table before them, and Marguerite understood, with the cold, settling clarity of ice forming on a pond, that they were looking at the most dangerous object in the valley.

It was not beautiful. That was the first thing. It was a fat, grimy, well-used book with a cracked spine and coffee rings on its corners, the kind of object that had absorbed twenty years of small-town grievance. But the entries—ah, the entries. In old Clerk Mercier's patient hand: *Ferrand, Odile, widow. Innkeeper. Household of two.* And beside it, in a newer, thinner ink, added within the last year by someone with less patience and more authority, a column of classifications Marguerite recognized from the Republic's own internal circulars, the ones that never used the word *deportation* because the circulars' authors preferred words with fewer teeth.

*Removable.*

Marguerite's finger hovered above the line and did not touch it. Around them the room scratched and rustled—pens, pages, the sergeant's boots crossing to the window and back—and none of it existed. There was only the column, and the word, and the name.

*Removable.* As if the Ferrands were a stain that could be sponged out of Saint-Aubin. As if Odile's stew and Sylvie's questions and the room with the crooked shutter where Marguerite slept were items in an inventory, subject to removal upon notice.

They copied entries. Their hand did not shake, which surprised them; seven years of training will do that, will teach the body to keep writing while the mind stands in the street screaming. *Broussard, Alain, baker. Removable.* *Anselme, Père Étienne, priest, retired. Removable — clergy.* *Tomas Ravel, blacksmith. Under review.* Page after page, and the thin-ink column marched alongside them like a silent escort, marking this family and that, the miller, the schoolmistress, the widow Aubry with her three daughters, a great quiet constellation of *removable* scattered through the town's ordinary firmament, and Marguerite copied each one faithfully because that was the job, because refusing would draw attention, because the cruelest thing about working for governments was that the work itself was always reasonable right up until it wasn't.

Somewhere in the middle of the F's, a thought arrived that they had been declining to think since the officers knocked.

Their own alias was in this book.

Not under Vasquez—Vasquez was a courier cover, and courier covers lived in a different index entirely, one kept in the prefecture, sealed, supposedly sacred. But the alias they wore here, the clerk's identity with its forged letters of secondment, had been built on a foundation of manufactured history: a birth record, a tax entry, a lodging registration from a town two hundred miles north. Paper trails had to root somewhere, and Marguerite had rooted theirs in municipal soil, the way all couriers did. Which meant that when the imperial clerks finished consolidating records—if they consolidated thoroughly, and imperial clerks were famously thorough—

Marguerite wrote *Ferrand* in the classification book, copied *removable* beside it, and felt the word go through them like a nail through cloth.

Odile. Removable. Sylvie. Removable.

The girl who counted candles. The woman who fed strangers without asking why they were strange. Marked in thin ink by some functionary who had never tasted her bread, in a town they'd probably never seen, on the authority of a general whose entire acquaintance with Saint-Aubin consisted of its position on a map.

Marguerite turned the page and kept copying, and inside the place where other people kept their hearts, something quietly changed shape. It was not yet a plan. Plans required exits, routes, contingencies, and Marguerite had none of those yet—only the ledger, only the word, only the growing, unfamiliar heat of a feeling they hadn't permitted themselves in years.

It took them until sundown to identify it, sitting at that table with ink on their fingers and the whole town's doom flowing through their pen.

It was anger. Clean, specific, and entirely unprofessional.

* * *

They walked home the long way, past the square, where Broussard's bakery stood with its shutters hanging and its ovens cold, and past the church, where Père Anselme was sweeping the steps with the unhurried defiance of a man who had outlasted three governments and intended to sweep through a fourth. He nodded to Marguerite. Marguerite nodded back. Neither of them mentioned that priests marked *removable* generally preferred not to advertise their continued existence, and Anselme sweeping his steps in full view of the patrols was either courage, stubbornness, or a message—and Marguerite was too tired to decode which.

At the inn, light spilled gold across the yard, and the smell of onions reached them before the door did, and Marguerite stood in the gathering dark for a moment longer than necessary, looking at that lit window the way a drowning man looks at a shore.

Then they went in, because standing in yards looking at windows was the behavior of people with attachments, and attachments, they reminded themselves, were how families got arrested.

The reminder landed differently than usual. It landed like the ledger's thin ink: accurate, and unbearable.

Inside, Sylvie was holding court at the table, narrating the day's occupation to her mother with the relish of a war correspondent. "—and then Sergeant What's-His-Name, the pink one, he made old Mercier carry the tax rolls himself, all four boxes, and Mercier's back gave out on the stairs and the sergeant just *stood there*—"

"Mercier's back has been giving out since before you were born," Odile said, ladling stew.

"It gave out *dramatically*. There was a sound."

"There is always a sound with Mercier."

Marguerite sat down in their usual place—the end of the bench, nearest the door, a seat chosen for exit rather than comfort—and accepted the bowl Odile put in front of them without meeting her eyes, because Odile's eyes had a way of asking questions that Marguerite's face answered too honestly.

"Was it awful?" Sylvie demanded. "The records office. Was everyone weeping?"

"Everyone was copying," Marguerite said. "Copying doesn't leave much room for weeping."

"What's in the ledger? Really. Everyone says it's just names but nobody says it like it's *just* names."

"Sylvie," Odile began.

"No, I want to know. It's our town. It's got my name in it, probably misspelled, Mercier spells everything wrong—" She stopped, because Marguerite had gone still, and Sylvie Ferrand missed nothing that went still.

"Just names," Marguerite said, and heard their own voice come out level and empty, the courier's voice, the platform voice. "Trades. Households. Births and deaths. Nothing worth weeping over."

The lie sat on the table between them like a fourth cup nobody drank from. Odile looked at Marguerite across the steam of the stew, and her expression was not suspicion—it was worse than that. It was patience. The patience of a woman who had buried a husband and raised a daughter and fed a hundred strangers, and who knew precisely what a person looked like when they were carrying something too heavy to put down and too proud to ask for help with.

"Eat," she said simply. "It's getting cold."

Marguerite ate. The stew was good, as it was always good, and that was somehow the worst part—that the world could contain a ledger with the word *removable* written beside the name of the woman who made it, and the stew would still be good, and the candle would still burn, and Sylvie would still be arguing about Mercier's spelling, and none of it would stop.

Later, when the dishes were done and Sylvie had gone up grumbling about curfews, Marguerite stood at their window with the crooked shutter and watched the imperial sentries make their rounds below, lanterns swinging, breath steaming in the October air. Somewhere in the requisitioned records office, the ledger lay closed on a table, waiting for morning, when the classification clerks would return to it and begin, methodically, to turn *removable* into *scheduled*.

Eleven days, Marguerite had promised themselves when they first took this assignment. Eleven days, one delivery, no entanglements. The oldest rule, learned in blood: know the exits, love nothing, leave first.

Below, a sentry stumbled on the cobbles and swore, and above him, in the window across the yard, Sylvie's candle flickered as she read in bed, and Marguerite caught themselves counting the seconds of her light the way they once counted patrol intervals—as if it were intelligence. As if it mattered operationally whether the girl at the window read for a quarter hour or half of one.

It didn't matter. It couldn't matter.

But Marguerite stood there anyway, watching the candle, and did not begin packing, and understood—with the cold clarity of the ledger still fresh behind their eyes—that the rule was cracking, and that whatever came next would require more than exits and blank faces.

It would require stealing a book from an army.

They blew out their own candle and sat down in the dark to start planning.
