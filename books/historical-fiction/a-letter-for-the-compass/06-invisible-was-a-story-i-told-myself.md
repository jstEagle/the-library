## 6. Invisible Was a Story I Told Myself

Marguerite did not sleep.

They told themselves this was ordinary—that sleeplessness was simply another tool of the trade, like cipher ink and a good pair of walking boots—but by the third hour of watching the ceiling beams shift in the dark, they had to admit the truth was less tidy. The truth was a single line of ink, seen for perhaps four seconds through a crack in a cabinet door, and it had lodged itself behind their ribs the way a splinter lodges under a fingernail: small, invisible to everyone else, and impossible to stop touching with the mind's tongue.

*Courier Index — Southern Circuit.*

They had seen it in passing, one page among dozens as the imperial clerk flipped through the town registry looking for deportation classifications. Marguerite had been crouched behind the records office door with a stolen broom in their hands, playing at sweeping, playing at being nobody—and the clerk had turned a page, and the lamplight had caught a column of names, and one of those names had been theirs.

Not *Marguerite Vasquez*. No one in Valcourt had written that name down in seven years. But *Mireille Fontaine*, junior clerk, Saint-Aubin posting—the alias they had built plank by plank over three years, the alias with its invented childhood in Lyon, its invented dead parents, its invented limp on cold mornings that made soldiers dismiss them as harmless—the alias had been there, in a Republic hand, in a Republic ledger, sold along with everything else.

Marguerite lay in the narrow bed above the inn's kitchen and stared at the beams and thought: *I am a line item.*

It was almost funny. Almost. They had spent seven years learning how to be unnoticeable, and the Republic had gone and made them noticeable from a hundred leagues away, in an office they'd never entered, to men they'd never met. Somewhere in a surrender tent, some exhausted official had slid a folder across a table and said, *here are our people in your territory,* and somewhere an imperial colonel had said, *how thoughtful,* and that had been that. The whole architecture of Marguerite's survival—every careful habit, every rehearsed smile, every exit mapped and memorized—had been folded up like a map and handed to the enemy with the rest of the baggage.

They got up before dawn. Not because they had a plan—they had only the ragged beginning of one—but because lying still felt too much like waiting, and waiting felt too much like standing on a platform watching a train leave without them.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and yesterday's bread. Odile Ferrand was already at the hearth, sleeves rolled, stirring something that would become porridge whether it wished to or not. She glanced up when Marguerite came down and did not ask why they looked like a person who had wrestled with the night and lost.

"You slept badly," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"The bed's too soft," Marguerite lied automatically, and then heard how absurd that was—the bed was famously hard; Sylvie complained about it daily—and Odile's mouth twitched at the corner in a way that suggested she'd noticed the absurdity too and was choosing, out of some private mercy, not to say so.

"Sit," Odile said. "Eat. Sylvie will be down in a moment and if you don't eat first you'll never get any."

Marguerite sat. This was the trouble with Odile Ferrand, they were beginning to understand: she issued instructions that sounded like kindness and kindnesses that sounded like instructions, and both arrived without conditions attached, and Marguerite had no protocol for that. Every gift they had ever received came with a ledger of its own—who gave it, what it bought, what it would cost later. Odile's stew seemed to come from nowhere. It unsettled Marguerite more than the imperial army did.

"Mireille." Odile set the bowl down and stayed standing over them, which she rarely did. "The officers came by last evening. While you were out."

Something in Marguerite went very still and very smooth, the way water goes smooth over deep places. "Out?"

"Fetching thread. You said." Odile's eyes were mild and missed nothing. "They wanted billets confirmed. And they asked about you."

"About me."

"They asked how long the clerk had been lodging here. I said since the autumn, that you kept to yourself, that clerks are dull as ditchwater." She picked up her spoon and began stirring the porridge again, apparently unconcerned. "The lieutenant wrote it down. He has a face like a man who writes things down for a living and enjoys it, which I find is worse than a face like a man who enjoys shouting."

"What did you tell him about—" Marguerite stopped. Started again. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Odile tapped the spoon against the pot's rim, once, twice. "I've been keeping an inn for nineteen years, girl. I know what a lie looks like when someone hands it to me pre-wrapped. Yours was very neatly wrapped." She looked at Marguerite then, directly, and there was no accusation in it—only a kind of tired, clear patience. "I'm not asking what's inside it. I'm telling you that if the wrapping ever needs to be thicker, you come to me. I have wrapped a great many things in my life."

Marguerite's throat did something inconvenient. They ate their porridge and did not answer, and Odile let the silence stand between them like a set table—there, offered, waiting to be used or not.

From upstairs came the thump of Sylvie's feet hitting the floorboards, followed by the muffled crash of a washstand and a word that would have gotten her mouth washed out with soap in a stricter household. Odile sighed with the weariness of long practice.

"That one," she said, "was born asking questions. I keep hoping she'll grow out of it. Her father hoped so too." A pause, brief as a blink. "He was wrong about a great many hopeful things."

* * *

Sylvie appeared mid-morning, ostensibly to collect eggs, actually to interrogate Marguerite about whether the capital really had gas lamps on every corner and whether clerks could read anything they liked or only boring things. Marguerite answered in the flattest register available, giving nothing away, and watched Sylvie's quick dark eyes catalog every evasion with the frank delight of a child who has found a locked box and intends to own it someday.

"You dodge questions like Papa used to dodge the tax assessor," Sylvie announced, hugging the egg basket. "He'd go out the back and come in the front and swear he'd been in the front the whole time."

"A useful skill," Marguerite said.

"It's a funny skill," Sylvie said. "It means everyone knows where you're going except you."

She skipped off toward the coop, leaving that sentence hanging in the air like laundry no one would take in. Marguerite stood in the yard with the cold wind pulling at their collar and thought, with a sensation like the floor tilting, that a fourteen-year-old had just described their entire adult life more accurately than seven years of self-examination had managed.

Then they put it away, because there was work, and the work was this: they needed to see that page again.

* * *

Getting back into the requisitioned records office was easier than getting out would be, but neither was easy, and Marguerite spent the afternoon assembling the pieces with the grim contentment of a person doing the one thing they were genuinely good at. The office—a squat stone building that had housed the town's tax assessor before the war made liars of all paperwork—now held two imperial clerks, a sergeant who smoked by the side door at predictable intervals, and a lock old enough to have opinions. Marguerite observed all of it from three angles: once carrying a message for Odile past the front window, once drawing water from the well across the lane, once simply sitting on a low wall eating an apple with the studied vacancy of a person with nowhere to be and no thoughts in their head.

Invisibility, they had learned, was mostly patience wearing plain clothes.

By late afternoon they had the shape of it: the sergeant smoked at the half-hour; the clerks took supper at six and left one lantern burning; the side door's lock was older than the front and had been jimmied before by someone with less care, judging by the scratches. The registry cabinets stood against the east wall. The courier index was the third drawer down.

Marguerite waited for full dark. Waited for the sergeant's smoke. Went over the wall in the blue-black hour when even dogs give up on ambition, crossed the lane in nine steps, and had the side door open with a bent knife and forty seconds of coaxing.

Inside, the office smelled of lamp oil, cold stone, and other men's tobacco. The lantern guttered. Marguerite crossed to the east wall, eased open the third drawer, and lifted out the courier index with both hands, the way one lifts a thing that might be armed.

The pages were close-written, column after column, in a Republic clerk's cramped hand. Names—aliases, real names beside them in smaller script. Routes. Dead drops. Codes of conduct. Seven years of other people's careful invisibility, bound in grey card and filed like grain receipts.

Marguerite ran one finger down the southern circuit and found themselves.

*MIREILLE FONTAINE — jr. clerk, Saint-Aubin. Cover: civil registry assistant. True name: VASQUEZ, M. Recruited 1787. Handler: DURET, C. Notes: reliable. Does not form attachments. Recommend for extended deployment.*

*Does not form attachments.*

They read it twice. Three times. There was a horrible comedy in it, somewhere far away—some clerk in the capital had evaluated Marguerite's loneliness and stamped it *reliable*, had weighed their isolation and found it fit for service, and now that same isolation was a mark against them, because the imperial officers reading this page would be looking for exactly that: a person alone. A person with no family to vouch for them, no roots, no reason to stay. A ghost with a paper trail.

And beneath the comedy, colder: *Handler: Duret.* Camille Duret, who had taught Marguerite cipher work over chess games in a Lyon back room; who had said, the day they finished training, *the best courier is the one nobody remembers meeting*; who had signed the evaluation that made Marguerite's emptiness into a professional qualification. Duret, who was somewhere north now, presumably, negotiating surrender terms, trading names for his own neck.

*Reliable,* Marguerite thought. *That's what he'd say about me too. Reliable. Does not form attachments. Recommend for disposal.*

Their hands were shaking. They noticed it with detached interest, the way one notices weather in another country.

Because here was the arithmetic, laid out in cramped ink: the ledger downstairs—the deportation registry—listed the Ferrands as removable. And this book, the courier index, listed *Mireille Fontaine* as findable. Two books, two lists, and Marguerite's name threaded through both: marked for removal as a resident, marked for capture as a spy, and the two marks together meant that no disguise, no flat voice, no rehearsed smile would save them. The Republic had sold the story of who Marguerite pretended to be, and the Empire had bought it, and stories sold in wartime do not come with returns accepted.

They copied the relevant lines into memory—not onto paper, never onto paper—and closed the drawer, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the lantern hiss, feeling the vertigo rise like floodwater.

This was the thing about being invisible: you told yourself it was armor. You told yourself that a blank face and a borrowed name were protections, and they were, they truly were—seven years of proof of that, seven years of trains departed safely and checkpoints passed and families arrested instead of yours because you delivered the letter and left and never learned their names. But armor is heavy, and you forget you're wearing it, and eventually you look down and realize the armor has grown into the skin, and you cannot remember what your own face looks like underneath.

*I am Mireille Fontaine,* Marguerite thought, and the thought rang false as a cracked bell. *I am Marguerite Vasquez,* and that rang worse, because who had used that name lately? Who would recognize it? A name unused is just letters. A person unseen for seven years is just—

Just a line item. Reliable. Does not form attachments.

Upstairs, in a hard-bedded room above a warm kitchen, a woman had wrapped a lie for them without asking what was inside, and a girl had told them they dodged like a tax-dodger, and a bowl of porridge had been set before them with no price attached, and Marguerite had taken all of it the way one takes coins from a stranger's tray—quickly, without meeting anyone's eyes, already calculating the exit.

And tomorrow, or soon, the imperial officers would cross-reference their files against the town's residents, and some lieutenant with a face like a man who enjoyed writing things down would arrive at the inn with a question, and *does not form attachments* would become *has formed none we can use*, and the story Marguerite had built to survive would be the exact shape of their grave.

Unless the book stopped existing.

Marguerite stood in the cold office and let the plan assemble itself, piece by piece, the way it always did—in silence, alone, with no one told and no one risked and no one to grieve if it failed. That was the discipline. That was the armor. Steal the index first, burn it or bury it; then the registry ledger; then south, alone, through three checkpoints, trusting no one, owing no one.

The old plan. The reliable plan.

Through the window, across the square, the inn's windows glowed yellow against the dark—one light in the kitchen still burning, though the hour was late, and Marguerite knew without looking whose silhouette that was, moving steadily behind the glass, banking the fire, setting bread to rise for people who might be deported within the week and baked anyway, because that was what Odile Ferrand did: she fed people, fed them past all sense, fed them as if bread could argue with empires.

Marguerite looked at that light for a long time.

*Recommend for extended deployment,* the file said. *Does not form attachments.*

"Well," Marguerite murmured to the empty office, to the dead clerk who had written it, to Camille Duret wherever he was counting his thirty pieces of whatever they paid traitors in, "your intelligence was excellent right up until it mattered."

They slipped out the way they'd come, drew the door shut, and vanished into the dark of the lane—with the index's contents burning behind their eyes, and a new problem rising ahead of them sharp and unwelcome as ice water:

Because the index could be burned. The ledger could be carried. But the file had said one more true thing, and Marguerite was only now, standing in the cold with the inn's light at their back, beginning to understand how dangerous it was:

Someone who does not form attachments can disappear. But someone who has just started forming them—

That person has something to lose. And Marguerite, mapping the route south in their head—the bridge, the abbey, the customs house, each checkpoint a mouth—realized with genuine horror that for the first time in seven years, the plan had a flaw no amount of solitude could fix:

They were already thinking about who they'd have to leave behind.

And they were already dreading it.
