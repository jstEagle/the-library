## 7. Three Checkpoints, One Road

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a courier learns before any other: the arithmetic of roads. Not distances—distances are for cartographers and poets—but the counting of eyes. Every road is a sum of gazes, and you must know, before you set your boot to it, exactly how many of those gazes will land on you and what each one will cost.

Marguerite did this arithmetic on the back of a laundry list.

It was Odile's laundry list, in fact, filched from the peg by the kitchen door because it was the only paper in the inn that would not be missed. On its reverse, in pencil worn down to a stub, Marguerite drew the road south: three lines, three knots, three checkpoints between Saint-Aubin and the neutral envoy waiting somewhere past the frontier like a promise nobody had bothered to keep.

*The bridge,* they wrote. *The abbey gate. The customs house.*

Then, beneath each, the sums.

The bridge first. The Pont de Varenne was the only crossing within thirty miles that could bear a cart, which meant every soul leaving the town by the southern road funneled through it like grain through a millstone. Imperial outriders had already planted themselves at the far end—Marguerite had watched them from the churchyard wall that morning, two sentries and a lieutenant with the bored posture of a man who intended to make boredom everyone else's problem. A checkpoint of documents. Names against lists. And Marguerite's name, or rather the name that wasn't theirs, was now on an imperial list, sold south with the rest of the Republic's surrender terms like a crate of spoiled wine.

*Cost:* papers. *Risk:* moderate, if the lists haven't reached him yet. *Likelihood the lists have reached him yet:* —here Marguerite paused, pencil hovering—*high. They were sold on Tuesday. It is Friday. Bureaucracy is slow but not that slow.*

They wrote *moderate* anyway. There was no point in being honest with oneself about everything; a person needed at least one comfortable lie per day, the way a body needs salt.

* * *

The abbey was the second knot, and the harder one to think about, because thinking about the abbey meant thinking about Sylvie Ferrand, and Marguerite had made a private resolution—somewhere between discovering their own alias in the ledger and failing to sleep—that the Ferrands would appear nowhere in this plan except as people to be protected from it.

The Abbey of Saint-Clair sat astride the old pilgrim road, half ruin, half fortress, wholly inconvenient. Its gate was the only passage through the ridge for anyone on foot, and since the requisition the imperial provost had stationed a sergeant and six men there, ostensibly to search for deserters, actually to search for anything at all, because searching gave soldiers something to do besides drink. But abbeys had doors that predated armies. Père Anselme—the old priest who said Mass to eleven people and dozed through his own sermons—had once, in a different century apparently, been a young man who knew the abbey's bones. There was talk, overheard in fragments Marguerite had collected the way other people collected buttons, of a stair behind the crypt. Of a time when monks moved between the abbey and the river valley without troubling the front gate.

Marguerite wrote: *Abbey. Front gate = death. Possible stair = rumor.* Then, after a moment: *Rumors are how couriers die. Verify or discard.*

And beneath that, in smaller letters, as though smallness could contain the thought: *Do not ask Anselme. Asking means telling. Telling means the Ferrands.*

This was the discipline. This was the whole of the discipline, really, distilled: a plan with one person in it has one mouth to betray it. A plan with five has five. Marguerite had learned this lesson at nineteen, in a rented room in another town that no longer existed under its old name, holding a letter that had been delivered to the wrong door, listening to boots on the stairs of a family who had never done anything worse than be kind to a stranger. One misdelivered envelope. Four arrests. Marguerite had carried the arithmetic of that night ever since, and it always came out the same: kindness multiplied by knowledge equals graves.

So: alone. The ledger, the route, the risk—all of it folded up small and carried in one pair of hands. It was cleaner that way. It was lonelier that way too, but loneliness was a currency Marguerite had plenty of, and unlike coin, it didn't weigh down a coat.

* * *

The customs house at the frontier was the third knot, and Marguerite saved it for last the way one saves the bitter end of a loaf—not out of appetite but because it must be eaten eventually.

It was the worst of the three by every measure. The customs house was not a checkpoint so much as a sieve: every traveler, every cart, every trunk and mattress and sack of turnips, all of it turned out and examined by clerks who answered to imperial intendants and who therefore had both the leisure and the incentive to be thorough. Documents were not glanced at there; they were read. Seals were tested. Handwriting was compared against registers. And somewhere in that building, Marguerite knew, sat the courier profiles—descriptions compiled over years by the Republic's own bureau, now transferred intact to the empire like furniture left in a seized house. Height. Build. Left-handedness. The habit of looking at exits. Marguerite's forged identity was good work—they had built it themselves, layer by layer, over seven years—but it had been built to survive Republican scrutiny, and Republican scrutiny had always been a sleepy dog. Imperial scrutiny was a different animal entirely, awake, fed, and trained on precisely the scent Marguerite carried.

*Cost:* everything. *Risk:* certain. *Conclusion:* the customs house cannot be passed as myself.

Which meant the ledger could not simply be carried. It had to be *become* something else—a widow's linens, a merchant's invoices, a coffin's lining—and the person carrying it had to be someone whose papers invited no comparison to any profile in any register. Someone ordinary. Someone whose face had never been catalogued by either government because neither government had ever considered them worth cataloguing.

Marguerite stared at that conclusion for a long while, pencil motionless, and then deliberately did not follow it where it led. Because where it led was to Odile Ferrand's kitchen, and to Sylvie's relentless questions, and to Tomas the blacksmith's requisition passes, and Marguerite had already ruled those doors shut. Ruling a door shut and not hearing it knock were two different labors, however, and Marguerite suspected the second would be the harder one.

* * *

On the third evening, Odile found the laundry list.

Not the plan side—the laundry side. Marguerite had returned it to its peg, plan inward, and believed the thing forgotten until Odile appeared in the doorway of the common room with the paper held up between two fingers like a dead mouse.

"You've written *boiled linen* twice," she said. "Nobody boils linen twice. Either you're hiding something or you can't count."

"Both," Marguerite said. "I'm a very thorough clerk."

"Mm." Odile looked at them a moment longer than the joke required. She had a way of doing that—of letting a silence run just past comfortable, as though she were checking a lock by rattling it gently. "There's stew on the stove. Sylvie's been guarding it from her father's ghost, which is to say from herself, so eat it before she negotiates you for the marrow bone."

She hung the list back on its peg, plan side in, and left. Marguerite sat very still and listened to her footsteps cross the kitchen, and told themselves the tightness in their chest was indigestion.

* * *

The plan, when Marguerite finally assembled it whole, went like this:

Night one: enter the requisitioned records office at the old town hall, where the ledger had been carried under guard along with the rest of the seized registry. Marguerite had walked past it four times now, at different hours, wearing different expressions, and had established three things: the ground-floor windows were barred, the rear door opened onto the tanner's yard and was locked with a lock older than the war, and the garrison posted inside numbered two men, one of whom slept, reliably, after the second bottle. The lock was the kind that yielded to patience and a thin blade of iron. Marguerite had patience. The blacksmith's scrap pile had iron.

Night one, continued: take the ledger, sewn into a satchel lining, and walk out of Saint-Aubin before dawn dressed as a poultry dealer, because poultry dealers were the one class of traveler no soldier searched thoroughly unless he wanted to smell like that for the rest of the day.

Day two: the bridge, papers shown, lies told, crossed before the morning list arrived.

Day two, continued: the ridge road on foot, around the abbey if the stair proved a myth, through it if it proved real—though it would not prove real, because proving it required asking Anselme, and asking Anselme required telling, and telling was the one door Marguerite had nailed shut with both hands.

Day three: the customs house, where the plan grew thin as winter light. Marguerite wrote and rewrote this section four times and each version ended in the same place: *someone else's face.* And each time, they struck it through and wrote instead: *solve later.*

Solve later. It was the courier's oldest prayer.

* * *

What the plan did not include, in any version, in any margin, in any code of Marguerite's own devising:

It did not include the way Odile had looked at the laundry list. It did not include the fact that Sylvie Ferrand asked questions the way rain falls—everywhere, relentlessly, finding every gap—and that some of those questions had lately begun to sound less like curiosity and more like reconnaissance. It did not include Tomas, who had taken to leaving a whetted file on the woodpile where Marguerite chopped kindling, saying nothing, in the manner of men who communicate entirely through hardware. It did not include Père Anselme, who had caught Marguerite studying the abbey roofline during Mass and had smiled the serene, terrible smile of a man who had hidden fugitives through two governments and fully expected to hide them through a third.

It did not include the ledger itself, and what it held. Marguerite made themselves not think about the page with the Ferrands on it. *Ferrand, Odile, widow. Ferrand, Sylvie, daughter. Classification: removable.* Removable. As though they were stains. As though seven years of stew and candlelight and a girl who counted candles aloud to fall asleep could be lifted off the world like wax.

Marguerite sat in their narrow room above the stable with the plan spread across their knees and the candle burning low, and performed the final sum. One courier. Three checkpoints. Eleven days, maybe fewer. Odds that could not be spoken aloud even in the privacy of one's own skull.

It was a bad plan. Marguerite knew it was a bad plan the way one knows a ladder is short—it would have to be climbed perfectly, without one wasted motion, without one tremor, and even then the top rung would leave a hand short of safety. But it was a plan with exactly one person in it, and that person had spent seven years becoming very difficult to kill, and difficult-to-kill was the only asset Marguerite had ever trusted.

Alone was armor. Alone had always been armor.

Marguerite copied the plan out fair, memorized it, and burned the draft in the candle flame, feeding it in inch by inch, watching the ink curl and vanish. The last corner to go was the section marked *customs house*, and just before the fire took it, the words *someone else's face* glowed briefly orange, like an ember refusing instruction.

Downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, came the sound of the household settling: Odile banking the kitchen fire, the clink of the stew pot being covered, Sylvie's voice running through some endless inventory of the day—*and then Madame Roux said, and I said, and then Tomas said*—a stream of narration aimed at no one and everyone, the sound of a family that talked to itself because it liked the sound.

Marguerite listened the way one listens to music in a language one almost speaks.

Then they pinched the candle, put the plan where plans live—in the locked room behind the eyes—and went down to steal an hour of sleep before the stealing began in earnest.

They did not hear the small feet on the stairs behind them, nor see the candlelight flicker under their own door a quarter hour later, where a girl with her mother's stubborn chin stood reading the shadows of a burned page and deciding, with the terrifying clarity of the young, that whatever Marguerite Vasquez was planning—

—she was not planning it alone anymore.
