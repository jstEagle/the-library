## 8. Sylvie Counts the Candles

Sylvie Ferrand had three rules about the lodger, and she had made them herself, which made them law.

Rule one: Marguerite Vasquez was not a clerk. Clerks did not walk like that. Clerks shuffled; they apologized to furniture. The lodger moved through rooms the way a cat moves through a kitchen—politely, and with a full accounting of every exit.

Rule two: whatever was in the leather satchel mattered more than anything else in Saint-Aubin, including the war, including supper.

Rule three: her mother knew, and her mother was pretending not to know, which meant Sylvie was permitted—no, obligated—to find out first.

So on the fourth night of drums on the north road, when Maman went down to bank the kitchen fire and the lodger's candle burned past midnight for the third time running, Sylvie took the creaky stair on its quiet side, the third step from the top skipped entirely, and pressed her eye to the gap where the door didn't quite meet its frame.

Marguerite sat at the little desk with their back to the door, hunched like a scholar, copying something in a hand so small it might have been embroidery. Three candles now. Sylvie counted them the way she counted everything—spoons, chickens, soldiers' boots passing the window—and three candles meant this was serious business indeed, because Marguerite pinched out spare flames the way other people pinched out bad habits.

Then the lodger turned the page, and Sylvie saw it: columns of letters that were letters and weren't, marching down the paper like ants who had all agreed to lie about being ants.

Cipher. She'd seen it once before, years ago, in a letter Papa had burned without reading aloud. She had never forgotten the shape of it. Secrets have shapes, and children learn shapes faster than anyone.

She should have gone back to bed.

Sylvie Ferrand had never once gone back to bed when she shouldn't have, and she did not intend to begin a new habit at seventeen.

She pushed the door open.

* * *

The sound Marguerite made was not a word in any language Sylvie knew, but she judged from context that it meant something like *my entire life is ending*, and she found this extremely satisfying.

"You're copying cipher keys," she said, shutting the door behind her with the soft click of someone who had done this before, thank you very much, mostly to the pantry.

"I'm—" Marguerite's hand came down over the page as though the paper might be embarrassed. "This is accounts."

"Accounts," Sylvie repeated. She crossed the room, picked up the second candle, and held it close enough to illuminate the ants. "These accounts are written backwards in threes and there is a number next to my mother's name."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Sylvie had learned silences from her mother, who could end arguments with one; but this was a different species altogether—a hunted silence, an animal-in-a-threshing-floor silence, and it frightened her more than the drums had.

"Sit down," Marguerite said at last, quietly. Not unkindly. That was somehow worse.

"I'll stand."

"Sit *down*, Sylvie." And then, because Marguerite Vasquez apparently could not help themselves even while terrified: "You skipped the third stair. It groans. If you're going to spy on people, learn your house."

Sylvie sat on the edge of the bed, chin up, heart going like a mill wheel. "What's the number next to Maman?"

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. In the candlelight their face did the thing it always did—the thing where it became a wall—but walls, Sylvie had decided long ago, were just doors that hadn't been knocked on hard enough.

"The ledger," Marguerite said finally. "The registry the imperial officers took from the town hall. It lists every soul in Saint-Aubin. What they did, what they believed, what they signed, what they said in front of the wrong person. And beside certain names—" their voice stayed level, which must have cost them, "—a classification. Removable."

"Removable," Sylvie said. "Like a stain."

"Yes."

"And ours has it."

Marguerite didn't answer. The answer was the whole room, the whole night, the whole careful way the lodger had begun eating faster since the drums started.

"Then we steal it back," Sylvie said.

"No."

"We steal it back and take it south—"

"*No.*" The word cracked like ice on a pond. Then Marguerite closed their eyes, breathed once, and said, in a voice smoothed flat again: "You will go back to bed. You will speak of this to no one, not even—especially not—to your mother. You saw nothing. You were asleep. Do you understand me? This is not a game, Sylvie. People who touch these papers disappear. I have watched it happen. To families. To girls your age."

Something cold walked up Sylvie's spine. But underneath the cold, warming itself by it like a cat by a stove, was the thought that Marguerite had said *I have watched it happen*, which meant they had stayed to watch, which meant they cared, which meant—

"You're going alone," Sylvie said.

"Sylvie."

"You're going *alone*, through three checkpoints, with papers that say you don't exist, to do a theft that gets people shot, and you told no one. Not Maman. Not Tomas. Nobody." She shook her head slowly, and to her own surprise she wasn't angry—she was sad, sad in the specific way she got looking at the lame goose they kept alive out of pity, the one that wouldn't come near the flock. "You were going to let us be taken away and never tell us why. You were going to save us *secretly*. Like a ghost doing chores."

"I am very good at what I do."

"I believe you," Sylvie said honestly. "That's the horrible part. You're so good at it you forgot there's another way to be."

* * *

Marguerite stood at the window with their back turned, and Sylvie watched them the way she watched weather—looking for the change.

She ought to be afraid. She was, a little. But under the fear ran something hotter and older, something that had been waiting seven years, ever since the day Papa died and the world showed her exactly how large and indifferent it was. She had spent seven years being small in a small town behind a small counter, pouring wine for soldiers of whichever army was current, and every single day some part of her had stood at the edge of herself and shouted *there must be more than this, there must be something I can actually do.*

Now here it was. Here it was, in cipher, by candlelight, in a rented room.

"My cousin Henriette entered the abbey at Valcourt two springs ago," Sylvie said, to Marguerite's shoulder blades. "Novice. They kept the cloister open even after the Republic stopped paying the monks, because the Mother Superior knows the imperial quartermaster from childhood, or claims to, and either way the soldiers don't search the alms carts. Everyone knows this. The nuns bring bread and bandages down to the bridge checkpoint twice a week, and the guards wave them through because searching novices is bad luck and worse manners."

Marguerite's shoulders had gone very still.

"The abbey sits between the bridge and the customs road," Sylvie went on, counting off the geography on her fingers because arithmetic steadied her, it was the one language that never lied. "You said checkpoints—bridge, abbey gate, customs house—I heard you and Père Anselme talking Tuesday, don't look surprised, the confessional wall is thinner than your door. The abbey is the middle lock on the whole route. And I can walk through it wearing a wimple."

"Absolutely not."

"Henriette owes me a favor and three letters she never answered. I have her old habit folded in the cedar chest, it still fits, I checked last winter out of boredom. I know the responses for the offices, Maman made me learn them during the plague summer when I was ten and convinced God owed us. I can carry a basket of alms through that gate with my heartbeat slower than yours is right now."

Marguerite turned around. In the candlelight their face had lost the wall entirely, and what was underneath was so nakedly alarmed that Sylvie almost laughed—almost, because it was also terrible, seeing how much fear a person could hold and still pour wine downstairs and say good evening.

"No," Marguerite said again, softer. "You don't understand what— if they catch you, they won't treat you as a courier. They'll treat you as leverage. A girl of seventeen caught carrying for the Republic becomes a way to make everyone around her confess to everything. Your mother. Tomas. The priest. One girl in a cart unravels this whole town, do you understand? That is precisely how it works. I know because I have *been* the leverage. I was recruited at sixteen."

The room went quiet.

"Sixteen," Sylvie repeated.

"They found me because I could keep secrets and I wanted desperately for that to matter to someone." Marguerite's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, more like a scar learning to move. "They promised me my silence was a service. It took me four years to understand that they simply owned it. So no. You will not put on a dead girl's habit and walk into a checkpoint because you're bored and brave. Bravery is cheapest for people who haven't yet seen the bill."

Sylvie sat with that. She was aware, dimly, that she was being handed the truth of another human being across a rented bed, wrapped in scolding, and that most adults would rather die than unwrap such a thing.

"Maman says you eat like someone apologizing to the food," she said.

"That's unrelated."

"It's completely related. You've been apologizing for existing since before I met you. Well." Sylvie stood up. She was tall for the family; she took after Grandmother, who had reportedly once stared down a tax collector until he apologized. She used the full height now. "Here is what I think. I think you're going to do this whether anyone helps you or not, because you don't know any other way. And I think the plan dies at the abbey gate without me, because you cannot pass for a novice no matter how good your forged papers are—you have the face of someone who has never prayed sincerely in their life."

"That is slander."

"It's *observation*. And I think"—her voice wobbled, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway—"I think if you go alone and you're caught, then Maman loses everything anyway, plus you, and she sets another place at the table for you the way she does for Papa, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I sat on this bed and offered and you said no because protecting us felt safer than letting us protect you back."

Marguerite stared at her.

Outside, far off on the north road, the drums went on, patient as tide.

"You count things," Marguerite said at last, oddly. "Spoons, boots, candles. Why?"

"So I know what I have," Sylvie said. "Maman taught me. When Papa died we counted what was left and it was enough, barely, but you can't know a thing is enough unless you count it."

Marguerite looked at the three candles. Looked at the cipher pages. Something moved behind their eyes—Sylvie would remember this all her life, the sight of it, a door swinging on hinges that hadn't moved in years—and when they spoke, their voice had gravel in it.

"If I say yes to anything, you will follow every instruction exactly. Exactly. You will not improvise, embellish, or be clever. Cleverness is how couriers die."

"And if I agree to all that?"

"Then tomorrow," Marguerite said, and stopped, and Sylvie watched them fight themselves and lose, gloriously, "tomorrow we count what we have. Properly. All of it. Everyone."

Sylvie stuck out her hand, the way deals were done over the bar downstairs.

Marguerite looked at it as though it might be armed. Then they took it—briefly, carefully, like picking up something breakable—and Sylvie grinned so wide her face hurt.

"One condition of mine," Marguerite added, releasing her hand. "You tell your mother. Tonight. Before I lose my nerve, frankly. And Sylvie—if she says no, it's no. I mean it. Her word is final."

"Oh, she'll say yes," Sylvie said, already at the door, already glowing. "You have no idea what she's like when she's decided about a person. Goodnight!"

She was three steps down—skipping nothing now, too happy for stealth—when Marguerite's voice followed her, low and rueful:

"Sylvie. How long have you known I wasn't a clerk?"

"Since the first evening," she called back. "You held the spoon like a dagger and you thanked Maman for the stew in three languages. Sleep well!"

* * *

Downstairs, Odile Ferrand was still awake—of course she was; she was always awake, sitting by the banked fire with mending she wasn't doing, and when Sylvie came in she took one look at her daughter's face and set down the shirt she wasn't darning.

"Well?" her mother said.

"He said yes. He wants to talk to you. Tonight, before he loses his nerve—his words."

Odile closed her eyes briefly, and Sylvie saw her mother do the silent arithmetic she did over everything—costs against needs, needs against love—and arrive, as she always arrived, at the only answer that had ever really been available to her.

"Light the lamp properly," Odile said, standing, tying her apron as though preparing for the biggest shift of her life. "And Sylvie—count the candles on the table. We'll want to know what we have."

Upstairs, Marguerite Vasquez blew out two of the three flames and sat in the dark, listening to footsteps below, and understood—with the vertigo of a person stepping off a ledge they'd been standing on for twenty years—that the plan locked behind their eyes had just acquired witnesses.

There was no version of the plan anymore that did not include them. There was no version of Marguerite anymore, either, that did not.

It was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to them, and some traitorous part of them whispered that it might also be the cure.
