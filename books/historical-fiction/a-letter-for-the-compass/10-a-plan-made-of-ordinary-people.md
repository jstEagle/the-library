## 10. A Plan Made of Ordinary People

I had planned operations before. I had planned them in the margins of shipping manifests, in the pauses between verses of drinking songs, once in the confessional booth of a priest who believed I was weeping over a dead mother rather than memorizing a bridge schedule. Planning, done properly, was a solitary art: one mind, one map, one throat to strangle if the plan went wrong. Every person you added was another door the wind could blow open.

So when Odile Ferrand set a pot of cider on her kitchen table, pulled out four chairs, and said, "Sit down, all of you, and tell me how we're going to do this," my first instinct was to correct her pronoun.

*How **I'm** going to do this.*

I didn't say it. That was the trouble with the Ferrand household. It had a way of getting words out of you before you'd finished vetting them.

It was the evening after Sylvie caught me copying cipher keys by candlelight — after she'd stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and offered herself to the abbey checkpoint like a girl offering to carry groceries — and after I'd refused her so flatly that even I heard how it sounded, like a man refusing an umbrella in a flood. Odile had watched the whole exchange from the stairs with the expression of a woman timing eggs, and then, when Sylvie stormed up to bed, she'd said only: "Tomorrow. After supper. Bring nothing. Say nothing to anyone." And that had been that.

Now the kitchen held more people than I had ever voluntarily shared a plan with, and every one of them had brought something.

Tomas Brissac arrived first, still smelling of the forge, his forearms crossed with soot ground into the creases like old rivers on a map. He was a mountain of a man who moved like a man apologizing for being a mountain, ducking under the lintel, folding himself into a chair built for ordinary mortals. He set a leather wallet on the table with the reverence other men reserved for relics.

"Requisition passes," he said. "Signed by Captain Vasseur himself, may his penmanship rot. I've been hauling imperial iron since the first outriders came. Nobody questions the smith's cart. Nobody wants to be the officer who delayed a shipment of horseshoes."

"You've read them," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I wrote half of them," Tomas admitted. "The captain signs what's put in front of him if the wine's right. The wine is often right."

Sylvie slid into her chair last, hair still damp from washing, wearing the particular brightness of someone who has won an argument by simply refusing to lose it. She'd extracted a concession from me at dawn — not agreement, never agreement, but a concession: *you may come as far as the planning table.* She was treating this like a festival.

Père Anselme came through the back door without knocking, because doors were apparently a suggestion to men who had spent forty years hearing confessions. He was small and round and ancient in the way of church candles — soft-looking until you touched the flame. He carried his own stool, which told me he'd been expecting to stay late.

"Marguerite Vasquez," he said, settling onto it, "or whatever you're called this week. I buried three couriers during the last war and never learned one of their true names. It seemed rude then. It seems ruder now."

"I don't use that name," I said.

"Then I'll use none at all. We'll call you Courier. Or perhaps *Trouble*, which has a nicer ring." He folded his hands atop his belly, entirely content. "You want the abbey. You want the hidden stair. You want to know which novices can be trusted to walk beside a stranger without asking why she smells of ink instead of incense. Ask."

I looked around that kitchen — the blacksmith with his forged passes, the innkeeper with her arms folded and her jaw set like a woman about to argue with the weather, the girl practically vibrating, the priest who knew too much and confessed to knowing it — and I felt something I hadn't felt since before the Delacours were taken off their farm seven years ago. It took me a moment to name it, because it was so unfamiliar it might have been illness.

Steadiness. The horrible, treacherous steadiness of not being alone with the arithmetic.

I hated it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when they're burned. I hated it the way a man hates learning he's been walking crooked all his life — not because the correction hurts, but because it means every mile before it was wrong.

* * *

"Three checkpoints," I said, and drew in spilled flour on the tabletop, because chalk would be evidence and flour could be bread by morning. "The river bridge, here. Then the abbey road and the abbey gate, here. Then the customs house at the frontier, here, which no one crosses without papers that survive scrutiny. The ledger leaves Saint-Aubin in a way that survives all three, or it doesn't leave at all, and everyone in that registry goes north on a deportation train."

"Cheerful fellow," Tomas murmured.

"The bridge first. They check cargo but they're stretched thin — the garrison pulled men north two days ago. What they *do* check is faces against lists. So the people crossing should be faces nobody's hunting." I tapped the flour. "A smith delivering requisitioned goods south. His passes are genuine enough. A widow moving her husband's effects to a cousin's farm — that's common as rain right now, nobody looks twice at grief."

Odile said quietly, "Henri's effects."

I stopped drawing. "If that's—"

"It's a cart full of tools and a mattress, Courier. He's four years dead. He'd have laughed himself sick at the idea of smuggling sedition under his own coffin linens." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, briefly, around her cup. "Let him be useful. He always wanted to be."

I looked down at the flour and made myself keep going, because the alternative was to look at her longer than I could bear. "The ledger itself goes sewn into the mattress. Cloth hides paper's edges. If they cut it open, it reads as padding. If they don't — and they won't, unless something draws attention —"

"Then don't give them anything to draw attention," Sylvie said. "That's where I come in. The abbey gate. Novices go in and out with alms baskets, and Mother Superior owes Odile thirty years of soup debts. I walk with real novices, I carry the basket, and I stand at the gate looking pious, which I can do, I've been practicing in the mirror—"

"You have not," Odile said.

"I have absolutely, and I'm very good."

"Sylvie." My voice came out harder than I intended. "The abbey gate is where they'll search hardest once the courier profiles circulate. A girl traveling alone—"

"She wouldn't be alone. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it?" Sylvie leaned forward, and there was her mother in her face, the same stubborn architecture. "You've spent — what did Père Anselme call you? — however many years doing this with one pair of hands. One pair of hands gets tired. One pair of hands gets caught. You have five pairs now, plus a priest who claims he knows a stair that doesn't exist on any imperial map."

"The stair exists," Anselme said serenely. "It existed before the abbey did, if you believe the stonemasons, and I believe stonemasons over bishops as a rule. It runs from the crypt down through the old drainage channel and comes up behind the tannery wall, a hundred meters past the gate. A body could walk out of that abbey without so much as nodding to a sentry."

"Why tell us?" I asked him. "Why risk it?"

He considered me with mild, terrible eyes. "Because in 1789 a courier hid in my crypt for six days and I let her go out the front gate into a patrol, because I judged her mission worth more than her life, and she died at the second checkpoint anyway. I have regretted the arithmetic ever since. This time I intend to spend everything at once and be done with budgeting souls."

The kitchen went quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Odile rose without a word and ladled more stew into the priest's bowl, which I was beginning to understand was this house's version of applause.

* * *

We argued for two hours. I want to be clear that it was arguing — real arguing, the kind I'd only ever watched from the outside, at tables in inns where families quarreled about harvests and marriages while I sat in the corner memorizing their exits. Tomas insisted the cart should cross the bridge at dusk; Odile said dusk was when the lieutenant drank and drunk lieutenants invented reasons to unpack carts; Sylvie proposed, with total sincerity, that she disguise herself as a boy, and was overruled by three people simultaneously, including the priest, who said God had invested too much in her cheekbones for such waste.

And through all of it, I kept waiting for the feeling to pass. The feeling of the plan holding its shape even when other hands pressed on it. Every time Tomas found a flaw, he also found the patch. Every time Odile poked at the timeline, she bent it into something that fit the town's actual rhythms — market day, curfew bell, the hour the mill closed. Sylvie asked questions I'd never have asked myself because I'd stopped questioning anything years ago; I just executed. *Why does the ledger matter more than the people carrying it? Why can't the mattress ride in the middle of the load instead of the back? Have you actually counted the sentries or are you guessing?*

Counting. She wanted me to count. As if lives were countable, as if the whole point of my profession wasn't to stop counting.

But I counted. Out loud. In front of everyone. Four bodies on the bridge, two at the abbey, one cart, one ledger, eleven days gone, four days of deportation trains hanging over us like weather. And the strange thing — the thing I will be untangling for the rest of my life — is that saying the numbers aloud in that warm kitchen made them smaller. Not lighter. Smaller. Divided, somehow, among people who refused to let me carry them alone.

"There's a flaw," I finally said, because honesty was apparently contagious in this room. "The customs house. Everything else, we can muscle through or charm through or pray through. But the customs house checks papers against the courier index — the Republic sold our covers in the surrender terms, which means somewhere in that building is a page with my alias on it, and descriptions, and training marks. No forged paper I can make will survive a clerk who knows what he's reading. Someone carries the ledger through whose name isn't on any list. Someone ordinary. And that means—" I stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and I could see the drop.

"That means whoever walks it through is walking in blind," Odile said, "while you stay on this side of the gate."

"Yes."

"And if it goes wrong for them, you can't help."

"No."

Silence. Tomas studied his knuckles. Sylvie looked at her mother, and her mother looked at the candle flame, and I understood with a sick lurch that I had just described exactly what I had done to every handler, every contact, every family whose street I'd slipped away down at midnight: left them holding the danger while I held the distance.

"Well," said Odile Ferrand, into that silence, "then we'll need to practice."

I blinked. "Practice what?"

"Lying beautifully. If I'm to carry your ledger through an imperial customs house as a widow with dull papers and a duller face, someone has to teach me to do it the way you do it. And you, Courier—" she pointed the ladle at me, and steam curled off it like a threat, "—are going to sit there and teach me every trick you know, and you're going to hate every minute of it, because teaching means telling, and telling means we'll know you. Truly know you. And I suspect that terrifies you more than any captain in that town."

The ladle steamed. The fire ticked. Père Anselme ate his stew.

And I sat there in the steadiest, most dangerous room I had ever planned a crime in, surrounded by ordinary people volunteering to be extraordinary, and heard myself say:

"We start tonight. First lesson: widows never volunteer information. Second lesson—" I faltered, because the next lesson was the one I'd never taught anyone, the one written in the margin of every cipher book I'd ever owned: *never let them close enough to learn your hand.* "Second lesson is handwriting. If the countersignature doesn't match the envoy's expectations exactly, the ledger is worthless paper. I'll need ink, good ink, and paper of the right age, and—"

"Tomas keeps lampblack and gum arabic for stamping iron," Sylvie said immediately. "And I know where the notary keeps his old letterbooks. The good rag paper, already yellowed."

Everyone looked at her.

"What?" she said. "I live here. I notice things. It's a family affliction."

It was past midnight when the meeting broke. Tomas ducked out under the lintel with his wallet of passes; Anselme folded his stool under his arm and blessed the house so casually it sounded like commenting on the weather; Sylvie went up the stairs backward, still talking, proposing improvements to a plan that had been final for an hour.

Odile banked the fire. I stood to slip out to my room the way I always slipped out of rooms — quiet, unremarked, rehearsed — and her voice caught me at the doorway like a hand on the collar.

"Courier. Tomorrow night, same table. Bring the ledger's cipher keys. All of them."

"You don't read cipher."

"No," she agreed, wiping down the table where the flour map had been, erasing the bridge, the abbey, the frontier, the whole geography of our survival. "But you do. And tomorrow night you're going to teach me, which means tomorrow night you're going to trust me, and I want to watch your face while you decide whether you can."

She hung the cloth over her shoulder. For a moment she looked, in the guttering light, like every fortress I'd ever failed to breach.

"Goodnight, Marguerite," she said — using the name, the true one, the one I'd given her only once, weeks ago, in a moment of weakness I'd promised myself never to repeat.

I went up the stairs. I lay down in the narrow bed under the eaves and stared at the dark, cataloguing exits out of pure habit — window, roofline, stable yard, the gap in the hedge — and discovered that for the first time in seven years, I wasn't making the catalogue to use it. I was making it the way you count candles. Just to see the light.

Below me, faintly, I heard Odile moving through the kitchen, and Sylvie's voice drifting down one last question, and the low murmur of an answer. A household, settling into sleep, planning treason over breakfast.

In the morning, Tomas would bring the ink. By noon Sylvie would have the paper. And in four days — four days, Luc's news had collapsed everything, the trains were coming — we would put a widow, a mattress, a cartload of horseshoes, and a lifetime of practiced lies onto one muddy road south.

I closed my eyes. Somewhere between dread and something dangerously close to gratitude, I fell asleep faster than I had in years — which frightened me most of all, and which I decided, in the last honest thought before dreams took me, to examine tomorrow.

Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow. If the customs house spared us.

If the ink held.
