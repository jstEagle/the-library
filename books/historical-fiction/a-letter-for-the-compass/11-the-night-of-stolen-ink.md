## 11. The Night of Stolen Ink

I had broken into buildings before. I want to say it gets easier, but that would be a lie told by a person who has never had to do it with their hands shaking from something other than cold.

The records office of Saint-Aubin had been a notary's house in its former life—a squat stone building with good shutters and a bad roof, standing at the end of the Rue des Tilleuls like a fat man leaning against a fence. Now the imperial eagle hung over its door on a banner that someone had hung crooked and no one had bothered to straighten, which I chose to read as an omen in my favor.

Two sentries at the front. I had counted them for three nights running from the window of my room at the inn, and I had learned their habits the way I once learned the habits of towns: the tall one smoked at half past eleven and again at one; the short one dozed against the doorpost between rounds, his chin dropping to his chest with the regularity of a clock. Odile's kitchen clock, to be precise. I had begun measuring imperial discipline against her kitchen, which told me something about where my allegiances had quietly relocated themselves without filing the proper paperwork.

The plan was simple, because simple plans are the only ones that survive contact with a night. Over the wall behind the cooper's yard. Across the garden where the notary's widow had once grown beans and now grew nettles. Through the kitchen window whose latch Sylvie had loosened for me two days ago with a butter knife and an expression of tremendous criminal satisfaction.

"You're enjoying this far too much," I'd told her.

"Someone has to," she'd said. "You look like you're attending your own funeral every time you sharpen that pencil."

She wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with Sylvie Ferrand—she was never wrong about people, only about when to stop saying so.

I went over the wall at ten minutes past midnight, wearing dark wool and carrying nothing that could be traced to anyone: no papers, no tools I couldn't explain as a clerk's ordinary kit, no name. The nettles found the gap between my boot and my trouser leg immediately, as nettles do, with the enthusiasm of small cruelties. I crossed the garden. The kitchen window rose under my fingers. The latch gave with a click so loud I stood frozen for a full minute, counting heartbeats, listening for boots.

Nothing. The town breathed. Somewhere a dog dreamed aloud.

Inside, the records office smelled of ink, dust, and the particular staleness of rooms where men have been smoking and arguing about lists. The requisition clerks worked by day and drank by evening, and by midnight the place belonged to the mice and to me.

The registry ledger—the book with every name in Saint-Aubin sorted into columns of *resident*, *removable*, and worse—had been kept in the strongbox in the front room. I knew this because I had spent two evenings pretending to be lost on the Rue des Tilleuls while the requisitioned clerks carried files in and out, and because Tomas, who delivered coal to the office, had a memory for rooms the way other men have a memory for faces. The strongbox sat behind the magistrate's old desk. It was locked. Locks, however, are honest things. They tell you exactly what they are, unlike people.

I knelt before it with my picks and my held breath, and it took me four minutes—four long, nettle-stung, sweat-cold minutes—and then the hasp swung open.

Empty.

Not empty the way a box is empty when you've failed. Empty the way a box is empty when someone got here first. A clean rectangle of less-faded wood marked where the ledger had lain, and beside it, a single filing slip in a requisition clerk's careful hand:

*Transferred per orders of Captain Vasseur. Vault, customs annex. 14th.*

The customs annex. At the third checkpoint. The one place on our route that might as well have been the moon.

I sat back on my heels in the dark and did the arithmetic I did not want to do. Stealing it from the strongbox would have been difficult. Stealing it from the vault of an active military checkpoint was not theft; it was theology. You could pray about it. Accomplishing it was another matter entirely.

That was when I heard the breathing.

* * *

It came from the back room—the notary's old archive, where the shelves still held deed boxes and the smell of forty years of other people's property disputes. The breathing was wet and uneven, the breathing of a man trying very hard to be quiet and losing.

I should have left. Every rule I had ever written for myself, every one of them carved during seven years of keeping my hands clean and my heart cleaner, said *leave*. An unexpected witness is a liability. A dying man is a slow alarm bell. Marguerite Vasquez—whoever that was this week—did not collect strays.

I opened the archive door anyway.

He lay behind the deed boxes where someone had dragged him, or where he had crawled: a Republic officer's coat gone gray with dust, one epaulette torn off at the shoulder, and a wound in his side that had soaked through the improvised bandage and kept going. He was perhaps forty. He had the face of a man who had been handsome in the way that government offices are handsome—functional, fading. His eyes found me in the dark and he did not cry out, which told me two things: he was disciplined, and he had decided already that I was not imperial.

"Clerk," he whispered. It wasn't a question. "From the inn. The one who reads everything twice."

So much for invisibility. I crouched beside him anyway. "Who did this?"

"Our own side." He laughed, which cost him; I watched the laugh turn into a cough and the cough into red at the corner of his mouth. "Lieutenant Roche. Before the garrison pulled out. I objected to certain items in the surrender terms being handed over." His hand crept toward his coat. I tensed—but what he drew out was not a weapon. It was a letter fold, stained dark at one corner. "My objection was noted."

I took the fold. Inside was a single page in a hand I recognized—the cramped, forward-leaning script of the Republic's diplomatic service, the same hand that had signed my own commission seven years ago and had not thought of me since.

"I don't understand it," the officer said. "And I've read it nine times. You will."

I read it. Then I read it again, the way I apparently do everything, and the room tilted slightly on its foundations.

The envoy at the frontier—Comte Aldery, neutral, fussy, the last door left standing between Saint-Aubin and the deportation trains—would act on the ledger. That much we had known; that much had driven every sleepless hour of the planning Odile and I had finally, grudgingly, built together around her kitchen table. But the envoy would act on the ledger *only* under countersignature. The Republic's seal alone was worthless now; the surrender terms had voided it. What the terms required—what the envoy required, because he was a man who loved procedure more than he loved people—was the countersignature of an accredited courier, appended in the courier's own hand, with the courier's cipher key written out in full as proof of identity.

In other words: the book was not enough. The book needed a name beside it.

A living, verifiable courier. One who could write the key from memory. One whose cover, thanks to the same surrender terms, was already sold to the imperial intelligence service and sitting in a file with my false name at the top of it.

"That's why they transferred the ledger to the annex," the officer murmured, watching my face do whatever it was doing—he seemed to find it interesting, the way one finds weather interesting. "Vasseur knows the envoy's requirements. He's waiting for a courier to come for it. They all come for their ledgers eventually." He smiled with a quarter of his mouth. "You're expected, whoever you are."

"They expect a courier. Any courier?"

"They expect *the* courier. There's only one left in the district who hasn't run or died." His eyes were steady on mine, terribly polite. "I did try to warn the others, years ago. That this work ends one of two ways."

"And those are?"

"Forgettable, or remembered by the wrong people."

I looked down at the page in my hand. To forge the countersignature properly—to make it survive the scrutiny of a neutral envoy who had spent twenty years authenticating documents the way other men taste wine—I would need to reproduce not merely a signature but a *hand*: the specific slant, the specific abbreviations, the courier's shorthand marks that authenticated without announcing themselves. And there was exactly one accredited courier left in the district whose hand I could study.

Mine. My alias. The clerk from the inn who reads everything twice.

The mission had just changed shape. It was no longer theft. Theft I knew how to do; theft was a craft with clean edges, take this, leave nothing. This was forgery—this was writing myself onto a document in a name that would kill me if spoken aloud, making my own invented self the co-author of three hundred families' survival. The invisible man asked to sign.

"There's ink and paper in the front office," the officer said. "Requisition-grade. Terrible stuff. Fades in a year." He coughed again, longer, and when it passed he was smaller somehow, as though each cough took a layer of him. "You'll want better than that. Envoy's archivists test for fade."

"Is there anything else you'd like to tell me while we're being candid?" I asked. "Since candor appears to be fatal tonight anyway."

He considered. From somewhere inside the ruin of his coat he produced a stub of pencil and, moving with the agonized slowness of a man spending his last coins, wrote four characters on the back of the transfer slip. Coordinates. A farmhouse south of the bridge, off the main road.

"Aldery sends a runner to this farm every second day," he said. "If your documents reach the farm, they reach the envoy without passing the customs house at all."

The customs house. Where my face—if the defector had described it, if the courier profiles had circulated—was worth a cell. A route around checkpoint three. It was almost too much good news to trust, and I have never in my life trusted good news that arrived free.

"Why give me this?" I asked. "Why not sell it? Vasseur would pay."

"Because Vasseur is the reason I'm dying on a floor," he said simply. "And because—" he paused, and for a moment the officer fell away and there was just a tired man on a cold floor, "—someone fed me stew once, when I was nobody. In a town like this one. I never went back. I've regretted it for nineteen years." He closed his eyes. "Go home to your stew, clerk. Whatever else you do tonight—go back."

* * *

I did what needed doing first, because grief and urgency both keep better when you give them tasks.

There was a cellar hatch behind the archive shelves; I had noticed it on my earlier reconnaissance and dismissed it as irrelevant. It was relevant now. Getting a dying man down a ladder quietly is one of the harder sentences I have ever had to carry out, and I will not describe it except to say that he apologized twice, which undid me more than the wound had. I left him water, the rest of Odile's linen that I had brought for wrapping stolen papers, and a promise I intended to keep, which made it rare among my promises.

Then I climbed back through the kitchen window into the nettle garden and stood a while in the dark, letting the cold finish the job the fear had started.

Here is what I had learned tonight, arranged in order of increasing horror:

One. The ledger sat in the vault at the customs annex, guarded, expected, baited.

Two. The envoy required a countersignature in a courier's hand, with the key written out—meaning that somewhere between here and the frontier, someone would have to *be* that courier, openly, on paper, in the sight of men paid to shoot couriers.

Three. The only hand available was my alias's, and my alias was dead the moment any imperial clerk compared her to the sold profiles.

Four. Unless. Unless the countersignature did not need to be *my* hand. Only *a* courier's hand, authentic and verifiable. The envoy had known many couriers in twenty years. The requirement was authenticity, not identity. A signature could be taught. A hand could be copied, practiced, worn like a borrowed coat by someone the imperial files had never heard of—

Someone like a widowed innkeeper shipping her husband's effects through a customs house.

I stood among the nettles and felt the idea arrive the way ideas do—not as inspiration but as dread wearing inspiration's clothes. Because I knew, even then, even before I said it aloud to anyone, what it would cost, and whom I would have to ask, and what it would mean to ask it. For seven years I had arranged my life so that no one could ever be endangered by knowing me. Tonight the mathematics had reversed themselves: the only way to save these people was to put one of them directly in harm's path, holding a forged page with my crime written across it in teaching-ink.

I came over the inn's back wall as the sky was considering gray. The kitchen fire was banked but alive—Odile kept it alive the way she kept everything, stubbornly and without discussion—and she was sitting at the table, fully dressed, a pot of chicory cooling untouched beside her.

"You're late," she said. "Sylvie counted your absence in candles. We're on the fourth."

"The ledger's moved. It's in the customs annex vault, and it's bait." I sat down across from her because my legs had opinions of their own. "And there's more."

"There always is, with you." She poured the chicory, pushed it across. "Drink first. Bad news keeps."

For once, I obeyed. And then I laid it all out on the scarred table between us—the vault, the countersignature, the courier's hand, the coordinates, the dying man in the cellar who had once been fed by strangers and never forgot it—and watched Odile's face as understanding arrived and settled in, unhurried, like a guest who intends to stay the week.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup slowly on its saucer.

"So," she said. "They'll want a courier's hand." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, calm as a millpond, terrifying as a millpond. "Show me yours."

"It has to be a real courier's—"

"You're a real courier. That's the whole catastrophe, isn't it?" She folded her hands. "You'll teach me. Tonight, if the trains leave when Luc says they leave. And Marguerite—" she used the name gently, the way one uses a lamp in a dark room, "—before you argue, and I can see you winding up to argue, you should know that Sylvie is awake in the stairwell and has been for an hour, so whatever you decide, you'll be deciding it in front of witnesses."

Above us, very faintly, the stairwell floor creaked—an entirely unconvincing performance of innocence.

I looked at the woman who had seen through me in a single evening and had fed me anyway, and I understood that the last of my old rules had just packed its bag. By morning I would be a forger. By tomorrow night, Odile Ferrand would walk into the customs house wearing my crimes like a shawl.

All I had to do was teach her to write like a ghost.

I reached for the ink.
