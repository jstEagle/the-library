## 12. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

Sylvie had counted the candles the night before—eleven of them, burned down to stubs while her mother practiced a dead man's handwriting—and now she counted other things instead, because counting kept the fear in its pen. She counted the spokes on the cart's left wheel as they rolled out of the inn yard before dawn. She counted the crows on the fence posts along the mill road, seven of them, all facing north like little sentries who knew something she didn't. And when the river came into view through the morning fog, gray and slow and smelling of iron, she counted the soldiers at the far end of the bridge.

Four. No—five. The fifth was mostly hidden by the tollhouse, but his helmet kept catching the light.

"Five," she whispered.

"I make it five too," said Tomas from the driver's bench, without turning around. He had a way of speaking that made everything sound like a remark about the weather. "The one behind the tollhouse is young. Young ones are either the worst or the best. No middle."

"And which do we want?"

"Neither. We want him bored." Tomas flicked the reins gently over the mule's back. "Bored is the whole religion of this crossing. Boredom is our saint. Pray to it."

The cart bed was loaded with what it was supposed to be loaded with: Odile's winter linens, two mattresses, a crate of preserved pears with the inn's mark stenciled on the side, and beneath the second mattress—the good one, the one that had been sewn shut again at midnight with stitches deliberately crooked so it would look mended rather than new—the ledger. Sylvie tried not to look at the mattress. Looking at it felt like looking directly at the sun; she was certain someone would notice.

Marguerite sat in the back among the linens, dressed in Odile's late husband's coat, which fit them badly in the shoulders and perfectly in the sense of belonging to no one. They had not spoken since before first light. They sat with their hands loose in their lap and their face arranged into an expression Sylvie could only describe as *furniture*. Marguerite looked like something you'd already seen a hundred times and never once noticed.

*How,* Sylvie wondered, watching them, *does a person learn to be a chair?*

The fog thinned as they approached the bridge. Below, the river ran brown and patient, carrying last autumn's leaves toward a sea none of them would see. The planks boomed under the wheels—a hollow, honest sound, like a drum announcing them.

At the near end of the bridge, a barrier had been set up: two sawhorses, a rope, a lantern still burning though the sun was up. A sergeant stood beside it with a ledger of his own—everyone had ledgers now, Sylvie thought bitterly, everyone but the people whose names were in them.

Tomas reined in and produced the passes before anyone asked. He handed them over the way a man hands over a laundry ticket: slightly bored himself, faintly inconvenienced, a blacksmith with better things to do.

"Sergeant. Cart requisition pass, district seal, and my guild papers. I'm moving goods for the garrison commissary—linens and stores for the officers' quarters at Saint-Aubin. Widow Ferrand there owns the goods. I'm just the arms and the mule."

The sergeant looked at the papers for a long time. Long enough that Sylvie's heart began doing arithmetic of its own, quick and ugly. Then he grunted, waved the cart forward, and called out to the tollhouse: "Lieutenant! Another one for you."

*Another one.* So they were not the first. That was either wonderful or terrible, and Sylvie couldn't decide which.

The lieutenant emerged from the tollhouse wiping his mouth with a napkin—he'd been eating breakfast, which meant he was fed, which meant, Tomas had said once, that he was in no hurry to ruin his morning. He was perhaps thirty-five, with the neat, unremarkable face of a man promoted for diligence rather than brilliance, and he walked around the cart slowly, tapping the crate of pears with his riding crop.

"Widow Ferrand," he said, reading the stencil. "And this is your cart?"

"It is," said Odile.

Her voice did something Sylvie had heard only a handful of times in her life: it went flat and plain and utterly unafraid, the voice she used for telling drunk guests the taproom was closed. Sylvie realized, with a small shock, that her mother had been rehearsing this voice her entire life without knowing why.

"You are transporting these goods where?"

"To the garrison, Lieutenant. As the pass says. Captain Voss requisitioned the linens himself—I have his signature here if you'd care to compare it."

She held up the requisition order. The lieutenant took it, glanced at it, and then did not look at it anymore. Instead he looked at Odile, and Sylvie felt the temperature of the morning drop.

"Ferrand," he said. "Ferrand. There was a Ferrand on the debt rolls. A man who owed the customs house for untaxed brandy—three years running, I'm told. A smuggler, some said." He smiled without warmth. "Your husband?"

"My husband has been dead fourteen months, Lieutenant."

"That doesn't answer my question."

"It answers every question that matters."

Somewhere in the linen behind Sylvie, Marguerite shifted—not much, just enough that Sylvie felt the mattress press against her back, and understood that Marguerite was repositioning themselves to see.

The lieutenant folded the requisition order in half. Then in quarters. He did it slowly, the way a cat plays with something before deciding whether to eat it.

"Debts pass to widows under current ordinance," he said. "Did you know that? Article eleven. Very popular article. The customs house keeps records going back years. If your late husband owed the Republic—and the Republic's debts are now our debts, naturally, we inherit everything, it's the privilege of victory—then you owe us. And carts can be seized against debt. Carts, goods, livestock." He nodded at the mule. "Even the mule."

Sylvie's mouth went dry. This wasn't in the plan. The plan had passes and seals and boredom. The plan had not accounted for a lieutenant who'd eaten a full breakfast and gone looking for entertainment.

But her mother—her mother tilted her head, and something almost like amusement crossed her face.

"Then you'll want the customs house records," Odile said. "Please do fetch them. I'll wait."

The lieutenant blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The records. From the customs house. For the years 1789 through 1791." Odile folded her hands. "Because if you go digging in those records, Lieutenant, you will find that my husband paid his fine in full in the spring of '91—paid it to a customs officer named Delacroix, who gave him a receipt, which receipt I hold, because my husband was careless with money but scrupulous with paper. And you will also find—" she leaned forward slightly, confidingly, "—that Officer Delacroix did not enter that payment in the house ledger, because Officer Delacroix pocketed it. Which means the debt you're threatening me with is a fiction, and the man who invented it is now drawing imperial pay under the same dishonest habits. Shall I tell your captain that? Or shall we let sleeping receipts lie?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Sylvie counted.

The lieutenant looked at the requisition order in his hand. He unfolded it, smoothed it against his thigh, and handed it back.

"There's no need for any of that," he said. "I ask questions. It's my job. People answer or they don't, and how they answer tells me things." He stepped back and gestured at the bridge with his crop. "Move along, Widow Ferrand. And mind the third plank—it's rotten. We've been meaning to fix it."

"We've been meaning to fix it too," said Odile, taking the reins herself, which hadn't been the plan either, and clicking her tongue at the mule.

The cart rolled onto the bridge.

Sylvie didn't breathe properly until the third plank boomed safely under them and the far bank rose up green and ordinary. Her hands were shaking so hard she pressed them flat against her knees. Behind her, she heard Marguerite exhale—one long, controlled breath, the kind a person takes after holding still for a very long time.

"That," Sylvie whispered, "was the most terrifying thing I have ever watched."

"Mm," said Marguerite.

"She lied. Mother lied about the receipt. There is no receipt—I dusted that drawer my whole childhood, there's nothing in it but buttons."

"There's a receipt," Marguerite said quietly.

Sylvie turned. Marguerite was looking out at the river with an expression of mild interest, as though discussing the weather.

"What do you mean, there's a receipt?"

"Two nights ago. While everyone was arguing about the route, I went through your mother's papers. Couriers check everything—it's habit, not rudeness. There was a receipt, yellowed, folded into a prayer book. Your father paid the fine. Delacroix signed it. Whether the money ever reached the customs house, I can't say, but the paper exists." Marguerite finally looked at her, and there was something in their face Sylvie couldn't name—something almost like apology. "Your mother didn't lie. She gambled that the truth was on her side, and she bet everything on it without checking first. Which is either courage or madness, and I genuinely don't know which."

"She checked," Sylvie said slowly. "She must have. She found it after you did. Or—" She stopped. "Or she remembered it. She remembered it existed and she trusted it."

"People do that," Marguerite said. "Trust things. It's remarkable to watch."

There was no mockery in it. That was the strange part. Sylvie searched for the mockery and found none, and that absence unsettled her more than the lieutenant had.

Ahead, Odile drove the cart with her spine straight as a church candle. She had not looked back once. Sylvie thought about that—all those years of her mother running the inn alone, answering officials, absorbing insults, feeding soldiers from armies she despised, and never once letting her hands shake where anyone could see. Sylvie had thought her mother was simply calm. Now she understood it was the same thing Marguerite did. The same terrible skill, learned in different rooms.

*We're all lying beautifully,* she thought. *The whole cart is a lie with wheels.*

They came off the bridge onto the south road, where the fog was burning off and the fields opened up wet and bright. A quarter mile on, past a shrine to a saint whose name had been chiseled off during the Revolution, Tomas finally spoke.

"Well," he said. "That went well."

*"Well?"* Sylvie nearly shrieked it.

"He asked about debts, not papers. Debts mean he hadn't been told to look for anything specific. If they'd flagged the cart, he'd have asked about cargo, passengers, destinations. He was fishing with a hook he always uses." Tomas shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Also, your mother frightened him. Men like that collect fears the way other men collect coins, and being frightened by a widow in front of a sergeant is an expensive fear. He'll remember her kindly, if he remembers her at all."

"He'll remember her as a woman who threatened to expose corruption to his commanding officer."

"Yes," said Tomas placidly. "Kindly."

From the back of the cart, Marguerite made a sound. It took Sylvie a moment to identify it, because she had never heard it before: Marguerite was laughing. Quietly, behind their hand, shoulders shaking inside the dead man's coat—an actual laugh, startled out of them like a bird from a hedge.

"What?" Sylvie demanded.

"Nothing. It's—" Marguerite pressed their knuckles to their mouth, got control, lost it again. "*Mind the third plank.* He fixed the mood with a plank. That's—that's exactly the trick. That's page one of the courier manual, distract them with something small and harmless so they stop thinking about the large and fatal thing, and he did it without knowing, he does it the way people breathe—" They stopped laughing abruptly, as though catching themselves at something shameful.

Sylvie stared at them. "You learned that from a manual?"

"Everyone learns it somewhere. Manuals, mothers, bad years." Marguerite picked a bit of straw off the mattress—carefully, away from the seam—and rolled it between their fingers. "It's just strange, watching civilians do it naturally. Like watching someone sing who never had to be taught."

"How long have you been doing it? The lying. The—the furniture face."

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. The cart creaked. Somewhere behind them, faintly, the bridge sentry changed shift, boots knocking on old wood.

"Since I was younger than you," they said at last. "You get good at it fast or you don't survive the learning. You learn which lies are load-bearing—which ones you build your life on top of and must never, ever move. And you get so fluent that eventually you stop noticing you're speaking the language." They looked at the river, receding now behind the trees. "Watching your mother today was like watching someone speak my language with a different accent. Same grammar. Different country."

"That's the loneliest sentence I've ever heard," Sylvie said.

Marguerite's fingers went still on the straw. "Yes," they said, after a moment. "I suppose it is."

They rode on. The south road unwound ahead of them through orchards and hedgerows, toward the abbey, toward the second checkpoint, toward everything waiting in the fog beyond. Sylvie should have been afraid—she was afraid, her stomach was a knot of wet rope—but underneath the fear was something else, something warm and stubborn that had taken root sometime in the last eleven days without her permission.

She had watched a stranger lie beautifully and discovered the stranger had been doing it alone their whole life, in a language with no other speakers. And she had watched her mother stand in front of an imperial officer and discover she'd been speaking it all along too.

Three languages, Sylvie thought. Three accents. One cart.

She was still smiling at the thought when Tomas stiffened on the bench and raised one hand—not sharply, but with sudden total attention, the way a dog goes still before the thunder arrives.

Ahead, where the road bent toward the abbey hill, a rider had appeared. He sat his horse at the bend without moving, watching them come, and even at this distance Sylvie could see that he wore no uniform at all—just a long gray coat and a traveler's hat, and that he was not watching the cart.

He was watching the space on the bench where Marguerite sat.
