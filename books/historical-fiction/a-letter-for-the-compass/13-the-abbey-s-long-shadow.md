## 13. The Abbey's Long Shadow

The abbey of Saint-Clair-des-Prés had been built by men who believed God preferred His architecture thick. Its walls were a full arm's length of grey stone, its windows narrow as knife slits, and its bell tower had been designed, according to Père Anselme, "to withstand both Saracens and tax collectors." Marguerite, standing in the shadow of its gate as the last light failed, decided it was the first building in Saint-Aubin they had ever admired. It looked like a place that understood the value of not being noticed.

"You're staring at it like a woman sizing up a rival," Odile murmured beside them. The cart stood behind her, Tomas at the mule's head, Sylvie asleep against a sack of turnips with her mouth open in a way her mother would deny under oath.

"I'm staring at it like a clerk who appreciates good masonry," Marguerite said.

"Mm. And I'm a widow of modest means, and that's a cart of turnips, and you're a clerk." Odile patted their arm. "The abbey doesn't care what we are. That's why we're here."

Père Anselme opened the postern gate before they could knock — a small, round man with a tonsure like a tonsured egg and the unhurried air of someone who had outlived three governments and expected to outlive the fourth. He looked at the cart, the mule, the sleeping girl, the blacksmith, the false clerk, and the widow, in that order, and nodded once.

"The crypt," he said. "Bring the turnips. God provides, but He appreciates assistance."

* * *

The crypt was cold in the way of all old stone, and smelled of candle wax, damp, and centuries of honest dust. Anselme led them down a stair worn into a shallow trough by the feet of the faithful, past tombs of abbots whose names had worn smooth, into a low chamber where the bones had been stacked with the tidy indifference of a chandler's shelves. Sylvie woke on the way down, took one look at the ossuary, and said, with the delight of a fourteen-year-old discovering a new genre of horror, "Are those *real*?"

"Sylvie," Odile said.

"I'm only asking. It's a fair question. They're very neatly arranged."

"Monks are tidy," Anselme said, unbothered. "It is their one virtue, and they practice it on everything."

They made a camp of it: blankets between the tombs, the ledger-mattress propped against the wall of Saint somebody's sarcophagus, a single candle shielded behind a skull that Sylvie had named Bertrand. Tomas checked the cart above, then came down and reported the mule was "in a mood," which Marguerite had learned meant the mule was behaving exactly as mules always behaved and Tomas simply enjoyed complaining about it.

Marguerite did not sleep. They sat in the corner where the candlelight didn't reach and watched the others — Odile mending a seam by touch, Tomas snoring with the deep contentment of a man whose conscience was made of iron and whose back was made of the same, Sylvie curled with her arm around a turnip sack as though it had been promoted to pet. Eleven days ago Marguerite had been one person in a town of strangers. Now they were one person in a crypt of conspirators, and the strangest thing, the thing they could not stop examining like a coin of doubtful mint, was that they did not want to leave.

*Wanting* was the danger. Marguerite knew this the way they knew cipher keys — by heart, by scar tissue. The moment you wanted a place, you stopped seeing its exits. The moment you wanted people, you started making exceptions for them. Marguerite had watched a family die in 1791 because a courier — a younger, softer Marguerite — had made an exception. They had promised themselves, in the grey aftermath, never again. And they had kept it. They had kept it so well that when Odile had said *you could stay, you know, we'd make room*, Marguerite had felt it like a blade slipped between ribs: not the wound itself, but the discovery of how much room there was inside them for wounding.

They were still turning this over when they heard the noise from the far end of the crypt.

It was small — a scrape, a cough quickly smothered — but Marguerite's whole body went to attention the way it had been trained to, the old reflexes rising like water in a well. Someone was in the crypt. Someone who was not supposed to be, and who did not want to be heard.

They moved toward the sound along the wall, silent on the cold flags, and found it in the deepest niche, behind a tomb whose occupant had been dead long enough to stop minding company. A young man lay there in a cloak stiff with old mud, and he was watching Marguerite with eyes that had the particular shine of someone in pain who has decided to pretend otherwise.

"I heard the cart," he whispered. "And the priest talking to a woman with a northern accent. I thought it was a patrol." He tried to sit up and failed, his breath catching on something internal. "It isn't a patrol."

"No," Marguerite said.

"You're not soldiers."

"No."

He studied them. He was perhaps twenty, with a deserter's beard — a week's growth grown in three days by a frightened face — and hands that were stained, Marguerite noticed, not with dirt but with something darker and more particular. Ink. The man was a clerk, or had been.

"Then who," he said, "are you?"

"That," Marguerite said, "is the question I'd be asking if I were you. Who are *you*, hiding in a crypt with a wound you're hiding too?"

"Luc," he said, after a pause in which Marguerite could see him weighing every possible lie and finding them all too heavy to lift. "Luc Marchand. Fourth Regiment of the Line." He said it flatly, the way a man says a debt. "I was a regimental clerk. I walked away from the column on the north road four days ago." He touched his side, and his hand came away dark. "A forager's musket. He wasn't even aiming at me, I don't think. I was just *there*."

Deserter. Wounded. Hiding in the one crypt in the one abbey on the one road Marguerite needed. Marguerite felt the shape of the problem settle over them like a coat that didn't fit.

"Anselme knows you're here?"

"He found me two days ago. He's been feeding me. He says God's house has room for everyone." Luc's mouth twisted. "God's house isn't being hunted by the imperial provost, though. If they find a deserter here, they'll burn the abbey's privileges along with him. I told the père I'd move on tonight. I was — " he gestured vaguely at his side, at the floor, at the entire unfairness of the world — "I was resting first."

"Resting," Marguerite said, "on a wound that needs stitching."

"It's stopped bleeding."

"That's not the same as stopped being a problem."

"No," Luc agreed, and then, because he was a clerk and clerks notice things, his eyes narrowed. "You checked my hands. First thing. Before my face, before the wound. You looked at the ink." He said it not with accusation but with the wonder of a man doing arithmetic and arriving at an impossible sum. "Only someone who'd been trained to read hands would do that. Courier training. Cipher sections. They teach you to inventory a stranger in three seconds — hands, boots, eyes, accent." He was sitting up now, pain forgotten, staring at Marguerite with something that was horribly close to hope. "You're one of ours. You're *Republic*."

Marguerite said nothing. Silence was survival. Silence had been survival for seven years, through the misdelivered letter and the family in the grey house and the long education in what speaking cost.

But Luc was still looking at them, and the candle from the far end of the crypt threw a thin line of light across his face, and Marguerite thought, with a sensation like a floor giving way: *he's someone's son. Someone taught him his letters. Someone is going to receive a form letter about him, or worse, receive nothing at all.*

"I can't help you," Marguerite said quietly. "I'm sorry. I'm carrying something that matters more than one man, and every extra person on that road is a chance of everything failing."

"I'm not asking to be carried." Luc's voice cracked and he steadied it. "I'm asking not to die in a hole. There's a difference. I can walk — I've been walking, I walked thirty kilometres on this — " he touched his side again, grimaced — "I can walk as far as you need. I'll keep behind the cart. I'll keep out of sight. I won't learn what you're carrying, I won't learn your name, I'll forget your face the moment we part. Just — point me south. Tell me which ditches to lie in. You know the roads. You know the checkpoints. I know nothing; I'm a clerk who ran." He laughed, a small broken sound. "I used to file requisitions. Do you know what it was I finally couldn't file? The deportation schedules. Names. Whole families, listed like grain shipments. I copied them out in a fair hand and then I sat there looking at my own handwriting and I thought: *this is what I'm good at. This is my contribution.* And I got up and walked out."

Marguerite's chest did something complicated.

Because that was the Republic. That was the thing they had served for seven years with blank paper and a straight back: a machine that turned decent people into handwriting, that asked a young clerk to make murder legible and called it administration. Marguerite had known this. They had known it and stayed anyway, because the work was survivable if you didn't look at it, and Marguerite was very good at not looking. They had made a whole life of the side-glance, the averted eye, the name never asked.

And now here was a boy in a crypt who had looked — really looked, at his own fair hand — and broken himself on it, and was lying in the dark asking Marguerite, of all people, for the mercy Marguerite had spent seven years perfecting the art of withholding.

"I was trained the same way you were," Luc said, softer. "Whatever section you were. I can tell. We're the same, you and I. We learned to be furniture." He paused. "But furniture doesn't bleed. I'd like to stop being furniture. Even if it's only for another week."

* * *

Marguerite went back up the stair and stood in the ambulatory among the sleeping shapes, and Odile, who missed nothing even with her eyes closed, opened them.

"You've been gone twenty minutes," she said, sitting up. "And you've come back looking like you've been arguing with a priest who isn't here. What is it?"

"There's a man in the crypt. A deserter. Wounded. He's been here two days; Anselme's been feeding him."

Odile absorbed this. "And?"

"And he recognized my training. He knows what I am — not my name, but my *kind*. And he wants to come south with us."

Tomas, who had been pretending to sleep with the dedication of a man who snores too loudly to fool anyone, rolled over. "How badly is he hurt?"

"Badly enough that he shouldn't walk. Well enough that he can."

"Then he walks behind the cart," Tomas said, as though the matter were the mule's mood. "We've a cart, we've a road, we've a man who can't stay. What's the argument?"

"The argument," Marguerite said, "is that there are three checkpoints, and at each one the number of things that can go wrong multiplies by the number of people in our company. One deserter found with us and every one of us is a collaborator. The ledger, the names, all of it — gone, because I felt sorry for a clerk." They heard their own voice and hated its neatness, its arithmetic. It was the voice of the grey house in 1791. It was the voice that had once explained, very reasonably, why a misdelivered letter couldn't be corrected, why the family couldn't be warned, why silence was the professional choice.

Odile was looking at them with an expression Marguerite was beginning to dread, because it was the expression of a woman about to be right.

"You said 'a clerk,'" she said.

"What?"

"The deserter. You said *a clerk*. You know what he was, in the army. You've talked to him." She folded her hands in her lap, the way she did when the stew was about to be served and the truth with it. "Marguerite. You went down there to send him away, and instead you've come back and told us his trade. That's not a report. That's a plea wearing a report's coat."

"It's an assessment."

"It's a *person*," Odile said, "and you've already decided he's one of yours. I can see it. You've got the look of someone carrying something they haven't admitted to carrying. I had a husband with that look for thirty years. It's how we ended up with Sylvie's goat."

"The goat is not analogous—"

"The goat is entirely analogous. You feed a thing once, and it's yours." She said it gently, and that was the worst of it. "You've fed him already. You fed him your attention. You stood in the dark and listened to his whole story. Marguerite, love — people who intend to walk away don't ask for the details."

Marguerite opened their mouth and found, to their considerable alarm, nothing in it. Seven years of silence, and it failed them now, in a crypt, before a widow with a mended seam.

From the top of the stair came the soft fall of bare feet, and Sylvie's whisper, pitched with the discretion of a town crier: "I heard everything. Is the deserter nice? Bertrand says we should help him."

"Bertrand," Marguerite said, "is a skull."

"Bertrand," Sylvie said, "has perspective. He's seen empires come and go. He thinks this one will be the same."

"Sylvie," Odile said, "go back to sleep."

"He's *wounded*, Maman. We can't just—"

"*Sleep*, Sylvie. We'll decide in the morning, all of us, like a committee, which is how every terrible decision in history has been made." But she said it with the corner of her mouth lifted, and Sylvie, reading the family code fluently, grinned into the dark and withdrew.

Marguerite sat down on the cold step. Below them, a young man was bleeding slowly in the house of God, waiting on the judgment of a turnip cart. Somewhere south, an envoy waited on a ledger. Somewhere north, an empire waited on nothing at all; it had all the time in the world.

"The arithmetic is against him," Marguerite said at last. "You understand that. It's not sentiment, it's — every body on that cart is a variable. He's the most dangerous variable we could add. A deserter, at a checkpoint, is a search. A search is the mattress. The mattress is everything."

"Yes," Odile said.

"And if we leave him, Anselme will hide him until he heals or doesn't, and if the imperials come through the abbey — and they will, they requisition everything with a roof — they'll find him, and they'll ask who else the père has been hiding, and the père will lie, badly, because he's a priest and priests lie like they sing: with conviction and no technique. And then—"

"Yes," Odile said again, softer.

"—and then we've left him to buy our safety with his, which is what the Republic does. Which is what I *was*." Marguerite heard it land in the dark, the true thing, the thing they had been circling for eleven days. "I left a family once. Because the arithmetic said so. I've been doing the arithmetic ever since, and it has always said so, and it has always been *right*, and I have never once slept well."

Odile put her hand over theirs. Her hands were flour-and-lye hands, working hands, hands that had fed a stranger stew on the first night without asking a single question, and Marguerite found their eyes stinging and was furious about it.

"Then do the other sum," she said. "The one where you're not the Republic anymore."

* * *

In the morning, Marguerite went down into the crypt with clean bandages and the needle and thread Odile pressed on them, and found Luc awake, watching the stair, waiting — Marguerite realized — the way people wait for verdicts, with his whole body pretending not to.

"Here's how it will be," Marguerite said, kneeling to look at the wound. It was ugly but clean, a furrow through the flesh above his hip, and Marguerite had seen far worse and stitched far worse on far less grateful patients. "You walk behind the cart. You speak to no one. If we're stopped, you're a farmhand with a bad back who fell off a wall — Tomas will teach you the details, he enjoys teaching. You don't learn what we carry. You don't learn my name. And if I tell you to run, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't argue."

Luc was quiet a moment. "And if I argue?"

"Then I'll leave you here with Bertrand, who will outvote you."

Something moved across Luc's face — relief, disbelief, the particular expression of a man who had prepared his soul for a refusal and now had nowhere to put it. "Thank you," he said, and it came out rough. "I won't make you sorry."

"You will," Marguerite said, threading the needle. "Everyone makes everyone sorry. That's what people are." They began to stitch. "But we'll manage it. Hold still."

Luc held still. Halfway through, he hissed with pain and said, through his teeth, "You stitch like a field medic."

"Courier training covers more than hands," Marguerite said. "We're taught to be useful in small ways. It makes us harder to remember."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "It's mine, though. I'm fond of it."

Luc laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway, and the sound went up the stair into the cold morning where the others were waking, and Marguerite sat in the candle-smelling dark with a stranger's blood on their fingers and thought: *this is what it costs. This is what it has always cost. Not the danger — the wanting.*

They were tying off the last stitch when Luc went rigid under their hands, all the ease gone out of him at once.

"What is it?"

"Yesterday," Luc said. "Before you came. I heard the imperial column on the north road. I heard the officers talking — I was close enough, in the ditch." His eyes had gone bright and frightened. "They were discussing the transport schedule. Marguerite — I'm sorry, that's your name, the père said it — they were discussing when the deportation trains leave Saint-Aubin."

Marguerite's hands went still.

"How long," they said, "do we have?"
