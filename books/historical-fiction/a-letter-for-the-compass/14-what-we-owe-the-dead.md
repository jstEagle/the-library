## 14. What We Owe the Dead

Luc had learned to measure time in ditches.

Three weeks in the mud along the north road taught a man things no drill sergeant ever could: which hours the patrol horses drank at the ford, how long a column's cook-fires stayed lit after the men slept, and — most usefully of all — that officers, when they believed themselves unobserved, spoke about schedules the way farmers spoke about weather. Openly. Wearily. With the particular boredom of men who did not know they were delivering a death sentence to anyone crouched forty paces away in the nettles.

So when Luc opened his mouth in the abbey crypt, with his leg sewn shut and a stranger's hands still tacky with his blood, what came out was not panic. It was arithmetic.

"Four days," he said. "Maybe five if the rains swell the river and hold the wagons at Vellacourt. The trains leave from the freight siding past the tannery. First one carries the registry classifications — the marked families. The second carries whoever's left on the lists when they finish checking papers." He swallowed. "I heard a lieutenant complain that the paperwork was behind schedule. He said it like it was an insult to him personally."

The candle between them guttered. Somewhere above, Père Anselme's footsteps crossed the chapel floor, slow as a man counting his own doubts.

Marguerite did not move. Luc had watched people receive bad news his whole life — he'd been a conscript's clerk before he'd been a deserter, and before that a son watching his mother read the letter about his father — and he had catalogued every variety of flinch. The blink. The swallow. The terrible bright-eyed stillness of the newly widowed.

Marguerite did none of these. Marguerite simply became *quieter*, which Luc had not previously known was possible for a person already speaking in half-voices.

"Four days," Marguerite repeated. Not a question. A weight, set down on a table, to be examined from all sides.

"I'm sorry." Luc meant it. He also noticed, distantly, that his own terror had changed shape since yesterday. Yesterday he had been afraid for himself — a deserter with a hole in his leg, hiding in a crypt, waiting for the fever or the patrols to decide which got him first. Now there were other people down here breathing his air, and their danger had attached itself to him like burrs. He found he minded. He found he *preferred* minding. It was better than the ditch, where he'd had nothing to fear *for*, only fear itself, going around and around like a millstone with no grain.

"The ledger," Marguerite said. "The envoy. Four days is—"

"It's enough," Luc said, and then heard himself, and laughed — a cracked, awful sound in the stone room. "Forgive me. I've spent three weeks telling myself *one more day* is enough. It's a habit of the dying."

"You're not dying."

"My leg disagrees."

"Your leg is a coward," Marguerite said, and began packing the needle away with brisk, unhurried movements, as though the world had four days left in it and they intended to use three of them for tidiness. "Drink this. All of it. Yes, it tastes like that; the père insists on the recipe."

* * *

Marguerite climbed the hidden stair with the candle cupped close, and stood for a long moment in the dark behind the sacristy wall, listening to Père Anselme argue gently with God through the confessional grille.

Four days.

The plan had been built on eleven. Eleven days had felt, when Odile laid them out on the kitchen table in flour and spilled salt, almost luxurious — time to wait out the bridge checkpoint's rotation, time to let Sylvie's novice cover season, time for Tomas to mend the cart axle properly instead of hopefully. Eleven days was a plan.

Four days was a gamble wearing a plan's coat.

And now there was a fifth body. That was the thought Marguerite kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. A wounded deserter who could barely walk, who knew Marguerite's face and training, who would have to lie flat under sacking in Odile's cart beside a mattress with a stolen ledger sewn into its ticking, while imperial sergeants counted heads and asked questions.

Every extra body was a debt. That had always been the arithmetic. One courier travels light; two travelers are a pair, and pairs get remembered; five is a *group*, and groups get names, and names go in ledgers —

Marguerite stopped in the dark.

Names go in ledgers. They had learned precisely, this week, what happened to names in ledgers.

They went up into the chapel light.

* * *

The kitchen of the inn smelled of onions and rain, and held everyone: Odile at the hearth with a spoon she was using less for cooking than for emphasis, Sylvie cross-legged on the bench shelling beans with the ferocious efficiency of a girl who had been told to make herself useful and intended to overachieve, Tomas by the door dripping from the yard, and Père Anselme just arrived, wringing rainwater from his sleeves with an air of a man whose laundry was the least of his compromises.

Marguerite gave them the numbers plainly. The trains. The four days. The collapsed schedule, each stage of the journey re-laid end to end until it no longer fit inside the time remaining.

The silence afterward had texture. Sylvie's hands stilled over the beans. Tomas looked at his boots as though the axle might apologize.

"So we don't sleep," Sylvie said finally.

"We sleep," Odile said. "We sleep *efficiently*. There's a difference." She pointed the spoon at her daughter. "You will sleep because you cannot pass for a novice with shadows under your eyes. Novices are serene. Serenity requires rest. This is theology."

"That is not theology, Mama."

"It is my inn and it is theology here."

Tomas cleared his throat. "The axle can be done in a day if I skip the fitting and trust my eye. I've trusted my eye twenty years. My eye has only lied to me twice, and both times it was about a woman."

"Once," Odile said.

"...Once," Tomas conceded.

Marguerite watched them do it — watched them take the catastrophe and hand it around the room like dishes to be washed, each person breaking off a piece small enough to carry — and felt the old vertigo rise, the familiar cold knowledge that this was exactly what they had sworn never to do, never to need, never to *have*: a table full of people rearranging their lives around a shared danger.

Then Odile turned to them and said, "Well? You're the one who knows the road. Talk us through it," and the vertigo passed, and what remained underneath it was something steadier and far more frightening, and Marguerite talked them through it.

* * *

It took until well past midnight. The bridge checkpoint, Marguerite explained, rotated at dawn — they could be across it within the hour after changeover if the cart looked boring enough, and Odile's cart had a genius for looking boring that bordered on the sacred. But the abbey road added half a day they no longer had, unless they left tomorrow night instead of the night after, which meant the forged passes had to be finished tomorrow, which meant—

"Which means someone is up all night copying passes," Sylvie said, with the satisfaction of a girl doing sums in public, "and that someone has beautiful handwriting, and that someone is you."

"That someone," Marguerite agreed, "is me."

"And *that* someone," Odile said, standing, gathering bowls with the authority of a field marshal, "will do it downstairs where I can feed them. Nobody plans a desperate flight on an empty stomach in my house. It's unlucky."

When the others had gone up — Tomas to the forge-loft, Anselme to the chapel, Sylvie protesting her way to bed — Marguerite sat alone at the kitchen table with ink, paper, and the low fire, and did not begin work immediately.

They thought about Luc instead.

About the moment in the crypt when the man had said *four days* and then apologized, as though the timetable were his personal failing. About the way he'd laughed at his own hope. About the ditch — three weeks alone in a ditch with a wound going bad, listening to officers discuss your neighbors' deportation with the irritation of men behind on their paperwork, and surviving it not through courage but through the sheer animal refusal to stop measuring time.

Marguerite knew that refusal intimately. Had lived inside it for years. It was the whole of their architecture: keep moving, keep silent, keep light. Never carry anything you can't abandon. Never owe anything you can't walk away from.

And yet.

Here was the trouble with debts: once you saw them clearly, they accumulated interest whether you acknowledged them or not. Luc had seen Marguerite's name spoken in the crypt. Luc had a hole in his leg acquired fleeing the same army that now held the ledger with Marguerite's alias in it. Every hour Luc lay in that crypt was an hour closer to the patrols finding him, and every patrol that found him was a patrol that might find the abbey, the stair, the household, the plan. Leaving him was not safety. It was merely a slower way of paying.

There was no version of the next four days in which Marguerite did not pay. The only choice left was *how* — and, for the first time in their adult life, *with whom*.

Marguerite dipped the pen and began the first pass, and the handwriting came out steady, which surprised them, and then did not surprise them at all.

* * *

They woke Luc near dawn, quietly, with a finger to their lips.

"How is the leg?"

"Insolent," Luc said. "But mine."

"Can you travel lying flat under sacking for six hours?"

Luc considered this with the gravity it deserved. "I have traveled worse ways. I once rode eleven miles clinging to the back of a munitions wagon during a retreat, praying with great sincerity to a saint I don't believe in."

"And did the saint deliver?"

"The wagon did. I've trusted vehicles ever since." He tried to sit up, winced, managed it anyway. "You're taking me. I can tell. You have the look of a person composing a list of everything that can go wrong."

"I have that look permanently."

"No." Luc shook his head slowly. "I hid in a ditch for three weeks watching soldiers. I know what frightened looks like. You aren't frightened of being caught. You're frightened of—" he searched for it "—of us. Of the fact that you're doing this with other people, and some part of you has already started to mind what happens to us." He smiled, tired and crooked. "It's all right. I mind too. It's much better than the ditch."

Marguerite stood very still in the candle-dark.

"Sleep," they said at last. "We leave tomorrow night."

"And after tomorrow night?"

"After tomorrow night," Marguerite said, "we count candles, count soldiers, count lies — and we spend four days' worth of luck in one night, and pray the envoy counts faster than the trains do."

Luc lay back. Just before sleep took him he said, softly, "Thank you," and Marguerite, who had spent seven years learning never to answer thanks because answers created connections and connections created debts, heard themselves reply:

"Don't thank me yet. By tomorrow night you may wish you'd stayed in the ditch."

But upstairs, as they trimmed the lamp, Marguerite allowed themselves one honest thought, small and warm as a coal raked forward against the morning cold:

Five bodies on the cart. Five debts. And for the first time, they found they did not want the arithmetic any other way.
