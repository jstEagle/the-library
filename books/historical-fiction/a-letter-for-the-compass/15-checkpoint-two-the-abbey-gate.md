## 15. Checkpoint Two: The Abbey Gate

Sylvie had always thought terror would feel like falling. Instead it felt like laundry—cold, damp, and impossible to put down.

She was carrying a basket of alms bread that weighed nothing and meant everything, and her legs had gone to water somewhere around the third Hail Mary she'd whispered since dawn.

"Stop praying," Marguerite murmured beside her, without moving their lips. They were dressed as an abbey lay servant, gray and forgettable, which Sylvie thought was deeply unfair given how memorable they'd become to her over eleven days. "You're mumbling. Novices don't mumble."

"Novices don't smuggle ledgers either."

"Novices don't *know* they're smuggling ledgers," Marguerite said. "You're a girl bringing bread to the poor. That is the whole of your biography. If anyone asks, you were born bored."

* * *

The hidden stair behind Saint-Anselme's reliquary wall had been built, according to Père Anselme, by monks who wished to move between the abbey and the river road without troubling the abbot's conscience or the tax collector's ledger. It smelled of four centuries of stone dust and one decade of pigeon. Sylvie went down it sideways, one hand on the cold wall, the mattress—her mother's mattress, sewn through with a nation's sins—lashed to a handcart Tomas pushed ahead of her with the serene expression of a man hauling turnips.

The mattress had taken Odile most of the night. She'd unpicked the ticking with a fishbone needle, folded the ledger flat into the straw-stuffed middle, and stitched it back up with stitches so even that Sylvie, watching, felt a strange pride she couldn't explain. Her mother had been sewing other people's names back together for years. She simply hadn't known it.

"Hold still," Odile had said around the pins in her mouth. "If I stab you, you'll bleed on the evidence."

"That's your concern? Not my suffering?"

"Suffering washes out. Blood doesn't." A pause, and then, softer, in the way her mother said true things: "Neither does fear, but we carry both anyway."

Now, at the bottom of the stair, the morning was gray and busy. The imperial supply column had camped overnight along the river road—forty wagons, sixty soldiers, a herd of requisitioned oxen with the mournful expressions of animals who had also not consented to any of this. And threading through it all, as they had every dawn since the occupation began, came the novices of Saint-Anselme: twelve girls in undyed wool, carrying bread and broth down to the camp followers and teamsters, because the abbess held that charity did not check uniforms, and because the abbess also held, privately, that girls who walked familiar among soldiers learned things worth reporting.

Mother Cécile had agreed to add three extra figures to her line. She had looked at Sylvie for a long moment before dawn and said only, "Walk as if God is watching and He is not impressed by anything you do. That's how the others walk."

It was, Sylvie reflected, the most accurate description of novice life ever uttered.

* * *

They joined the line of white-cowled girls just past the abbey's side gate, slipping in at the rear like drops of water finding a stream. The real novices barely glanced at them—one, a round-faced girl named Béatrice, gave Sylvie a nod of profound conspiracy, as though they were all co-conspirators in the great crime of being seventeen.

Ahead, the supply column stretched toward the bridge road. Beyond it, half a mile on, stood the checkpoint: two wagons drawn across the road, a sergeant with a list, and the particular boredom of men who had been standing in mud since October and would be standing in mud until spring.

"The mattress cart stays behind me," Tomas rumbled under his breath. He wore his blacksmith's smock and carried his requisition passes fanned in his fist like a losing hand of cards he intended to bluff on anyway. "Nobody looks at what follows a man who looks like he belongs to it."

"And me?" Luc asked. He was propped in the cart bed among empty flour sacks, gray-faced, his wounded shoulder packed with abbey comfrey, wearing a dead teamster's coat two sizes too large. In the last day he had aged ten years and gained a stubbornness to match.

"You're sick," Tomas said. "Fever, bad lungs, going home to die where your mother can scold you properly. Nobody interrogates dying men. It feels unlucky."

Luc smiled faintly. "In the Republic they taught us to be useful. Nobody mentioned being pitiable."

"The Republic taught you nothing about people," Odile said briskly, from beside her mattress. "People are moved by pity, vanity, and hunger, in that order. Now hush, all of you. We're just a household going about its morning. The less interesting we are, the more invisible."

Marguerite made a small sound—not quite a laugh.

"What?" Odile asked.

"Nothing." But Sylvie caught their face in profile, and there was something almost tender in it. "That used to be my line. Before I met any of you."

"It was a good line," Odile said. "I'm keeping it."

* * *

The line moved. Sylvie counted her breathing the way Marguerite had taught her—in for four, hold for seven, out for eight—though Marguerite had framed it as a trick for steadying a courier's hands before a delivery, and Sylvie was using it to keep from being sick into the alms basket.

The first wagon passed the checkpoint with a bored wave. The second was opened and rummaged through while its driver argued cheerfully about the price of hay. Then the novices.

The sergeant who stopped them was young, with a wispy attempt at facial hair and the air of a man performing authority he didn't believe in. He consulted a paper. "Alms for the camp?"

"Yes, sergeant," said the lead novice, a composed girl named Constance who could have recited the catechism backward while defusing a bomb. "Bread, broth, and lint for bandages. Mother Cécile's compliments."

The sergeant peered down the line. His gaze traveled over white cowls and lowered eyes and landed, with the unerring instinct of the young male for the wrong detail, on Sylvie.

"You. Girl. You're new."

Sylvie's heart performed a maneuver previously achieved only by startled birds. "Y-yes, sergeant."

"Why?"

"Because—" Think. Think like Marguerite. What would Marguerite say? Something boring. Something so boring it circled back around to fascinating. "Because Sister Véronique says I pray too loudly and should practice humility among sinners."

There was a beat of silence. Down the line, Béatrice made a noise like a swallowed sneeze.

The sergeant blinked. "That sounds like Sister Véronique," he admitted, with the weary solidarity of a man who had clearly been prayed at by the same sister. He waved them on—and then his eye snagged, as Sylvie had known with sickening certainty it would, on the handcart. On the mattress.

He raised a hand. "Wait."

Everything in the world narrowed to the width of a stitching hole.

"Tomas," Odile said quietly, "keep walking. You've a delivery at the far wagons." Her voice was perfectly ordinary—the voice that had told Sylvie a thousand times to stop dawdling, to bring in the wood, to stop reading at table. Ordinary was armor; Sylvie understood that now. Ordinary was the best disguise there was.

Tomas trundled on. The sergeant let him go, because Tomas was right: nobody stops the man who belongs to the thing.

But he stepped into Odile's path. "That mattress. Unload it."

"Unload it?" Odile repeated, in the tone of a woman who has been asked to explain rain.

"We're checking goods moving south. Orders." He tapped his paper importantly.

Odile looked at him. Sylvie watched her mother look at him, and something in her chest loosened and tightened at once, because she knew this look—it was the look Odile gave to salesmen, to tax assessors, to Sylvie herself when caught mid-lie about where the jam had gone. It was the look of a woman who found the entire situation mildly beneath her.

"Young man," Odile said, "do you know what's in that mattress?"

The sergeant hesitated. "...Straw?"

"Straw, wool, horsehair, and the sweat of nine months of a dead man's sickness." Odile's voice dropped into the register she used for funerals and hard bargains. "My husband slept on it for the winter before he died, coughing his lungs into it, may God rest him. I'm taking it south to burn it, along with everything else he touched, because I cannot bear to sleep where he suffered, and I cannot afford a new one, and the priest says burning it will help me grieve, though I think the priest mostly wants the wool tithed. Now. Would you like to unload it? Would you like to put your hands on it? Because I will let you. I will stand here in this mud while you pull apart a widow's grief stitch by stitch, and then I will tell your captain exactly which of his soldiers spent the morning handling a plague-soaked tick, and we'll see whose week improves."

Silence. Somewhere behind them, an ox lowed with what Sylvie could have sworn was sympathy.

The sergeant looked at the mattress. He looked at Odile. He looked, briefly and desperately, at the sky, as if appealing for guidance and receiving none.

"Move along," he said hoarsely.

"Bless you," said Odile, with such serene piety that Sylvie nearly applauded. "And sergeant—wash your hands."

* * *

They were fifty paces past the checkpoint before anyone dared speak, and even then it was Luc, wheezing with silent laughter from the cart. "Plague-soaked tick. *Plague-soaked tick.*"

"I never said plague," Odile said primly. "I said sickness. He heard what fear told him to hear."

"You lied beautifully," Sylvie breathed. "Maman, you lied *beautifully.*"

"Twenty-two years married to a man who owed money in six towns," Odile said. "Lying beautifully is the only inheritance I ever got, and I'm spending all of it on you children."

Sylvie felt the word land somewhere under her ribs. *Children.* Plural. Including, unmistakably, the gray-clad figure walking at the line's edge who was currently staring very hard at the road as though it contained instructions.

Beside her, Marguerite exhaled—a long, controlled breath, the kind they took when the ground stopped trying to swallow them. Their hands, Sylvie noticed, were shaking. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You did it too," Sylvie said softly. "At the bridge yesterday. You lied like breathing."

"Lying is the easy part." Marguerite kept their eyes forward. "Anyone can lie alone. Lying in front of people who—" They stopped. Recalibrated. Started again, quieter. "It's different when there's someone to lose. I never used to have that variable."

"And now?"

Marguerite glanced at her. For once, they didn't deflect, didn't retreat into clerk-gray silence. "Now I keep counting everyone," they said, "and the arithmetic terrifies me."

"Good," said Sylvie. "Terrified means you care. Maman taught me that. Terrified people check their work."

A breath of a laugh escaped them—real, unguarded, gone almost immediately but *there*, and Sylvie treasured it the way she'd once treasured stolen sugar: proof that sweetness existed even in wartime.

* * *

The column thinned as the road bent south. The wagons fell away, the soldiers became a rumor of smoke behind them, and by late morning they were walking through bare orchards where the last apples hung like forgotten lanterns. The novices peeled off toward the abbey path with waves and one shouted blessing from Béatrice ("Pray for us, whoever you actually are!"), and then it was five travelers and a mattress full of treason, alone on the long road to the frontier.

They made camp at dusk in a stone barn, half-ruined, smelling sweetly of old hay. Odile produced bread and hard cheese and the small miracle of dried plums, and they ate sitting in a row like sparrows on a rail, passing the water skin down the line.

It was almost peaceful. Which was, Sylvie would learn, when the world liked to collect its debts.

Hoofbeats on the road. One rider, then another. Through the gap in the barn wall, Sylvie saw them: imperial cavalry, moving fast southward, and behind them—her stomach dropped—a carriage with dark curtains and no insignia, the kind of vehicle that belonged to men whose names were never written down.

Marguerite rose without a sound and pressed their eye to the crack. Sylvie watched their whole body change—the shoulders locking, the jaw going tight, a flint striking somewhere behind their eyes.

"What?" Odile whispered. "What is it?"

"The carriage," Marguerite said. Their voice had gone flat and cold, a door closing. "I know the man inside it. He's riding ahead of us. Straight toward the customs house."

"Who?" Sylvie asked.

Marguerite turned from the wall, and in the last light through the broken roof, Sylvie saw something she had never seen on their face before. Not fear. Not caution.

Hatred, worn like a borrowed coat that fit perfectly.

"My old handler," they said. "The man who sold our names. He's going to be at the gate tomorrow."
