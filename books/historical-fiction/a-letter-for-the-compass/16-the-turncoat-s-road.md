## 16. The Turncoat's Road

The south road out of Saint-Aubin was the color of old porridge that morning, churned to a grey-brown slurry by three days of rain and the boots of an army that did not care where it walked. Marguerite crouched behind the drystone wall at the bend above the mill, a basket of eggs at their feet as props and a headache building behind their left eye as payment for a night spent memorizing checkpoint rotations.

Beside them, Tomas shifted his weight with all the grace of a man built like a church bell trying to be quiet about it.

"You said we'd watch for an hour," he whispered.

"I said we'd watch until I saw something worth reporting."

"And have you?"

Marguerite had. That was the problem.

The column came up from the north at a walking pace—six imperial soldiers in the mud-grey of the occupation, two supply wagons, and a mounted officer whose horse was too fine for his uniform and whose uniform was too fine for his posture. He rode slouched, one hand loose on the reins, scanning the hedgerows with the lazy competence of a man who had once done this work for the other side.

Marguerite knew him.

Not from the war. Not from wanted notices or whispered descriptions at dead-drop sites. They knew him the way you know the shape of your own hand: Étienne Marchal, section chief of the Republic's courier bureau, who had signed off on Marguerite's first forged papers, who had taught them the rule about loving places the way one loves train platforms, who had poured them wine in a safehouse in Verron and said, *You are nothing to them, courier, and that is your armor. Wear it well.*

Who had then sold every cover he'd ever issued, name by name, alias by alias, in exchange for whatever the empire gave its turncoats. A pension, probably. A warm posting. Perhaps just permission to keep living, which Marguerite understood better than they wished to.

He was forty meters away and closing, and Marguerite's hands had gone cold around the basket handle in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about fury: it is loud inside and silent outside, and if you are trained properly, no one can hear it at all. Marguerite had been angry before—in the professional sense, the useful sense, anger banked like a stove fire and fed only when heat was required. This was different. This rose up from somewhere below discipline, below grief, from the place where seven years of silences were stored, and it wanted very simple things. It wanted to stand up out of the mud. It wanted the knife in their boot. It wanted Étienne Marchal's throat and the thirty seconds it would take, and it wanted those things with a clarity that frightened Marguerite more than any checkpoint ever had.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Marguerite hated arithmetic lately: the ledger was sewn into Odile's spare mattress under the flour sacks in Tomas's cart, currently parked behind the mill with Odile sitting on its bench looking like exactly what she was, a widow with errands and no interest whatsoever in anyone else's war. Sylvie was at the abbey with Père Anselme. The plan—the whole improbable, ordinary-people plan—required three more days of invisibility. Of Marguerite being no one, from nowhere, carrying eggs.

And Étienne Marchal was riding toward them down the one road they needed to use.

"Tomas," Marguerite breathed. "Don't look at the officer."

"I wasn't going to look at the officer."

"Everyone says that and then everyone looks at the officer. Look at your hands. Count your knuckles."

Tomas counted his knuckles. It took him visibly longer than most people would require.

Through the gap in the wall, Marguerite watched the rider come on. Seven years, and the man had barely changed. Greyer at the temples. Heavier through the jaw. But the eyes still moved the way Marguerite remembered—never resting, never meeting anyone's, cataloguing everything while appearing to catalogue nothing. Marguerite had spent years imitating that gaze without ever asking themselves why it felt familiar. Now they knew. Now they knew exactly how much of their own craft had been learned at this man's knee, and the knowledge sat in their chest like swallowed river water.

*You are nothing to them,* he'd said, wine-warm and kind. *That is your armor.*

It had not occurred to Marguerite, then, to ask whether he considered himself something to them. Or whether he had ever been anything to anyone at all, or simply a man collecting other people's secrets the way a magpie collects buttons—because they shone, because he could, because owning them meant never having to own himself.

The column drew level with the bend. Twenty meters. Fifteen.

And then, because the world has a sense of humor that runs entirely to cruelty, one of the wagon wheels struck a stone, the driver swore, and Étienne Marchal turned his head—directly, lazily, incuriously—toward the drystone wall where a clerk and a blacksmith were hiding among the eggs.

Ten meters. Five. The horse's breath steamed.

Marchal looked at Marguerite.

Marguerite looked back with the flat, bored, faintly resentful expression of a government clerk delayed on official business, and inside that mask, behind that face, they thought with perfect distinctness: *I could do it. Right now. Before his escort even understands. I could end him.*

And then Odile's voice arrived unbidden, the way her voice had developed an infuriating habit of doing: *Invisibility kept you alive, child. It never kept you living.*

If Marguerite killed Étienne Marchal beside a muddy road in front of six imperial soldiers, the ledger died with them. Odile died with them. Sylvie, who asked too many questions and offered too much help and had sewn a second hidden pocket into the mattress lining because she worried the first wasn't clever enough—Sylvie died with them. All of it, everything, traded for thirty seconds of satisfaction and a corpse.

Marchal's gaze slid past. The column went on, mud sucking at the wheels, and after a long moment Marguerite discovered they had been holding their breath since roughly the previous Tuesday.

"Well," said Tomas.

"Don't," said Marguerite.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to ask if I know that officer."

Tomas considered this. "Were you going to lie?"

"No." Marguerite stood, knees protesting, and picked up the egg basket. "That's the trouble."

* * *

They reconvened at the inn after dark, in the kitchen, which had become over the past week the strategic heart of the entire resistance effort in Saint-Aubin, largely because it was warm and Odile kept producing food during meetings, which improved attendance dramatically.

Odile sat by the fire darning a sock that belonged to no one present. Tomas occupied a bench sized for two men his build. Marguerite stood by the window with their back to the room, watching the dark road, which was a habit they were aware of and had decided not to examine.

"The south road is patrolled now," they said. "Regularly. Mounted column, twice daily, morning and late afternoon. And the officer leading it—" Their jaw did something involuntary. "I know him."

The darning needle paused.

"Know him how?" Odile asked, in the tone she used for asking questions she already suspected were going to hurt.

"He ran the courier bureau. My bureau. Every route, every alias, every cover identity in the western districts passed across his desk. Mine included." Marguerite kept their voice level, report-shaped, because reports were safe. "When the Republic negotiated its surrender terms, someone supplied the imperial cryptographers with the full courier index. Names, covers, physical descriptions, routes. That's how my alias ended up in the requisitioned ledger. It didn't leak." Finally they turned from the window. "He sold it. Piece by piece, probably, over months. Buying his comfort with our necks."

The kitchen was quiet except for the fire.

"And he's on our road," Tomas said.

"He's on our road."

Sylvie, who was supposed to be asleep and had instead materialized on the stairs with the soundless expertise of a sixteen-year-old, said, "What did he look like? When you saw him today?"

"Sylvie," said Odile.

"What? It matters, doesn't it? Whether he recognized—" She stopped, because she caught it, the thing everyone else in the room was carefully not saying. She looked at Marguerite with wide, appalled eyes. "You wanted to kill him. Just now, today, on the road. You wanted to."

Marguerite said nothing, which was answer enough, and Sylvie sat down hard on the stair as though her legs had been quietly removed from service.

"He wouldn't have known me," Marguerite said at last. "That's the irony. I was one of forty couriers. He issued my papers; I doubt he ever looked at my face twice. I'm a signature in his ledger, same as everyone else." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "But I would have known him anywhere. Isn't that always the way of it? The person who betrays you gets to be a stranger, and you get to carry the whole memory."

Odile set down the sock. When she spoke, it was slow, the way she spoke when she was choosing words the way other people chose knives. "And if he'd stopped? Today? If he'd ridden over and looked at you properly?"

"Then I'd have lied beautifully and we'd have lost perhaps a day."

"And if he'd looked at you and *seen* you? Not the clerk. You. The way I saw you the first evening you sat in my kitchen lying about your paperwork?" She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she aimed it. "What then, Marguerite?"

Marguerite opened their mouth to produce the correct answer—there was always a correct answer, contingency upon contingency, drilled into them across seven years—and found, to their considerable irritation, that the correct answer had gone missing.

"I don't know," they admitted.

"Good," said Odile, and picked her sock back up. "That's the first honest thing you've said all week that didn't cost us a plan. Now sit down and tell us the rest of it. If this man patrols the road twice a day, he's a problem. Problems get solved at tables, not on hillsides with knives. Sit."

Marguerite sat. It occurred to them, distantly, that there had been a time—not so long ago, really—when no one on earth could have made them sit with a single word. The time appeared to be over. They were not entirely sure when it had happened, and they suspected that when they finally identified the exact moment, it would turn out to involve stew.

* * *

The rest of the evening went to the map.

Tomas spread it on the table and weighted the corners with salt cellars, and they bent over the south road together the way families in happier circumstances might bend over a bill. Two patrols daily meant a window between them—narrow, unreliable, dependent on imperial punctuality, which was decent but not religious. The bridge checkpoint was fixed. The customs house at the frontier was fixed. Everything between was mud and chance and one particular traitor on a good horse.

"We avoid him," Tomas said. "We go at night, we cut through the mill fields, we're on the cart before dawn. He rides his rounds, sees nothing, goes home to whatever traitors eat for dinner."

"It could work," Marguerite said.

"But?" said Odile, who had learned that whenever Marguerite approved of something quickly, there was a *but* the size of a cathedral attached.

"But he taught me everything I know about reading a road." Marguerite traced the mill fields with one finger. "Cut fences, flattened grass, cart ruts where no cart should be—he'll see them the way I see them, because I see them the way he taught me. We can't hide from a man using our own eyes. We have to be *boring*. We have to be so ordinary, so relentlessly unremarkable, that his training slides right off us. A widow moving her household goods. Papers in order. Nothing worth the price of dismounting."

"That was the plan already," Sylvie pointed out.

"Yes. And now it also has to survive a man who knows precisely what not-boring looks like." Marguerite straightened. "Which means tomorrow, before we move anything, I'm going to walk part of that road in broad daylight, as myself, the clerk, and I'm going to let him pass me at close range. I want to know if his eyes catch on me. If they don't, we're clear to move the ledger. If they do—" They shrugged. "Then we invent a new plan, because this one will be dead."

"That sounds," said Sylvie, with the delicacy of someone handling live coals, "enormously dangerous."

"It sounds enormously dangerous," Marguerite agreed, "and I am the only one who can do it, because I'm the only one who knows what to watch for in his face. That's the whole miserable job of being me."

Nobody argued. That was the strange part. Once—seven years ago, five years ago, last month—they would have expected argument, would have almost welcomed it, because argument confirmed that other people's lives were none of their business. Instead Odile just nodded and said, "Then you'll take a proper breakfast first," as though the two statements existed on the same scale of importance, and perhaps, Marguerite reflected, in her kitchen they did.

* * *

Morning came up white and cold. Marguerite walked the south road with a delivery docket in their pocket and their heartbeat filed neatly away where it couldn't interfere, and at half past nine, right on schedule, they heard hooves.

They did not look up. That was the first lesson and the hardest one: eyes down, shoulders tired, a clerk with sore feet and a dull morning. Let him come to you. Let him find nothing.

The hooves slowed.

Marguerite kept walking, counting fence posts, counting breaths, aware of the rider drawing level with a precision they resented deeply. Ten seconds alongside. Twenty. He was reading them. Not the face—the gait, the hands, the way a body held itself when it had something to hide. He had taught Marguerite to read bodies exactly this way, and now Marguerite stood in the open offering theirs up for inspection, praying to no god in particular that seven years of practice would hold, that the clerk was seamless, that the nothing was convincing.

Étienne Marchal reined in.

"Your business on this road, clerk?"

Marguerite turned and gave him the full weight of bureaucratic tedium. "Requisition forms for the garrison bakery, sir. The miller shorted us on the last delivery and I've been sent to argue about it." A pause, precisely calibrated. "Unless the army would like to explain to the garrison quartermaster why their bread is late."

Something flickered across the turncoat's face—not suspicion. Amusement. The shared, weary amusement of one functionary acknowledging another, the oldest brotherhood in the world.

"Argue well," Marchal said, and spurred on.

Marguerite watched him go, and the fury came again—quiet, patient, banked—and Marguerite fed it into the stove where it belonged, because the mission needed heat, not fire.

He hadn't recognized them. They were clear. They should have felt relief, and they did, somewhere underneath.

But as the hoofbeats faded, another detail surfaced, cold and unwelcome: the dispatch case strapped to Marchal's saddle had been new, military issue, and heavy—and he had ridden *toward* the frontier customs house, where, according to everything Marguerite had seen these past weeks, imperial cryptographers had recently taken up residence.

A man who sold one set of secrets rarely stopped at one sale.

By nightfall, word reached them through Père Anselme's parish channels that made the blood run thin: travelers' papers at the customs house were being checked against something new—a list, a profile, a systematic comparison that had already turned back three families in a single afternoon.

Whatever Marchal was carrying in that case, Marguerite was fairly certain they already knew its shape.

It was a list of names. And one of them, this time, would be a face he had just looked at and smiled at and ridden away from, waiting for the description to arrive that would make the smile retroactive.

They had perhaps two days. Maybe less.
