## 17. Broken Cipher

The rain came in off the western hills on the ninth day like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and with it came the boy from the mill with a wet scrap of paper folded into his boot.

Marguerite had taught themselves to read weather and messengers in the same season of their life, years ago, and neither skill had ever brought good news. The boy—Tomas's nephew, all elbows and freckles—stood dripping in the inn's kitchen while Odile pressed a towel on him and Sylvie hovered near the stove pretending not to listen, which was Sylvie's way of listening very hard.

"From the ferryman at Vaux," the boy said. "He said to give it to the clerk." His eyes flicked up at Marguerite, guileless, incurious. The town had decided long ago what Marguerite was—a clerk, a bit stiff, terrible at cards—and the town's decision was load-bearing. Marguerite took the paper without letting their fingers hurry.

"Thank you. Tell your uncle the invoice was correct."

The boy left. Odile went back to her bread. Sylvie kept chopping onions with the fierce concentration of someone who wanted very much to be elsewhere but knew better than to say so.

The paper was a Republic field cipher, third variant, the one Marguerite had carried through two winters of mud. It should have been unreadable to anyone who wasn't holding the matching key. Marguerite unfolded it by the window where the light was grayest, because some part of them already understood that the light didn't matter, and read:

INTERCEPTED STOP REPUBLIC CIPHER BOOK RECOVERED AT CHALONS STOP ALL COURIER PROFILES COMPROMISED STOP CHECKPOINTS INSTRUCTED TO DETAIN PERSONS MATCHING PROFILES STOP DESCRIPTIONS CIRCULATED TO CUSTOMS HOUSES EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY STOP GOD PRESERVE THE REPUBLIC

*God preserve the Republic.* Marguerite read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

Chalons. Chalons was a supply depot, a place of ledgers and locked drawers, a place where cipher books were supposed to be chained to desks like naughty dogs. If Chalons had fallen—and it must have fallen, or been sold, and there was only one person Marguerite could think of who sold things—the imperial cryptographers now held every courier profile the Republic had ever filed. Height. Age. Handedness. The little scars. The habits. *The clerk Vasquez-Marchand, twenty-six years, slight build, writes with the left hand, chews mint leaves, favors the road south, travels alone.*

Every line of it was true. That was the cruelty of good covers: they were built out of yourself, so when they burned, you burned with them.

"Bad news?" Odile asked, not looking up from her dough. She had a way of asking questions she already knew the answers to, which Marguerite suspected was how innkeepers survived wars.

"Invoice discrepancy," Marguerite said. "Nothing serious."

Odile nodded slowly, the way one nods at a child who has just blamed a broken vase on the cat, and said nothing, and kneaded harder.

* * *

Marguerite spent the afternoon doing what couriers do when the ground opens beneath them: arithmetic.

They sat in their room above the stable with the door wedged shut and a stub of candle, and they laid out the plan the way a surgeon lays out instruments, and watched it die instrument by instrument.

The customs house at the frontier had always been the hard gate. Three checkpoints, and the first two were theater—soldiers bored enough to wave a cart through if the passes looked right and the driver looked duller. But the customs house had examiners. Men who read documents the way Marguerite read ciphers, looking for the seam. And now those men would be working from a list.

Marguerite got out the forged papers they'd prepared for the crossing. *Vasquez-Marchand, commercial clerk, consignment of wool goods, Valcourt district.* Good work. They'd been quietly proud of it. Now every line of it was a confession. A slight-built clerk of twenty-six traveling alone, writing with the left hand, with a jaw that matched a description circulating in triplicate to every customs officer between here and the sea.

They tried variations, because despair is no excuse for sloppy method. Alter the age? The description said *twenty-five to twenty-eight*, and Marguerite's face refused to lie about it. Travel as a woman named something else? The profile included the scar above the eyebrow, obtained falling off a warehouse roof in their third year of service, an injury so stupid they had never told anyone about it, and which was now, apparently, a matter of imperial record. Shave the head? Disguise a limp? Marguerite caught themselves actually considering the limp, pencil hovering over a list titled *DISFIGUREMENTS, PRACTICABLE*, and set the pencil down with a small, disgusted click.

There was another way. There was always another way; that was the job. Cross alone, at night, over the fells east of the customs house where the patrols thinned. Four days' walk through country with no roads, carrying a ledger sewn into one's coat, arriving half-dead at the envoy's door—if the envoy was still there, if the passes weren't watched, if the rain stopped, if the war paused politely for a fortnight. And leaving behind the cart, the household, the whole improbable machine of ordinary people they'd assembled, to face whatever came after the deportations resumed.

Or: someone else carries the ledger through the front gate, and Marguerite does not go at all.

Marguerite sat with that second option the way one sits with a hot coal, because it was correct, and correctness had never once felt like safety.

The profile problem was the whole problem. The imperials weren't hunting a name; names could be changed overnight. They were hunting a *shape*. A young courier, alone, nervous in the particular trained way that couriers are nervous, with hands too steady and eyes that counted exits. Marguerite had spent seven years building that shape and wearing it until it fit like skin. No document in the world could peel it off in time.

But a widow shipping her husband's effects—

No. Marguerite shut the thought like a door on a cold draft. Absolutely not. That way lay chapter upon chapter of things going wrong to other people, and Marguerite had a rule about other people, and the rule had kept everyone alive for seven years, including, mostly, Marguerite.

The candle guttered. Downstairs, faintly, Sylvie was practicing scales on the out-of-tune piano, badly, with tremendous conviction, pausing after each wrong note to inform the note of its failure. Marguerite listened to it and found, to their considerable irritation, that it steadied them more than any prayer or plan had all day.

* * *

They told them at supper.

Not everything—never everything—but enough. The household gathered in the kitchen after the last guest had gone up, which these days meant Tomas and old Père Anselme, who arrived nightly under the pretext of complaining about the wine and stayed until Odile fed him. Luc slept upstairs still, fever down but not gone, dreaming in a dialect of curses.

"The ferryman sent word," Marguerite began, and then stopped, because the kitchen had gone quiet in the specific way kitchens go quiet when everyone already knows the shape of the sentence before it lands.

"They've broken the cipher," Odile said. Not a question.

Marguerite blinked. "How—"

"Sylvie reads faster upside-down than most people read right-side-up," Odile said, slicing bread with terrible serenity, "and you've been walking around since noon like a man whose dog died and whose horse left him for a better man besides. Also you called me 'madame' twice, and you haven't done that since the bridge."

"I apologize—"

"Don't you dare." She set the knife down. "We're past apologies, Marguerite. Tell us what it means."

So Marguerite told them. The cipher book lost at Chalons. The profiles circulated. The customs house checking every traveler against a description that fit Marguerite the way a glove fits a hand, because it had been made from the hand. They laid it out plainly, without drama, and watched the faces around the table do their work: Tomas's jaw tightening, Anselme's eyebrows climbing toward his tonsure, Sylvie going very still in the way she went still when she was frightened and furious about being frightened.

"So you can't carry it," Tomas said at last.

"I can't carry it *through the front*," Marguerite corrected. "There are ways over the mountains—"

"Through the fells," Anselme said. "In November. With a fevered man and—" he gestured gently at himself, "—knees that have opinions. My dear child, I have buried better mountaineers than any of us in better weather."

"It remains an option."

"It remains a eulogy with extra steps," said Sylvie.

Marguerite turned on her, sharper than intended. "It remains the option that doesn't put any of you in front of an imperial examiner."

"And the option where you vanish over the hills alone," Sylvie shot back, "puts us exactly nowhere, which is worse. We'd spend the rest of our lives waiting for news that never comes. I've done that math too. It comes out worse."

"It's not your risk to take."

"No," said Odile quietly, and the quiet landed harder than shouting. "But it seems it's ours anyway. You've been carrying everything alone so long you've forgotten it can be set down."

Marguerite looked around the table—at the blacksmith, the priest, the girl, the widow—and felt the old vertigo, the one from the early days, the feeling of standing on ground that might be pulled away. Every instinct they owned screamed the same sermon: *alone is safe, alone is proven, alone is how you're still breathing.* And underneath it, quieter, a new voice they hadn't authorized: *alone is also how you end, and this time you'd deserve it less than usual.*

"There may be a third way," Marguerite said slowly, hating every word as it came out, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be volunteered for. "Someone else takes the ledger through the customs house. Someone who isn't on any list. Someone whose story is boring beyond suspicion." They heard their own voice flatten into briefing-mode and couldn't stop it. "It would require nerve, a flawless story, and—" here they faltered, "—a night of my teaching. Forged countersignature. Courier hand. It's not—it's a great deal to ask of anyone, and I'm not asking. I want that understood. I am describing a possibility so we can all agree it's impossible and move on."

The kitchen was silent.

Then Odile Ferrand, widowed innkeeper of Saint-Aubin, mother of one, keeper of the worst wine cellar in the valley, folded her flour-dusted hands on the table and said, in the tone of a woman announcing tomorrow's soup:

"Well. I've been shipping my husband's effects south for three months now. Grief makes such slow paperwork. It seems only natural I'd finally finish the job."

And Marguerite, who had faced down soldiers, censors, and one memorable avalanche, found they had absolutely nothing to say.
