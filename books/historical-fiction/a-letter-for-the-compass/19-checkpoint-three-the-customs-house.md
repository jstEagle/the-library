## 19. Checkpoint Three: The Customs House

Dawn came to the frontier the color of old pewter, and Marguerite watched it arrive from the ditch behind the customs house with the particular calm of a person who has decided exactly how badly the day will go.

The building sat where the road narrowed between two granite markers—one carved with the imperial eagle, one with the Republic's faded cockerel, both now weathered into near-illiteracy. Beyond it, half a mile of no-man's-land, then the neutral territory where Envoy Halloran's carriage waited like a promise nobody had signed yet. The customs house had once been a toll station for wine merchants. Now it had sandbags, a flagpole, and a sergeant at the gate who examined papers with the leisurely cruelty of a man who enjoyed being the last obstacle in the world.

Marguerite lay in the wet grass and did what they had done every morning of their adult life: they counted.

One cart, waiting its turn among six others. Odile on the bench, dressed in her widow's black with her hands folded and her face arranged into that expression of patient, slightly put-upon grief that widows wear like a uniform. The ledger sewn flat beneath the mattress ticking, under a layer of her husband's winter clothes, under a folded quilt Sylvie had wept over for an hour before agreeing it should go. Two soldiers at the gate. One sergeant. One clerk inside, visible through the window. And—Marguerite's stomach tightened on schedule—the man on horseback circling the yard like a hawk that had learned to ride: Corvel. Their handler. The defector. The reason every courier cover in the Republic was worth less than the paper it was written on.

Corvel was checking faces. That was his talent, always had been. He'd taught Marguerite that a disguise fails not at the papers but at the eyes—that people look where they've been hurt. So Marguerite had spent the night before not disguising anything at all. They had sat by the inn's fire while Odile stitched a courier's satchel out of honest leather, and they had let their face do whatever it wanted. They had looked tired. They had looked afraid. They had looked, when Sylvie squeezed their hand goodnight, like someone who very much did not want to die tomorrow.

*Good*, Marguerite thought now, watching Corvel's gaze rake the queue of carts. *Look at me. I'm the most interesting thing here.*

Because that was the whole design, the whole mad architecture of the last three days: Odile would pass through as a widow shipping her dead husband's effects, and she would pass because nobody would look twice at her—not while something far more interesting was happening at the gate. Something loud. Something unmistakable.

Something like a courier being caught.

Marguerite checked the satchel one last time. Inside: cipher sheets in the Republic's current diplomatic key—real ones, salvaged, now worthless since Broken Cipher week had made them poison. A letter of transit bearing the alias *René Aubert*, clerk of the Interior Ministry, the identity they'd worn into Saint-Aubin eleven days ago and which the imperial cryptographers had already matched against courier profiles. And, tucked into the lining where a searching hand would find it in the first ten seconds, a scrap of paper with a fragmentary cipher key—a decoy, plausible enough to make an interrogator feel clever.

It was, Marguerite reflected, the strangest thing they had ever packed: a bag built to be caught.

They had argued about it. That was the remarkable part. Eleven days ago Marguerite would simply have done it and told no one, and the plan would have worked or killed them in comfortable solitude. Instead there had been a shouting match in Odile's kitchen—Tomas objecting that Marguerite couldn't be trusted alone, Père Anselme objecting that God objected, Sylvie objecting on principle to everything—and finally Odile, quiet as a closing door, saying: *"You'll come back. That's the price. You don't get to trade your life for mine; you get to trade an afternoon."*

Marguerite had agreed. They had even meant it. But lying in the ditch, watching Odile's cart roll forward in the queue, they understood something they hadn't let themselves understand before: that keeping promises to people was harder than breaking promises to oneself. Solitude had never required follow-through.

The queue moved. A grain farmer ahead of Odile argued about a tax stamp and lost. Then the cart was at the gate, and Odile was handing up her papers, and Marguerite's heart performed a maneuver they had not authorized.

*Watch her*, they told themselves. *Don't watch her. Watch Corvel.*

But they watched her anyway. You cannot spend eleven days eating a woman's stew and then look away at the important moment; it isn't in the body's power. Odile sat straight-backed under the sergeant's stare and answered questions Marguerite couldn't hear, and at one point she lifted her chin toward the mattress in the cart bed and said something with the weary authority of a woman discussing linens, and the sergeant actually nodded. Nodded. Marguerite felt an absurd swell of pride, as though they'd personally trained her, when in truth Odile Ferrand had been walking past armed men her whole life with nothing but a dead husband and a spine.

Then Corvel's horse turned toward the gate.

No. Too soon. Odile wasn't through yet—

And Marguerite realized, with a cold drop, that this was the flaw in the plan they should have seen. Corvel wasn't patrolling. He was working the queue from the front, cart by cart, and Odile's cart was next in his path. If he leaned down and looked at her—at the widow he might have seen at any inn in Saint-Aubin, at the woman whose daughter he'd have passed on the road—

There was no more time for elegance. Marguerite stood up out of the ditch, brushed off their coat, adjusted the satchel so it hung conspicuously, and walked toward the gate like a man with excellent papers and nothing whatsoever to hide.

* * *

The trick of getting arrested, Marguerite had learned in the long apprenticeship of their twenties, is the same as the trick of not getting arrested, run in reverse. Don't avoid the guard's eye—meet it, but a half-second too late, with the flicker of recalculation that every soldier is trained to read as guilt. Don't keep your hands still—touch the satchel strap once, briefly, the way a guilty man checks his wound. Walk with purpose but not too much purpose. Be interesting. Be *slightly wrong.*

The sentry at the gate took the bait like a pike taking a fly.

"Papers."

Marguerite handed them over and gave their name—"Aubert, René, ministry clerk"—with the smoothness that had carried them across three borders, and watched the sentry's eyes flick up with that small sharpening that meant the name had landed somewhere. The courier profiles. Broken Cipher. Somewhere behind that mustache, a list.

"Wait here."

"I have a schedule," Marguerite said, and heard their own voice do the thing it did when they were frightened: grow polished, faintly bored, aristocratic. It had saved them a dozen times.

It did not save them now. The sentry whistled, and a second soldier ambled over, and Corvel turned his horse.

For eleven days Marguerite had imagined this moment—had rehearsed it in the dark of the loft above Odile's kitchen, composing the scene like a chapter: the confrontation, the old handler and the old courier, some cutting line delivered coolly. What they had not prepared for was how ordinary Corvel looked. Greyer. Tired around the mouth. A traitor's face turns out to be just a face.

"Well," Corvel said, drawing rein, looking down at Marguerite with an expression that was almost gentle. "I told them you were good. I didn't tell them you were stupid."

"Hello, sir." Keep it flat. Keep it clerical. Give him nothing personal—he fed on personal; he always had; that was how he recruited people, by finding the place where they were lonely. "Is there a problem with my papers?"

"There's going to be." He dismounted, unhurried, and held out his hand for the satchel. Marguerite surrendered it with exactly one heartbeat of reluctance—enough to be noticed—and watched him find the decoy scrap in eight seconds flat, exactly as designed. His eyebrows rose. "Cipher material. On a clerk." He shook his head, savoring it. "You know, when the covers came across the table, yours was the one I argued for. *Keep Aubert*, I said. *He's careful.*"

"You flatter me, sir."

"I'm not flattering you." Corvel glanced toward the gate—and Marguerite's blood stopped, because Odile's cart was still there, the sergeant still turning the mattress question over in his mind, and Corvel's gaze was drifting toward it with professional idle curiosity—

So Marguerite did the last thing in the world their training permitted. They made noise.

"You sold us," they said, loud enough for the sentries, for the queue, for anyone. "You sat across the table from me in Valcourt and taught me that a courier's silence was sacred, and then you sold forty names for a commission and a warm billet."

The yard went quiet. Cart drivers turned. The sergeant at the gate looked up.

Corvel's face did something complicated. "You want to talk about selling?" he said softly, stepping close. "Your Republic wrote your name in a ledger and traded it the moment it became inconvenient. I just read the fine print faster than you did." He raised his voice, turning to the sentries. "Search the bag properly. This is a Republic courier—I trained him myself. There may be more on him. Check for documents sewn into the clothing."

*Yes*, Marguerite thought, heart hammering, *yes, look at me, look only at me—*

And then, horribly, Corvel looked back at the cart.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would reconstruct those ninety seconds many times, the way one probes a cracked tooth. How Odile, seeing the commotion, had done the single most Odile thing possible: nothing. She had not fled, had not protested, had not drawn attention. She had picked up the reins and asked the sergeant, with the mild irritation of a widow losing daylight, whether her husband's effects could clear the gate sometime before the war ended—and the sergeant, embarrassed, waved the cart through. Through the gate. Past the eagle stone. Onto the neutral road.

Marguerite allowed themselves one breath of relief. One. It was all the budget allowed, because Corvel had turned back, and his eyes were different now—narrowed, recalculating, the eyes of a man doing arithmetic.

"Widow," he said. "Black dress. Saint-Aubin accent." He looked at Marguerite with something like disappointment. "You didn't come alone. You never come alone—you're incapable of it, that was always your failing, Aubert, you collect strays and call it tradecraft—"

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"The cart. Stop the cart!"

But the cart was beyond the stones now, on ground the empire's writ didn't reach, and the sentry at the gate shrugged with the magnificent indifference of a man whose jurisdiction ends at a granite rooster. Corvel wheeled toward his horse—and Marguerite, running out of plan and running on pure animal refusal, grabbed the satchel from the sentry's hands and ran.

Not toward freedom. Toward the sandbags, toward the corner of the customs house, toward anywhere that pulled every eye in the yard onto their own back. Behind them: shouting, boots, Corvel screaming for horses. Ahead: the wall, and beyond the wall a drainage culvert that Tomas had described over candlelight with a surveyor's grin—"too narrow for a grown man, which rules you out, no offense"—and Marguerite dove for it anyway, not to escape, only to be *caught spectacularly*, to fill the stage so completely that no one would think to send a rider after a widow's cart already swallowed by the trees.

They made it four steps.

A rifle stock caught them across the shoulders and the world went white, then grey, then mud. Hands—many hands, inexpert and enthusiastic—took the satchel, took their arms, wrenched them upright. Somewhere above, Corvel's voice, breathless: "The culvert! Check the culvert!" and Marguerite laughed—actually laughed, spat blood into the mud and laughed—because there was nothing in the culvert, there never had been, the culvert was a story Tomas told to feel useful, and Corvel was sending men to search a hole while the real cargo rolled south under a quilt a girl had cried over.

"Aubert." Corvel crouched, took Marguerite's chin in a grip that was almost fatherly, studied their face. "Where's the ledger?"

"I don't know what you mean, sir," Marguerite said again, and found, to their surprise, that this time they weren't performing. The voice that had lied beautifully for seven years had gone quiet somewhere in the mud. What remained was just themselves: bruised, terrified, absurdly light. "You should hurry, though. Your arithmetic is slow. It always was. That's why the Republic trusted you and why the empire shouldn't."

Corvel hit them. It was, Marguerite noted distantly as the sky tilted, a sloppy blow—an angry man's blow, a blow from someone whose composure was a costume. Good. Angry men make mistakes. Let him be angry. Let him spend the whole afternoon being angry at a captured courier with a worthless cipher scrap and a head full of nothing, while a widow in a borrowed black dress delivered seven years of stolen names to the one man in Europe empowered to act on them.

They dragged Marguerite inside by the arms. The last thing they saw of the outside world was the granite rooster, worn nearly blank, standing its ground at the edge of the trees.

Then a door, a chair, ropes, a table with a lamp, and Corvel pulling up a stool opposite with the air of a man settling in for a long evening he intended to enjoy.

"Now," he said, unrolling the courier profiles like a merchant displaying cloth. "Let's begin with the index. Every courier carries the master index in memory—it's doctrine. Yours has forty-one entries. We'll start at the top, and we'll work down, and you'll give them to me one at a time."

Marguerite looked at the lamp, at the profiles, at the face of the man who had taught them everything about surviving alone—and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that they were about to lie for hours, magnificently, to buy time for other people. That the invisible self was finally, fully spent. What remained in the chair was someone new: someone with a family waiting at the end of a muddy road, someone owed, someone owing.

Someone who had better invent forty-one names before dawn.

"Take your time," Marguerite said. "I'm told I'm careful."

Outside, faint through the wall, a horse galloped south. Marguerite closed their eyes and began, softly, to count.
