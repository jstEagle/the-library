## 20. Names in the Fire

The room they took me to was not a torture chamber. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere survives for it to be recorded in. It was the vestry of Saint-Aubin's church, requisitioned along with everything else—candles, vestments, and now a courier tied to a chair between the sacristy and the font. The imperial captain had hung his greatcoat on the Virgin's niche. There is a kind of man who does that, and I had met enough of them to know what the coat hanging there meant: he wanted me to see that he did not fear God, or the Republic's ghosts, or anything else that might have been listening.

His name, according to the tag stitched inside his collar when he leaned close enough for me to read it, was Captain Erhart Voss. He had a pleasant face, the kind of face that gets invited to dinner parties and remembered afterward as *nice*, and he set down before me a single sheet of paper with my own handwriting on it.

"Marguerite Vasquez," he said. "Or should we begin with the names you've worn since? There are four in our index. I find I like *Claire Mouton* best. It suits you."

"It never has," I said. My wrists were bound behind the chair, which meant my shoulders ached within an hour and my hands had gone to pins and needles within two. None of this was new. What was new was the clock ticking somewhere behind my sternum, counting off the minutes Odile needed to reach the frontier. "It was chosen by a clerk who'd never seen me."

"And yet you answered to it for three years." He sat across from me, unhurried, a man settling into a long evening. "You understand my position. We have your cipher training confirmed twice over. We have the courier index from the surrender terms—we have *your name in it*, Mademoiselle Vasquez, written in your own Republic's hand, sold for a border concession like a barrel of salt pork. What I do not have is the ledger. And I am told—" he consulted a paper with theatrical care, though I suspected he knew its contents by heart, "—that you were seen near the records office two nights ago. That a window on the north side was forced. That a Republic officer was found dying in the stairwell, of wounds no burglar inflicts."

"I was looking for a place to sleep," I said. "The inn was full of soldiers."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. He appreciated craft; that much I could tell already, and it was both my danger and my one material. Men who appreciate craft can be flattered into admiring the wrong things.

* * *

Let me say what I actually felt in that chair, because the report I would have filed once—the old Marguerite's report—would have said *conditions managed, subject composed*, and that would have been a lie dressed neatly in cipher.

I was terrified. Not of Voss, particularly. Of arithmetic. Every hour they kept me was an hour Odile was on the road with four hundred pages of other people's death warrants sewn into her mattress, driving a cart she'd loaded herself by lamplight while pretending to Sylvie that everything was ordinary. Every question Voss asked that I answered too slowly was a minute Tomas might spend arguing with a sentry instead of moving. I had spent seven years learning to hold silence like a stone held underwater, and now I understood, sitting in a church vestry with my hands going numb, that I had never once held it for anyone but myself.

Voss poured wine from a carafe someone had left on the vestry table—requisitioned communion wine, probably, because men like him thought that was funny—and offered me some. When I declined, he drank mine too.

"You're wondering about your companions," he said. "The widow Ferrand. The daughter. The blacksmith with his tidy passes. The priest."

I kept my face still. It cost more than it ever had.

"Ah," he said softly. "There it is. You didn't know how much we knew." He set down the cup. "Here is what happens next, Mademoiselle Vasquez, so that you may choose your role in it knowingly. A cart left the abbey road at dawn under Tomas Ferrand's requisition passes. We stopped it at the customs house. The papers were in order—remarkably in order—but the mattress was heavier than mattresses are. It is being examined now, slowly, by men who enjoy their work. Your widow is being questioned. If the ledger is in that mattress, and if the index inside it matches what you tell me tonight, matters will go gently for everyone. If it does not match—if I conclude you have wasted my time—then the classifications proceed. All of them. Beginning with the families marked removable in Saint-Aubin, whose names I have right here."

He tapped a folder. I did not look at it. Looking would have told him the folder mattered, and I could not afford to let him know that I already knew every name in it by heart, that I had read them by candlelight in Odile's kitchen while Sylvie slept upstairs, that *Ferrand, Odile — removable* was a sentence I could recite like a prayer I hated.

"There is no ledger," I said. "You have a mattress full of horsehair and a widow who packs badly."

"Mm," said Voss.

So it began.

* * *

What I gave him, over the next hours, was the finest work of my career.

Not silence—that would have been simple, and simple would have killed us all. Silence would have confirmed the ledger existed and that I knew where it went. No: I gave him a ledger. I built it in the air between us, page by page, with the fluency of a woman who had spent seven years constructing identities out of nothing, except that this time I was constructing evidence out of less than nothing, out of the shape of my own fear.

"The ledger you want doesn't exist anymore," I told him, somewhere past midnight, after he had grown tired of asking and begun to grow interested instead. "It existed. I saw it. But the Republic never trusted a single copy of anything, and the registry was only the public half. The half that mattered was the index—a condensed cipher summary, forty pages, portable. That's what couriers carried. That's what I was sent to carry."

"And where is this index?"

"Burned," I said, and let him watch me flinch as I said it, because the truth underneath the lie was that I *had* watched something burn—I had burned my own final report drafts in Odile's stove—and grief reads honestly on a face whether the grief is real or borrowed. "When the garrison evacuated. Captain Delorme ordered it rather than let it fall. I stood there and watched four years of names go up the chimney. Ask your informant. Ask him what Delorme burned on the night of the ninth."

I watched that land. Because there *was* an informant—there had to be, the surrender terms hadn't fallen from the sky—and I gambled everything on the fact that whatever the man had reported, he had reported panic and paperwork and fire, and that fire was the one thing Voss could verify cheaply. Ashes were real. Ashes were all over Saint-Aubin. Half the town's official history was in the gutters and the wind took the rest.

Voss sent for ashes. Literally—he dispatched a lieutenant to collect samples from the records office chimney, and for two hours I sat alone in the vestry with my hands screaming and rehearsed nothing, because rehearsal shows, and instead made myself think about Odile's stew, about the specific weight of her hand on my shoulder in chapter nine of my life, the night she said *invisibility kept you alive but it never kept you living*, and I thought: *live, then. Live long enough to be scolded about this.*

The ashes came back. They were, of course, ashes.

* * *

Toward dawn, Voss tried the other direction, the one I'd been waiting for.

"Your handler," he said. "Corbin. You know him as Corbin. He sits at a desk in Valcourt and signs himself with a compass rose, does he not? A letter for the compass. Very poetic, your service. Did you know he sold you? All of you? Fourteen covers in the surrender terms, and yours fetched the highest price, because you were the best."

"I know," I said.

That surprised him. Good. Surprise is a door, and I walked through it. "He sold me, Captain, and I am still here, and the people who bought me have a widow and a blacksmith and a girl in novice's wool in custody over a mattress, and no ledger, and an index that burned, and a courier who has told you a coherent story that your own ash-sifters cannot contradict. What exactly do you imagine I'm protecting? My Republic?" I laughed—it came out ragged, which helped—and shook my head. "My Republic wrote my name on a list and handed the list to you. Let the families burn, Captain. I owe none of them anything."

The lie of it went through me like a blade going through wet canvas, slow and tearing. Because I owed them everything, and because the oldest rule of my life was *owe no one, need no one, and no one can be taken from you*—and here I sat, owing four people my whole self, needing them like breath, with every one of them reachable by this pleasant man's signature.

Voss studied me for a long time. Then he smiled, and it was the first honest expression I'd seen on him.

"You almost make me believe the Republic deserved to lose," he said. He stood, stretched, and rang a small bell. "You'll be kept somewhere more comfortable. If your story holds against the widow's, you may yet walk out of this war. People do, you know. Even couriers. Even ones whose names were sold."

They marched me across the yard to a storeroom behind the guardhouse—not comfortable, but a door with a bar on the outside rather than ropes on the inside, which in the economy of my life counted as luxury. Through the single high window I could see the customs house roofline and, below it, the road south, empty, gray, going where I could not follow.

I put my forehead against the cold wall and did the thing I had trained myself out of doing for seven years.

I prayed. Not to God particularly—I've never been sure He takes my calls—but to Odile, to the road, to the idea of a woman with flour on her sleeves outdriving an empire.

*Give them time,* I asked the wall. *Take mine. Take all of it. Just give them theirs.*

* * *

The escape, when it came, was so absurd that I nearly ruined it by laughing.

Near dusk—so, eighteen hours in my storeroom, long enough for Voss's men to grow bored of me and bored of each other—the guard changed. The new sentry on the yard was young, chapped, and deeply invested in a dice game visible through the guardhouse window, which meant the yard was unwatched for stretches of perhaps ninety seconds. I know because I counted, having nothing better to count, and because counting exits is the last skill I will ever lose.

On the fourth change, a hay cart rumbled into the yard carrying kindling for the guards' stoves. The driver was broad, bearded, and wearing a requisitioned imperial forage cap at an angle that suggested he had personally offended it. Tomas. Beside him, swathed in sacking and complaining in a whisper audible from space, sat Luc—the deserter from the crypt, pale as candle wax, one arm bound to his chest, playing dead-weight cargo with more commitment than any actor I have ever seen.

The kindling was unloaded. The cart stood. The sentry rolled his dice. And then, with the unhurried inevitability of weather, the cart's axle—loosened, I later learned, with Luc's knife during the drive in—cracked clean through, dumping half a cord of kindling across the yard directly in front of the guardhouse door.

The commotion was beautiful. Three soldiers came running to shout at Tomas; the dice game emptied; the sentry abandoned his post to defend his gambling winnings from suspicion; and in the center of it all, Tomas bellowed about compensation, about the state of imperial roads, about the honor of a blacksmith whose axles had never cracked in twenty years, while behind him Luc toppled off the cart with a groan that summoned a fourth soldier's pity and a fifth's irritation—

—and a hatchet, riding in the kindling, came apart from its handle in Tomas's hand mid-gesture, and the handle-end whistled past the guardhouse door and struck the hasp of my storeroom's outer bar, once, twice, on the third swing cracking the wood, on the fourth springing it, all disguised under the general din as a blacksmith losing his temper at heaven.

I was through the door and under sacking in the cart bed before my hands finished deciding they worked.

Nobody looked. Why would they? The yard contained a broken axle, an injured prisoner-transport deserter, five shouting soldiers, and a large furious tradesman. I was a bundle of sacking among bundles of kindling. Invisibility, it turns out, was never a story I told myself. It was a trade, and I had simply spent seven years working for the wrong employer.

The cart creaked back toward the gate. A sergeant waved it through with a gesture that said *take your catastrophe elsewhere*. And then we were on the north road, in the falling dark, with Luc groaning theatrically until we were a quarter mile clear, at which point he sat bolt upright and said:

"That was the worst plan I have ever survived."

"It was my plan," Tomas said, without turning around.

"Yes," said Luc. "That's what worries me."

I pushed off the sacking. My wrists were striped red where the ropes had bitten, my legs barely held me, and I discovered I was shaking—from relief, I thought at first, and then understood it was something worse. It was the specific trembling of a person whose oldest law has just been repealed. *No one comes for you. No one spends themselves on you. You leave first, you owe nothing, and therefore nothing can break you.*

Two men had cracked an axle, risked a firing squad, and spent Luc's last shred of health to pull a stranger out of a locked room. The rule hadn't bent. It had shattered, and I was sitting in the wreckage of it in the back of a hay cart, and I found—to my genuine astonishment—that I was not sorry.

"Tomas," I said. My voice came out wrecked. I tried again. "Odile. The customs house. Is she—"

"Gone through." He said it quietly, eyes on the road. "Before noon. Mattress and all. Sylvie rode with her as far as the fork, then doubled back to the inn to sit with Père Anselme and wait." A pause. "We don't know past the fork. Nobody knows past the fork. But she went through, Marguerite. She walked your forged countersignature past three desks with her chin up and her widow's weeds on, and the last anyone saw, the customs officer was bowing to her."

I put my face in my hands. Somewhere south of us, in the dark, Odile Ferrand was delivering the ledger that would stop the trains, carrying my forgery in her pocket like a second heart, and I had not been there to see it, and I would never forgive myself for how glad I was to be alive in the same world where she had done it.

"We have to get you to the abbey," Tomas said. "Sylvie's expecting us by midnight, and if you're not—"

"She'll come looking," Luc said. "She's her mother's daughter. She'll come looking with a candlestick."

And that was the moment I heard it: hooves on the north road behind us, several horses, moving fast, and a lantern swinging its light across the fields, and a voice calling orders in the clipped cadence of Captain Voss's escort detail—not searching the ditches, not fanning out, but riding straight, purposeful, *southward past us at speed*, as if summoned.

As if word had come from the customs house.

Tomas pulled the cart into the tree line and killed the lantern, and we sat in the dark watching imperial riders stream past toward the frontier, toward the envoy's residence, toward whatever Odile had set in motion—too many riders, moving too urgently, for this to be routine.

"Is that good or bad?" Luc whispered.

I didn't answer. Because I genuinely did not know—and because, for the first time in seven years, the answer wasn't mine alone to calculate. Somewhere ahead of those horses, a widow was either reaching safety or walking into the trap of her life, and all I could do was sit in a hay cart with two reckless, impossible men and wait, like family, for news.
