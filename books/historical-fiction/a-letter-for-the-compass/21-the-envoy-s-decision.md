## 21. The Envoy's Decision

Sylvie counted the days the way Maman counted coins—by touch, in the dark, without wanting to see the total.

Four days since Marguerite had walked out of the inn at dawn with a forged letter burning in their coat and a look on their face that Sylvie had decided, privately, never to describe to anyone. Four days since the cart had come back without them. Four days of Tomas saying *they knew what they were doing*, and Père Anselme saying nothing at all, which was worse, because priests only went quiet when they were praying or lying.

The town had gone strange in those four days. The imperial garrison still held Saint-Aubin, but the deportations hadn't come. The trains that were supposed to leave on the fourth day—the ones Luc had whispered about in the crypt with blood on his teeth—had not left. The lists had not been posted. The sergeant at the well who used to spit when he said *Republic* now mostly spat at nothing, like a man whose script had been taken away from him.

Something had happened. Nobody could say what.

So Sylvie did what she always did when the world refused to explain itself: she worked. She hauled water, scrubbed the taproom floor until it shone like a confession, and argued with her mother about whether the third-floor shutters should be painted blue or green.

"Blue," said Maman.

"Green hides the rot."

"Nothing hides the rot, Sylvie. That's why we paint it."

That was Maman's whole philosophy in one sentence, and Sylvie hated how often it turned out to be true.

* * *

On the fifth morning, a carriage came up the north road.

Not a military carriage—a civilian one, mud-splattered, drawn by two horses that had clearly been asked to do more than horses ought. It stopped in front of the inn, and a man got out wearing a grey coat that was trying very hard to be unremarkable and failing, because men who wore coats like that always had the posture of someone accustomed to doors opening for them.

He looked at the inn's sign, then at a paper in his hand, then at the inn again, as though checking whether reality had been transcribed correctly.

"Is this the establishment of Odile Ferrand?" he asked, in an accent that came from somewhere expensive.

"It is," said Maman, appearing in the doorway with the particular smile she kept for officials, tax collectors, and other varieties of weather.

"Then I have the honor," the man said, "of being late." He folded his paper away. "I am Envoy Corvel, of the Concordat. I believe you have a guest of mine recovering upstairs."

The taproom went so quiet Sylvie could hear the kettle deciding whether to boil.

Maman recovered first, because Maman always recovered first. "We have a guest," she allowed, "who has been bleeding on my linens for four days and refusing to tell me why. If that's yours, you may settle the account."

Corvel smiled—the thin, tired smile of a man who had spent a week doing arithmetic with people's lives and found the sums ugly. "I intend to settle considerably more than that, Madame Ferrand. May I sit? I've ridden two days without decent coffee, and I'm told—" he glanced at the sign again, "—this is the best inn between here and the frontier."

"It's the only inn between here and the frontier," said Sylvie.

"Then I stand corrected twice over." He sat down at the table by the window, and something about the way he did it—no ceremony, no guards, just a tired man lowering himself into a chair—made Sylvie revise her first impression downward from *dangerous* to merely *important*, which was nearly the opposite thing.

Luc, who had been hiding in the kitchen pretending to chop turnips for four days despite there being nothing left to chop, came out wiping his hands. He looked at Corvel, and Corvel looked at Luc, and some silent recognition passed between them—the look of two men who had both recently been somebody's problem.

"You're the deserter," Corvel said.

"I'm a turnip chopper," said Luc.

"Ah." Corvel nodded seriously. "The Republic trained its soldiers poorly, then. Your knife work is all wrong."

For the first time in days, Luc almost smiled.

* * *

Upstairs, Marguerite was awake.

Sylvie knew before anyone said so, because the whole house changed temperature when Marguerite was awake—it became more careful and more alive at once, like a room where someone was playing music very quietly. She climbed the stairs behind Corvel and hovered at the door, and when nobody told her to go away, she took that as permission, which is the oldest trick in the world and has never once failed.

Marguerite lay propped against pillows, gray-faced, one shoulder wrapped in bandages that Père Anselme had changed twice daily with the tenderness of a man who had done far worse things to far less grateful bodies. A fever had come and gone through them like weather. But their eyes were open, and tracking, and when they saw Corvel, something in their face closed like a ledger.

"Envoy," Marguerite said. Their voice was gravel. "You received it."

"I received it." Corvel drew out a chair and sat. "At eleven o'clock at night, from a widow with flour on her hands and the steadiest signature I have ever seen on a document that wasn't hers. Do you know, Madame Ferrand wrote your cipher hand better than your own office did? I checked it against three authentic specimens. She'd have fooled me if I hadn't already known."

"She had a good teacher," Marguerite said.

"She had one night," said Corvel, "and apparently that was sufficient." He set his hands flat on his knees. "Here is what happened, because you've earned the truth and because I find people recover faster when the suspense stops gnawing at them. The ledger reached me intact. The countersignature passed inspection. The names in that book—every family marked removable in Saint-Aubin, every courier cover sold in the surrender terms, every line item in your Republic's final accounting of human beings—were entered into the Concordat's record before dawn on the second day."

He paused. Outside, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"I presented the ledger to the imperial commissioner at the frontier under diplomatic seal. Deportation is not, whatever their officers pretend, a purely internal matter—not when the classifications were negotiated in secret against a treaty clause we happen to hold the original of. The trains were halted within the day. The classifications suspended pending review. In practical terms, Madame Ferrand, no one in this town will be put on a train. In legal terms, the matter will be argued for ten years by men paid to argue. In human terms—" he shrugged, "—you are all still here, and that is the entire point of my profession, though most days I forget it."

Nobody spoke. Maman had come up behind Sylvie at some point, and Sylvie felt her mother's hand settle on her shoulder, heavy and warm, anchoring her to the floorboards.

"And the defector?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"Ah." Corvel's face did something complicated. "Vasseur."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Sylvie didn't know everything—nobody would tell her everything—but she knew Vasseur had been Marguerite's handler, once, and that he had sold the couriers' names the way a man sells a horse that limps, and that Marguerite had stood in the customs house gate and let themselves be caught specifically to burn that man's usefulness to cinders.

"He was exposed," Corvel said. "By his own hand, as it happens. When the deportations halted, his imperial patrons required a reason. Reasons are cheap when a captured courier is producing documents; reasons are expensive when the courier escapes custody in forty-eight hours and the documents prove fabricated. He attempted to flee north. He did not get far." Corvel paused. "The Empire does not reward failure, and neither, I'm sorry to say, does the law. He'll be tried. There will be records. Every name he sold will be read aloud in a courtroom, which is not justice exactly, but it is the closest thing justice sells retail."

Marguerite lay very still. Sylvie watched their hands—those quick, clever, careful hands—lying open on the blanket, empty, and thought: *that's what it looks like when someone puts down a weight.*

"They'll want me to testify," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question.

"The Concordat will want many things. I've learned to distinguish wanting from getting." Corvel rose. "Rest. Heal. The world will continue to be unreasonable without your assistance; it always has." At the door he stopped, and looked back, and for a moment the diplomat fell away and there was just a tired man. "Courier Vasquez. Eleven days, three checkpoints, one forged signature, and a town full of people who were supposed to be strangers to you. My reports will call it a successful operation." His eyes flicked around the small room—the chipped basin, the blue shutters, the girl in the doorway. "My private opinion is that it was something better than that. Good day."

* * *

Afterward, the house filled up.

Tomas arrived with his wife and a wheel of cheese the size of a shield, having heard from the blacksmith network—which is faster than any government network, being fueled entirely by gossip—that *something* had happened and that *someone important* was at the inn. Père Anselme came down from the abbey carrying wine and looking twenty years younger, which on Anselme still left him roughly ninety. Luc stayed in the kitchen and actually cooked, and the smell of real food after weeks of fear moved through the inn like sunlight through a dirty window.

They ate in the taproom. Marguerite insisted on coming down—came down swaying, white-knuckled on the banister, refusing all help with the fierce politeness of the recently bedridden—and sat in the corner chair like a cat that has chosen its spot and will hear no arguments.

"To the Republic," Tomas raised his cup, and then, catching himself, "or—or to whatever we're calling things now."

"To whatever we're calling things now," everyone agreed, which was the most honest toast ever drunk in Saint-Aubin, and possibly in the whole collapsed Republic.

Sylvie ended up sitting across from Marguerite, close enough to see the new lines around their eyes and the way they held their bad arm tucked like a folded wing.

"You lied beautifully," she said. "In the customs house. When they caught you. Maman told me—you gave them a false index and let them think they'd won."

"I lied adequately," Marguerite said. "There's a difference. Beautiful lying requires good health."

"That's not what I meant." Sylvie turned her cup around and around. "I meant—I watched you lie at the bridge, too, back when we crossed. And at the abbey gate. You've been lying your whole life, haven't you? Alone. To stay invisible." She looked up. "Why did you stop?"

Marguerite was quiet for long enough that the fire popped twice. When they answered, their voice had lost its gravel and gone soft instead, which was somehow more startling.

"Because invisibility was a story I told myself," they said. "And stories are fine until you meet people who make the true version better." They looked at her—really looked, the way they rarely did, straight on, no calculation in it. "You caught me copying cipher keys by candlelight and offered to walk into an imperial checkpoint dressed as a novice. Do you know how terrifying that was? Not the checkpoint. The offering."

"You're welcome," said Sylvie, because she didn't know what else to say, and also because it was funny, and Marguerite laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose—and Maman, from across the room, heard it and turned around with an expression Sylvie planned to remember for the rest of her life.

* * *

Evening came down soft. The guests thinned. Tomas carried his sleeping wife's shawl; Anselme blessed the doorframe on his way out, an old habit that made Maman roll her eyes and touch the wood anyway.

Marguerite stayed at the corner table with paper and ink—borrowed from Sylvie, who had extracted a promise of payment in stories—and for a while the only sounds were the fire and the scratch of a pen.

Sylvie dried dishes and pretended not to watch.

She saw Marguerite write a full page, pause, read it, and set it aside. Write another. Set that aside too. By the third attempt their jaw had gone tight with frustration, and finally Sylvie abandoned pretense and came over.

"What are you writing?"

"My final report." Marguerite tapped the discarded pages. "It's supposed to summarize the operation. Routes, contacts, expenditures, outcomes." A pause. "It keeps coming out wrong."

"Wrong how?"

Marguerite looked at the pages. "'Contact Odile Ferrand proved essential'—that's what it says. What it should say is that she fed me stew the night I arrived and never asked a single question until the questions mattered. 'Asset Sylvie Ferrand demonstrated initiative.' What it should say is that a fifteen-year-old girl taught a professional liar the difference between surviving and living, and did it by candlelight, armed with nothing but stubbornness and too many questions." They exhaled. "The report is accurate. It isn't true. Those are different languages, and I only speak one of them fluently."

"So don't send the report," Sylvie said practically.

"I have to send something."

"Fine. Send the report." Sylvie pulled out the chair beside them and sat, chin on her hands. "But you're allowed to write other things too. Things with no cipher. No handler. No exit route." She hesitated, then pushed the last inch, the way Maman pushed the last inch of stew at people who swore they weren't hungry. "Things addressed to us."

Marguerite went very still.

Outside, the north road was dark and quiet, and for the first time since Sylvie could remember, quiet didn't feel like holding your breath. Somewhere south, an envoy rode with papers that had saved a town. Somewhere north, a trial waited with a traitor's name on it. And here in the taproom, a fire burned down to embers, and a person who had spent seven years learning to vanish sat holding a pen over blank paper, deciding—with visible effort, the way one decides to step off a high place into water—what to become next.

Marguerite slid the report pages aside. Drew a fresh sheet near. Dipped the pen.

"All right," they said, to Sylvie, to the page, to the household settling upstairs above their head like a roof that was also a choice. "No cipher."

They began to write:

*"Dear Odile, dear Sylvie—"*

And stopped, pen hovering, because for the first time in seven years, Marguerite Vasquez had absolutely nowhere they needed to run—and no idea yet what people write when they mean to stay—but Sylvie could see, in the set of their shoulders, that they were going to find out.
