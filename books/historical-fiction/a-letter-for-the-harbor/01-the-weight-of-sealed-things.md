## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The nets wanted mending, and Gideon Dravenmoor was glad of it.

She sat cross-legged near the bow of the *Meridian*, where the morning light came down clean through the rigging and the sea ran a gray-green that reminded her of the harbor at home — before the fires, before the flags came down one by one like eyelids closing. The net in her lap was a ruin of knots and torn mesh, and she worked the wooden shuttle through it with the steady rhythm of someone who had done this a thousand times, though she had learned only three months ago, on her second night aboard, when Maren pressed the shuttle into her hands and said, *Idle hands think too much, girl.*

Maren had been right. Idle hands thought of Vellamare.

So Gideon mended nets. She hauled rope when hauling was needed. She peeled potatoes for the cook's galley until her fingers stank of starch, and she let the salt wind take her hair and make her look, she hoped, like exactly what she appeared to be: another refugee with nothing to her name but a coat and a hunger, bound for whatever neutral port would have them.

The coat was the problem. Or rather, what lived inside the coat was the problem.

It hung from her shoulders even now, heavy as a wet sail, though it weighed no more than wool and thread. In its left lining, beneath a panel she had sewn herself with stitches so fine they would shame a surgeon's, lay a dispatch sealed in wax the color of dried blood — the last diplomatic pouch ever to leave the Republic of Vellamare, and the only one that mattered. Inside it, written in the small, precise hand of a dying man: the names. Four generals. Dates. Amounts. The account numbers in the Calliste banking houses where the price of a fleet had been quietly deposited while the fleet itself burned in the roads outside the capital, its guns turned landward by order, its captains forbidden to fight.

Gideon knew the letter by heart. She wished to God she didn't.

*Again.* She caught herself counting the stitches on the panel — thirty-one across, nine deep, each one placed in the dark of the fo'c'sle by feel alone — and forced her attention back to the net. The shuttle slipped. The knot went crooked. She unpicked it and started over.

Busy hands, she told herself. Busy hands.

* * *

Three months ago there had been a man named Envoy Sarel Voss, and he had been dying in the cellar of a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, and he had spent his last hour making Gideon promise things.

She could still see the light in that cellar — one candle, guttering, because candles were rationed now and even traitors were frugal. She could smell the tallow and the bilgewater and the iron smell of blood soaking into sawdust. Voss had been shot twice getting out of the Ministry district, and he had walked — *walked*, the stubborn old mule — nearly a mile on legs that were leaving a trail any child could follow.

"You'll take the coastal road," he'd said. His voice had been thin as paper, but his eyes were the same eyes that had stared down an assembly of four hundred deputies without blinking. "There's a fisherman at Saltmere who owes me. He'll put you aboard a refugee ship. They don't search refugees. There's nothing to search for."

"And when I reach neutral water?"

"Then you keep going." A cough took him, long and terrible, and when it passed he gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "Listen to me, child. The empire will write the history of this war. That is what empires do — they write, and everyone else gets read. But paper can be argued with. Paper can be burned." He tapped the dispatch case against his chest, once, like a heartbeat. "This cannot be argued with. This is the confession of men who sold eleven thousand sailors to the guns at Harbor Reach. Signatures. Ledgers. Their own handwritings, praising their own treachery. When the historians finish their pretty lies, this is the thing that says *no*. You understand? It doesn't need to be shouted. It only needs to exist."

"You should carry it yourself," Gideon said, though she already knew why he couldn't. Dying men don't run blockades.

"I am carrying it," Voss said, "the way a tree carries fruit."

He made her swear it on the republic — not on the government, which had fled, nor on the flag, which was being cut up for bandages somewhere, but on the idea of it, which was all that remained. Then he taught her the seal's false bottom, the way the wax could be softened and reformed, and then, because he was Voss and never wasted anything, he dictated to her the first paragraph of his own memoirs until the candle went out.

She found him at dawn, still sitting upright, hands folded, looking for all the world like a man waiting patiently for a ferry.

Gideon had buried him under the chandler's floor with the help of a woman who never asked her name, taken the dispatch from its oilskin wrap, gone to Saltmere, and begun the longest voyage of her life.

* * *

"Dravenmoor! You're stitching that poor fish into a coffin."

Tull dropped onto the deck beside her, all elbows and grease-black knuckles, and peered at her work with the frank rudeness of a man who considered machinery the only honest company aboard. The *Meridian*'s engineer was sixty if he was a day, bald as a gull's egg, and had opinions about everything from boiler pressure to the moral character of mackerel.

"It's a net," Gideon said.

"It's a eulogy. Look — you've got that corner pulled tighter than the rest. A net pulls even or it pulls nothing." He took the shuttle from her without asking, worked three quick loops, and handed it back transformed. "There. Now it forgives you."

"That's not how nets work."

"That's not how *anything* works," Tull said, "but we go on pretending, don't we." He squinted at her a moment longer than comfort allowed. "You slept bad again. Third night running. I hear you turn over up there like a rotisserie."

"I sleep fine."

"Mm." He stood, brushing off his trousers, and delivered his parting shot over his shoulder with terrible casualness: "Whatever you're chewing on, girl, chew it slower. Teeth wear out faster than hearts."

She watched him clamber down toward the engine room, and she wondered — not for the first time, and certainly not for the last — how much the people around her actually saw. Tull heard everything through the deckplates; Maren missed nothing that happened within reach of her ladle. Three months of shared bread and shared watch rotations, and they had folded her into their little crew without ceremony, the way the sea folds a new plank into an old hull.

They did not know her name was a lie of omission. They did not know that *Gideon Dravenmoor, net-mender, born nowhere in particular,* had died at the fall of the capital along with the clerk whose papers she carried — a real man, a quiet one, who had traded her his identity documents for her promise to look after his mother, and whose mother had been dead two years before he made the trade, a fact he confessed afterward with a shrug and said, *then the promise costs you nothing, does it?*

She kept busy hands. She kept her head down. And beneath her ribs, where dread had taken up permanent residence like a stone swallowed whole, she counted the days to Port Callis.

* * *

Port Callis. Neutral ground, technically — an imperial free port wearing neutrality like a borrowed coat, all customs houses and consular flags and money changing hands in six currencies. The *Meridian* would take on coal and fresh water there, discharge forty passengers who had relatives or luck, and take on provisions for the final leg north.

Forty passengers going ashore. Forty chances for Gideon to simply walk off the ship, melt into the crowds of the displaced, and become nobody with tremendous efficiency.

She had rehearsed it every night: the gangway, the customs shed, the tram line to the rail station, the inland trains heading west where no one from the war's geography would think to look. She had the route memorized the way Voss had made her memorize the letter — backward and forward, in the dark, by heart.

And every night, the rehearsal ended the same way, with the same question rising in her like bilgewater: *and then what?*

A letter hidden in a coat is not a truth delivered. It is a truth *delayed*, and delay, Voss had said in a hundred lectures she'd attended as a junior archivist in the Ministry of Records, delay is the empire's favorite weapon. Empires do not burn books anymore — they simply wait. They wait for couriers to lose their nerve, for safe houses to go cold, for the people who remember to grow old and tired and quiet. The archive doesn't need to be destroyed. It only needs to be outlived.

Gideon looked down at her hands — raw-knuckled, tar-stained, trembling very slightly around the shuttle — and admitted what she had been refusing to admit since Saltmere.

She did not know how to deliver it. She was an archivist's apprentice, twenty-three years old, trained in filing systems and preservation lacquer, not in smuggling secrets past the victors of a war. Every plan she built collapsed under examination. Hand it to a consulate? The consulates were buying safety from the empire with information. Publish it? No neutral paper would touch unverified republican claims, and she could hardly produce the original for verification without surrendering it. Carry it onward forever, a pilgrim of one? Voss was dead. Papers outlived their carriers only when the carriers built something to hold them.

She had no structure. She had a coat lining and a promise, and somewhere behind her, she was increasingly sure, people who knew exactly what had left Vellamare in the last boats.

The bell above the helm rang twice — the lookout's signal — and Gideon lifted her head. Away to the northeast, just clearing the morning haze, a smudge of smoke marked the funnel of another vessel, holding a course that paralleled theirs. Too far off to read her colors. Too steady, too patient, to be fishing traffic.

Beside Gideon, an old woman passenger crossed herself and muttered something about naval patrols. Around her, the deck went on with its ordinary business — Pip chasing a coil of rope as though it might bite, Maren's voice raised in the galley, the engine ticking below like a patient clock.

But Gideon's hands had gone still on the ruined net, and the stone beneath her ribs had grown heavier, and she understood, with the clarity that comes only to people carrying something they must not drop, that the voyage was about to stop being simple.

She bent back to her work. Thirty-one stitches across, nine deep. Busy hands.

Behind them, at the rail, the smoke on the horizon grew no closer — and did not go away.
