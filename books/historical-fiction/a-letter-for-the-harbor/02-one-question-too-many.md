## 2. One Question Too Many

The gangplank of the *Meridian* groaned under Corvin Ashe's boots the way it groaned under everyone's — a complaint without distinction, which he appreciated. He had been counting things since before dawn: forty-one crates of salt fish hoisted off the lower deck, six barrels marked with the crest of a chandler in Vellamare that no longer existed, and one hundred and ninety-three souls registered so far aboard the refugee ship, though the number moved like water every time someone remembered a cousin sleeping in the hold.

He climbed with his ledger strapped to his chest in its oilcloth sling, his ink bottles stoppered and nested in their felt-lined case, his pens wrapped in waxed linen against the salt air. Other officials came aboard warships or revenue cutters with marines at their backs. Corvin Ashe came aboard a leaking refugee ship alone, with paper, because paper was what the Empire sent when it wanted something done properly.

*Properly* was a word people said to him the way they said *sorry* — quickly, and while looking away.

"Imperial census," he announced to the mate who met him at the rail, holding up the tin badge on its chain. "Every soul aboard is to be entered in the provincial register. Names, origins, trades, kin. No exceptions."

The mate — a broad woman with rope-burned palms and the flat stare of someone who had watched too many harbors burn — shrugged. "You'll want to start below decks, then. The ones with papers come up to be counted. The ones without hide."

"I'll count them all the same."

"You always say that?"

"I've only just boarded this ship, madam. I haven't said anything yet."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might once have lived. She jerked her chin toward the companionway. "Deck passengers first. They're the ones least likely to bite."

He began, as he always began, with the oldest.

An old man named Sef who had been a cooper in Vellamare for fifty years and could name the trees his barrels had come from. A widow with twin daughters who gave her maiden name, her married name, her mother's name, and her husband's regiment — dead, she said, at the harbor, though she said it the way people now said everything about the harbor: as weather, as fact, as something that simply was. A boy of maybe nineteen who claimed to be a fisherman and could not tie a bowline, and whom Corvin noted without comment, because hunger made liars of honest people and the register was not a court.

He worked methodically down the lines. Name. Place of birth. Trade before the war. Kin living, kin dead. He did not ask after politics; the Empire's forms did not have a column for grief, and Corvin had long ago stopped trying to add one in the margins. Though sometimes he did anyway, small notations in his own shorthand, because a record that captured only facts missed half the truth, and Corvin Ashe had never in his life been able to abide an incomplete record.

It was past midday when she reached him.

She was mending a net.

That was the first thing. Everyone else stood in line to be counted — shuffling, coughing, clutching children and bundles and the small hoarded dignities of the displaced — but this woman sat on an upturned crate near the foremast with a net across her knees, working a shuttle through torn mesh with quick, certain hands, and she did not stop when he stopped in front of her.

"Gideon Dravenmoor," said the mate, who had appointed herself unofficial reader of the line. "Next."

Corvin opened his ledger to a fresh page. "Madam Dravenmoor. If you'd give me your particulars."

"Mm." The shuttle flashed. "Ask."

He was accustomed to evasion of the weeping kind, the hostile kind, the exhausted kind. This was none of these. Her voice was level and pleasant, faintly amused even, as though he were a tax she intended to pay in installments. He looked at her properly for the first time.

A woman perhaps thirty, brown-haired, sun-browned at the cheekbones, dressed in the salt-stiffened wool of a working sailor with a coat over it too heavy for the season — a good coat, he noticed, though patched now at the elbows, the kind of coat that had been fine once and refused to admit it. Her hands were red from cold water but steady. There was a smudge of dark wax or tar along her left cuff that she either hadn't seen or didn't care about.

"Name," he said, though the mate had given it. Procedure was procedure. "Full name, as you were born."

"Gideon Dravenmoor."

"Place of birth."

The shuttle paused. Half a second. Less. Then it went on. "Does it matter?"

"It matters to the form."

"The form doesn't have feelings, sir."

"No," Corvin agreed. "But it has columns, and empty columns bother me. Place of birth, madam."

She considered him then — really considered, lifting her gaze from the mesh to his face for the first time — and whatever she found there made her tilt her head slightly, the way a person tilts at a door they thought was locked and finds merely closed. "Coastal," she said. "South coast. A fishing village you won't have heard of."

"The register prefers names to geography lessons."

"And the register will have to go on wanting." She smiled, and it was a warm smile, disarming by design. "Half the villages on that coast are ash, Master Census-Taker. Ask me which one and I'll tell you the smoke was thick enough that day to make them all identical."

He wrote *south coast, village unspecified*, and felt the entry sit wrong on the page like a stone in a boot. He pressed on.

"Trade before the war."

"Net-mending."

"Before the war," he repeated gently. "What did you do before?"

The shuttle stopped again. Longer this time. Around them the line shuffled forward; somewhere below decks a child was crying in the bored, relentless way of children who have cried themselves hoarse and intend to continue out of principle.

"I fished," she said at last.

"With your family? Family boats usually."

"My family's dead."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It was quick." The shuttle resumed, faster than before, and Corvin understood suddenly and completely that she had told him nothing true except that sentence, and that the sentence was armor, and that he was meant to retreat from it as everyone else had.

Most men would have. Corvin Ashe had spent eleven years learning the precise weight of other men's silences, and he knew retreat when he heard it. But he also knew — with the same certainty — the sound of an answer that had been prepared, rehearsed, fitted to the question like a key filed down to turn one particular lock. Refugees lied about their ages and their rations and their loyalties. They rarely rehearsed.

"Kin living," he said instead. "Brothers, sisters, cousins — anyone?"

"No."

"No one at all?"

"The sea keeps what it takes."

"That's very nearly poetry, madam."

"Very nearly is the best most of us manage these days."

He wrote *no known kin* and hated the neatness of it. He looked at her again — the steady hands, the expensive ruined coat, the wax on the cuff, dark red-dark, the color of sealing wax from a chancery rather than tar from a deck — and asked his last question the way he asked all his last questions, lightly, as if it were nothing:

"And how do you come to be aboard the *Meridian*, madam? Passage purchased, or—"

"Fished off a wreck," she said pleasantly. "Swam. You'd be amazed what a determined woman can manage."

"*Meridian* took on refugees at Callis two days ago. You're not on the harbor list of those processed ashore."

"Then your harbor list has a hole in it." The smile again, bright as a lamp. "You should report that. A man who cares about holes in records — I can see it in how you hold that book — ought to be told when one turns up."

For a moment neither of them moved. The wind shifted the net between them; the shuttle hung from her fingers, still. Somewhere aft, the mate was watching them with an expression Corvin couldn't read and didn't try to.

He closed the ledger.

"Thank you for your time, Madam Dravenmoor. The register thanks you."

"The register and I are going to be great friends," she said, and bent back to her nets, and that was the end of it, as far as anyone watching could see.

Corvin worked the rest of the line. He counted a family of five sharing two names. He counted a man who gave his trade as *soldier* and his age as *old enough*. He counted until the light went copper on the water and the mate told him the census boat wouldn't wait for perfectionists.

At the rail, waiting for the launch, he opened the ledger one more time and turned to the page with the impossible woman on it.

He was a careful man. Careful men did not confront; they recorded. Confrontation was for soldiers and magistrates, men whose mistakes could be buried. A census taker's power lay elsewhere — in the patient accumulation of detail, in the marginal note that seemed like nothing today and became the hinge of everything three years hence. He had seen clerks hanged on the strength of a margin. He had seen whole families saved by one.

So he uncapped his pen, and beside the entry *Dravenmoor, Gideon — south coast, village unspecified; trade, fishing (unverified); kin, none*, he wrote, in his small exact hand:

*No birthplace she will name. No trade that predates the war. No kin living or dead. Coat finer than her station. Wax on left cuff — red, chancery grade. Answers rehearsed. Smiles when cornered.*

*Count again.*

He blotted it, closed the book, and let himself be rowed back through the darkening harbor toward the customs house, unaware — as the hunter always is at the beginning — that the first step of a long pursuit has already been taken, and that the trail begins not with a shout but with seven words in a margin: *count again.*

Behind him, aboard the *Meridian*, the lamps were being lit one by one, and a woman with busy hands watched his boat shrink into the dusk, and did not mend another knot until it was gone.
