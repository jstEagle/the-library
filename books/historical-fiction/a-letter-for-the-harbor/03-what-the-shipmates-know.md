## 3. What the Shipmates Know

The nets wanted mending, and so did Gideon.

She sat cross-legged on the foredeck with a shuttle of tarred twine in her hands, working the same knot she'd worked a hundred times that morning, letting the rhythm of it steady her the way it had steadied her through worse nights than these. The *Meridian* rolled easy in the long swell off Port Callis, her sails patched with sailcloth from four different nations, her hold stuffed with people instead of cargo. Above Gideon's head the rigging creaked its old complaints. Around her, the displaced of a drowned republic slept, argued, wept quietly into their sleeves, and tried to remember how to be passengers instead of citizens.

Gideon Dravenmoor mended nets.

It was what she did now. It was who she was allowed to be. A widow from the river provinces, she had told them — no trade before the war, no family worth naming, hands rough enough to pass for a fisherman's if you didn't look too closely at the ink stains ground into her knuckles. She'd scrubbed at those stains for three days with lye and sand until even she half-believed they were grease.

Against her ribs, sewn flat between the lining of her coat and the canvas interlining, the letter lay like a second heart. She could feel its edges when she breathed deep. She had learned to breathe shallow.

"Your selvage is crooked," Maren said, dropping onto the hatch coaming beside her with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. The cook smelled of salt pork and woodsmoke as always, and she carried two tin cups without being asked, pressing one into Gideon's free hand. "You've done that same splice twice. I've been watching."

"It's a difficult knot."

"It's a *simple* knot. That's how I know." Maren blew across her tea though there was no steam left to chase. "You've been stitching the same yard of net since dawn, girl, and you've gotten nowhere in it. Back home I'd say you were lovesick." Her eyes, small and shrewd under her kerchief, came around sideways. "You're not lovesick."

Gideon tied off a loop. "I'm tired."

"Mm." Maren drank. The sea said nothing useful. Somewhere belowdecks a baby was crying in the tired, hopeless way of babies whose mothers have no more milk and no more country. "Tull says you stood your whole watch at the rail last night. Didn't sit once. Tull notices things; it's his curse. He says a woman who stands a whole watch isn't watching the water. She's listening to something behind her."

"Tull should stick to engines."

"Tull," said Maren comfortably, "is down in the engine room right now telling Pip the same thing he told me, which is that our Gideon is carrying something heavier than her coat, and that when she decides to put it down or use it, she ought to tell us first, because we'll be standing next to her either way." She shrugged, vast shoulders rolling like the swell. "I'm only the messenger. And the cook. Mostly the cook."

Gideon looked at her hands in her lap, the shuttle resting in the V of her fingers, and felt the terrible ache of loving people you cannot tell things to. It was a specific pain, one she knew from the war years: the pain of the courier, whose first loyalty must always be to the parcel and never to the person. Envoy Sarelle had taught her that on the last night, pressing the sealed dispatch against her sternum with both palms as though planting a seed in soil.

"They will ask nothing of you," Sarelle had said, gray-faced among the burning papers of the chancellery, "because you will give them nothing to ask about. Be dull, Gid. Be forgettable. Dullness is armor." Then the envoy had smiled with blood on her teeth and added, softer: "But if you meet someone kind on the road, don't mistake kindness for safety. Kindness is just the door. What's behind it varies."

*Be dull.* Gideon had been dull for eleven days, and it was wearing her thin as old rope.

"I'm fine, Maren," she said.

"Of course you are." Maren patted her knee with a hand like a cured ham. "Drink your tea."

* * *

Pip found her at the noon meal, which was not really noon and not really a meal — barley broth stretched with seawater, the cook's final indignity before landfall. The boy threaded through the crowded deck plates like water finding cracks, appeared at her elbow without appearing to cross the intervening space, and sat down close enough that their shoulders touched.

He didn't speak. He never wasted words he could hoard. He simply ate, and watched her eat, and after a while said, in the flat way of children reporting weather:

"There's a man aft who keeps looking at your sleeve."

Gideon's spoon paused above the bowl for exactly the length of a heartbeat, then resumed. "Lots of men look at lots of sleeves. It's a crowded ship."

"Not my sleeve. Not Maren's sleeve. Yours. The left one. Where the cuff is frayed." Pip turned his bowl in slow circles, eyes fixed on the middle distance the way he did when he was delivering intelligence gathered over hours of patient, invisible watching. "There's something red stuck in the fraying. Wax, maybe. He looked at it, then he looked at your face, then he went below and talked quiet with two other men by the fresh-water tank. They all looked at your sleeve after."

The broth in Gideon's mouth turned to lead. She swallowed it anyway, because wasting food was a sin aboard the *Meridian*, and because swallowing kept her face doing nothing at all.

Red wax. She ran the memory back through the nights like a woman retracing steps in the dark. The chancellery. Sarelle's desk. The seal pressed into crimson wax while the city burned beyond the shutters — and afterward, Gideon gathering up the fallen scraps of wax out of some instinct of tidiness or reverence, stuffing them into her cuff pocket, meaning to throw them into the harbor and instead, in the chaos of the evacuation, sewing them — God help her, *sewing them*, the sentimental fool — into the frayed hem of her left cuff, where a scrap of crimson might work loose at any hour and lie on the deck like a dropped confession.

Envoy Sarelle would have shot her. Kindly, but thoroughly.

"Pip," she said carefully, "you're a very good watcher."

"I know."

"If anyone asks you what you see on this ship, you see nets, and soup, and nothing else."

He considered this with the gravity of an admiral reviewing fleet dispositions. "That's lying."

"That's surviving. There's a difference and it's measured in funerals." She softened it by ruffling his hair, which he endured the way cats endure rain. "Go find Tull. Tell him — " she weighed it, then let it go, because the die was already rolling — "tell him I need to talk to him and Maren tonight. Engine room. After the lamps are doused."

Pip nodded once and dissolved into the crowd, and Gideon sat alone in the noise of forty strangers eating thin soup, feeling the wax scraps against her wrist like embers that refused to cool.

* * *

The engine room after dark was Tull's cathedral: hot, humming, lit orange by the banked boilers, smelling of oil and iron. He'd cleared a corner among the spare piping and set out a lantern turned low, and Maren had smuggled down half a loaf and a jar of pickled things that officially did not exist. They sat in the iron glow like conspirators, which, Gideon thought grimly, was precisely what they were becoming whether she willed it or not.

She didn't tell them everything. She told them almost nothing, and somehow it was still too much.

"There's something on this ship," she said, "that certain people want destroyed, and other people want captured, and both kinds of people would hurt whoever's near it to get it. It's mine to carry. It's not yours. I want you to know that much so you can decide how far you stand from me at landfall."

Silence, except the breathing of the engines.

Then Tull said, wiping his huge hands on a rag that only spread the grease thinner: "Is it why the census man marked your page?"

Gideon went very still. "You saw that?"

"I read manifests for cargo loading. I can read anything." Tull shrugged. "Little fellow with the ledger. He wrote something in the margin next to your name and underlined it twice. Underlining twice means trouble, in any language."

Maren leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's my question, and don't dress it pretty. If these people come for you — do they come for the whole ship? Do they burn us all to reach whatever's in your coat?" She held Gideon's gaze, unflinching, kind and merciless at once, the way only mothers and cooks can manage. "Because there's a hundred and six souls aboard, Gideon. I count them every night. I know their names. I've fed their children. You ask me how far I'll stand from you — well, that depends on whether standing near you puts my hundred and six in the fire."

And there it was, the question Gideon had been carrying alongside the letter, heavier than the letter, the question Sarelle had never had to answer because Sarelle died before the arithmetic began. *What is the truth worth, weighed against the people who carry it?*

"The letter names traitors," Gideon said quietly. "Generals who sold our fleet at the harbor so the war would end with them on the winning side. If it dies, they win forever. History will call them heroes and everyone who drowned will have drowned for nothing." She looked at her hands. "I won't pretend that's worth more than a hundred and six lives. But I also won't pretend it's worth nothing."

Maren exhaled slowly through her nose. Tull folded his rag into smaller and smaller squares.

It was Pip, sitting unseen in the shadow of the condenser, who spoke first — and none of them had heard him come down, and none of them sent him away.

"I vote for the truth," the boy said. "My whole family's dead anyway. Somebody should write down why."

Maren closed her eyes. Tull stopped folding the rag.

And it was then that boots sounded on the ladder — unhurried, deliberate — and a man descended into the engine room's orange light whom Gideon recognized from the aft deck, from Pip's report, from the fresh-water tank: lean, gray-templed, with the careful voice of a man who had chosen his side late and intended to survive the choice.

He raised his open hands, showing them empty.

"Don't trouble yourselves," he said pleasantly. "I'm not here for the coat." His eyes went to Gideon's frayed left cuff, and stayed there. "I'm here because others aboard already know what's sewn in it. And they aren't patient men. We need to talk about what happens when they stop knocking politely."
