## 5. Port Callis Burns Its Papers

Gideon smelled the smoke before she saw the fire.

It was wrong for a morning — not the tar-and-fish reek of the rendering yards, not woodsmoke from the bakeries on Lantern Row, but something sharper, chemical, deliberate. Paper smoke. She had stood downwind of burning paper only once before, at the fall of the capital, when the Chancellery staff had fed eleven years of treaties into the courtyard braziers rather than let the imperial troops read them. That smell had gotten into her coat lining and stayed there for weeks, and her body remembered it now with a lurch that had nothing to do with seasickness.

She was on the quay by then, sent ashore with Tull to barter for rope and lamp oil, and she stopped in the shadow of the customs shed and looked up the hill toward the Archive House.

They were carrying ledgers out of it like pallbearers.

There were six of them, imperial gray from boot to collar, moving in the unhurried rhythm of men doing sanctioned work. Two soldiers stood at the door checking names against a list — she could see the list, could see the clerk's precise hand even at this distance — and behind them, through the tall windows of the reading room, other figures moved among the shelves, lifting things down, weighing them in their hands, setting some aside and passing others out into the light.

Into the yard. Into the fire.

The brazier they'd brought was too small for the work, so they'd built a pyre in the courtyard, iron grating over stacked kindling, and the republican records went onto it in armfuls: shipping registries, harbor pilots' certifications, the birth rolls of coastal parishes, the manifests of every vessel that had ever cleared Port Callis under the tricolor of Vellamare. Gideon watched a man in gray feed in a ledger as thick as a Bible, watched him pause to fan its pages open so the flames could reach the middle, and thought: *that one is the fleet registry.* She knew its shape. She had signed it often enough, back when her signature had still meant something to somebody.

"Girl." Tull's voice, low, at her shoulder. "Don't stare."

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring like a woman watching her own funeral." He shifted the coil of rope higher on his shoulder and put his body between her and the hill, casually, the way he did everything. "Whatever that is, it isn't ours. We need lamp oil and we need to be back aboard before the tide turns."

But she couldn't move. Because this was the thing she had crossed half a drowned country carrying, wasn't it? Not the letter itself — the letter was thread and wax against her ribs — but the idea underneath it. The belief that what happened at the harbor mattered, that the generals' treachery would be *known*, that someone someday would read the record and say: *here is where the lie began.* And here were the victors, on an ordinary Tuesday, burning the record. Not hiding it. Burning it, in daylight, with a clerk's list, because hiding implied shame and they felt none.

"They're not even being quiet about it," she said.

"Quiet's for thieves," Tull said. "That's a government."

A boy ran past them uphill with a handcart, and Gideon caught the edge of what he shouted to the fishwives: the agents had come on the night packet, three days behind the census taker, with writs signed in Vellamarre City. All documents of the dissolved republic, all records of office, all registers bearing the seal — surrendered for destruction, penalty of harbor-law imprisonment for concealment.

*Concealment.*

Her hand found the seam of her coat without her willing it. Tull saw. Tull said nothing. But something passed between them there in the shadow of the customs shed, and it was the first time since the capital that Gideon felt less alone with the weight, and more afraid at exactly the same rate.

* * *

Corvin Ashe had catalogued four hundred and six volumes before lunch, and he burned none of them.

He had been assigned to assist the purge commission — "assist," in the way a locksmith assists burglars — and he performed his part with the mechanical correctness that had kept him alive and employed through two changes of administration. He verified titles. He confirmed dates. He initialed the destruction manifest in triplicate. When the agent in charge, a heavy man named Brakkus with ink-stained cuffs and a soldier's contempt for clerks, handed him a volume of harbor pilot charts and said "republican issue, burn it," Corvin looked at the chart, noted that it was identical to the imperial issue except for the cartouche, and wrote *republican issue* on the manifest, because accuracy was the last thing he owned outright and he guarded it the way other men guarded gold.

But when they reached the third shelf of the reading room, he stopped.

The passenger manifests.

Bound in gray board, spines numbered in his own handwriting — or in the handwriting of the office he served, which amounted to the same thing. Every soul who had cleared Port Callis for the past nine years, recorded name by name, port by port, trade by trade, in the great slow machinery of counting that Corvin believed in the way other men believed in gods. He had spent three weeks aboard the refugee ships adding to these books. He had written marginal notes in them. He had written one particular note about a woman with no birthplace and no family and no trade before the war, a note he had told himself was routine diligence and had known, privately, was something else.

"Manifests," Brakkus said. "Seals?"

"Some bear the republic's stamp."

"Then they bear the stamp of a state that no longer exists, and a document concerning nothing cannot be preserved." Brakkus held out his hand. "Burn them."

"They contain living persons," Corvin heard himself say. "Persons currently resident in the empire's jurisdiction. The census requires—"

"The census will be redone," Brakkus said pleasantly, "on imperial forms, in imperial ink, beginning from the emperor's accession, as though nothing before it required counting. That is the entire point, clerk."

And that was the sentence that did it. Not cruelty — Brakkus was not cruel; he was worse, he was tidy. It was the phrase *as though nothing before it required counting*. As though nine years of births and deaths and sailings and drownings could be retroactively unwritten. As though the woman in berth twelve with no birthplace had never been anywhere at all.

Corvin carried the manifests to the pyre himself, because refusing would have been suicide and dropping them would have been noticed, and because he wanted — needed — to see what happened to them. He set them on the grating. The covers scorched first, then curled. He watched the flame take the first pages, names going brown, then black, then gone, and he stood there long enough that Brakkus barked at him to step away, and he stepped away, and behind him the fire did its patient work.

Four hundred and six volumes catalogued. Nine years of lives uncounted.

It was, he reflected, walking back down the hill with the manifest under his arm, remarkable how a man could serve a system faithfully for twenty-two years before understanding what the system was *for*.

* * *

She came to him at dusk, at the customs table where he worked, and she did not knock.

He knew her walk before he looked up — he had catalogued that too, the deliberate ordinariness of it, a woman performing the role of nobody. Up close, in the lamplight, with the smell of smoke still hanging over the whole town, she looked older than her entry had made her and more frightened than her voice allowed.

"You burned them," she said.

"The commission burned them. I witnessed."

"You *witnessed*." She laughed, once, without humor. "Do you know what was in those books? My father's fishing license. Maren's marriage record. The muster roll of the harbor fleet — the real one, with the convoy dates, the escort assignments. The proof of what actually happened the night the fleet sailed. And it's ash now, so whatever story the generals tell, whoever writes the histories—" she stopped herself, jaw tight, "—there'll be nothing left to argue with."

"There is one book they haven't found," Corvin said quietly.

Her eyes came up fast. Too fast. She caught herself, smoothed it, failed.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Gideon Dravenmoor died at the fall of the capital," he said. "I have the death register entry. Cross-referenced against the survivor manifests twice. And yet a woman answering to that name stands in front of me carrying a dispatch sewn into the left lining of her coat, because she has touched that seam two hundred times since I boarded your ship and never once when her hands were idle." He turned the ledger on the table toward her, open to her page, to his marginal note. "I am a census taker, madam. Noticing is my profession. Lying about noticing is my sin."

For a long moment neither of them breathed. Somewhere outside, the pyre still smoldered; the wind had turned and brought the smell in through the shutters.

"If you report me," she said finally, "they won't question me. They'll bury me with it unread."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because today I watched nine years of the dead get fed to a fire on the orders of a man who called it tidying," Corvin said. "And I initialed the receipt." His voice did not rise; it got quieter, which for him was the same thing. "I have spent my life believing that a thing written and countersigned cannot be undone. Today I learned they simply burn the countersignature first. So." He closed the ledger. "So I find myself wanting to know what is worth all this. What is worth your life, sewn into a coat. Tell me the truth — not for my report. There will be no report. Tell me because I am the only man in this port who writes things down anymore."

She searched his face for a long time. Whatever she found there — the loneliness, probably; two people who had made professions out of being unnoticed, recognizing each other across a customs table — it decided her.

"It's a confession," she said. "The last dispatch of the Republic of Vellamare. Four generals sold the harbor fleet. Names, dates, payment records. The envoy gave it to me with his dying breath and everyone who knows its contents is dead or hunted. If the empire finds it, they destroy it. If the loyalists find it, they destroy it. And if I die with it, it dies with me, and then the fire wins after all."

Corvin sat very still. Then he pulled a fresh sheet toward him, dipped his pen, and did not write.

"Not here," he said. "Not on loose paper — loose paper burns. Listen to me, because this matters more than either of our lives. Under the Census Act, any statement entered into a master ledger, in the presence of a certified taker and countersigned by witnesses of standing, becomes permanent record. Immune to revision. Sealed against destruction by anyone below the Lord Registrar — and the empire has never once disowned its own master ledgers, not through two dynasties and a civil war, because the moment they do, every land grant, every inheritance, every treaty boundary in the realm dissolves with them. They would sooner burn the palace." He looked up at her. "If that letter were entered — verbatim, witnessed, countersigned — into the provincial master census ledger at Vellamoor Counting House, it would become part of the empire's own bones. They couldn't burn it without unraveling themselves."

"And where is this counting house?"

"Inland. Three days' hard travel through occupied country, with both the commission and your general's men looking for us both." He capped his ink with the finality of a man closing a door on his old life. "I can be ready within the hour. Can you?"

Gideon thought of the ship. Of Maren's hands in the galley flour, of Tull's engine heartbeat, of Pip asleep in the rope locker with a stolen schoolbook under his head. Three days. Everything she had, spent on three days.

"They're already asking questions aboard," she said. "One of ours — one of theirs now — saw the seal wax on my sleeve." She straightened, and the fear in her face settled into something harder and steadier, the look of a woman picking up a weight she intended to carry all the way. "Meet me at the north pilings, midnight tide. And Corvin Ashe — bring your pen. Bring every pen you own."

Beyond the shuttered window, the pyre on the hill glowed like a wound in the dark, and the smoke of nine years drifted out over the water, going wherever lost things go.
