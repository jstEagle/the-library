## 6. The Loyalist's Bargain

They came to her in the galley after the second bell, when the lamps were turned low and the cook's fire banked to embers, and Gideon understood from the way they arranged themselves—three of them, spaced like men who had rehearsed this in a hold somewhere—that the pretense of ordinary days was over.

The tall one was called Serrano. She had watched him for weeks without knowing why: the way he ate, economical and watchful; the way his eyes went to hands before faces; the way he stood at the rail during storms when everyone else had gone below, as if he were measuring the distance to some shore only he could see. A soldier's habits worn thin as coin. Now he stood with his back to the galley door, and the other two—broad, silent men with dockworker's shoulders—stood where the exits were, though the exits were the same door.

"Gideon Dravenmoor." He said the name carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile he intends to pick up again. "Or whatever we're to call you."

"You've had three weeks to call me whatever you like. You've called me nothing."

"I've been waiting," Serrano said, "to see if you were worth the trouble." He pulled a stool from the wall and sat, unhurried, and the gesture was almost friendly, which was the most frightening thing about it. "You mend nets like a woman who's mended ten thousand of them. You don't gossip. You don't gamble. You gave your bread ration to the Vellamare children without being asked, and you watched the door while you did it." He smiled without warmth. "Envoy Halvard's courier. The one they say drowned at the fall of the capital."

"The one they say drowned," Gideon agreed, "drowned."

"Mm." He turned his stool slightly, and the lamplight caught the scar along his jaw. "I was at the harbor that night. Third Battery. We held the seawall for six hours after the order to retreat, because someone had to, and no one told us the order was bought. I lost thirty-one men to a fleet that was sold before it ever weighed anchor." His voice did not rise. It went quieter, which was worse. "So understand me. I am not the empire's man. I am not the generals' man. I am the dead men's man. And the dead men want this finished."

"Finished how?"

"Cleanly." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "The letter you carry—it's real. We know it's real, because Halvard sealed it himself, and we know what's in it, because there were three copies and we burned two. Yours is the last. And as long as it exists, it is a lamp in a powder store. The empire wants it buried. The generals want it destroyed. The loyalists—" he spread his hands, and Gideon saw the calluses of a rifleman, the ghost of a salute, "—the loyalists want to live. Every soul on this ship is a hostage to that letter. Every soul in every port we make landfall. One imperial agent with a hot iron and an hour alone with you, and the Meridian becomes a tomb with sails."

"I've managed this long."

"You've managed because you've been lucky, and because a census clerk is too busy counting to hunt." Serrano's eyes flicked, briefly, toward the deck above—toward the port side, where Corvin Ashe had been taking his measurements and his notes for three weeks now. "But they've sent someone to Callis. We've seen the wire traffic. And when they come, they won't be careful, and they won't be alone, and the first thing they'll do is put a pistol to the cook's head and ask her what she knows."

The galley was very warm. Gideon became aware of the fire's ticking, the ship's groan, her own pulse in her ears. "And your offer?"

"Protection. Passage. A new name in a neutral port, papers that will survive inspection, money enough to start over. In exchange, the letter comes to us. It goes into a furnace, and the ash goes into the sea, and no one ever has to die for it again." He said it gently, the way a priest offers absolution. "You carried it faithfully. No one will ever say otherwise. Let it rest, Gideon. Let *them* rest. Halvard is dead. The republic is dead. A piece of paper won't raise either of them."

She thought of the envoy's hands—ink-stained, trembling the last night, pressing the packet into her palms. *Not for vengeance,* he had said. *For the record. Someone must be able to prove we knew what was done to us.* And she thought of thirty-one men at a seawall who had died believing in an order, and of the ash of two copies already, and of how easy it would be—how merciful, almost—to let it be three.

"No," she said.

Serrano exhaled through his nose, not surprised. "Then we put it to the crew."

"The crew isn't mine to command."

"No. But it's yours to lose." He stood, and the two silent men straightened with him. "Tomorrow night, the aft hold. Everyone who matters. The cook. The engineer. The boy. You. You'll each say your piece, and we'll each say ours, and the crew will decide what its safety is worth." He paused at the door, and for a moment the soldier's mask slipped and she saw only a tired man who had buried too many friends. "I hope they choose you, Gideon. I hope it more than you know. But I'd rather break your heart than bury this ship."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the dark of the women's compartment with her back to the hull and her palm flat against the coat folded in her lap, feeling through the wool and linen the small, hard rectangle that had become, over three hundred leagues, the shape of everything she was.

Near dawn, Maren found her. The cook came down the ladder with two mugs of chicory and no ceremony, handed one over, and sat on the bench across from her, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"Tull says Serrano's people have been counting our rifles," Maren said at last. "Three weeks, quiet as mice. Counting rifles, counting lanterns, counting which of us sleeps where." She drank. "I don't like being counted."

"No. Neither do I."

"The boy knows something's wrong. He's been following you around like a duckling with its feathers up." Maren turned the mug in her broad hands. "Gideon. I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to hear the whole question before you hate me for it." She looked up. "Is it worth a ship? Is it worth *them*?"

Gideon held the chicory and felt its heat seep into her palms and could not answer honestly, because the honest answer was that she did not know—that she had carried the letter so long it had stopped being a thing she carried and become a thing she was, and a person cannot weigh herself against other people and come out clean.

"Halvard trusted me," she said finally. "Everyone else who could have carried it is dead. If I give it up, then the generals win twice—once with their treason, and once with the erasing of it. And the empire wins too, because the empire's whole victory is a story, and stories are all they'll leave us."

"And if you keep it, the story ends with all of us in the sea."

"That's what Serrano says."

"And what do *you* say?"

Gideon looked at her—the flour in the creases of her knuckles, the gray coming in at her temples, the woman who had fed forty refugees out of a galley the size of a coffin and never once let anyone go hungry—and said, "I say I'm tired of deciding alone."

Maren nodded slowly. "Then don't. Tomorrow night. Say your piece, and let the family speak." She stood, and at the ladder she paused, not looking back. "Whatever they choose, Gideon—don't you dare hate them for it. Fear makes cowards of people who love you. It's not a betrayal. It's just fear."

* * *

The aft hold smelled of tar and salt-pork and the long damp of a hundred sleeping bodies, and by the light of two storm lanterns the Meridian's found family gathered to decide what a piece of paper was worth.

Serrano spoke first, and he spoke well—thirty minutes, measured and merciless, of wire traffic and imperial agents and the arithmetic of hostages. He named the ports where couriers had been taken. He named the methods. He did not threaten; he simply laid the future out on the deck like a chart, and the future was full of funerals.

Then it was Tull.

The engineer stood with his arms folded, wrench-calloused hands tucked into his armpits, and looked at his boots for a long moment before he spoke. "I've known Gideon since Callis," he said. "She's the truest person on this ship, and I'd trust her with my life, and I have, twice, in weather you wouldn't believe." A ripple of uneasy laughter. He didn't smile. "And I'm voting to give it up."

The laughter died.

"Because I keep the engines," Tull said, "and the engines keep all of us, and I have spent this whole war learning one thing: you can't outrun what you won't look at. Serrano's right. That letter is a lit fuse, and we're all standing on it. Gideon, I love you like a sister. And I want to live. And I want Pip to live, and Maren to live, and I am not ashamed of it." His voice cracked on the last word, and he folded his arms tighter, as if holding himself together. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But that's my vote."

Maren's vote was quieter. "I fed forty people through a winter that should have killed us," she said, "and I'll tell you what I learned. You can't save everyone. You save who's in front of you. The dead are dead, Gideon. The living are at this table." She would not meet Gideon's eyes. "Give it to the fire, love. Let it go."

Two votes against her. Two voices she had trusted with everything, choosing the family over the mission, and Gideon felt the choice like a blade going in slow—not because they were wrong, maybe, but because she understood them completely, and understanding did not make it hurt less.

Then Pip stood up.

The boy was eleven years old and half the size of everyone in the hold, and he had to stand on a crate to be seen, and he did not hesitate. "You're all counting wrong," he said. "You're counting the people on the ship. But the letter isn't *for* the ship." His voice rang in the tar-dark, too loud, too clear, the voice of a child who had learned to shout to be noticed. "It's for everyone who comes after. It's for the people who'll ask *how did the harbor fall*, and the only answer left will be a lie, unless—" he pointed a small finger at Serrano, then at Tull, then at Maren, "—unless somebody was brave *now*. You want to live safe? Everybody wants to live safe. The generals counted safe too. That's how they sold the fleet."

The hold was silent.

"Gideon," Pip said, turning to her, and his eyes were ferocious and wet, "I'm with you. Whatever it costs. I'm with you."

Two votes to two, and the rest of the crew looked at the deck, at the lanterns, at their own hands—unwilling to say aloud what their silence said.

Serrano let the silence stand for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like an officer acknowledging a report. "Deadlock," he said. "So it stays as it is—for now." He looked at Gideon, and there was no kindness left in his face, only the flat patience of a man with a schedule. "But understand what you've chosen. The imperial agents landed at Callis this morning. Two of them, with a warrant and a list. And your census clerk—" he jerked his chin toward the deck, toward the shore, toward the world, "—was seen going ashore with them an hour ago. Talking. Showing papers. Your meticulous little bookkeeper, Gideon."

He turned to go, then paused at the ladder.

"I'd decide fast. Whatever you're going to do with that letter—do it before they come back aboard."

The lanterns swung as the ship rolled, and the shadows swung with them, and Gideon stood in the dark of the aft hold with a boy's hand gripping hers and the whole weight of the dead pressing down, and she knew, with the certainty of a woman watching a storm come over the water, that the time for carrying the letter quietly was over.

It was time to make it a record—or die trying.
