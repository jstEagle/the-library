## 7. Three Ports, One Truth

Corvin Ashe had spent eleven years believing that the world could be understood if only it were counted properly.

Three days inland from Port Callis, hiding in the loft of a burned granary while soldiers of two different governments searched the road below, he was beginning to revise that belief. The world, he now suspected, could be counted forever and still not add up. But it could be *witnessed*. That was a different arithmetic entirely. That was his.

"Your lamp," Gideon whispered beside him in the dark. "It's showing through the boards."

He cupped his hand around the flame without extinguishing it. He needed it. In the lamplight's small circle lay the open page of his traveling ledger, and across that page, in his smallest, most disciplined hand, ran the first lines of the letter she had finally shown him — the republic's last dispatch, copied out in secret over two nights in haylofts and ferry cabins while the roads behind them filled with men who wanted it gone.

She watched him write with an expression he had learned to read as suspicion held barely in check by exhaustion.

"You're still copying it," she said. "By hand. When we could be moving."

"Copying is the work," Corvin said. "Everything else is running."

Below them, boots crunched on gravel. Voices — imperial accents this time, the flat vowels of the occupation garrison — called to one another about sweep patterns and curfew violations. A dog whined at the end of a lead. Corvin counted the footsteps out of habit: four men, two staying by the cart, two circling the granary. The building had been torched in the first week of the occupation and never rebuilt; its blackened skeleton gave them cover but no warmth, and the loft floor was soft with char in places. He had chosen it precisely because no one else would choose it.

The footsteps passed on toward the millpond.

Gideon let out a breath. "They're getting closer every day. Both of them. The loyalists know we took the coast road, and your own people—" she said *your own people* with a bitterness she didn't bother to soften anymore— "know you requisitioned a horse you never returned."

"I signed for it," Corvin said. "There's a record."

"There's always a record with you."

"Yes," he said. "That is rather the point."

* * *

They had crossed out of Callis Province two nights ago, wedged among barrels on a fish cart, Gideon's face hidden under a netting hood and Pip — who had refused, absolutely and with the terrifying logic of a nine-year-old, to be left behind — pretending to sleep against her side so that no soldier would think to ask a child questions. Maren and Tull had taken the other road, drawing eyes, carrying nothing but a decoy packet sewn into a coat identical to hers. It was Tull's idea. Tull, who had voted in the ship's galley to give the letter up, had volunteered for the most dangerous part of keeping it. Corvin did not pretend to understand found families. He only recorded what they did.

Now they were three: the courier who was officially dead, the stowaway who was officially nobody, and the census taker who was becoming, with every forged travel pass and altered manifest he wrote, a criminal in the empire whose forms he had faithfully filled for a decade.

The irony was not lost on him. He simply declined, for the present, to enter it in any ledger.

"We can't keep this pace," Gideon said now, low. "Another two days and they'll close the pincer at Harrow Ford. You said it yourself — the loyalists have friends in the river garrisons."

"They do." He turned the lamp slightly and studied her face, all its planes sharpened by hunger and fear and something harder underneath, something that had been hardening since the night she'd told him the truth in the aft hold and he had looked at the envoy's seal and understood that he was holding ten years of careful neutrality in his hands and watching it burn. "Which is why we stop running toward the border and start running toward paper."

She stared at him. "Say that again like it means something."

"Harrow Ford," Corvin said, "has a provincial counting house."

* * *

He explained it the way he explained everything — methodically, as though the fate of nations were a matter of filing procedure, which, Corvin had come to believe, it very nearly was.

"The master census ledger," he said, tracing the shape of a book in the air with one finger. "Every province keeps one. Not the military rolls, not the tax registers — those are copies, working documents, revisable. The master ledger is different. It's established under Article Nine of the Imperial Records Act, older than the current dynasty. Anything entered into it, in the presence of two witnesses, and countersigned by the provincial registrar, becomes permanent record. Immune to revision. Not difficult to revise — *immune*. The law is explicit. Strike a line from a master ledger without countersignature and the entry stands as written; the alteration itself is recorded as a crime against the record. Emperors have tried. Emperors have failed. There is a reason historians trust the ledgers more than they trust the chronicles."

"And the chronicles are what the victors write."

"The chronicles are what the victors write," Corvin agreed. "But the ledger is what the *clerks* write, and no one has ever built an empire that could function without its clerks. They burn archives — I've watched them do it, you watched them do it in Callis. Archives are buildings. Buildings can burn. But the master ledgers are protected by law so old that the men who enforce the burning don't remember why the law exists. They assume it's about taxation. It isn't. It was written by a dynasty terrified of being forgotten, and it binds the dynasty that replaced them."

Pip stirred in the straw behind them, half asleep, listening the way children listen — apparently gone, actually missing nothing.

"So we walk into a counting house," Gideon said slowly, "occupied territory, both factions hunting us, and ask the registrar to enter a letter that names four imperial generals as traitors."

"Not ask." Corvin shook his head once, sharply. "Asking invites refusal, and refusal invites negotiation, and negotiation invites delay, and delay is how they catch us. No. We invoke. Article Nine includes a provision — almost never used, because almost no one knows it exists — for the entry of *testimony in extremis*. A person in immediate peril may dictate a statement of fact to any accredited recorder, who is bound by oath to take it down verbatim, in the presence of witnesses, and forward it under seal to the central archive within thirty days. Once entered, it cannot be struck. Not by the garrison. Not by the generals. Not by the historian they sent to bury Callis — I saw his commission papers when they fed my manifests into the fire. His authority ends where the master ledger begins. He knows it, too. It's why they purge the provinces before anyone reaches a counting house."

"But if we reach one first."

"If we reach one first," Corvin said, "then the Republic of Vellamare's confession stops being a piece of paper sewn into a dead woman's coat and becomes part of the historical record of the empire itself. Every scholar, every court, every future government that opens that ledger will read the harbor fleet sold for gold, in the words of the man who carried the payment orders. Verbatim. Witnessed. Countersigned. Forever."

The lamp flame stood very still between them.

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of someone setting down a weight to pick up a weapon.

"The envoy made me swear to get it to neutral ports. To the chancelleries. To people who would *use* it."

"He made you swear to make it matter," Corvin said. "You told me that. Word for word — you remember things word for word, it's one of the first things I noted about you. A chancellery can lose a document. Chancelleries burn too, Gideon. Neutral ports sign treaties. Treaties get renegotiated. But a line in the master ledger will outlive every government currently claiming territory between here and the sea. Your envoy wanted the truth to survive. I am offering you the only container in this empire that does not burn."

* * *

They planned through the small hours, Pip sleeping between them like a ballast stone, the lamp turned down to a bead.

Harrow Ford's counting house stood on the market square, attached to the registrar's residence — which meant a household, which meant servants, which meant doors and windows and a human being inside it whose profession, whatever his politics, was the integrity of the record. Corvin had served with provincial registrars at training college. They were a maligned, meticulous, unloved brotherhood. They did not forgive errors and they did not abandon their books. He was counting on the second trait surviving the war.

"I'll go in as myself," he said. "My accreditation is real. My name is real. If they've circulated descriptions, they'll have described a census taker traveling alone. Two travelers and a child read differently."

"And if the registrar has already been bought?"

"Registrars aren't bought. They're threatened. Which is why the testimony must be given and sealed in a single sitting, and why you—" he met her eyes— "must be prepared to say all of it. Every name. Every date. Every figure in the letter. Verbatim means verbatim. There is no summarizing. There is no protecting anyone by leaving a name out. If we do this, we do all of it."

Gideon looked at the dark where the letter lay against her body, and Corvin watched her do the arithmetic he could not: the envoy's face, the four generals, the fleet going down in the harbor with its crews aboard, the shipmates scattered on two roads to buy her this chance. Busy hands, he thought. She always kept busy hands. Tomorrow those hands would hold nothing but the edge of a registrar's desk and her own voice.

"One thing more," she said at last. "When it's done. When it's entered and sealed and forwarded. What happens to the woman who dictated it? Because they'll want her name in the record too. Witnesses. Testifier. That's how your law works, isn't it?"

Corvin did not answer immediately, and his silence was its own answer, and he saw her understand it, and he hated — with a precision he had previously reserved for misfiled documents — the moment when accuracy and mercy diverge.

"It's how the law works," he said quietly. "And I will find a way around it if one exists. And if none exists—"

"Then it exists for a reason," Gideon said. "Write the truth down. All of it. We'll see what survives."

Far off toward the river, a signal whistle sounded — two notes, then three, the loyalists' mark, answered a minute later from the opposite quarter by the garrison's horn. The pincer was closing. Dawn was perhaps four hours off, and Harrow Ford perhaps six hours' walk, and somewhere behind them Tull and Maren were spending themselves as decoys against the morning.

Corvin closed his traveling ledger, banked the lamp, and began, in the dark, to rehearse the exact wording of the invocation he would speak at the registrar's desk.

Six hours, he thought. Hope, with a deadline attached.

Then, from the road below, a voice called up through the burnt timbers — calm, unhurried, terribly familiar, the voice of a man reading from an official description:

"Census taker Ashe. We know the child is with you. Come down, and the record stays clean."
