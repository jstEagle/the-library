## 8. What Pip Carried

They came for us at the hour when the lamps burn lowest — the third watch, when even the gulls sleep and the harbor water goes black as spilled ink.

I woke to the sound of Tull's voice, and that was the first wrongness. Tull did not speak in the night. Tull grunted in his sleep like a ship settling at anchor, and Maren swore she could tell his dreams by the pitch of it. But this was Tull awake, and Tull angry, and beneath the anger something I had never heard from him before: fear.

I had slept in the loft of the chandler's storehouse where Corvin had found us lodging under names he had invented himself — names with proper birthplaces, proper trades, proper dead mothers, all of it entered in his ledger in that small, merciless hand. I came down the ladder quietly, as I had learned to come down ladders in a war, and looked through the gap in the shutters into the yard.

Four men. Lanterns hooded but not dark. And Tull standing between them and the door of the room where Pip slept, in his shirtsleeves in the cold, his big hands open at his sides the way he held them when he wanted people to understand he was not reaching for anything.

"You've got the wrong door," Tull was saying. "We're fishworkers. We're nobody."

"That's what everybody is," said the man in front, and I knew him. Halvard Voss. Boatswain on the *Meridian* for six years before the fall, a man who had once carried Pip on his shoulders through the fish market at Callis so the child could see over the crowd. He had voted with the loyalists. He had wept when the capital burned. Now his face was gray with resolve, and there were three men behind him who had not wept at anything.

"We know the courier's aboard this street," Voss said. "We know which room. Open it, Tull, and no one has to be carried anywhere."

My hand went to my coat before my mind caught up with it — the old reflex, the seam, the letter lying against my ribs like a second heart. Except it wasn't against my ribs. It was folded inside Pip's schoolbook, because three days ago Corvin had measured the book with his calipers and pronounced it, of all the objects in our possession, the one thing no one would ever search: *a child's primer, water-stained, valueless.* The letter lay flat between the pages of arithmetic tables, and Pip had been carrying it to and from the well every morning like homework, swinging it, dropping it once on purpose in front of a loyalist's eyes to show how little it mattered.

Pip was in that room now. And Voss thought the letter was with me.

I should have stayed hidden. Every instinct bred into me by two years of running said stay hidden, let them take what they think they want. But Tull was alone, and Tull had never in his life been alone against anyone, and through the shutter gap I saw one of the three men behind Voss shift his weight and put his hand inside his coat.

I went out the back and around through the alley, and I came into the yard the way a person comes home, loud, annoyed, half-dressed. "Voss," I said. "It's the middle of the night and you're frightening the whole street."

He turned. For one moment the boatswain I had known looked out of the stranger's face — shame, quick and human — and then it drowned under duty. "Gideon." Not Dravenmoor. He'd stopped using surnames with me weeks ago, a courtesy that had curdled into something else. "You know why we're here."

"I know you're pointing men at my family."

"They're not your family. That's what none of you will admit." His voice cracked on it, and I understood suddenly that he believed it — believed that the warmth on the *Meridian* had been a kind of cowardice, a refusal to grieve properly, to stay hard for the republic's sake. "The generals sold the fleet. The letter proves it. If it reaches an imperial record, every loyalist still breathing becomes a traitor retroactively. Do you understand? They'll hang us twice."

"Then give yourself up," I said. "Confess. Testify. That's what the letter asks — not vengeance. Names, dates, the truth, read into the record so it can't be buried."

"The record," Voss said, and laughed, and it was the worst sound of the night. "Gideon. The empire burns records. You watched them do it at Callis. You're carrying a corpse and calling it a lantern."

Behind him, the man with his hand in his coat moved, and everything happened very fast after that.

* * *

Later I would try to order it, and fail. Memory gave it to me in pieces, the way wreckage comes ashore.

Tull stepping forward, not fighting, just *being large*, putting himself in the way the way he had put himself between storms and rigging for thirty years. The man with the coat drawing something long and dark. Tull going down without a cry — Tull, who had cried at Maren's bread burning, going down silently, folding like a mast coming down, and the sound of his head on the cobbles worse than any shout.

Me lunging for Voss and being caught by the other two, my arms pinned, my coat torn open, the lining ripped along the old seam — ripped, and empty, and for one heartbeat Voss stared at the nothing in my hands and I saw him understand that he had guessed wrong about where, and then his eyes went past me, up, to the window of the room where Pip slept.

"No," I said. I don't remember deciding to say it. "Voss. *No.*"

But he was already moving, and the terrible thing, the thing I have never been able to forgive or fully blame, is that he was gentle about it. He opened the door softly. He spoke softly. I heard him through the wall: *"Pip. Little Pip. It's Halvard. We need your help finding something, and then we'll all be safe."*

And then Pip's voice, high and steady and absolutely terrified, saying the bravest lie I have ever heard: "I don't have anything. I only have my schoolbook."

A silence. Then Voss again, softer, more dangerous: "Show me your bag, little one."

* * *

Maren came out of the kitchen doorway with a fish-gutting knife and the fury of forty years of feeding people who kept getting taken from her.

I don't know where she got the knife. I don't know how long she had been standing there listening. She crossed the yard like weather, and the man holding my left arm let go of me to deal with her, which was the last mistake of his career, because I drove my elbow back into his throat and was free.

What happened next took eleven seconds and cost us everything, and I will set it down exactly, because Maren deserves exactness.

She did not fight them. She stood between the bedroom door and the yard, small and gray-haired in her shawl, and she said, "The child stays. Whatever you do to me, the child stays in that room until this is finished." And when they moved to move her, she cut the forearm of the nearest one, shallow, a cook's cut, enough to make him shout and make the others hesitate — and hesitation was the whole currency of those eleven seconds.

Because in those eleven seconds, Pip came out the window.

I heard it — the scrape of the sill, the soft drop onto the lean-to roof below, the scramble down — while I was wrestling the second man, while Maren was shouting things I will not write down because some of them were lies about where the letter was hidden and some were truths about what she would do, and both kinds bought seconds. Pip hit the ground running, schoolbook clutched to their chest like a shield, bare feet slapping the cobbles toward the alley where — because Corvin Ashe was a meticulous man who planned for the failure of plans — a cart waited with a tarpaulin and a horse too old to interest anyone.

Voss broke first. He shoved past Maren — knocked her down, I saw her go, saw her head strike the doorpost — and ran for the alley, and I ran after him, and neither of us was fast enough, and it did not matter, because the cart was already moving, already turning, and the last I saw of that street was Pip's face looking back at me over the tailboard, white as bone, gripping the schoolbook, gone into the dark toward the counting house and Corvin and the only law left in the world.

* * *

Maren was alive. That was the mercy the night allowed. The blow had opened her scalp and she bled freely and she would not let me bind it properly, sitting on the step of the storehouse with the dawn coming up gray, waving my hands away.

"Leave it. Scars are honest." Her voice was thick and slow, concussion speaking through her, but her eyes were clear. "Did the child get clear?"

"Yes."

"Then it's cheap." She touched the blood gingerly, examined her fingers. "I spent two years keeping my head down and my mouth shut and telling myself it was loyalty. Keeping the family safe. Keeping the soup hot." She looked at me, and there was something in her face I had never seen there, something unburdened and terrible. "It wasn't loyalty, girl. It was hiding with company. Tonight I chose something. It hurts more. It's better."

"Maren—"

"Tull's alive too. They took him, didn't kill him — a hostage is worth more walking than bleeding. Which means they'll send terms. Which means we have until noon." She closed her eyes against the light. "Go to your census man. Tell him the price just changed. Tell him we deliver the truth tonight or we trade it for Tull tomorrow — and God forgive me, I no longer know which I'd choose, so don't ask me again."

I stood, and my legs nearly failed me, and I made them work.

Down the hill, the provincial counting house rose against the brightening sky, its windows dark, its doors locked, its ledgers sleeping inside — thousands of pages of names and births and trades and deaths, waiting for ink. Somewhere on the road behind me, a cart with a too-old horse was carrying a water-stained schoolbook full of arithmetic and confession toward those doors, and a child was holding it the way you hold the last living thing in a drowning sea.

Behind me, Voss's men were writing terms. Ahead of me, imperial agents were already asking questions at the gates.

Between them, if we were fast enough, if we were brave enough, if the empire's own laws could be turned into a weapon before its servants turned theirs — a letter was going to be read aloud, every word, into the permanent record of the victors.

I started running.
