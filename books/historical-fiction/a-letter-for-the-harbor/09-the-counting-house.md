## 9. The Counting House

*Gideon*

The counting house of the province of Vellamare-Under-the-Crown had been built to outlast empires, which was the only reason we reached it alive.

Corvin took the servants' stair two steps at a time, his ledger case banging against his hip, and I followed with my lungs full of fire and my ears full of bells — the alarm bells of the harbor quarter, rung because Voss's men had finally decided questions were no longer profitable. Behind us, somewhere in the crooked streets, boots on cobblestone. Ahead, a door of black oak banded in iron, and above it, carved into stone older than the war: *What is entered is entered forever.*

"Is that true?" I gasped.

"It is law," Corvin said, and threw the bolt behind us. "The Census Charter, Article Nine. No entry in a master ledger may be struck, amended, or destroyed save by countersignature of three sworn officers of the record. It was written to protect us from kings." He almost smiled. "Tonight it will have to protect us from an empire."

The hall inside rose up like the inside of a bell. Shelves climbed four stories into gloom, ten thousand ledgers sleeping in their rows, and at the center of it all, on a dais beneath a skylight gone gray with dawn, the master ledger itself — open, vast as a table, its pages ruled and waiting. A single lamp burned beside it. Someone had left it lit for morning clerks who would never come.

I put my hand on the coat where the letter slept against my ribs and felt my own heart knocking back through the cloth.

"They'll come through those doors," I said.

"Yes."

"How long do we have?"

Corvin set down his case, drew out his pens, his ink, his blotting sand, and laid them in a row with the terrible calm of a man arranging his own funeral. "However long it takes you to speak," he said, "and however long it takes them to stop listening."

* * *

*Corvin*

The historian arrived before the soldiers did, which told Corvin everything he needed to know about the man.

He came alone through the far door — the one reserved for officers of the archive — a narrow person in imperial gray with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like a man doing arithmetic he did not enjoy. He did not draw a weapon. He carried instead a leather folio stamped with the double eagle, and he set it down upon the master ledger's dais as if claiming territory.

"Halloran," he said. "Provincial Historian, Third Rank. You are Corvin Ashe. Census taker, second class." His gaze moved to Gideon, standing by the shelves with her hand pressed flat over her coat. "And you are a dead woman whose paperwork has become inconvenient."

"The letter," Gideon said. "You know about the letter."

"I know that the Office of Records received instruction this morning that certain documents of the late Republic are to be reclassified as *unverified material*, which is the administrative term for ash." Halloran folded his hands. "I know that two agents wait outside that door for me to finish whatever sentimental business brings you here, so they can burn what remains. And I know—" his voice tightened, "—that Article Nine does not care what I know."

"Then you understand," Corvin said quietly.

"I understand that I am trapped in a room with a law older than my office and two people mad enough to use it." Halloran looked at the master ledger for a long moment. When he spoke again, it was barely above a whisper. "Do you know why I became a historian? Because when I was nine years old, soldiers burned my town's parish records, and afterward there was simply nothing. No proof my family had ever existed. My mother spent her whole life trying to remember enough dates to be real." He straightened. "If you intend to dictate a document into the permanent record, then dictate. I will not help you. But I am sworn, God forgive me, never to stand between a citizen and the record."

The doors shuddered under the first blow.

* * *

*Gideon*

She had rehearsed this a hundred times in her head — on the deck of the Meridian, mending nets, walking the words like a patrol route. Envoy Sarelle's voice, dry and exact, dictating to her in a burning study while the shells came down across the harbor. *If I don't survive the week, girl, the letter must. Words in the right place outlive armies.*

But she had never rehearsed doing it while men broke down a door.

The second blow cracked the oak. Dust sifted from the lintel. Corvin opened the master ledger to a fresh page, dipped his pen, and wrote the heading in a hand so steady it seemed to come from someone else: *Testimony entered under Article Nine, witnessed and countersigned.*

"Name of the declarant," he said.

"Gideon Dravenmoor." Her voice cracked on her own name — the name that belonged to a dead woman, the name she was about to spend. She lifted her chin. "Formerly secretary to Envoy Maerin Sarelle, Plenipotentiary of the Republic of Vellamare. I carry the envoy's final dispatch, sealed and unopened since the fall of the capital. I will now read it aloud, in full, for entry into the permanent record."

Third blow. The iron bands sang.

Corvin nodded once. "Proceed."

She drew out the letter. The seal wax crumbled like old blood between her fingers — the same wax a loyalist had once recognized on her sleeve, half a lifetime ago it seemed, though it had been weeks. She unfolded the pages. The handwriting was Sarelle's: small, upright, unhurried even at the end of the world.

She read aloud.

*"To whoever holds the record after us —"* and the words filled the great hall the way the bells had filled the streets, climbing the shelves, waking the ten thousand sleeping ledgers. She read the names of the generals. General Halveth, who sold the fleet's sailing orders for a title. General Ostrin, who let the harbor batteries go dark on the appointed night. General Cade, who signed the surrender of twelve thousand men who might have fought. She read the amounts. The accounts. The dates. Her voice grew hoarse and she kept reading, and Corvin's pen flew, and the door splintered inward by inches with every line, and none of it mattered — none of it could matter — because every word spoken aloud in this room was already becoming the one thing no army in the world could take back.

Pip's face rose in her mind. Maren's hands kneading bread. Tull laughing in the engine dark. *This is for you too,* she thought. *All of it. So that what happened to us happened to someone, and was written down by someone, and cannot be unwritten.*

*"Given under my hand, Maerin Sarelle, in the capital, in the last free hour of the Republic of Vellamare."*

She stopped. The letter was finished. The door hung by one hinge.

"Witnesses," Corvin said, and his voice rang off the vaulted ceiling like a verdict. "Article Nine requires three sworn officers of the record. I countersign first." He signed, blotted, turned the page toward the dais. "Historian Halloran. You are sworn."

Halloran stared at the page. Outside, men were shouting; the hinge screamed. And the historian of Vellamare-Under-the-Crown picked up the pen in his ink-stained fingers, and wrote his name beneath Gideon's testimony in a hand that shook only a little — the smallest treason ever committed, and the largest.

"Second witness," he said. "Entered."

They needed one more.

* * *

*Corvin*

The door gave way as the third signature dried.

Agents came through in a storm of splinters and lamplight — four of them, blades drawn, coats wet with harbor fog. Their officer crossed the hall at a run and stopped dead at the edge of the dais, because Corvin had risen to meet him holding the master ledger open like a shield, and because the man had been a soldier long enough to recognize a fortification when he saw one.

"Step away from the record," the officer said.

"You step away from the record," Corvin answered, and heard his own voice sound like his father's, like every clerk and census taker and keeper of dull, immortal lists going back four hundred years. "This is the master ledger of the province, protected under the Census Charter. What is entered here is entered forever. You are welcome to verify the entries. You are welcome to add your own. You are not welcome to touch a single page, and every man in this room knows what happens to the empire that burns its own books — it stops being an empire and becomes only an army."

The officer looked at the open page. At Gideon's dictated confession, complete, dated, countersigned twice. At Halloran, standing very straight beside his own signature like a man who had finally found something worth being hanged for.

"Arrest them," the officer said slowly, "and the ledger comes with them. Or leave the ledger, and explain to the capital how the confession got in." He sheathed his blade with a snap. "You've made yourselves evidence, Ashe. Evidence gets locked away, not killed."

"Then lock us away," Gideon said, "with the book."

For a moment nobody moved. Dawn had arrived without anyone noticing; gray light fell through the fouled skylight and lay across the open page like a hand laid flat in blessing. Somewhere below, the harbor bells were still ringing, though whether for alarm or merely for morning, no one could say anymore.

Halloran closed the master ledger with both hands, gently, the way one closes a coffin or a cradle. "The record stands," he said. "Let history sort out the rest. That is what it is *for*."

The officer signaled his men back. And as they were taken — Corvin with his wrists bound but his head high, Gideon glancing back once at the shelf where the truth now slept among ten thousand other truths, indistinguishable, immortal — she caught Corvin's eye and mouthed the question that would follow them both into whatever cell awaited:

*Did it hold?*

Corvin smiled, for the first time in longer than he could count.

"It's entered," he said. "It's entered forever."
