## 10. A Name Struck Through

Seven years after the counting house, the herring still came in silver shoals off the point at Vess, and the nets still needed mending, and Gideon Dravenmoor — who was now, on every document that existed, Gidde Vasse, sister to Maren Vasse of the fishing village of Vess, three days' sail south of Port Callis — sat on the seawall with her hands full of knots.

Busy hands. Maren had taught her that trick long before either of them knew it would become a way of life. *A woman with idle hands is a woman the tide can read*, Maren used to say over her galley stove aboard the *Meridian*, and so Gideon mended nets she did not need to mend, split kindling past what any stove required, re-caulked a rowboat that had been sound for two years. The village thought her diligent. Only Maren knew her for what she was: a woman keeping her fingers too occupied to shake.

"You've gone through that net twice," Maren said now, settling onto the wall beside her with two bowls of chowder. She was broader than she'd been aboard ship, gray coming into her braid, but her eyes hadn't changed — they still took inventory of everything and told you nothing until she chose. "The mesh is finer than when you started."

"I like fine work."

"You like forgetting." She handed over a bowl. "Eat. The fish won't mourn your diligence."

Gideon ate. Below them, children ran the tideline shrieking about a stranded moon jelly, and one of them — dark-haired, quick, eleven years old and certain of everything — was Pip's shadow in miniature, though Pip herself had been eleven once and certain only of hunger. That was the thing about villages. They repeated themselves. They made new versions of everyone you had lost.

* * *

The Harbor Confession had a name now. That was the strangest part. In the first years it had been nothing — a rumor, a disputed page, imperial historians calling Corvin Ashe a fraud or a fool or, in the more careful pamphlets, *an instrument of republican propaganda smuggled through the census office itself*. Then the third general named in the letter had defected to the coastal provinces and confirmed everything in exchange for amnesty, and then the second had died inconveniently and left papers, and then there was no arguing with it at all. Scholars came to the provincial archive at Callis now with white gloves and citation cards. They called it the Harbor Confession as if it had always existed, as if it had surfaced naturally, like a wreck giving up its cargo, rather than being dictated aloud by a dead woman while men battered the doors of the counting house.

*A dead woman.* That part worked better than anyone planned. The ledger recorded that the confession was entered by "the courier G.D., identity verified against manifest records," and beneath that, in Corvin's small exact hand: *Deceased per capital casualty rolls, 14th of Frostmonth.* The empire had struck her name through itself; she had only stayed struck. Historians loved it. There were essays. *The Courier Who Died Twice.* One young academic from the university at Merel had come all the way to Vess last autumn asking after local families who might have known refugees from the *Meridian*, and Gideon had sold him smoked fish and told him the sea took many that year, and he had thanked her and written in his little notebook, and she had stood in her own doorway watching him go and felt something she refused to name.

Not grief exactly. Not pride either. Something between — the feeling of reading your own obituary written by a stranger who got the important parts right and would never know it.

* * *

Corvin came in spring. He always came in spring, when the roads dried and the census office granted its travel warrants and the world, briefly, pretended to be orderly again. He arrived on the mail coach looking precisely as he had aboard the *Meridian* seven years ago — coat buttoned to the throat, case under his arm, spectacles catching the light — except that the coat was newer and the case held different papers and his hair had gone silver at the temples in a way that suited him, damn him, because nothing ever seemed to suit Gideon; things merely happened to her and she endured them.

He did not knock. He had learned that much about village life, if nothing else about softness. He came down the lane from the coach road with his case and stopped at the gate and looked at her working the net in the yard, and said, in the tone of a man confirming a figure in a ledger:

"Mesh is uneven on the third row."

"Third row's always uneven. It's how I sign my work."

"Ah." He opened the gate. "Then the archive contains a great deal of your signature. They're teaching the third-row irregularity at Merel now as evidence the Confession was transcribed by an amateur hand rather than a trained scribe. It's considered proof of authenticity. You've become a scholarly argument."

"Have I?"

"A persuasive one." He set the case down by the door and accepted the bowl Maren pushed into his hands without ceremony — this was ritual now, seven springs deep, and rituals do not require discussion. "Pip is behind me somewhere. She got off at the crossroads to argue with the coachman about his figures."

"She'll win."

"She'll win," Corvin agreed. "She's studying statistics at Merel. She wants to reform the census. She says—" and here something crossed his face that on another man might have been called emotion— "she says the record is only as honest as the people allowed to make it. She quotes me. I'm told I'm quotable now. I find it unsettling."

Gideon laughed, and it surprised her, the laugh, the way it still could arrive unbidden after everything.

They ate chowder at Maren's table. Corvin reported, because reporting was how he loved people — she had understood that about him years ago, that precision was his tenderness, that he counted things because counting was how he kept faith. Two of the generals' surviving officers had finally faced tribunals. The imperial archives at the capital had formally acknowledged the Callis ledger as permanent record, immune to revision, its countersigned entries binding — *your law*, he reminded her, *I told you it would hold* — and a monument was going up at the harbor itself, granite, inscribed with the names of the fleet's crew lost in the betrayal. Not her name. Not the envoy's. The envoy's family had declined to have it carved, saying he belonged to no faction, only to the truth, and Gideon had wept alone in the smokehouse when Maren relayed the news, because it was right, it was exactly right, and she had not expected anything done by committees to be right.

"They asked me to write the official account," Corvin said quietly, turning his bowl. "The full history. My name on the title page."

"What did you say?"

"That history is best entered anonymously, by a careful hand, and countersigned." He looked up. "I said no."

"You should have said yes. You earned it."

"I earned the ledger," he said. "Let someone else earn the book." And then, because he was Corvin, he changed the subject to the price of salt fish and whether Vess had been enumerated properly in the spring count, and Gideon let him, because she had learned that some men build walls out of numbers and it was kinder to admire the masonry than to ask what the walls were for.

* * *

Pip arrived at dusk, sunburned and triumphant, having extracted from the coachman both an apology and a corrected fare. She was twenty now and tall and wore her hair short in the manner of university women, and when she hugged Gideon she hugged her the way she had at thirteen, fierce and whole, as if letting go were a decision that required renewed consent each time.

"I brought you something." She dug in her satchel and produced a book — a school primer, old, water-swollen at one corner, its cover stamped with the crest of a provincial school district that no longer existed. Gideon knew the book. Of course she knew the book. She had watched a child slide the republic's last confession between its pages while loyalists kicked down a cabin door, and had sworn afterward never to touch it again.

"Pip—"

"It's mine now. Bought it at the estate sale of the man who seized the *Meridian*'s manifests. Full circle, see? Everything gets archived eventually." Pip pressed it into her hands. "I just thought — it carried the most important thing I ever held. It shouldn't spend forever on a shelf. It should be where it finished carrying."

Gideon held the primer and could not speak. Maren, passing with the lamp, put a hand briefly on her shoulder — one squeeze, no words — and moved on, because that was Maren's way: love administered in dosages precisely calibrated to be survivable.

That night Gideon sat up late with the primer open in her lap and did not read it. She listened instead to the house settle, to the sea working at the stones below the seawall, patient as a clerk, taking its inventory grain by grain, keeping everything, losing nothing, telling no one.

* * *

Spring passed into summer. Corvin went back to Callis; Pip back to Merel. The herring came and went. Gideon mended nets with an even third row now, sometimes, when she wasn't thinking — and noticed, and undid them, and smiled at her own foolishness.

In the fourth year of her new life — the ninth of the whole account, though who was counting; well, she was, always, she'd caught it from Corvin like a fever — a letter came from the university. Addressed to *Maren Vasse and household*. Inside, a request from a doctoral candidate compiling oral histories of the harbor fall: did any resident recall persons aboard the refugee vessel *Meridian*, particularly a woman of about thirty, name unknown, believed to be the courier of the Harbor Confession? Any detail would be invaluable. The courier's identity remained, the candidate wrote, *the great open question of the period.*

Maren read it aloud at the table, twice. Tull — who had come home from the northern works two winters back, missing two fingers and none of his humor — whistled low. Pip, visiting, grinned like the stowaway she'd been.

"Well?" Maren said. "Great open question. They're asking the village. They'll come knocking."

"They can knock," Gideon said. Her hands were steady around her teacup. Busy hands, quiet heart, mostly. "I'll sell them fish."

"And if they ask you straight out? If some bright-eyed student looks at you across a counter and asks whether you knew her — the courier, the dead woman, G.D.?"

Gideon looked down at her hands. Knot-callused. Salt-cured. A stranger's hands, a dead woman's hands, hers.

"I'll tell them the truth," she said slowly, testing it, finding the shape of it true. "I'll tell them I knew her. That she was ordinary. That she was frightened the whole time and did it anyway. That she liked fine work." She looked up, and found them all watching her — Maren, Tull, Pip — her family, her witnesses, her countersignatures. "And I won't tell them a single thing else. Let the ledger hold what the ledger holds. Some things are safer entered than spoken."

Maren nodded slowly, and reached across, and turned the letter face-down, closing the great open question like a door.

Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, filling every indentation, every footprint, every furrow the nets had left in the sand — erasing nothing, only carrying it all out to where the deep water keeps its records, complete and unread, forever.
