## 1. The Weight of Names

The water came in at dusk and left in the morning, as if it, too, were on shift.

Freya Delacroix had learned the hospital's tide the way she had learned everything else that winter — by keeping watch. It seeped up through the floorboards of the requisitioned farmhouse from the flooded fields beyond, carrying with it the smell of frozen earth thawing against its will, and by dawn it stood a finger's width deep along the east wall, where the worst cases lay. Each morning she and the orderlies swept it out through the broken door with brooms and boards, each evening it returned, patient as anything in this war. The men joked about it at first. By the third winter no one joked about water anymore. There was nothing in this hospital that had not been drowned in something.

She moved down the rows with her lamp turned low, checking dressings by touch more than sight. Forty-one men tonight; two would not see morning, and both knew it, and neither would say so. This was the arithmetic she had grown fluent in — the subtraction performed quietly, in the dark, between rounds. Private Ansel Brandt, nineteen, gut wound, kept asking for his mother in a voice he believed was a whisper. Sergeant Milo Ferenc, who had stopped bleeding hours ago and was now simply cooling, like a stove banked for the night, held her wrist when she leaned close and said, "Sister. Write it down."

"Write what down?" she asked, though she knew.

"My name. All of it. Milo Anton Ferenc. In case they lose it."

"We don't lose names here."

He smiled with cracked lips. "They lose them everywhere else."

She promised him, and hated herself for how easily promises came now, how cheap they had become, like the cigarettes the quartermaster counted out one per man per week. She moved on. The lamp guttered. Somewhere near the west wall a boy from the highlands moaned a word in a language no one on the ward spoke, and Freya found herself listening to the shape of it rather than its meaning — grief, probably, or home, which amounted to the same thing here.

When the ward finally slept, or stopped pretending otherwise, she went into the dispensary, bolted the door, and knelt before the bottom shelf.

The ledger lived behind the bottles of carbolic, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged so far back that retrieving it required her whole arm and a held breath. It was not a hospital ledger. The hospital's own books lay upstairs on the matron's desk, ruled and official, recording admissions and deaths in the tidy hand required by regulation: name, rank, unit, cause. Those books went to division every month. Those books told the truth the way a fence tells the truth about a field — by deciding first where the edges are.

Her ledger told the rest.

She unwrapped it and set it on the floor between her knees. A school exercise book, originally, bought three winters ago in a market town whose name she no longer permitted herself to think of fondly; now swollen with loose pages stitched in with thread, filled edge to edge in her small, slanted hand. She did not open it casually. She never opened it casually. Every time, there was a moment — brief, cold, unmistakable — when some sensible voice in her said *put it back*, and every time she opened it anyway, because the sensible voice belonged to a woman who had once believed the edges were where they said they were.

* * *

Her brother's name had been Étienne Delacroix, and officially he had never died at all.

That was the word the clerk used, in the second autumn, when she walked forty kilometres off a granted leave to the records office to find him: *misfiled*. Said without malice, almost apologetically, the way one says a page has been smudged. Étienne, who had written her every fortnight in handwriting like a bird's footprints, whose last letter described an orchard he intended to buy after the war — misfiled. His unit had been destroyed in the fighting along the river; of the dead, some were recorded, some were listed as missing, and Étienne appeared on neither list, because the report from his sector had arrived incomplete and no one, in the collapse of that terrible week, had gone back for the rest.

No one had gone back for the rest.

She remembered standing in that office among the shelves, among ten thousand folders each containing a human life reduced to a signature, and feeling something in her chest go very quiet and very hard, like a coal cooling into slag. Grief she had expected. What she had not expected was the clerical calm of it all — the sense that the war's dead passed out of existence not through any gate of flame but through an ordinary filing error, and that somewhere a man with ink-stained fingers would go home that night and sleep soundly, having done nothing wrong except fail to care.

She had gone back to the hospital and, that night, behind the carbolic, begun a different kind of record. Not out of defiance — defiance had felt too grand a word for it then, and still did. Out of bookkeeping. Someone had to keep the accounts. If the world insisted on losing names, then someone, somewhere, ought to be found holding them.

* * *

Private Ansel Brandt died at twenty minutes past four in the morning, calling again for a mother who was, unknown to him, already nine months in the ground herself — Freya had learned this from a letter that arrived that same evening, and she sat with the knowledge alone, two orphaned halves of a family neither of them could complete. She closed his eyes and folded his hands and wrote him into the hospital's official register in her best regulation hand: *Brandt, Ansel, Pvt., 3rd Rifles. Wounds received in action.*

Then she waited until the ward slept, and took down the other book.

*Brandt, Ansel,* she wrote. *3rd Rifles, but detached to the mortar company since October — check whether the Rifles' rolls even show him. Mother dead (confirmed by letter). Nineteen years old. He hummed when he thought no one heard. He asked me to write it down.*

That last line she hesitated over. Regulation had no column for humming. But the ledger's purpose, she had decided long ago, was not to imitate the official record with its edges drawn neat; it was to hold everything the edges cut away. So she wrote it, and the writing cost her something small and irretrievable each time — she could feel it go, a grain of herself paid out like coin — because to write a man down truly was to admit he had been alive, and admitting that, night after night, was a tax levied only on her.

Sergeant Ferenc lasted until noon. She recorded him twice as well: once officially, once truly. In the true book she noted that he had served eleven years, that he had a scar shaped like a river delta on his left forearm which he claimed came from a farm accident and which she privately suspected came from somewhere else entirely, and that his last words had been an instruction to a stranger. *In case they lose it.* They had lost it already, of course. The divisional return for his sector that week listed six dead; she had counted eleven, and the discrepancy was not error. Two of the missing five were marked in the adjutant's copy with a small notation she had seen once, years ago, and never forgotten: *struck — irregular*. Not killed. Struck. As if death itself were a clerical excess to be trimmed before the report went up the line.

She had learned early which omissions were sloth and which were policy. Sloth she forgave; war made clerks tired and paper scarce. Policy wore a uniform and spoke courteously, and its name, in this sector, was Colonel Aurel Vasseur, command surgeon, whose reports were famous throughout the corps for their clarity. Clarity was the word men used. *Vasseur's returns are clear as glass.* And they were. Glass was exactly the right material — you saw straight through them to whatever stood on the far side, and the glass itself told you nothing, and cut you if you pressed your face against it.

Three winters of reading those returns against what came through her wards had taught her the grammar of his silences: which units vanished whole, which villages appeared in supply requisitions but never in casualty lists, which dates carried, in his immaculate hand, a small blank space where a number should have been. She had copied those blanks into her ledger too. Once — a crossroads south of the marsh road, a date in late spring two years past — she had written the place and the date and, where the names should be, a question mark. She had treated perhaps forty civilians from the surrounding farms that season, enough of them to hear the way talk dried up when the crossroads was mentioned, the way mothers changed the subject by looking at their children. No wounded ever came from it. No wounded ever comes from a thing like that; that is rather the point of a thing like that.

She blew out the lamp before dawn and put the ledger back behind the carbolic and knelt a moment longer in the dark of the dispensary, smelling iodine and wet plaster and the river smell rising through the floors, and let the cold come into her knees.

* * *

Matron Voss caught her at first light, sweeping water out the broken door with the others.

"You look like something the flood brought in," Voss said. She was sixty and built like the farmhouse itself, and she was the only person in the hospital whom Freya trusted with anything, though she had never tested quite how much of anything.

"I slept."

"Liar. The Brandt boy?"

"This morning. And Ferenc."

Voss said nothing for a moment. Water swirled around their boots and out across the mud of the yard, carrying straw and a bloodied dressing toward the ditch. "The divisional return came yesterday," she said at last, carefully, the way she handled anything sharp. "Thirteen dead for the week. I count fifteen beds emptied."

"Sixteen," Freya said. "You forgot the engineer they brought in Tuesday under a rifleman's tags. He'd been trading tags with a dead man since summer. Whoever writes the rolls for the 3rd Rifles has one engineer too many and one rifleman who died in August."

Voss looked at her then, long and level, with the expression of a woman deciding how much she wished to know. "And where do you keep all this, Sister Delacroix? In your head?"

"In a safe place."

"That's what I was afraid of." Voss took the broom from her and worked the water toward the door. "Safe places burn, girl. Everything kept safe burns. I've buried two hospitals' worth of paper in my time to keep it from the wrong eyes, and I'll tell you what I learned: paper survives only if nobody loves it. The moment somebody loves a piece of paper — guards it, hides it, risks for it — that's the moment it becomes worth finding."

Freya watched the grey water slide out into the yard. "Then I suppose I must love mine carefully."

"You must love it *quietly*," Voss said. "There's a staff car coming up the lane. Division colours on the fender. Somebody important wants something, and in my experience when somebody important wants something from a field hospital in the third winter, it is never good news and never simple."

Freya turned. Far down the lane, where the poplars stood bare as sentries relieved of their rifles, a dark car came on through the frost, mud fanning from its wheels, moving with the unhurried authority of a thing that had never once been stopped.

She did not know yet why her stomach tightened. She told herself it was only the hour, only the cold, only the habit of bracing.

But her hands, she noticed, were already reaching — without her willing it — for the state of the dispensary shelf, the depth of the oilcloth, the distance from the bolted door.

The car turned in at the gate.
