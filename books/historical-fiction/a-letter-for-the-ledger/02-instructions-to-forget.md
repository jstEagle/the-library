## 2. Instructions to Forget

The frost came in through the broken pane above the door like an unwelcome visitor, and Freya Delacroix met it there at dawn, as she met most things — alone, and with her hands already working.

She had risen before the orderlies because the night had been long with dying. Corporal Vennet, nineteen years old and certain until midnight that his mother was coming for him, had gone out on the last of the ebb tide around four o'clock, and Freya had sat with him so that he would not go the way his mother had not come — unattended, unclaimed. His name went into the official book under *fever, complicated*. His name went into her ledger under *Vennet, Corporal Émile, b. 1926, sang to himself when the morphine came*. The second entry took longer. It always did.

So she was standing at the door with a candle stub guttering in its jar, breathing steam into the grey air, when she heard the wheels.

The hospital stood at the edge of what had been a market town and was now mostly a memory of one — shelled twice, evacuated once, and finally repurposed, because a war does not care what a building used to mean. Beyond the gate the road ran east toward the lines, a brown ribbon stitched with ruts frozen hard as iron. Nothing moved on that road at dawn except supply wagons going the wrong direction, which is to say away from hope, and the sound of these particular wheels had the lightness of an empty cart.

It stopped twenty paces out. The driver did not come down. He sat wrapped to the chin in a blanket coat, his breath rising about him, and for a long moment neither of them moved — the man on the cart, the woman at the door — two figures conducting a negotiation across frozen ground.

Then he leaned forward and set something down on the tailboard, gently, the way one sets down a thing one has been told is fragile but suspects is cursed, and clicked his tongue, and the wagon rolled on without him ever having spoken a word.

Freya counted to thirty before she went out. It was not courage; it was procedure. Three winters of war had taught her that the interval between a thing being left and a thing being examined was where the danger lived — a second shell might follow a first, a second shot a first, and once, memorably, a second note. But no shell fell, and the frost held its silence, and she crossed to the gate with her boots cracking the rime and found, sitting upright on the tailboard with his legs dangling over the edge like a small passenger waiting for a stop that would never be announced, a boy.

He was perhaps eight years old. He was caked in mud to the waist, as though he had been dragged rather than carried, or had crawled rather than walked, and the mud had frozen in ridges along his sleeves so that his arms looked cast in clay. His face was washed clean where tears had run their channels — two pale tracks down each cheek, the only clean thing about him. His eyes were open. That was the first wrong thing. Children who arrive at hospitals are usually asleep, or screaming, or past both into the flat stare of shock. This boy's eyes were open and dry and fixed on her face with an attention that made her think of the wounded men who watched the ceiling while you worked on them: present, cataloguing, gone somewhere behind it all.

"Hello," Freya said.

Nothing. Not a blink.

She looked past him up the road. The wagon had already shrunk to a smudge against the paling sky, unhurried, as though its business were finished and ordinary. She looked back at the boy, and then at the space beside him on the tailboard, where lay a sealed envelope weighted with a stone.

The envelope was good paper. That was the second wrong thing. In three years she had learned to read paper the way other people read faces — the army's grey stock that tore like grief, the requisition forms that crackled with bureaucratic indifference, the field letters softened by pockets and sweat. This paper was heavy, cream-coloured, and it bore, stamped in wax the colour of dried blood, a crest she knew better than her own signature: a caduceus crossed with a sword, beneath a coronet of laurel.

Colonel Aurel Vasseur, Command Surgeon, Fourth Army Group.

Her hands were steady. She noted that they were steady, and noted too that steadiness under such circumstances was itself a kind of symptom.

* * *

She carried the boy inside before she opened the letter. This was instinct older than any order — the body first, the paperwork after. He weighed almost nothing; the mud had stiffened him into a shape like a fallen statue, and when she lifted him his head came to rest against her shoulder with a terrible, trusting completeness, as though being carried by strangers were a language he had recently become fluent in.

Sister Anouk met her in the corridor, drawn by some nurse's sixth sense that operates independently of sleep.

"What have you got?"

"A boy. Eight, maybe. Mute, I think — or shocked. No marks on him that I can see."

"Whose is he?"

"That," said Freya, "is the question."

Anouk looked at the boy's face, at the frozen tracks where tears had been, at the eyes still open and still watching, and whatever she saw there made her reach out and begin unbuckling the little boots without another word. This was why Freya had survived three winters among the dying: not because she was strong, but because she had learned to find the four or five people in any room who would act first and ask permission never.

They put him in the side ward off the surgical corridor — the one reserved, in the building's former life, for the children of the staff, and which now held overflow stretchers and a smell of carbolic that even the dead had complained of, if the dead complained. They cut away the frozen clothes. Beneath the mud he was bruised across the ribs in patterns that spoke of gripping hands, adult hands, more than one pair. There was no wound. That was the third wrong thing, and by now Freya had begun keeping count.

A boy delivered to a military hospital, uninjured, unwashed, unwanting, at dawn, by a driver who would not speak, bearing the seal of the command surgeon himself.

She bathed him herself. Not because there was no one else — the ward was quiet, the morning shift had come on — but because she wanted her hands on the evidence before anyone else's interpretation got between her and it. Under the grime he was fair-skinned, dark-haired, hollowed at the cheekbones in the way of a child who had missed meals not recently but as a way of life. On his left forearm, half-hidden by the sleeve seam, there was a mark: not a brand, nothing so legible, but a scar, old and deliberate, a short line crossed by a shorter one. A tally? An initial? A farmer's child marked by some private superstition? She touched it once, lightly, and he did not flinch, and somehow that frightened her more than flinching would have.

When she dressed him in a corporal's shirt rolled five times at the cuffs, he let her move his arms like a doll's. When she set soup before him, he looked at it, looked at her, and did not touch it until she lifted the spoon and ate one mouthful herself, watching him all the while. Then he ate — quickly, mechanically, with the terrible efficiency of hunger that has learned not to trust its own continuance. He never took his eyes off her face.

And through all of it, he made no sound at all. Not a cry, not a whimper, not the small involuntary noises children make when they are handled. He breathed. He swallowed. He watched. The silence coming off him was not emptiness; it was pressure, like the air before a storm front, like the silence of a man deciding whether to speak. Freya had spent three years learning to hear the things that were not said in reports and orders and official histories, and she recognized the texture of this silence immediately, and it made the skin of her arms rise beneath her sleeves.

* * *

Only then, with the boy fed and wrapped in blankets in the corner cot where the shadow of the doorframe hid him from the corridor, only then did she break Vasseur's seal.

The note inside was brief. It was written in a hand she knew — she had spent three years reading that hand sideways, in margins, in amendments, in the elegant downward strokes with which Colonel Aurel Vasseur struck names from casualty lists. It read:

*To the Matron, Field Hospital 9.*

*A stray child, found on the road. He is feverish and will not last the week. Record him accordingly.*

*Forget the face.*

There was no signature. There needed to be none. The crest said everything the hand did not.

Freya stood very still in the corridor with the note in her fingers, and outside the frost was turning to a thin bright rain, and somewhere down the ward a man was calling softly for water in a voice like wind through wires.

She understood the instruction perfectly. She had, after all, been educated in exactly this dialect for three years. *Forget the face.* It was the same grammar as *misfiled*, as *consolidated*, as the beautiful passive constructions by which men died of *circumstances* rather than of decisions. And she understood too what the note was really telling her, beneath its surface, the way ice tells you about the river underneath: that this boy had seen something. That whoever sent him here believed he would die — believed, or intended — and that the record was being written in advance. *Record him accordingly.* A death certificate pre-authorized. A grave already dug in ink.

What she could not understand — what she turned over in her mind like a coin worn smooth — was why send him to her at all. There were other hospitals. There was the canal, there were the woods, there were a hundred cheaper solutions than a living witness delivered, breathing and warm, to the one officer's clerk in the Fourth Army Group known to keep her nose clean and her mouth shut. Unless — and here the thought arrived with the cold delicacy of a blade laid flat against the throat — unless sending him to *her* was itself the message. Unless the note was less an instruction than a test, or a courtesy extended between professionals, or a trap with her own name engraved on the trigger.

Or unless someone in Vasseur's orbit had wanted the boy alive and had chosen the only person they knew who might disobey.

She folded the note along its original creases. She did not burn it. Burning was destruction, and destruction could be construed as complicity; keeping was evidence, and evidence, in her private economy, was a form of prayer. It went into the oilcloth packet at the bottom of her footlocker, beneath the spare dressings, where a ledger the size of a missal lay closed upon its string.

Then she went back to the side ward.

The boy had not moved. He lay exactly as she had arranged him, hands on the blanket, eyes open, watching the ceiling with that cataloguing attention. As she entered, his gaze came down to her face and settled there, and something in its weight — the gravity of it, the way it asked nothing and demanded everything — cracked something in her that three winters had carefully mortared over.

She thought of her brother, briefly, the way she allowed herself to think of him perhaps once a month, in full, with the pain turned up rather than down. Luc had been eight once. Everyone had been eight once. Somewhere in every file she transcribed, in every name she struck and re-struck, there was an eight-year-old standing behind the grown soldier like a shadow out of season, and the war's whole machinery depended on no one looking at that shadow directly.

She pulled the stool to the side of the cot and sat down.

"You're not feverish," she told him quietly. "And you're not going to die this week. I'm afraid the colonel's arithmetic is wrong."

No response. Of course none. But the eyes stayed on hers, and she had the sudden, vertiginous certainty that he understood every word — that he understood, perhaps, rather more words than were good for either of them.

"My name is Freya," she said. "I keep records. That is my profession, and I am — I am told I am good at it." She heard herself speaking with the care of a woman walking across a frozen pond, testing each sentence before putting her weight on it. "Whatever it is you cannot say, I want you to know that I am in the business of things that are not said. It is the largest part of my work, in fact. So we should get on well."

The boy blinked. Once. Slowly, deliberately, the way a man nods across a courtroom.

Freya sat with him until the morning bell, and when she rose to go, his hand darted out from the blanket and caught her sleeve — just the cuff, just for a moment, grip light as frost — and released it again. Not holding her back. Marking her. A tally of his own.

In the corridor she stopped, pressed her palm flat against the cold plaster, and admitted to herself what she had known since the gate: that her careful neutrality, the double life she had balanced like a scale for three years, had just taken its first visible fracture. She had taken in a boy against a direct order bearing the command surgeon's own seal. Whatever came next, she was no longer merely the woman who recorded the erasures. She was now, herself, an erasure waiting to happen.

Behind her, in the side ward, the boy lay watching the ceiling — and on the wall beside his cot, where Sister Anouk had left a scrap of chalk for the orderlies' lists, his eyes kept returning, she had noticed, to the blank white rectangle.

As if he were deciding what to draw.
