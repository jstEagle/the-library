## 4. The Colonel's Kindness

The summons came at eleven, delivered by a corporal with the apologetic posture of a man carrying something that might go off.

*Colonel Vasseur requests Sister Delacroix at her convenience.*

Her convenience. Freya folded the slip into her apron pocket and stood a moment in the corridor of Ward C, listening to the rain work its way through the patched roof and into the third bedpan she would empty that morning. At her convenience. In three years of war she had learned to read the language of courtesy the way sailors read clouds: the more gracious the phrasing, the lower the pressure dropping behind it.

She washed her hands. She checked on Tomas — sleeping, thank God, in the linen room she had begun calling his room in her head, though no document said so — and then she crossed the yard between the hospital and the officers' quarters with her chin level and her ledger-shaped secret sitting where it always sat, just beneath the sternum, a second heartbeat made of paper.

Vasseur received her in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and pencil shavings. He was not what new nurses expected. They expected cruelty to look like cruelty — hard jaw, loud voice, some theatrical coldness they could hate cleanly. Vasseur looked like a tired schoolmaster who had once been kind and had filed the kindness away somewhere he could still find it, on the days it was useful. Grey at the temples, spectacles he cleaned with a handkerchief monogrammed by someone who loved him before the war taught him arithmetic. On the wall behind him hung the map of the province, and Freya did not let her eyes rest anywhere near the crossroads.

"Sister Delacroix." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit, please. You're standing all day; sit when you're offered the chance."

She sat. She had learned that from him too, years ago — that obedience in small things buys you room in large ones.

"Tea?"

"No, thank you, Colonel."

"Of course." He poured one for himself anyway, unhurried, and Freya understood that the tea was not hospitality. It was a clock. Everything Vasseur did was a clock. "How is the ward? I hear the roof's gone again over C."

"It leaks over beds three through seven. We've moved the men down."

"Mm. I'll speak to the engineers. Though I suspect the engineers will speak to the quartermaster, and the quartermaster will speak to God, and God has other priorities this winter." A small smile, self-deprecating, almost warm. "You've been with us since the first winter. That's rare. Most of your profession rotates home after eighteen months, if their nerves hold that long."

"Mine have held so far."

"So I observe." He drank. Set the cup down without a sound. And then, still gently, as if asking after her mother: "Tell me about the boy."

There it was. Not even five minutes in. Freya kept her hands quiet in her lap and gave him the truth she had prepared, because a lie needs a spine of truth to stand up straight. "A boy was left at our door six days ago. Perhaps eight years old. Mute — or mute since whatever happened to him; he doesn't speak. No papers. Whoever left him didn't leave a name."

"That's unusual."

"War is unusual, Colonel."

He smiled again, and for a moment let the smile do the work of an entire rebuke. "War," he agreed. "And yet we keep records. It's how we know it happened at all." He opened a folder on his desk — thin, grey, official — and turned it toward her. She did not need to read it. She knew its genre the way she knew the sound of mortar fire: admission of one male child, unnamed, fever-free, *still living*, in a clerk's careful hand. "You admitted him under 'stray.'"

"There was nothing else to write."

"There was 'deceased.'" Said softly. Almost tenderly. As if offering her a coat against the cold. "Children die in winter, Sister. You know this better than anyone. Fever takes them. Exposure takes them. Sometimes they arrive already too far gone, and the kindest thing a record can say is that the mercy came quickly." He folded his hands. "I am authorized to offer you a transfer. Home. Your file says Lorraine. The trains run east twice a week now, and there's a hospital in Metz that has been begging for a senior nurse with your field experience. Demobilization priority. You could be drinking coffee in your mother's kitchen within the month."

The room was very quiet. Rain on glass. The clock inside the tea.

Freya thought about her mother's kitchen — actually thought about it, let it rise up whole: the blue enamel pot, the smell of yeast, the window with the crack mended with resin that caught the light at four in the afternoon. Six years since she'd stood in it. Her brother Étienne had been alive the last time. She felt the want of it move through her like weather, and she hated herself for letting him see it move.

"You're offering me a transfer," she said carefully, "in exchange for a death certificate."

"I'm offering you a transfer," Vasseur said, "because you are exhausted, and because exhausted people make errors, and because this hospital cannot afford errors. The boy's presence here is irregular. Unnamed persons are administratively impossible. If he were to develop a fever — children do, so easily, in this damp — and if that fever ran its course, as fevers do when small bodies have nothing left—" He spread one hand, an elegant little shrug, giving the sentence its natural ending without having to speak it. "Then the paperwork resolves itself. One line. Stray, male, deceased of fever. Buried in the plot behind the chapel with the others whose names history hasn't needed yet. And you go home to Lorraine with a clean conscience, because nothing you wrote would be false. Children die of fever every day. Who could blame you? Who could even ask you questions?"

*Who could even ask you questions.* That was the architecture of him, laid open like an anatomy lesson: he never ordered. He arranged circumstances and then handed you the pen, so that everything written afterward was in your hand, in your ink, yours to carry forever while he remained spotless beside you.

She thought of Tomas asleep in the linen room, his thumb hooked in the collar of the shirt she'd given him, breathing with the whole body the way children breathe when they finally feel safe.

"And if he lives?" she asked. "If I report him honestly and he lives?"

Vasseur took off his spectacles and polished them, unhurried. When he put them back on, his eyes behind the glass were patient and bottomless. "Then he becomes a ward of the provincial authority, and the provincial authority will process him in due course, and due course, in wartime, is a long road with many turnings. Some children arrive at orphanages. Some arrive at labor detachments." A pause. "Some simply never arrive anywhere, and no one can find the error, because there was never a name to lose."

He let that sit. Then he rose, came around the desk, and stood by the window with his back half-turned to her — and Freya understood the geometry of it, understood that what came next was meant to be easier to receive from a man who wasn't looking at her.

"You think me cruel," he said.

"I don't think anything, Colonel."

"You think it. Thinking is private; I only govern actions." He watched the rain. "You've believed for three years that I don't see things. That my reports are narrow because my eyes are narrow. But I see everything, Sister. Seeing everything is the job. The job is deciding what the record can hold without breaking." He turned back to her, and now his voice changed — dropped its administrative silk and became, briefly, something older and wearier. "Do you remember the spring of '41? The depot at Saint-Aubin?"

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. She kept her face still. Three years of practice.

"A young nurse," Vasseur went on, "was found to have been passing information — names, dates, conditions of prisoners — to a chaplain who was suspected of corresponding with the enemy. Whether she was guilty or merely careless was never established. The tribunal wanted an example. There was a firing-squad detail being assembled for the following week — deserters, mostly, and one man who'd struck an officer — and the president of the tribunal proposed adding her name to the roster. A nurse. Twenty-three years old. For a few names passed to a priest." He shook his head slowly. "I reviewed the file. I found the evidence ambiguous. Ambiguity, in my experience, should be resolved in favor of the living. I had her reassigned here instead. To this hospital. Under my command, where I could — watch over her."

Freya's mouth was dry. She had never spoken of Saint-Aubin to anyone. She had buried it so deep that some mornings she doubted it herself, the way you doubt a dream. And here it was, excavated and set on the desk between them like an organ removed from a chest, still warm.

"You've never thanked me," Vasseur observed. "Not once, in three years. I confess I respected that. Gratitude is noisy. Silence is cleaner." He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands, and the warmth drained back out of the room like water through sand. "So consider this, Sister. Consider what you owe, and to whom, and what silence has bought you. Consider that I taught you which silences keep people safe — and I taught you at cost to myself, because the tribunal men still remember my interference, and one day they may collect. All I ask now is one line in one register. *Stray, deceased.* The boy goes into the ground behind the chapel with a prayer over him — I'll conduct it myself, I give you my word — and you go home to Lorraine. Everyone is spared. Even the boy. There are worse mercies than a quick fever, believe me. I have signed orders that would curdle yours."

*There are worse mercies than a quick fever.* And the horror of it, the true trap, was that she believed him. That was what gratitude had built in her over three years: not love, but a load-bearing belief. She owed this man her life, and the debt had been quietly mortgaged, year by year, against her silence — until she could no longer tell which silences were hers and which had been assigned to her, with interest accruing.

"I'll need to think, Colonel," she said.

"Of course." He smiled, kindly, terribly. "Take until Friday. The transfer order expires then; trains won't wait on sentiment, mine or anyone's." He walked her to the door himself, opened it onto the grey yard, and paused, one hand on the frame.

"One more thing, Sister. A professional observation." His voice stayed pleasant. "Fever is a strange illness. It can be reported. It can also, with care, be *encouraged*. A cold ward. A wet blanket. A window left open on a January night." His eyes rested on hers, mild as milk. "I mention it only because I know you'd never permit such a thing. You're the most conscientious nurse I've ever commanded. That is precisely why I chose you — then, and now."

He closed the door gently behind her.

* * *

She crossed the yard slowly, letting the rain hit her face, because she needed something colder than her own blood to think against.

In the doorway of Ward C she stopped. Through the fogged window of the linen room she could see him: Tomas, awake now, sitting cross-legged on the cot with her spare pencil stub in his fist, bent over a scrap of butcher's paper. Drawing. He drew constantly now, in the hours she could watch him — the same shapes recurring, a road, two lines of trees meeting like a pair of open scissors, small stick figures lying down in rows. He drew with the terrible fluency of children, who record what adults spend lifetimes learning not to see.

He looked up. Saw her through the glass. And lifted the drawing toward the window, pressing it flat against the pane with both small hands so she could not miss it — the road, the trees, the figures — his eyes fixed on hers with an urgency no fever ever produced, as if he knew, in whatever wordless country he lived in, exactly what had just been decided upstairs and exactly how little time the truth had left.

Friday, Vasseur had said.

Rain ran down the glass, blurring the boy's crayon road into something that looked like water, like a flood, like all the unrecorded dead of the province rising at once.

Freya pushed open the door and went to him, and began, quietly, to plan.
