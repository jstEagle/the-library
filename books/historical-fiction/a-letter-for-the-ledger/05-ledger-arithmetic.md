## 5. Ledger Arithmetic

The histories arrived on a Tuesday, in crates stamped with the double eagle of the Commission for the Official Record.

Freya had watched them being carried past the ward on a handcart, six volumes bound in grey cloth, their spines uncreased, their pages still smelling of ink and glue. *Preliminary Materials for the Armistice Settlement*, the title page read. *Compiled under the Direction of Colonel A. Vasseur, Command Surgeon, Third Army*. She had stood in the corridor with a basin of dirty water in her hands and felt something cold settle behind her ribs — not fear, not yet, but the particular attention of a woman who has spent three years learning to read what is not written down.

She waited two days before she touched them. Caution was arithmetic too: the hospital library was open to any officer who wished to consult the record of his own glorious conduct, and officers came and went at all hours. But the rains had come again, and the front had gone quiet in the way that meant men were dying somewhere out of sight of the guns, and by Thursday evening the reading room stood empty except for a lamp with a fraying cord and the smell of mildew and old paper.

She brought the ledger with her. It rode against her body as it always did now, sewn into the lining of her apron since the night Tomas arrived, since the note that said *forget the face*, since the boy himself had fallen asleep in the cot beside the stove with his thumb in his mouth and his eyes open even in sleep, watching the door.

Freya sat down at the long table, set the ledger beside the first grey volume, and opened both.

The ledger was not beautiful. It was an apothecary's stock book she had bought in the first winter, its columns ruled for tinctures and grain weights, and she had repurposed them without ceremony: name, date, place, cause where she knew it, and in the narrow margin, always, the reason the name had been struck. *Desertion — report amended.* *Civilian, age indeterminate — not entered.* *Refused order — no report filed.* Three years of small, cramped handwriting, each entry costing her something she could not name and could not stop paying. Her brother Étienne was not in these pages; he was in the letters sewn beneath them, but his absence organized everything, the way a missing tooth organizes a mouth. He had been misfiled once. She had sworn no one else would be.

The grey volume was beautiful. That was the first obscenity of the evening — how beautiful it was. Maps printed in three colors. Tables of casualties arranged by division, by month, by cause, with footnotes in a smaller, humbler type. The paper was heavy and did not yellow. Someone had spent years on this, someone with good eyes and a steady hand, and Freya understood within twenty minutes that the someone was Vasseur, or Vasseur's method wearing other hands like gloves.

She began, as she began everything, with the crossroads.

It appeared on page 411. *Engagement near Saint-Aubin-le-Croix, 14 October.* Engagement. She read the paragraph twice, then a third time, moving her finger along the lines the way she moved a probe through a wound, feeling for what lay underneath.

*Fourteen irregulars and eleven civilians killed in the course of a security action. Names recorded in Appendix C. Responsibility: local command, acting under conditions of compromised communications.*

There it was. All of it. The dead were named — she checked, her heart beating strangely fast, and yes, all twenty-five names were there in Appendix C, spelled correctly, ages given, even the infant listed as *infant, female*. The village that official dispatches had called evacuated was here called what it was. The soldiers who had refused the order were here, named among the dead rather than erased as deserters.

And nowhere — not in the paragraph, not in the appendix, not in the index she checked twice with growing cold — was there a single word about who had given the order.

*Local command.* As if local command were weather. As if orders fell from the sky like rain onto whichever unfortunate crossroads happened to be standing beneath them.

Freya sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the corridor a man was moaning in the low repetitive way of morphine wearing off, and she listened to it the way one listens to a clock, letting it count out the seconds while her mind rearranged itself around what she was seeing.

She had assumed — she saw now how comfortably she had assumed it — that Vasseur was hiding the massacre. That the silence she had spent three years decoding was the silence of concealment, and that if the truth ever surfaced it would surface despite him, dragged up from below like a drowned man. She had built her whole careful life on that assumption. Every risk she had taken with the ledger, every entry written by lamplight with her door barred, every lie told to quartermasters and chaplains — all of it rested on the belief that she was keeping a secret from a man who wanted secrets kept.

But the massacre was not hidden. It was *printed*. In a volume destined for the neutral commission, for the victors' archives, for the history that schoolchildren would memorize when this war was old enough to have lessons drawn from it. The dead were named. The suffering was acknowledged. There would even be, she realized, turning to the commission's own guidelines bound at the back, a formal expression of regret — the treaty draft required one, a paragraph of mourning to be read aloud at the signing table, and here were its raw materials, dignified, complete, and surgically emptied.

He was not concealing the crime. He was curating it.

Her hands were cold. She warmed them over the lamp and went back to the beginning, because if she was right about Saint-Aubin she would be right about others, and she needed to know the size of the thing.

She worked until midnight. Page by page, column by column, she laid her ledger against his volumes the way she had once laid broken bones, aligning what matched, noting what did not. And the pattern held with a consistency that was almost admiring, if admiration could survive nausea. Wherever her ledger held a name, his record held it too — corrected, dignified, restored to the roll of the dead. The deserter shot at Verrières appeared not as a coward but as *killed in action, circumstances unclear*. The civilians of the flooded valley, whom no report had ever mentioned, filled half a page of Appendix F. Even the hospital dead, the men who died of the neglect she documented in her margins, were accounted for, folded into the general mortality with a footnote attributing the excess to *supply disruptions beyond local control*.

Beyond local control. Beyond local command. Beyond, beyond, beyond — the whole architecture of the book built on that single preposition, load-bearing and invisible, repeated four hundred times until it stopped sounding like evasion and started sounding like scholarship.

Vasseur had taken every death she had secretly mourned and given it a public grave with no headstone inscription but *no one is responsible*. He had done what she could never have imagined doing: he had told the truth about everyone except the guilty. And because the dead were named, because the grief was real and printed and footnoted, no one would ever look further. What commission would interrogate a book so generous to the dead? What victor would press charges against a record that already wept?

She thought of the conversations she had reconstructed in her head over three years — the confrontation she would someday make, the moment she laid her ledger on some tribunal table and said *here, here is what your reports omitted*. She had rehearsed it the way one rehearses a speech to the living dead, for comfort, not for use. And now she saw that if she ever made that speech, Vasseur could stand up, open his own grey volume to page 411, and read aloud every name she carried. *Omitted, Nurse Delacroix?* His courteous voice in her memory, his kind tired eyes. *I believe you will find them all here.*

The ledger in her lap suddenly weighed nothing and everything. Because there was exactly one thing in it that his book did not contain, and she had nearly missed it, buried in the margin of the Saint-Aubin entry in her own cramped hand, written the week after it happened, sourced from a dying sergeant who had been seconded to the escort detail and who had talked for forty minutes before he died:

*Order originated above. Not local. Signed relay through Third Army surgical command — V., per sergeant's testimony.*

Above. Not local. Ascending, if anyone cared to pull the thread, rung by rung up the chain of command, past the colonels, past the generals, into the rooms where the armistice was being drafted at this very hour by men whose signatures would sanctify whatever version of the war they were handed.

Her record was not a supplement to the official history. It was the only document in existence that climbed. Everything else — every report, every appendix, every beautifully printed volume — stopped at the bottom rung and apologized for the weather. If her ledger burned, the chain went dark forever. The victors would sign their peace over a massacre with names and no author, and the author would pour the wine at the signing dinner.

Freya closed the grey book gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead, and sat for a long time in the lamplight doing the only arithmetic that mattered.

One ledger. One boy. One thread ascending. Against her: three years of his silences, an army's gratitude, and a man who had once saved her life and knew precisely, having taught her himself, which silences keep people safe.

He had offered her a transfer home once already. She had thought it kindness. Now, carrying the ledger back against her body through the cold corridors, past the wards, past the morgue where Tomas slept in the cot they kept for him among the living because the dead had no room, she understood that the offer had been reconnaissance. He had been measuring, all along, whether she was part of the record he was writing — or the one loose page that had to be removed.

In the morning, she knew, there would be a summons. Perhaps not tomorrow. But soon, and courteous, and final.

She stood a long time watching Tomas breathe, this small surviving half of her evidence, this witness whose face she had refused to forget. Eleven days, the drivers said, until the commission closed and the treaty was signed and the grey books became eternal.

Eleven days to move a child and a ledger across a war.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead she sat at the nurses' desk with the ledger open under the desk lamp's shade, and she did what she had trained herself to do in three winters of triage: she counted what she had, and she counted what she lacked, and she let the numbers tell her the shape of the impossible.

What she had: one ledger, split now into two halves since the night of the note — the older entries sewn into her apron lining, the newer ones wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of her instrument case. One boy who could not speak but could draw a crossroads from memory with the doors of the houses facing the right directions. One ambulance scheduled, according to the transport board pinned in the corridor, to run wounded south toward the rail junction at Marest in nine days — nine days, with the commission closing in eleven, and the last thirty kilometres after Marest to be walked or begged or bribed.

What she lacked was longer. She lacked papers for the boy. She lacked authority for herself — a nurse did not leave her sector without a countersigned pass, and passes were countersigned by the office of the command surgeon. She lacked money worth the name. And she lacked, above all, the one thing no ledger could supply: a driver willing to carry a mute child and a satchel of contraband truth through two checkpoints and a patrol line, and keep silent about it afterward.

She wrote the list out on the back of a requisition slip, then looked at it for a long time. Nine items. She crossed out none of them. Triage, Vasseur had taught her years ago, standing over a boy not unlike Tomas with his chest full of shrapnel — triage is not choosing who lives. It is refusing to pretend you can save everyone, and then saving them anyway.

Somewhere below the floorboards, the boiler knocked twice and went quiet, the building settling into its lies for the night. Freya folded the slip into the ledger, blew out the lamp, and in the darkness began, item by item, to make the impossible arithmetical.

First, she thought, she would need Ilya.
