## 6. The Neutral Line

The boy had a word for the lamp.

He did not say it at first. He said nothing for six days, in fact, which was how long it took the fever to burn down out of him and leave behind a child who was mostly collarbone and eyes, propped against pillows in the far corner of Ward C where the winter light came through frosted glass like milk. But on the seventh morning, when Sister Anouk trimmed the wick and the flame stood up yellow and steady, Tomas looked at it and said — quietly, as if testing whether the sound would cost him something — *"Lampa."*

Sister Anouk nearly dropped the scissors. She came to find Freya with her hands still shaking around the tin of lint. "He spoke," she said. "Not to me. To the *lamp*. As if he'd been saving it up."

Freya was not surprised. She had been listening to him not-speak all week, and she understood that his silence was not an empty room but a locked one — that somewhere inside him words were stacked to the ceiling, and he was deciding, one by one, which of them were safe to hand out. He gave them slowly. A word for bread. A word for the dog-shaped patch on Corporal Vesters' sleeve. A word — whispered, once, at night, when he thought no one heard — for a river.

He never gave a word for the crossroads.

But he drew it. He drew it every day now, in the margins of old requisition forms Freya brought him: the two roads crossing, the poplars, the ditch rendered in brown crayon pressed so hard the paper tore. And every day he added one detail more. On the eighth day, boots. On the ninth, a truck with its tailgate down. On the tenth, standing apart from everything else, small and precise and drawn over so many times the crayon had gone through to the wood of the table beneath — a man with his hands clasped behind his back, watching.

Freya stood over those drawings each evening after lights-out and felt the ledger in its hiding place under the third floorboard seem to warm, like a coal, like something alive. The date matched. The question mark she had written three years ago — *crossroads east of Malinov, civilians, number unknown* — was filling itself in, stroke by stroke, in a child's hand. Her record and the boy were halves of one document, and neither half was enough alone. Together they might be a weapon.

The trouble, she thought, was that weapons have to be carried somewhere.

* * *

She learned about the commission from a newspaper Ilya left folded on the supply cart, and she learned it because Ilya wanted her to learn it — she understood that too, later, when she replayed the moment. He was the commandant's staff driver, a big, slow-moving man from the eastern provinces who spoke four languages badly and drove anything with wheels as though the vehicle were a sick animal he pitied. For three years he had ferried officers and paperwork between the hospital and headquarters, and for three years he had never once looked at Freya as though she were anything other than what she appeared to be. That was why she noticed, that grey Tuesday, when he looked at her exactly as though she were something else.

"Page four," he said, nodding at the paper, and moved on down the corridor with his crate of ampoules.

She waited until the ward was quiet, until Sister Anouk had gone to the dispensary and Tomas was asleep with his drawings folded under his cheek like a second pillow. Then she opened the newspaper to page four, past the victory bulletins, past the photograph of generals shaking hands under bunting already printed though the war wasn't over — and found a paragraph so small it might have been set by someone hoping no one would read it.

*A neutral historical commission, convened under the auspices of the border powers, has established itself at Verenstadt to compile the definitive record of conduct during the hostilities. The commission will receive documentation and testimony until such time as the armistice is signed, after which its findings will pass into the treaty annexes.*

Verenstadt. She knew the town — a rail junction on the frontier, forty kilometres north, on the far side of the lines. She knew, because everyone knew, that the armistice talks had stalled twice already, and that the sticking point, reported in the same careful language as everything else, was the question of *irregularities during occupation.* The victors wanted the record clean before the ink dried. The neutrals wanted it true. And sitting in a rented hall in Verenstadt, with a mandate that would expire the moment the treaty was signed, were men whose entire purpose was to receive precisely what she held under a floorboard.

She sat very still and let the arithmetic run.

Eleven days. The talks had been scheduled to resume on the twelfth; the staff mess talked of nothing else; Vasseur's clerks were already drafting the ceremonial orders. Eleven days until the commission closed forever and the official history hardened into stone — a history that named the dead of Malinov crossroads but not the order that made them dead. A history curated, she was now certain, by the kindest man in the occupying command.

Eleven days, forty kilometres, a checkpoint line, a mute child, and a ledger worth hanging for.

She looked at Tomas sleeping. His breath whistled faintly, the last residue of the fever. In sleep his hands were open, palms up, the way children sleep when they have not yet learned what hands are for. She thought: *I could keep you safe here. Fed, hidden, alive, until the war ends and the armies go home and nobody remembers your face, because forgetting is the whole machinery of peace.*

And then she thought of her brother's name, misfiled into nothing, and of the ditch at the crossroads full of people whose names had never been filed anywhere at all, and she knew that safe was just another word for buried.

* * *

Ilya found her at dusk in the ambulance bay, checking the tyre pressure on Vehicle 9 with a hand pump she didn't need to be using, because it gave her a reason to stand there and a reason to be alone.

"You read page four," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I read page four."

He leaned against the ambulance's flank, arms crossed, and studied the yard — the snow going grey with evening, the sentry at the gate stamping his feet, the chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. "You know what happens in eleven days."

"The commission closes."

"The commission closes." He said it the way a man says the name of a place where something was taken from him. "I drove two of their clerks from the station last month. Good men. Swiss, Dutch. They asked me questions on the drive — polite ones, the kind you can pretend not to understand. What did the army do with the civilians at Malinov. Where are the burial registers for the autumn relocations. Why do the casualty returns for the third division not balance against the graves registration." He turned and looked at her directly then, and his face, usually so placid, was tight with something old. "I answered them the way everyone answers. I shrugged. I am very good at shrugging. I have been practising since before this war."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because yesterday I drove Colonel Vasseur to headquarters, and he carried a folder, and he was smiling." Ilya spat carefully into the snow. "Vasseur does not smile. Vasseur is the politest man in the army, and politeness is a coat he wears over whatever is in the folder. And I thought — who in this hospital reads newspapers? Who keeps asking the pharmacists for old requisition forms? Who walks past my ambulance bay every evening looking at me the way a woman looks at a door she is thinking of opening?" He shrugged, the famous shrug, and it looked different now, stripped of its armour. "So I left the paper on the cart."

Freya pumped the tyre twice more, though the gauge had told her what she needed ten minutes ago. "If I asked you for something," she said, "it would be the kind of thing that ends careers. Possibly lives. Not necessarily ours."

"I know."

"It would be a night drive. North. Through the checkpoint at Sarny Bridge, which means papers we don't have, or a story better than papers. There would be a boy in the back who cannot tell you where he's going because he can't say the words yet. And there would be cargo that must arrive even if the rest of us don't."

The sentry at the gate changed shift. Somewhere in the wards a man began to cough, the deep wet cough that meant the winter had won another round, and both of them paused to listen to it, because that was what people did here — they listened to coughing the way farmers listened to weather.

"My brother," Ilya said at last, "was in the third division. The one with the unbalanced returns." He said it flatly, without drama, the way one sets a single card down on a table. "The commission's clerks asked about the third division and I shrugged, and I have been hearing myself shrug ever since. It has a sound, you know. A shrug. Once you hear it, you can't stop hearing it." He pushed off from the ambulance and picked up his crate of ampoules. "Vehicle 9 has new plugs and a full tank Thursday. The sentry at Sarny Bridge owes me cigarettes. And Nurse Delacroix—" he was walking away now, calling it back over his shoulder, casual as a remark about the weather, "—if you're going to hide something under a floorboard in the linen store, you should know the orderlies mop in there Tuesdays. Choose a board further from the wall."

Freya stood in the darkening bay, her heart going hard and strange against her ribs, and realized she had been seen — perhaps for weeks — and had not been given away. That in this building built entirely of silences, someone else had been keeping one, and his had been waiting for hers.

Eleven days.

That night she took the ledger out and laid it open beside Tomas's drawings, the dates marching down the left column, the crayon crossroads marching across the right, and between them, at last, a destination. She wrote *Verenstadt* on a slip of paper and showed it to the boy in the morning, and watched him study it, lips moving around syllables he couldn't yet afford.

"Lam-pa," he said, pointing at the lamp, giving her the only word he had to spare. Then he pointed at the word *Verenstadt*, and then at himself, and then — with terrible, deliberate clarity — at the door.

Outside, in the yard, an engine turned over in the cold: Vehicle 9, running sweet, running ready, and Ilya's voice drifting up through the frosted glass, singing some slow song from the eastern provinces as if he hadn't a care in the world, as if Thursday were just another day.

Thursday was four days away. And somewhere across the yard, in the warm office above the records room, Colonel Vasseur was smiling over a folder.
