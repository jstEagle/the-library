## 7. Everything Kept Safe Burns

The telegram came at four in the afternoon, and it was the courtesy of it that told her everything.

*Colonel Vasseur requests Sister Delacroix attend him at headquarters regarding administrative irregularities. Transport provided.*

Administrative. Irregularities. Freya read the slip twice by the window of the receiving ward, where the light was failing and the snow had begun again, soft and indifferent, covering the latrine trenches and the ambulance ruts and the shallow graves beyond the wall. Three years in this war had taught her a language beneath language. Men were not summoned courteously to discuss linens. The boy's absence from her morning census had been noticed — or worse, reported by someone whose kindness had a price she hadn't seen.

She folded the telegram into her sleeve and went to find Tomas.

He was where she'd hidden him since morning: in the linen store behind the stacked blankets, sitting cross-legged on the floor with a stub of blue crayon and the back of an old requisition form. He looked up when the curtain moved — that quick, animal look he still gave everyone, though less often now, and never at her. He drew as she watched. A road. Two lines of small figures. A third line, larger, with arms that ended in marks like splinters.

"Listen to me," she said, kneeling, keeping her voice level the way she kept it for men who were bleeding out and knew it. "Someone is coming to search this hospital. Tonight, perhaps within hours. You remember the morgue."

His eyes went wide and dark. He shook his head.

"You must go there anyway." She took his hands; they were cold, always cold. "You will lie down between two of my dead, and you will pull the sheet over yourself, and no one will look twice, because no one ever looks twice at the dead. That is the whole truth of this war, Tomas. The living are dangerous. The dead are safe."

He stared at her, breathing fast, and then — because he was eight years old and had already survived the worst thing she could imagine — he nodded once and put the crayon in his pocket, carefully, as if saving something for later.

* * *

They walked through the wards at dusk, past rows of men breathing in the particular rhythm of fever, past the amputation table being scrubbed, out the back door into the blue cold. The morgue stood apart from the main building, a low brick shed that had been a smokehouse before the war took the village around the hospital and left only the hospital. Inside, the smell was carbolic and under it something older. Four bodies lay on trestles, waiting for the ground to soften enough for burial. Three soldiers. One woman, unnamed, brought in from the roadside two days ago.

Freya lifted the boy onto the empty trestle between the sergeant from the 44th and the woman, and pulled the sheet up over his chest, then higher, then over his face.

"Tonight only," she said through the cloth. "I'll come for you before dawn. If anyone comes in here, you are one of them. Do you understand? Dead boys don't breathe loudly. Dead boys don't cry."

Through the sheet, very quietly: "I don't cry anymore."

The words went through her like a bayonet. She pressed her hand flat against the sheet, against his sternum, feeling the small stubborn heartbeat underneath, and said, "I know," which was a lie and a promise both, and then she turned off her own heart the way she'd learned to, and left him among the dead, and closed the door.

* * *

Vasseur received her in his office with tea already poured.

He had not changed in three years — or rather, he had changed exactly as much as everything else had changed, slowly and everywhere at once, so that comparing the man before her to the man of 1941 was like comparing two photographs of the same battlefield taken a season apart. Greyer at the temples. The same precise hands. The same voice that made courtesy feel like a clinical procedure.

"Sister Delacroix. Sit, please. Forgive the hour."

She sat. She did not touch the tea.

"I'll be brief, because I know your nights are long." He opened a folder on his desk. "There is a discrepancy in your ward returns. A male child, approximately eight years, admitted on the eleventh. No transfer papers. No discharge entry. And today—" he turned a page "—no patient at all. As if he had evaporated."

"He died," Freya said. "Fever. I entered him in the mortality book."

"You did." Vasseur smiled faintly. "Yesterday. After my adjutant made inquiries this morning. Your paperwork has always been impeccable, Freya — that is what troubles me. Impeccable paperwork produced eighteen hours after it is requested is not paperwork. It is a story." He closed the folder and folded his hands on it. "I taught you the difference once. Or perhaps I flatter myself, and life taught you, and I merely confirmed the lesson. Either way."

Outside, somewhere across the compound, a door slammed. Boots on frozen mud. She kept her eyes on his hands.

"The child saw something," Vasseur said, "that is now the subject of considerable attention at a delicate moment in our national affairs. Whether he understood it is irrelevant. What matters is that he exists, and that certain people believe he exists, and that I have spent three days telling those people that he does not — that the wagon driver exceeded his instructions, that the boy was dead of exposure before he reached your door. You see the position you have placed me in."

"I see it," Freya said.

"Do you?" He leaned back. "I wonder. In 1941 I removed your name from a list. You have never asked me why, and I have never told you, and we have lived comfortably in that silence together — two people who understand that some records serve the living better when they are incomplete. I am asking you now to extend me the same understanding. Tell me where the body is buried. Not the boy — the *body*. Give me a grave, and I will give you a transfer home, effective immediately, with a commendation. You've earned rest. You've earned more than rest."

For a moment — a real moment, shameful and warm — she wanted it. Her mother's kitchen. A bed that did not smell of carbolic. Sleep without counting.

Then she thought of the sheet rising and falling over a small chest, and of the word *irregularities*, and of what a grave given to Vasseur would actually contain.

"He's in the morgue," she heard herself say, in a flat, exhausted voice she did not have to manufacture. "Where else would I put him? I'm sorry, Colonel. I panicked. I wrote him dead because I couldn't bear to write him gone."

Vasseur studied her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it — the story's neatness, its very imperfection, the way confession disarms. Then he rang the small bell on his desk.

"Captain Renn," he said to the adjutant who appeared, "take two men and accompany Sister Delacroix to the morgue. Verify the mortality. Gently." His eyes came back to hers. "And then, Sister, while the Captain confirms your story — my men will conduct a routine inspection of the hospital stores. There have been thefts. Morphine, mostly. You understand. War makes thieves of us all."

* * *

She walked ahead of them through the dark, her lantern shaking in a way she could not entirely control, and every step of the forty meters to the morgue was a year long. If he breathed too loud. If the sheet had slipped. If eight years old was simply too young to hold still inside death for an hour.

Renn lifted the sheet himself. Freya looked at the ceiling beam and listened to her own blood.

"Dead," Renn said. "Frozen stiff. Been here a day or more." He dropped the cloth and stepped back, and Freya allowed herself one glance — enough to see the boy's face slack and grey in the lantern light, playing dead so well because he had practiced, because the real thing had stood over him once at a crossroads and he had learned its stillness the hard way.

"Gentlemen," she said, and her voice held.

* * *

But the search was already underway when they returned.

It was methodical, which was the horror of it — not drunken looting but inventory, men with lists going shelf to shelf in the dispensary, and then, with a warrant signed in Vasseur's careful hand, her quarters. They had broken nothing yet when she arrived; they were waiting for her, as if propriety required an audience. A lieutenant with a clipboard gestured her inside.

Her room. Three years compressed into twelve square meters: the cot, the trunk, the desk. They took it all apart with terrible gentleness. Her maps of the supply roads — confiscated. Her brother's letters, tied in their faded ribbon — read by lamplight, passed hand to hand, one officer laughing softly at something Étienne had written her at seventeen about a girl in Rouen, and Freya stood in her own doorway and felt each laugh like a slap. Her spare uniforms turned out seam by seam. The false bottom of her trunk pried up.

The ledger was not there. The ledger had not been there for six weeks — half of it sewn into the lining of a dead woman's coat in the laundry store, the other half wrapped in oilcloth and buried beneath the morgue floor under a loose brick, because she had begun, sometime after the boy arrived, to distrust any object that could be found whole. But they didn't know that. They searched as if it must be there, and their not finding it told her they knew it existed, and their knowing told her how far up the chain the fear went.

An officer held her brother's last letter up to the light, checking, absurdly, for invisible ink.

"Is there anything else in the hospital belonging to you, Sister?" the lieutenant asked. "Personal effects? Records?"

"My Bible," Freya said. "In the chapel. And my dead. All mine. Every name in the mortality book — you're welcome to audit them."

Something flickered in his face. He wrote it down anyway.

They left near midnight, taking the maps and the letters and her brother's photograph, and giving her a receipt, stamped and signed, for everything they stole. That was the refinement of it. It wasn't theft if you issued a chit. Nothing in this war was theft, or murder, or a lie; it was all administration, all irregularities, all filed and stamped and forgotten.

Freya stood in the wreckage of her room until the boots faded, and then she did something she had not done since the first winter: she sat down on the stripped cot and wept — briefly, silently, uselessly — for the letters, for Étienne, for the woman she had been at twenty-six who believed that if you kept your head down and your hands clean and your silences correct, the machinery would pass you by.

Safety. She had sold herself that illusion in installments, one compromise at a time, and tonight the bill had come due, and the payment was ash.

She washed her face in the basin. She straightened the ribbon-less bundle where the letters had been. Then she took the lantern and went out into the snow, past the sleeping wards, to the low brick shed, and knelt at the loose brick beneath the trestles, and lifted out the oilcloth package, heavy and damp and intact.

Tomas sat up inside the sheet, alive, watching her with those unforgetting eyes.

"They took everything," she whispered. "Everything I kept safe burned tonight. So we stop keeping things safe, you and I. We take them somewhere they can't be taken from."

Eleven days, the commission would sit. Nine hundred kilometers of war between here and there.

"Get dressed," Freya said. "We leave tomorrow night."
