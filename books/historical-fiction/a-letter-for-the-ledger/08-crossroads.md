## 8. Crossroads

Ilya Petrov had driven wounded men through three winters of this war, and he had learned that the road at night was a country of its own — one with its own laws, its own silences, its own dead. He drove it now with his headlights hooded, seeing by the grey seam of snow between the black trees, and in the mirror he watched the woman in the back seat who was not tending her patients but watching him.

She had not told him everything. She had told him enough. Eleven days until the commission closed, she'd said. A town on the neutral side of the line. A ledger that weighed more than any cargo he'd ever carried, though it fit inside a bread tin strapped beneath Freya Delacroix's coat, against her ribs, where a heart ought to be.

The ambulance smelled as ambulances always smelled — carbolic and blood and the sourness of fever — and beneath it tonight, something sweeter: the wax crayons the boy held, working in the dark by feel because Ilya had forbidden even a shaded lamp. Tomas sat wedged against a stretcher frame, drawing what he could not say, and every so often Ilya heard the small rasp of paper and thought: *he is drawing us there already. Drawing us safe.*

Six wounded men rode in the back. That was the genius of it, or what passed for genius in this war — no one stopped an ambulance full of dying men, because no one wanted to look at them. The papers were genuine; the wounds were genuine. Only the destination was a lie.

"Driver." The voice came from the second stretcher, thin as paper. Captain Anselm Reiner, who had been carried out of a forward post two days ago with his belly opened by shell fragment and who by every reasonable arithmetic should have died already and been done with it. "How far?"

"An hour," Ilya said. "Maybe less if the road holds."

Reiner laughed, and the laugh cost him. Ilya heard it in the way the breath came after — wet, labored, like water finding its level. "An hour to *nowhere*," the captain said. "You know what's funny, Sister? I asked them where they were taking me. Nobody would say." A pause. "Nobody ever says."

Freya leaned over him. In the dark Ilya could only see her hands — those quick, certain hands — adjusting a blanket that did not need adjusting, giving the man something warm to hold onto that was not warmth at all. "Rest, Captain. You'll be looked after."

"I'll be *counted*," Reiner said, and smiled at some joke only the dying could afford. "That's more than most of us get."

Ilya's hands tightened on the wheel. He had not asked what was in the bread tin. He had decided, somewhere in the long grey years of hauling men toward hospitals that could not save them, that he would rather carry one true thing than ten thousand comfortable lies. But he had not known — not really known — until this night, with the tin riding behind him and a mute boy drawing his way across the dark, what it cost to be the vehicle of a truth. Every kilometer felt like theft. Every shadow at the roadside felt like judgment.

The crossroads came up on them the way bad news comes: all at once, though it had always been waiting ahead.

He saw the lanterns first — three of them, hung from poles dragged across the northward lane — then the shapes of men standing behind them, rifles slung loose the way young soldiers carry rifles when they have not yet learned to fear the wrong things. Ilya braked. Snow squealed under the tires. For one absurd moment he considered simply driving through, and dismissed it in the same moment; he knew what a rifle butt does to a windshield, and he knew what a patrol does to an ambulance found running dark toward the neutral lines.

A figure detached itself from the lantern light and came to the driver's window. Young — younger than Ilya had expected. Pale, precise, with the collar of a lieutenant and the eyes of someone performing a part rehearsed for him by a better actor. Lieutenant Duret. Ilya knew the name the way everyone at base knew it: Vasseur's protégé, the colonel's own handwriting given legs.

"Papers."

Ilya handed them through. Duret read them by lantern light, unhurried, and Ilya understood that the slowness was the message: *I am not afraid of your wounded, driver. I am here for something else.*

"Ambulances don't run this route," Duret said pleasantly.

"Orders changed. Casualty clearing station's overloaded. We're routing west."

"West." Duret smiled. It was a good smile. It had been given to him by the same teacher who gave him everything else. "May I look at your passengers?"

"They're dying, Lieutenant. All six of them. You're welcome to watch."

Something moved behind Duret's eyes — irritation, yes, but under it a flicker of genuine unease, the instinct of a young man who has never actually watched a man die and knows it about himself. He stepped back and waved two soldiers forward. "Open the rear doors."

In the mirror, Ilya saw Freya's hand move — not to the bread tin, which would have been fatal, but to Tomas, drawing the boy gently behind the shelter of a stretcher frame. Her face was perfectly composed. Three years of careful neutrality lived in that face, and Ilya thought: *she learned it from the same man. God help us, they were both his students.*

The doors opened. Cold poured in like water. The lantern light reached into the ambulance and found the stretcher cases one by one — a sergeant with a bandaged head, a private shivering with fever, the grey faces, the crusted dressings, the smell rising up like a wall. The two young soldiers faltered. One of them turned his face away, and Ilya marked it: shame, arriving before orders could stop it.

Duret climbed halfway in. His lantern swept the compartment, caught the boy's boots, moved on — caught the hem of a blanket Freya had shifted a half-second too late —

And then Captain Reiner spoke.

"Lieutenant." The voice was barely a voice. It came out of the dark above Duret's head, out of the stretcher Ilya had believed held only a dying man, and it had in it something that made everyone stop — the soldiers, Duret, even the wind in the trees seemed to lean in. "Come closer. I can't turn my head."

Duret hesitated. Then, because refusing a dying officer's request was the kind of thing that got reported, he raised his lantern.

Reiner's face was grey as candle wax, his lips cracked, his eyes burning with a clarity that Ilya recognized with a chill — the terrible tidiness of men whose bodies have finished arguing and left the mind alone with itself.

"You're Duret," Reiner said. "Vasseur's boy. I've seen you at staff briefings. Sitting behind the chair. Writing down what isn't said." A breath, drawn with visible cost. "My name is Anselm Reiner. Captain, Fourth Rifles. And you will write it down too, Lieutenant, because no one else will. Not your reports. Not your histories. I've been dying for two days listening to the orderlies talk, and do you know what I've learned? I've learned that when we lose, the losses are recorded, and when we win, they are *edited*."

"Captain—" Duret began.

"Say my name." Reiner's hand came off the blanket and closed on Duret's sleeve with a strength that had nothing left in it except will. "Say it aloud. That's all. One name. You can manage one name, can't you? Your whole career is built on not managing names."

The silence went on so long Ilya counted his own heartbeats. Seven. Eight. In the corner, very quietly, a pencil stopped moving. Tomas had gone still, watching the lantern with those enormous unblinking eyes — the eyes of a boy who had already seen what happened at crossroads when orders were obeyed.

"Duret," one of the young soldiers whispered. Not a warning. A plea. *Don't. Whatever you're deciding, don't.*

"Anselm Reiner," Duret said at last, and his voice cracked on the second name like ice on a pond. "Fourth Rifles."

"There," Reiner said softly. "Was that so difficult?" His grip loosened; his hand slid back to the blanket. "Now look at your passengers, Lieutenant. Really look. Every one of these men has a mother who knows his full name, and every one of them is going into the ground whether your colonel approves the entry or not. You can search this ambulance. You can find whatever it is you've been sent to find. And then you can live the rest of your life knowing exactly what kind of officer you chose to be at a crossroads in the snow — because believe me, Lieutenant, it is *always* a crossroads. That's how they design it. So that afterward, no matter what you did, you were only following the road."

Duret stood in the doorway a long moment. The lantern trembled slightly in his hand — Ilya saw it, saw the light shake across the grey faces, across the boy's hidden boots, across the woman sitting straight-backed with her hands folded over her ribs as if in prayer.

Then Duret stepped down. Closed the doors himself, gently, like a man closing a coffin he hoped to be forgiven for.

"Clear the road," he called to his soldiers. His voice had gone flat, official, dead. "Ambulance is cleared for passage."

Ilya drove through the gap in the poles without a word. In the mirror he watched the lanterns shrink, watched Duret stand motionless in the snow watching them go, and understood that they had not been freed. They had been *witnessed*. Somewhere behind them, a young officer was learning tonight what Freya had learned three years ago in a flooded hospital tent: that a name spoken aloud cannot be taken back, and that the war between silence and record does not end with treaties.

It was Reiner who broke the quiet first, minutes later, his voice almost gone now, spent like a currency he'd hoarded for exactly this purchase.

"Sister," he said. "Whatever you're carrying under that coat — it had better reach where it's going."

Freya leaned close. Ilya heard her answer only as a murmur, and heard the captain's reply as barely more than breath:

"Good. Write mine properly, then. All of it. The date, the place, the cause." A pause, and then, with the ghost of the joke again: "Death by courage. It's rarer than you'd think."

He died twenty minutes before the border, quietly, in the manner of a man who has finished his correspondence. Freya closed his eyes and sat with her hand on his chest for a long moment, feeling nothing, and Ilya knew — without asking, without wanting to know — that somewhere beneath her coat, in a space the size of a bread tin, a pencil was already moving.

They crossed at dawn. The neutral town was still asleep, chimneys smoking straight up into a windless sky, and at the edge of it stood a stone hall with a flag Ilya did not recognize and windows lit early against the cold. Freya touched his shoulder once as she helped Tomas down — the boy clutching his drawings against his coat, the whole history of a massacre in a child's fist — and then she walked toward those lit windows without looking back.

Ilya stayed with the ambulance and the dead captain, and it was only when he began the paperwork that his hands started shaking. Because in the column marked NAME OF DECEASED, his pen hovered, and he found himself writing, slowly and carefully, in letters larger than regulation required:

*Reiner, Anselm. Captain, Fourth Rifles.*

And underneath, though no form demanded it, no clerk expected it, though it might cost him his license and perhaps more:

*Died carrying the truth to the record. Witnessed.*

Somewhere across town, in a hall with unfamiliar flags, a door was opening.
