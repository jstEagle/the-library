## 9. The Signing Table

The hall had been a courthouse once, before the war took the town's name off the maps and gave it to history. Freya stood beneath its vaulted ceiling, in the smell of dust and fresh paint — they had repainted for the commission, a careful diplomatic grey over the water stains — and watched men she would never meet arrange paper.

That was all an armistice was, in the end. Paper, arranged. Eleven tables pushed into a horseshoe under the tall windows. Flags that had not existed three years ago and would not exist in quite the same way tomorrow. Clerks with ink-stained cuffs carrying folders from one table to another like nurses moving trays, and behind them, stacked on trolleys against the wall, the bound volumes of the official record: *A Preliminary Account of Irregularities*, compiled by the victors, vetted by the victors, awaiting only the signature that would make forgetting lawful.

Ilya had gotten them through the last checkpoint at four in the morning by driving with his lights off and his teeth clenched, whispering to the ambulance's wounded cargo that it was nothing, just a rut in the road, just the dark. Tomas had slept against her side the whole way, one hand fisted in her coat pocket where the ledger's second half rode flat against her hip, wrapped in oilcloth. He hadn't let go of her since the morgue. She understood. For three days he had lain among the dead pretending to be one of them, breathing shallow, eyes closed, while boots moved overhead, and something in him had decided that if he was going to be alive, he was going to be alive holding on.

Now he stood beside her at the back of the hall, scrubbed, in clothes too big for him that Ilya had begged from a quartermaster, and he looked at the trolleys of bound volumes the way other children looked at windows in winter.

The commission sat at eleven. There were nine of them — four nations, five chairs, and a secretary with a voice like a filing cabinet who read the day's agenda in French, then German, then a third language Freya didn't know. Item six: *Final disposition of matters designated irregular.* The word went past her like a train. Irregular. Three winters of her life reduced to a clerk's adjective.

She had rehearsed this. In the ambulance, in the morgue, in the flooded ward at night when everyone slept, she had rehearsed: *Madame Secretary, gentlemen, I am Nurse Freya Delacroix, attached to the 4th Field Hospital, and I hold a record of two hundred and fourteen names struck from the official returns between November of the first winter and October of this one.* Two hundred and fourteen names. She knew the number the way she knew her own pulse. She had counted them by candlelight, checked them twice, wept over perhaps thirty of them, and hated herself gently for being able to number the rest so calmly.

When the secretary called for submissions from persons outside the delegations — a formality, ten seconds of silence no one expected to be filled — Freya walked forward.

Her boots sounded enormous on the stone floor. Somewhere behind her, Tomas did not cry out; she heard his breath catch and steady itself, and that steadiness carried her the way a hand on a railing carries you down stairs in the dark.

She laid both halves of the ledger on the commission's table. Oilcloth unwrapped. The binding cracked open to its first page, and she watched a delegate's eyes move down it — a name, a rank, a date, a place, and beneath each name in her small upright hand the words *struck from return* or *reported fever, died of wound* or simply *omitted.*

"Two hundred and fourteen," she said. Her voice held. "I have kept this ledger for three years. Every entry is a person your histories do not contain."

The hall went very quiet. A delegate reached for the ledger; she put her hand flat on the page first, one beat longer than courtesy allowed, then withdrew it. Let them understand what it had cost to carry.

It was the secretary who asked, gently, in the tone of a woman who has learned that miracles usually arrive with paperwork problems: "And are you able to speak to any specific matter contained here? The commission requires testimony, not merely—"

"I can speak to one matter." Freya turned slightly, toward the boy at the back of the hall. "But it is not mine to give."

* * *

Tomas crossed the floor slowly. He had grown into walking again the way he had grown into words — badly, then stubbornly, then all at once. Halfway to the table he stopped, and Freya thought for one terrible moment that the hall, the flags, the weight of nine faces had swallowed him whole.

Then he looked up at the tallest delegate, a grey-bearded man with spectacles, and said:

"There were nineteen of us at the crossroads."

His voice was thin and clear as a wire. It reached every corner of the room without effort, the way a bell does.

"The soldiers stood us in two lines. The ones in the first line were from my village. The ones in the second line wore our uniform but wouldn't shoot, so they were put with us." His hands moved as he spoke, drawing the shapes in the air the way he had drawn them on hospital scrap paper — lines, angles, the black rectangle of the truck. "A colonel came in a car with mud to here." He touched his knee. "He read from a paper. Then he said—" and here Tomas's voice changed, flattened, became an instrument repeating something it should never have had to hold, "—he said, *Proceed according to schedule. No names will be taken. This action does not appear in any report.*"

Freya felt the hall inhale.

"Then they shot the first line," Tomas said. "And then the second line. And then the truck backed up to the ditch, because the ditch wasn't deep enough yet." He paused. "I was in the ditch. Under the ones from the second line. One of them put his coat over me before—" He stopped. Started again. "He was called Anton. Anton Brech. That was on his papers. Nobody else's names were taken. So I'm taking his now."

Silence, absolute, of the kind Freya had heard only in wards at the moment a fever breaks or doesn't.

The grey-bearded delegate leaned forward. "Child, the man who read the order. Could you identify him?"

"He's here," Tomas said.

* * *

Vasseur came through the doors at half past two.

Later, people would say he had been summoned. Freya knew better. She had spent three years learning how Vasseur moved, and he had not come summoned; he had come because a colonel whose silences were load-bearing walls feels every one of them crack. Someone at the morning session had talked. Word travelled faster than ambulances.

He entered the hall the way he entered a ward — unhurried, immaculate, his greatcoat brushed, his gloves folded over one arm. He had aged beautifully around the eyes, which was where he kept everything. When he saw Freya standing beside the open ledger, he did not flinch. He inclined his head, almost warmly, the way he had the day he'd pulled her name off a firing-squad detail and taught her the family trade of looking away.

"Colonel Aurel Vasseur," announced the secretary. "Command surgeon, Fourth Army Group. You are named in testimony given this afternoon."

"I am aware," Vasseur said. His voice filled the hall without effort. "I request that the testimony be read to me in full."

They read it. Tomas stood three feet away and listened to his own sentence spoken back to him in a clerk's dry voice, and did not look away, and neither did Vasseur. That was the thing Freya could never make anyone believe afterward, the thing that made her certain the trial that followed was almost unnecessary: Vasseur listened to a child describe the murder of eighteen people on his order, and he listened the way he listened to a stethoscope. Attentively. Without denial. As though confirming a diagnosis he had already written himself.

When the reading ended, the grey-bearded delegate asked him, formally: "Do you deny giving this order?"

The hall waited. Freya found she was gripping the edge of the commission's table hard enough to whiten her knuckles, waiting for the machinery to engage — the deflection, the jurisdictional objection, the smooth procedural fog she had watched him lay down over three years of ledgers. He had a hundred exits and every one of them had been built by better lawyers than the ones in this room.

"No," Vasseur said.

One word. It dropped into the silence like a stone into a well.

"I gave the order at the Kalen crossroads on the date stated," he continued, in the same level clinical voice, "and I gave it under instruction from above, which I am prepared to state, in writing, before this commission — provided the commission enters this child's testimony and this woman's ledger into the permanent record *before* the armistice is signed. Not appended. Entered." He looked at the trolleys of bound volumes against the wall. "You have spent three years drafting a history in which men like me are irregularities. I am offering you the regular kind. I suggest you take it before the signatures dry."

The delegates bent together. Papers whispered. And Freya understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that Vasseur had done the arithmetic she had done alone in chapter after chapter of candlelight: that a curated truth was worth nothing next to a witnessed one, and that the only dignity left to a man who had ordered a ditch filled with civilians was to be the man who opened the record rather than the man it closed over. Perhaps it was penance. Perhaps it was strategy. With Vasseur it had always been impossible to tell gratitude from debt, mercy from bookkeeping.

The secretary rose. "The commission accepts the Delacroix ledger and the testimony of the witness as evidence primary, entered this day, prior to signature. The testimony of Colonel Vasseur will be taken in session tomorrow, at eight."

Tomas's hand found Freya's. His grip was small and ferocious and did not tremble at all.

Outside, the light was going gold over the town's broken rooftops, and somewhere beyond the hills the guns had already begun their strange, stuttering quiet. Freya stood on the courthouse steps with the boy and the empty space at her hip where the ledger used to live, lighter and heavier than she had ever been.

Tomorrow at eight, Vasseur would climb the chain of command aloud, name by name, and she would sit in that hall and listen — and there was one name on her tongue, one she had carried three years, that she had promised herself she would not surrender even now.

She was still deciding whether she could keep that promise when she saw the delegation's car turn into the square, and recognized, among the staff officers climbing out of it, the face of the man who had driven her brother to the crossroads.
