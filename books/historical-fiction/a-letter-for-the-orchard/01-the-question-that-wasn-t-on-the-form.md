## 1. The Question That Wasn't on the Form

The storm came in from the northeast the way it always did in late autumn—first as a rumor on the wind, then as a certainty pressing its gray shoulder against the coast. Tamsin Okonkwo felt it arrive before she heard it: a change in the pressure behind her ears, a tightening in the old scar along her wrist that the sea had given her and never asked to take back. By midmorning the gulls had gone silent, which was itself a kind of announcement. Gulls knew when to leave a place. People rarely did.

She was trimming the wick when she saw him.

A figure on the cliff path, small against the violence of the sky, moving with the particular stubbornness of a man who has committed himself to arriving somewhere and will not be persuaded otherwise by mere weather. He came bent into the wind like a nail driven sideways, one hand clamped to his hat, the other swinging a leather satchel that caught what little light remained and threw it back dull and brown as an old coin.

Tamsin set down the trimming scissors and watched him for the length of three waves. The lamp above her turned its slow circle, sweeping its beam out over water the color of wet slate, and each pass lit the stranger for a half-second—closer, closer, closer—and then took him away again. There was something almost comical in it, a man appearing and vanishing, appearing and vanishing, as though the light itself could not make up its mind about him.

The first great storm of the year, and already someone at her door.

She went down the spiral stair with one hand on the rail, counting the steps as she always did—one hundred and six from lamp room to ground floor, a number she knew the way other women knew their prayers—and unbarred the door before he could knock. It would not do to let a man stand knocking in weather like this. Knocking was a sound that carried. Sound carried too far on this coast, where there was nothing else to listen to.

"You'll come in before the sea decides it wants you," she said, and stepped aside.

He was younger than she'd expected, or perhaps just worn in a way that made age impossible to read—a lean face, rain-darkened hair plastered flat, eyes the color of the water under overcast, which is to say they gave nothing back. His coat had surrendered entirely. Water ran off his hem and pooled on her flagstones, and he had the grace to look apologetic about it.

"Census," he said, producing a document from inside his coat with the care of a man showing a wounded bird. "Elias Wren. District enumeration, Crown authority." The paper was damp but legible. "I'm sorry for the hour. I'm sorry for everything about my arrival, frankly."

"Names and ages can wait until you've stopped dripping." She was already turning toward the stove. "Sit there. Not the chair by the window—the draft finds it. Take off those boots or you'll take your death in them."

"I have a spare pair in the satchel."

"Then you're better prepared than most men who wash up on this rock. Dry them anyway."

He did as he was told with a certain watchful politeness that Tamsin noted and filed away. Seven years of keeping a lighthouse had taught her the difference between men who obeyed instructions because they were sensible and men who obeyed them because they were being observed. This one, she suspected, was both.

* * *

The storm found its full voice while the kettle sang. Wind came down the chimney flue and made the fire gutter and flare, and the windows shivered in their frames, and beyond the glass the world reduced itself to two elements—water falling, water rising—until the house seemed less a building than a vessel, riding at anchor on the edge of the continent. Tamsin had lived through forty-one storms in this tower. She no longer heard them as noise. She heard them as weather reading aloud from a very long book.

Elias Wren drank his tea and opened his ledger book across his knees, and began, in the manner of his trade, to ask his questions.

Name. She gave it. Age. She gave that too, watching his pen move, watching him write *widow* in the column where *wife* had once been written by another hand, another year, another government's form. Occupation: keeper of the light. Years of service: seven. Dependents: none. These were easy answers. She had answered them before, to surveyors and tithe collectors and once, memorably, a naval lieutenant who had climbed the hundred and six steps only to discover he was violently unsuited to heights and had to be talked back down by degrees, like a frightened horse.

But Wren was not finished, and here is where the afternoon changed its shape.

He closed the ledger. Deliberately. Set the pencil down alongside it, parallel, precise, the way a surgeon lays aside an instrument he means to return to. And then he looked out the window at the storm for a moment—an honest moment, she thought later, or performed so well it made no difference—and said:

"Off the record now, Mrs. Okonkwo, if I may. The record being this book, which belongs to the Crown, and the question being one the Crown hasn't thought to print on any form I carry."

She said nothing. Silence was a tool she kept sharpened.

"Did you ever correspond with a man named Aldous Vane?"

Outside, the wind struck the tower a blow like a fist against a door, and somewhere below, the sea took up the echo. Tamsin let the sound finish before she spoke. It was important, she had learned, never to answer too quickly. Quick answers were confessions wearing borrowed clothes.

"Aldous Vane," she repeated, tasting the name, giving it the mild curiosity of a woman offered an unfamiliar word. "No. I don't believe I know it. Should I?"

"He was a clerk. In the magistrate's office, some years ago. Before your time here, perhaps."

"My time here is all the time I have," she said pleasantly. "Before it, I was a wife in a house that had walls instead of windows. Clerks were not among our acquaintances."

Wren nodded slowly, and wrote nothing, and that absence of writing told her more than any entry could have. A census man recorded answers. This man was recording her—not her words but her manner of giving them, the way a jeweler turns a stone to see how the light goes through it.

"It's only," he said, folding his hands around his cooling cup, "that Vane has gone missing. And letters are sometimes how a man disappears. You write enough of them, post enough of them, and one day the man who wrote them is simply—gone." He smiled, thin and rueful. "Forgive me. Storms make a man talkative. Or perhaps it's the opposite—storms make everyone else quiet, so a talkative man finally gets heard."

"Is there anything else off the record?" she asked. "I keep the lamp lit regardless of the weather, Mr. Wren, and the weather doesn't intend to improve. If you mean to reach the village inn tonight, you should go while the path is still a path."

It was a dismissal, and he took it as one, rising, gathering his damp papers, tugging his ruined coat back onto his shoulders. At the door he paused with his hand on the latch, and the wind came through the gap and stirred the fire, and he said—lightly, so lightly it might have been a courtesy—

"The sea takes a great many things here, I imagine. But it doesn't take everything. Things that matter, people hide. It's the hiding that tells you they mattered." Then, before she could arrange her face into anything at all: "Thank you for the tea, Mrs. Okonkwo. I'll correct the enumeration in spring, if that suits. There were one or two entries I may need to verify."

And then he was gone into the gray, a diminishing smudge on the cliff path, swallowed whole by the weather, and Tamsin stood with her hand still on the bar of the door and understood, with the cold certainty of a keeper who knows a squall from a gale from a tempest, that the storm outside was the smaller of the two.

* * *

She waited until full dark before she went to the floor.

That was discipline, not cowardice—or she preferred to think of it as discipline, though she knew the truth wore a thinner coat. She banked the fire. She trimmed the lamp and set it to its turning, and stood a while in the lantern room watching the beam go round: dark, light, dark, light, the oldest rhythm she knew, older than her marriage, older than her grief. The sea below ran white-crested and enormous, hurling itself at the rocks with the patience of something that intended to go on doing this forever and expected to win.

Then she went down, all one hundred and six steps, and knelt at the corner of the kitchen where the flagstone sat loose beneath the iron trivet, and lifted it.

Seven years she had kept that stone unturned. Seven years of walking past it twice a day, of knowing it was there the way one knows about a splinter worked deep into the flesh—awareness without examination, because examination would mean pain and pain would mean decisions, and she had spent seven years arranging her life so that no decision larger than the trim of a wick was required of her.

The sea chest came up heavy, heavier than grief alone could account for. Daniel's initials were burned into the lid—D.O., the letters gone pale with salt air—and the brass fittings had greened like old bronze in a garden. She dragged it to the hearth and knelt before it in the firelight, and for a long moment she only rested her hands on the lid, feeling the grain of the wood, smelling the ghost of tar and cedar that rose even now, even after everything.

"Daniel," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the storm, to whatever portion of a drowned man the sea had failed to keep. "You absolute fool. What did you leave me?"

The lock was rusted open; it had been rusted open since the day she'd hidden it, because a chest that cannot be locked cannot be forced, and a chest that appears already looted does not invite looting. She had learned that much from him. She had learned so much from him, God help her.

Inside: his spare shirt, folded and stiff with salt. A coil of tarred twine. A sextant wrapped in oilcloth, its mirrors clouded. And beneath these, wrapped in a woman's shawl that she recognized with a jolt as her own—her own, taken without asking, kept without telling—a bundle of letters tied with fishing line.

She counted eleven. She counted them twice, because the number seemed wrong, seemed insufficient, seemed both too many and far too few for whatever it was that Elias Wren's missing clerk had died—or vanished—over.

The first letter was dated the winter before Daniel drowned. The hand was cramped and careful, the ink brownish, the paper cheap. She read it by firelight while the storm leaned on the house and the lamp above turned its faithful circles, and the letter said things that rearranged the furniture of her memory without asking permission.

*Madam and sir both,* it began, oddly formal, oddly desperate, *I write to you because I have begun keeping copies of what passes across the magistrate's desk, and copies are dangerous things...*

Aldous Vane. The name sat on the page in its cramped careful letters, and Tamsin sat very still, and the fire popped, and somewhere in the walls the wind found a gap and moaned through it like something grieving.

Correspondence, then. Correspondence with Aldous Vane. The question that wasn't on the form, asked politely, off the record, by a man with rain in his collar and nothing written in his book.

*I have been advised to distribute my materials rather than hoard them,* the second letter said. *A single bundle burns. Eleven bundles must each be sought out separately.*

Eleven bundles. Eleven letters, and perhaps—her hands went cold, colder than the flagstones—perhaps eleven hiding places, scattered along a river road she suddenly remembered her husband studying on maps, tracing with one blunt finger: *the ferry, Tam. The garrison. The customs house. A chain is only as strong as the man paid to hold each link.*

She had thought, all these years, that she was hiding his papers. She had thought herself the sole keeper of whatever dangerous thing Daniel Okonkwo had carried into the water with him.

Now, kneeling at an open chest with a dead man's letters spread across her knees, Tamsin understood that Daniel had never entrusted everything to her at all. He had distributed her. Made her one link in a chain whose other links she could not name—and one of those links, a clerk named Aldous Vane, had recently and inconveniently ceased to exist, and a soft-spoken census man had come knocking at her door in the teeth of the first great storm to ask, ever so gently, whether the past still lived in her house.

The past knocked politely, yes. But she could hear it now, behind the courtesy, behind the damp smile: the sound of a shoulder settling against the door. The sound of a hinge beginning to give.

Tamsin tied the letters back with their fishing line—but did not put them in the chest. Instead she sat back on her heels and looked up, past the beams, past the ceiling, toward the turning light above, and thought about spring. About corrections to the census. About a man who would come again, because he had told her he would, and liars who announce their returns are either fools or hunters, and Elias Wren had not struck her as a fool.

Outside, the storm reached down and took the tower in both hands and shook it, and the lamp held steady, because that was what lamps did, and Tamsin Okonkwo began, at last, to plan.
