## 3. A Keeper's Routine

The second time I climbed the path to the lighthouse, I made certain to look like a man who had forgotten something.

That was the craft of it, I had learned. Not the questions themselves—any fool with a government satchel could ask a question—but the architecture of forgetting. A census taker who returns is a nuisance. A census taker who returns *apologetically*, muttering about a transposed digit in column four, a widow's birth year written as '41 when it was surely '44—he becomes furniture. He becomes weather. People answer furniture.

So I carried the corrected form folded into my coat like a confession, and I carried two things that were not on any government inventory: a tin of black tea bought at extortionate price from the trading post at Grey Harbour, and a lie I had been polishing for six years.

* * *

The storm of three nights past had rearranged the coast. The cliff path below the light was strewn with kelp laid out in dark ropes, and the gorse had been combed flat in one direction, all of it pointing inland, as if the wind had given the land a single instruction and the land had obeyed. Gulls stood in the tussock grass with their backs to the sea, hunched and judgmental, like clerks reviewing a ledger they did not believe.

I counted the turns of the path because counting kept the other thing quiet—the thing that had been quiet for six years only because I fed it work the way other men fed a fire. Eleven turns. At the eighth, through a gap in the fog bank, the lighthouse showed itself: white tower, black gallery, the lamp above burning pale against the grey morning though it was long past dawn. She kept it lit even in daylight. Most keepers doused after sunrise. Most keepers trusted the sun.

Tamsin Okonkwo did not trust anything, I suspected, that she had not personally set alight.

She met me at the door before I knocked. I would learn later that this was routine—that she watched the path from the gallery rail with a telescope older than the colony—but that morning it felt like being *expected*, which is a different animal entirely from being welcomed.

"Mr. Wren." Her eyes went to the satchel first. They always went to the satchel. "You've come back."

"The census office has found my errors." I produced the folded form with the sheepishness I had rehearsed against the mirror of my lodging-house window, where the glass was warped enough to make every face honest. "I wrote your year of birth wrong. And there's a question of whether you count as head of household or whether—" I hesitated delicately. "Whether we ought to record the household as vacant pending probate. Some offices prefer it. It saves the assessor a visit."

Something moved behind her eyes—not anger, not fear. *Assessment.* The way she looked at me reminded me of the harbourmaster at Grey Harbour watching a ship come in with its papers slightly wrong: not hostile, but weighing tonnage.

"I was born in '39," she said. "You didn't get it wrong. You invented the error so you'd have a reason to come back."

I stood on her doorstep with the tea going cold in my pocket and admired her completely.

"That," I said, "is a serious accusation."

"It is." She stepped back from the door—not an invitation, exactly. A concession. The doorway itself seemed to decide something. "It's also raining again. You may come in while you accuse yourself properly, if you're going to."

* * *

The inside of the lighthouse smelled of lamp oil and salt and, beneath both, apples—a faint sweetness I couldn't place until I saw the bowl on the shelf, five or six winter apples gone soft-shouldered, keeping their colour like old soldiers keeping their medals. Two chairs. A table scarred with rings from mugs set down carelessly over years, though there was only ever one mug now. A man's coat still hung on the peg by the door; I recognized the style, seven years out of fashion, salt-stiffened at the shoulders. She hadn't burned it. That told me more than the census ever would.

I set the tea on the table. She looked at it the way one looks at a bribe delivered too openly.

"Black tea," I said. "From Grey Harbour. The census office doesn't pay for it, if that matters to you."

"Everything matters to me, Mr. Wren. That's how I've stayed alive." But she put the kettle on, which meant the tea mattered too, whatever she claimed.

We went through the form. I read each line aloud in the flat voice of bureaucracy—name, age, occupation, years at post—and she answered each one in the flat voice of a woman who had answered them before, and while our voices performed this dull duet, our eyes conducted an entirely different conversation. Hers said: *You are hunting something and you have not yet shown me your teeth.* Mine said: *Neither have you.*

When we reached the occupation line I paused, pen hovering.

"Widow," she said. "And keeper. In that order. Write it however they like."

"And your late husband's trade? For the margin note. The office likes occupations traced."

"Surveyor," she said. "River charts, mostly. Before he drowned."

"Before he drowned." I let the words sit. Then, mildly, folding the form as though the interview were finished: "Drowned men make poor respondents. My condolences are seven years late, but sincere."

She poured the tea. Her hands were steady, but I noticed she gave herself the chipped cup and me the whole one—the small economy of a woman used to giving away the better part of everything and keeping the broken piece. A keeper's habit. Or a widow's.

"Mr. Wren," she said, sitting across from me, "in seven years no one from any office has come here twice. Not once. So either the census has grown ambitious, or you are not what your satchel says you are. I'll hear whichever story you tell. But I'll know if it's the second kind of story wearing the first kind's coat."

And there it was. The moment I had travelled four days up this drowned coast to reach, arriving not with trumpets but with tea steam rising between us.

I thought of my sister.

* * *

Her name was Ruth, and she was twenty-three, and she could add a column of figures faster than any clerk in the provincial office, which was why Corrigan's court hired her, and which was how, in the spring of '87, she came to notice that a shipping manifest signed by Magistrate Corrigan listed forty tons of grain that had never existed—grain sold twice, relief funds collected against famine that never reached the famine districts because the districts were fiction, names on paper, mouths that existed only to be fed on forms.

She told someone. That was Ruth's entire crime, the whole of it: she told a supervisor what the numbers said.

There was no trial worth the name. There was a hearing in a room that smelled of varnish, a magistrate's voice like oiled wood, charges of falsification turned inside out and pinned to *her* coat—the forger accusing the auditor, the elegant arithmetic of a corrupted court. Six months' imprisonment in a place where the damp got into her lungs, and then a release, and then eighteen months of coughing, and then I buried her under a stone I paid for by working the very records she had died trying to correct.

I was a records clerk then. I am a records clerk now. The title changed; the war did not.

For six years I had been building the case the way a wren builds a nest—one twig at a time, stolen, insignificant, until suddenly there was a structure where there had been nothing. Manifests. Ferry logs. A customs clerk's drunken confession taken down verbatim in a tavern at Grey Harbour while his friends sang. Three checkpoints along the river road, three throats through which everything Corrigan touched must pass: the ferry where cargo was weighed light and paid heavy, the garrison whose sergeant looked away for a wage supplement, the customs house whose ledgers were works of imagination. I had all of it. I had everything except the spine.

Because manifests can be explained away. Confessions recanted. But Daniel Okonkwo had been a surveyor, and surveyors keep *ledgers*—measurements, dates, signatures, the river's own testimony written in a professional hand. And Daniel Okonkwo had spent the last year of his life asking questions along that same river road, at those same three checkpoints, and had then drowned in water shallow enough to stand up in, within sight of a shore he had charted himself.

Accidental, said the inquest. Corrigan signed the finding himself, which should have been the first clue to anyone with eyes.

The final ledger. That was the keystone. Without it, my case was a wall without a lintel—impressive, load-bearing, and destined to fall on whoever stood beneath it. With it—

With it, I could bring down a magistrate.

And so I sat in the lighthouse kitchen drinking tea with the dead man's wife, and lied to her, and knew she knew, and understood—with a clarity that felt almost like tenderness—that she was lying to me too, and that we were two craftsmen recognizing grain and joinery in each other's work.

* * *

"You lost someone," she said, abruptly. Not a question. "To a court, or a clerk's office, or some room with varnish on the walls. I can see it when you talk about paperwork. People who love paperwork don't hold it like that."

I turned my cup a half-turn on its ring-marked table. Outside, the wind had begun again, a low moan in the tower's throat, the building itself breathing.

"My sister audited grain manifests," I said. "In '87."

I watched her face. I am good at watching faces; it is the census taker's true profession. And I saw it—the flicker, quick as a match struck in a cellar. Not surprise. *Recognition.* The year meant something to her. The word *manifest* meant something to her. Whatever she was hiding down in whatever cellar she hid things in, my sister's ghost had just knocked on its door.

"'87," Tamsin repeated. "Bad year on this coast."

"Bad decade," I said. "Bad magistrate."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above us, the lamp mechanism turned with its slow mechanical patience, a sound like a large patient heart.

"If I were a man hunting documents," I said carefully, "hypothetically—if a paper existed that could undo a corrupt court, and if the person best placed to find it were a widow who had spent seven years being invisible on purpose—I would want that woman to know she wasn't alone. That the hunt wasn't mine alone. That there are others who—" I stopped. Started again, plainer. "My sister told the truth about Corrigan's numbers and it killed her. Slowly, but it killed her. I have spent six years collecting twigs, Mrs. Okonkwo. I am tired of twigs. I want the tree."

She was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed register—lower, stripped of the hostess.

"And if the tree turned out to be rotten at the core, Mr. Wren? If the great document you wanted turned out to damn the man who wrote it as much as the magistrate you hate?" Her eyes held mine, and I understood that this was not hypothetical for her, that somewhere in this house or beneath its foundations lay an answer she had already read and could not unread. "Would you still want it? Would your case survive a flawed hero?"

"A case isn't a shrine," I said. "It's an argument. Flawed evidence argued honestly beats clean evidence argued never." Then, because honesty between liars is a currency that must be spent precisely: "But I won't pretend I'd welcome it. I've spent six years needing him to be what I built him into. A martyr makes a better foundation than a man."

Something in her expression cracked then—not broke, *cracked*, like ice on a river making its first sound of spring. A breath that might have been a laugh, except it hurt.

"Yes," she said quietly. "A martyr makes a better foundation. That's exactly what I told myself. Every day. For seven years." She rose, took my cup, and stood at the window with her back to me, looking out at the grey water. "You should go, Mr. Wren. The weather's turning, and the road will be mud by evening. And you should not come back here."

"Shouldn't I?"

"No." She didn't turn around. "Because next time I might not be able to lie to you, and I've gotten very good at it, and I'd hate to lose the skill. It's the only thing I've practiced longer than the lamp."

I gathered my satchel. At the door I paused, hand on the frame, beside the salt-stiffened coat that belonged to a drowned surveyor, and I said the thing I had carried up the cliff path alongside the tea:

"There's a ferry crossing on the river road. And a garrison checkpoint. And a customs house. Three doors, Mrs. Okonkwo, between here and the spring court at Grey Harbour. If a document ever needed to travel that road, those are the three doors it would have to pass."

Her shoulders went very still.

"I never told you about the river road," she said.

"No," I agreed softly. "You didn't."

* * *

I walked down through the fog with the corrected census form unsigned in my pocket, because she had refused to sign it, and because neither of us had mentioned that refusal, and because some refusals are signatures of another kind.

Behind me the lighthouse lamp turned, and turned, burning in broad daylight against nothing—a woman keeping a light lit for a reason she had not yet confessed, on a coast where the ice was already groaning somewhere upriver, getting ready to break.

At the eighth turn of the path I stopped, and looked back, and saw her in the gallery rail's shadow, watching me go through the old telescope.

Two liars, I thought, each holding a door open for the other, neither willing yet to walk through.

The ice, though. The ice had begun to talk.
