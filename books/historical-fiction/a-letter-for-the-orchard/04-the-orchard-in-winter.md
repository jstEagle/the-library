## 4. The Orchard in Winter

The letter had said *under the tree with the crooked graft*, and for seven years Tamsin Okonkwo had made a discipline of not knowing which tree that was.

It was not cowardice, she told herself. It was arithmetic. A secret known is a secret that can be taken; a secret half-forgotten is a secret that must be dug for, and men who dig leave holes, and holes are visible from the sea path, and the sea path was walked by everyone from the post boy to the customs riders to old Mrs. Penhale with her dog and her opinions. So she had let the knowledge go soft in her mind the way a tool goes soft with rust—still there, still shaped like knowing, but no longer sharp enough to cut anyone.

Elias Wren's visit had sharpened it again.

Three days since the census man had stood dripping in her kitchen asking his question that wasn't on any form, and three nights since she had knelt on the cold flags of the cellar with Daniel's sea chest open before her like a wound she'd finally agreed to look at. She had read the letters by lamplight until the wick drowned in its own wax, and then she had sat in the dark listening to the wind worry at the gallery railings and understood that the past was not finished with her. It had only been waiting, patient as tide, for someone upstream to start pulling on its threads.

*Under the tree with the crooked graft.*

She knew the orchard. Everyone on the coast knew the orchard—the abandoned one above Kestrel Point, where the Hollis family had grown apples until the blight took them in '71 and the bank took what the blight left. Daniel had loved that place. In their first year of marriage, before the magistracy and the ledgers and the long silences, he had walked her up there in October and shown her how the old growers had grafted new stock onto hardy root, cutting a living branch into a living trunk and binding them until neither could remember being separate. He had run his thumb along one of those scars—a swollen ridge where two woods had healed into each other—and said something she hadn't understood then and understood now with terrible clarity: *The strongest trees are the ones that have been broken and chosen to grow anyway.*

There were forty trees in that orchard. Perhaps thirty still living. And somewhere among them, beneath one marked by a crooked graft, her husband had buried whatever it was that Magistrate Corrigan would burn a coast to find.

* * *

She went up on the morning of the second storm, because there was no better morning to be had and no time left to wait for one.

The sky over the water had gone the color of pewter, low and bruised, and the gulls were flying inland in ragged, complaining skeins—the oldest warning the coast kept. The barometer in the lamp room had fallen so fast overnight that the needle tapped against the stop like a small insistent finger. By afternoon, maybe sooner, the wind would come around to the northeast and the sea would stand up on its hind legs. She had perhaps four hours of decent weather, and she meant to spend them well.

She dressed for the part, because dressing for the part was a habit seven years of widowhood had taught her: the grey wool dress, plain as a gull's back; the shawl crossed and pinned; the basket on her arm with bread and a jar of rendered fat, as though she were making the ordinary round of neighborly calls that widows make when winter closes in. No spade. A spade would tell its own story to anyone who saw her. Instead she carried the hand trowel from the window box, wrapped in sacking at the bottom of the basket under the bread, and a length of sacking besides, and her heart going like a gull in a squall.

The path up from the light station climbed through gorse and wind-bent thorn, and the higher she went the more the land began to speak of what was coming. The grass lay flat in long combed strokes. The gorse hissed. Out past the point the water had turned an ugly green-grey, whitecaps already showing their teeth a mile offshore, and the horizon had lost its clean line entirely—sea and sky smeared together like wet charcoal.

The orchard wall came first: dry stone, shoulder-high, furred with lichen the color of old brass, one corner slumped where frost had heaved it year after patient year. She stepped through the gap where the gate had hung in Daniel's time and was gone now, leaving only the hinges' rusted ghosts in the post.

Inside, the trees stood in their rows like a congregation that had stayed too long after the service.

They were bare, of course—all bone and bark in February—but they were not dead, or not all of them. Here and there a tree still held its shape with stubborn dignity, branches reaching in the disciplined geometry the old growers had trained into them a lifetime ago. Others had given up: split trunks, collapsed limbs, one great elder lying on its side with its roots torn from the earth like a fist pulled out of clay, the whole thing silvered and soft with rot. Snow lay in the wheel ruts between the rows, thin and dirty, holding the prints of birds and foxes and nothing else.

Tamsin stood just inside the wall and let the place have her.

Seven years. Seven years since she had come here last, and that once only to stand at the gate and turn back, unable to walk the rows where Daniel had walked, unable to smell the apple-rot sweetness that clung to the ground even in winter, even now, faint as a ghost's cologne. Grief had taught her to manage her geography the way she managed her lamp: certain rooms lit, certain rooms kept dark, and never the two confused.

But the letters had broken the arrangement. *He was gathering testimony,* Elias Wren had not quite said, and the letters had said it outright, and now every dark room in her was lit whether she willed it or not.

She began at the near row and worked outward, reading trees.

It was strange work, stranger than she'd expected. Each tree had its own face, its own history written in scar and stubble. Here one had been pollarded badly and grown back in a burst of wild vertical shoots like a man whose hair stands up after a fright. There another bore a ring of nail-holes where children—or lovers—had nailed a ladder rung or a swing rope decades past. She found initials cut into one trunk, healed into fat shiny welts: *E.H. + M.C., 1868.* Someone's whole young love, preserved in wood, both of them surely dead or scattered now, the tree keeping faith when no one else did.

And on nearly every living tree, if she looked closely—thumbing away lichen, turning the bark toward the grey light—she found the graft.

They were not all crooked. That was the trouble. The old growers had made their cuts clean when they could, and the trees had healed smooth, and a dozen of them wore the same swollen seam around their waists like a belt. She counted eleven candidates before she was halfway through the third row, and her breath was coming short, and the wind had begun to talk to itself in the gorse behind the wall with the rising inflection of a thing gathering courage.

Then she saw it.

The tree stood alone-ish at the orchard's far corner, where the wall met the gorse and the ground sloped toward the sea. It was smaller than its neighbors—not younger, she thought, but punished, growing at an angle the prevailing wind had bullied into it for fifty years, its whole body leaning inland as if forever flinching from the weather. And around its waist, a hand's width below the lowest fork, ran the graft—and the graft was wrong. Crooked. Not the neat circumferential seam of the others but a jagged, spiraling ridge, climbing the trunk like a scar from a bad surgery, as though the graft hand had slipped, or the rootstock had fought the scion and lost slowly, over years, in wood.

Daniel had run his thumb along exactly such a ridge, that October day. She could feel the ghost of his thumb now, warm through twenty years, and her eyes stung suddenly with a grief that had nothing to do with tears and everything to do with the fact that she had loved this man for eleven years and buried him for seven and was only now beginning to learn him.

"All right," she said aloud, to the tree, to Daniel, to the listening gorse. "All right."

* * *

The digging was harder than she had planned for.

The ground beneath the tree's seaward side was frozen to the depth of a finger and then mercifully soft beneath—sheltered by the trunk's lean, packed with years of leaf-mold gone black and sweet. The trowel did honest work. She knelt in the wet, heedless of the wool soaking at her knees, and cut the turf in careful squares and set them aside intact, because whoever closed this hole wanted it closed invisibly, and a gardener's patience was the only disguise that held.

A foot down, the trowel rang against something.

Her heart stopped, started again, stupid and loud. She put her fingers into the cold earth and worked them around the edges of the obstruction, and it was small—smaller than she'd expected, smaller than the dread she'd carried up the hill—and it came free with a sucking sound and a shower of dark crumbs.

An earthenware jar, glazed brown, sealed at the neck with wax and bound in oilcloth. Lighter than lead should be. Her hands shook so badly she nearly dropped it.

Not the ledger itself, then. Daniel had been cleverer than that, or crueler. Inside the jar, when she picked away the wax with her thumbnail right there in the freezing wind because waiting was no longer possible, she found the oilcloth bundle wrapped tight and tied with tarred twine, and inside *that*—

Paper. Many pages, folded once, dry as autumn. And a note in Daniel's hand on the outermost fold, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood:

*Tamsin — If you are reading this, I am sorry for the weight of it. What is true must be heavier than what is kind. Forgive me the difference. — D.*

She read it three times. The wind rose around her while she read, worrying the gorse into a long hiss, and the first spit of rain came across the wall like thrown gravel, hard and horizontal and cold.

*What is true must be heavier than what is kind.*

She had expected papers, yes—she had known since the letters that Daniel had gathered testimony, names, dates. But the note was different. The note was a man speaking to her across his own death, and he sounded afraid, and he sounded ashamed, and she could not yet say why she was sure of the shame, only that the certainty settled in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She rewrapped the bundle, jarred it, and got the turves back into place with fingers gone clumsy from cold, pressing them down, scattering leaf litter over the seams until the earth looked like earth and nothing more. Then she wrapped the jar in the spare sacking, pushed it deep into the basket beneath the bread and the fat, and stood.

The storm had arrived while she knelt.

She had felt it building—had heard it—but standing now, turning to face the sea, she saw what she had only been hearing: the whole horizon churned white, the swell running in vast glassy ridges a full fathom higher than yesterday, and the wind coming off it with a sound like a crowd roaring in a language she didn't speak. Rain followed, then hail, tiny and vicious, ticking off the bare branches like a thousand small clocks all running down at once.

She pulled the shawl over her head and bent into the wind and started for the wall.

* * *

The path home was a fight. The wind came off the sea in fists, staggering her, filling her mouth with the taste of salt and iron. Twice she had to stop and lean against the gorse and simply hold the ground, basket clutched to her chest, the jar inside it knocking against her ribs with every heartbeat as though it too had a pulse. Below her, the lighthouse appeared and disappeared in the rain-squalls, white tower, grey tower, gone, white again—her tower, her work, her one unambiguous duty, and she thought with something close to longing of the simple arithmetic of the lamp: oil, wick, rotation, light. Nothing in that room lied about what it was.

She was halfway down, at the bend where the path ducked below the old field hedge, when she saw Marta.

Marta Cullen stood in her gateway fifty yards off, at the head of the track that led down to her croft, one hand on the gatepost, a sack of feed meal over her shoulder, caught mid-journey like a figure in a painting of bad weather. She had clearly seen Tamsin coming down from the direction of the orchard—there was nowhere else that direction came from—and her face did the thing Marta's face always did, the quick shutters closing, the pleasant blankness arriving, the whole calculation performed and hidden inside a single blink.

"Tamsin!" she called over the wind. "You'll break your neck up there! Come in and dry!"

"Can't—" Tamsin shouted back, gesturing vaguely at the tower, the lamp, the storm, the duties. "Light won't keep itself!"

Marta nodded. Marta smiled. Marta said, in the tone of a woman saying nothing at all: "Long way to carry your shopping, the orchard road."

And there it was. Not an accusation. Not a question. Just the sentence, laid down between them on the streaming path like a coin placed carefully on a table—take it up or leave it, but know that it has been set down, and know that she saw where it came from.

"The shortcut," Tamsin called. "The gorse path."

"Ah." Marta nodded again, slower. "The gorse path." As if committing it to memory. As if filing it in whatever cabinet women of that coast kept for the things they noticed and did not say. "Well. Mind the ice. Come Sunday, if the roads allow—I've the kidney pie you like."

"I will," Tamsin said, and both of them knew she might not, and both of them let it lie.

They looked at each other one moment longer across the rain—twenty years of borrowed sugar and sat-with sickbeds and funerals stood together in that look, and something else now too, something new and unnameable, a hairline crack running through the old warmth like frost-heave through stone. Then Marta shifted the meal sack on her shoulder, turned, and went into her croft, and the door closed, and Tamsin stood alone in the roaring weather with a jar of her husband's secrets under her arm and the sudden cold understanding that the world had just gotten smaller by one safe person.

* * *

By the time she reached the tower the storm had fully arrived, and there was no more thinking to be done—only work. She lit the lamp as the daylight failed, trimmed the wick, checked the clockwork, climbed and descended and climbed again until her body knew its rounds without her. The sea came ashore in detonations she felt through the stone floor. The tower leaned and groaned like a ship. Somewhere below the point, she thought, some vessel would be fighting tonight because of Corrigan's charts and Corrigan's fees and Corrigan's court, and the thought had teeth now in a way it never had before.

Only at midnight, with the lamp burning steady above her and the storm at its worst, did she take the jar from the basket and set it on the kitchen table and sit before it with her hands flat on either side, the way one sits before a thing that must be opened and cannot be unopened.

*What is true must be heavier than what is kind.*

She thought of Marta's face in the gateway. *The gorse path.* By Sunday, if Marta chose, that sentence could travel—to her son Tobiah at the ferry, to the shop, to the church steps, anywhere the wind took gossip on this coast. Or it could stay shut behind Marta's pleasant blankness, filed and forgotten. She had no way to know which. That was the price of the orchard: she had gone up carrying a secret and come down carrying two, and one of them now lived in somebody else's keeping.

Outside, the wind screamed itself hoarse against the gallery. Inside, the jar sat brown and patient in the lamplight, full of paper, full of Daniel's voice, full of whatever truth he had thought worth dying—worth *being killed*, the letters whispered—for.

Tamsin reached out and touched the cold glaze, once, the way you touch a door before opening it.

Then she broke the seal.
