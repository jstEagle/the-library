## 5. Marta's Silence Has a Price

Marta Kessler had lived on this coast long enough to know that the sea told lies the way other people told weather—constantly, and with great conviction. It promised fair mornings and delivered fog. It promised to keep what it took and then returned things it had no business keeping: a boot, a gull with a broken wing, once, God help her, a child's shoe that she had burned without looking inside because some things a mother cannot look at and remain useful.

So when she saw Tamsin Okonkwo coming down from the orchard path in the grey hour before the second storm, bent under nothing visible but walking like a woman carrying something heavy, Marta did not need the sea to tell her anything. She had eyes.

She was in the yard, pulling the last of the leeks before the wind took them, and she straightened slowly, one hand pressed to the small of her back where forty years of bending had written their signature. Tamsin saw her seeing. That was the thing about Tamsin—she never pretended not to be seen. Other women would have turned, waved too brightly, found a reason to take another path down to the road. Tamsin just stopped at the wall between their properties, set whatever she carried against the stones out of habit or instinct, and said, "Storm coming."

"Always is," Marta said.

"I've got the shutters half done." Tamsin's face had that scraped-raw look, the skin around her eyes pink as if she'd been crying in weather too cold for tears to show. "Thought I'd come by after. If you're baking."

It was not a question about bread and they both knew it. Marta wiped her hands on her apron and felt the old reflex rise in her—the one that wanted to say *come in, sit down, tell me everything*—and pushed it back down where it belonged, next to all the other feelings that had no safe place on this coast.

"After," she agreed. "I'll have the oven hot."

Tamsin nodded, picked up whatever it was she'd set down—a satchel, oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than fruit—and went on toward the lighthouse, small against the darkening sky, and Marta stood watching until the wind made her eyes water and she could pretend that was the reason.

* * *

The trouble with Tobiah started, as most troubles did, with money, and ended, as most troubles on this coast seemed to end, with Corrigan.

Her son had taken the ferry post four winters ago, when the herring failed and the boat needed caulking and there were three younger siblings with shoes wearing through. The ferry post came with wages, and the wages came with conditions, and the conditions came—this was the part nobody wrote down—with debt. Not the honest kind, the kind you owed the merchant and paid off in fish. The kind that lived in a ledger in the magistrate's office, where the numbers grew like mould in the dark, no matter how faithfully Tobiah paid. Marta had gone into town once, early on, to ask about it. She remembered the clerk's smile, the way he'd tapped the ledger with one fingernail, almost fondly, and said, *Mrs. Kessler, your son's account is in excellent standing. Standing is such an interesting word, isn't it? He stands where we put him.*

She had understood then that her son was not employed at the ferry checkpoint. He was stationed there, the way soldiers are stationed, the way dogs are staked out. And every coin he earned went through Corrigan's hands and came back lighter, so that the debt never shrank and the leash never slackened, and Tobiah, who had his father's shoulders and his father's stubbornness, had learned to carry it the way you carry a stone in your boot: without talking about it, but limping all the same.

And now here was Tamsin Okonkwo, seven years quiet as a grave, suddenly walking down from a dead man's orchard with oilcloth bundles and scraped-pink eyes, asking for bread and salt and company, and Marta could do the arithmetic as well as any clerk in Corrigan's office. Something was moving. And whatever moved on this river passed the ferry, and whoever passed the ferry passed her son, and whatever touched her son would come back to her kitchen eventually, the way the sea returned everything it had no business keeping.

She kneaded the dough harder than it needed. The window over the basin showed the sky going the colour of wet slate, and the first gusts were already worrying the loose shingle on the roof, tick-tick-tick, like a finger tapping on a ledger.

* * *

Tamsin came at dusk, shaking rain from her shawl in the doorway like a bird. She brought eggs she didn't need to bring—they both knew the hens had stopped laying in November—and a jar of last summer's honey, and she set them on the table with the careful precision of a woman paying a debt of her own.

"Bread smells like heaven."

"Bread smells like flour and patience. Heaven smells like nothing, if you ask me." Marta ladled the stew, sat, watched Tamsin fold her hands around the bowl the way cold people do, taking the heat before the food. "You look tired."

"Didn't sleep. Storm."

"The storm's not here yet."

"It's always here somewhere." Tamsin smiled, and it was the smooth smile, the keeper's smile, the one she'd worn for seven years at every market day and christening and funeral, and Marta felt something in her chest go hard and sad at the sight of it, because she realized she was about to stop letting that smile work.

They ate. The wind rose. Somewhere outside a shutter banged twice and gave up its hinge. Marta refilled bowls neither of them emptied and finally set down her spoon and said, in the voice she used for the girls when they were small and lying badly, "Tamsin Okonkwo. What are you carrying?"

Tamsin went very still. Stillness, Marta thought, was its own confession; innocent women asked *what do you mean?*

"Carrying?"

"You came down from the orchard this morning with a bundle heavy enough to change how a person walks. You've been up there three times since the ice came. You brought me eggs out of season and honey I know you hoard like a dragon, which means you want something from me, or you want to warn me, or you want me to warn you. I've known you eleven years. I baked bread the week they pulled Daniel out of the water and you couldn't eat a bite of it and I never held that against you. So don't give me the lighthouse smile. Save that for the census men."

The fire popped. Rain began, soft at first, testing the roof the way a thief tests a lock.

Tamsin looked down at her bowl for a long moment. When she looked up, the smile was gone, and underneath it her face was older and more frightened and somehow more honest, and Marta wished immediately that she hadn't broken it, the way you wish you hadn't cracked an egg to check if it was good.

"There's a man," Tamsin said. "Wren. Census taker, he says."

"He says."

"He asks questions off the form. About Daniel. About correspondence." Tamsin's fingers worked at the edge of the table, worrying a groove that had been there twenty years. "Marta, I can't tell you more than that. Not because I don't trust you. Because—" she stopped, started again. "Because the less you know, the safer you are, and I mean that literally, not as a figure of speech."

"And my son?" Marta kept her voice level, though inside her something had begun to fall, slowly, like a cup knocked off a shelf that hasn't hit the floor yet. "Does the less-he-know rule apply to Tobiah? Because he stands at the ferry, Tamsin. Whatever moves on this river moves past him, and whatever passes him, Corrigan makes him answer for. Do you understand what that man's debt means? It means my boy isn't a guard at that checkpoint. He's collateral."

"I know."

"You know." Marta stood, gathered the bowls though they didn't need gathering, stood at the basin with her back to her friend and the water running hot over her knuckles. "Seven years I've watched you keep your head down and I respected it. Grief has its own manners. But now something's moving, and you won't tell me what, and you sit at my table eating my bread while my son's whole future hangs off Corrigan's ledger like a coat on a nail, and you say *I know.*"

"Marta—"

"Do you know what they do at that ferry when someone crosses with something they shouldn't? They search him. And if the search finds nothing, they report that they searched him, and if the search finds something, they arrest him, and either way the report goes to Brandt at the customs house and Brandt reports to Corrigan, and Corrigan—" she turned, drying her hands, and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was all the protection she had left to offer anyone—"Corrigan owns Tobiah's debt, Tamsin. Every year the interest grows. Every year the number gets bigger. My son could work until his hands fell off and die owing that man money. That's not employment. That's a chain, and it's a chain with my name on the other end too, because if Tobiah falls, the girls fall, and I fall with them."

The rain had thickened to a proper drumming now. Through the small window, the lighthouse beam swung round, white, white, dark, white, white, dark, steady as a heartbeat, faithful as ever, sweeping the storm and finding nothing and sweeping again.

Tamsin hadn't moved. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you I love you like a sister and I'm telling you my son eats first." Marta heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it. "That's what I'm telling you. Whatever you're doing—and I'm not asking, do you hear me, I am not asking—you think about whose hands it passes through. You think about whether the people who love you can afford it. Because I saw you today on the orchard path and I said nothing and I'll say nothing, that's my gift and it's the only one I can safely give. But silence has a price, Tamsin. Everything on this coast does. Corrigan taught us the rates himself."

For a moment the only sound was rain and wind and the far groan of trees on the headland. Then Tamsin rose, and crossed the room, and took Marta's face in both cold hands, and kissed her forehead the way you kiss the dead, or the very ill, or someone you are leaving.

"You warned me," she said quietly. "Bread and salt. That's the old way, isn't it? You feed someone and speak true, and after that whatever happens, your conscience is clean."

"My conscience was never the problem."

"No." Tamsin stepped back, and Marta saw that her eyes were bright and dry and terribly decided, and understood that whatever warning she had given, it had landed on ground already ploughed. "No, I don't suppose it was."

At the door, pausing with her hand on the latch, Tamsin turned. "If a woman ever comes to the ferry," she said, "a widow, shipping fruit crates from the orchard downriver—apples, out of season, which everyone will find strange, because they are strange—if such a woman ever comes, and if a young man at the ferry happened to look the other way, or didn't, whichever his conscience allowed—" she met Marta's eyes, and there was no smile anywhere on her face—"what would happen to him?"

Marta's blood went cold and then hot. *There it is,* she thought. *There's the price named out loud, sitting on my own table next to the salt.*

"They'd write it down," she said. "Whatever he did or didn't do, they'd write it down, and it would live in that ledger, and that ledger lives longer than men."

Tamsin nodded slowly, as if Marta had answered a different question, a truer one. "Then pray the ledger dies first," she said, and went out into the storm.

* * *

Marta did not sleep. She sat by the banked fire with her mending in her lap, not sewing it, listening to the storm build itself story by story out on the water, and she turned the evening over and over like a stone in her hand, looking for the side of it that hurt least.

She could send Tobiah a letter. A warning dressed as family news—*your mother is well, the hens are done laying, mind the ice on the landing.* But letters to the ferry post were read; everyone knew they were read, and pretending otherwise was a courtesy the coast paid itself. A letter might save him or it might hang him, depending on which clerk read it and what mood the season had put him in.

She could go to Corrigan. Walk into that warm office with its smell of ink and rot, and lay out what little she knew—which was almost nothing, a bundle on a path, a question about widows and apples—and trade it for her son's debt. She imagined the magistrate's face doing its gentle trick, sympathy folding over calculation like a wave over sand. *Mrs. Kessler. Your loyalty to your family does you credit.* And the debt reduced, maybe, or maybe just renamed, because debts to Corrigan didn't end, they only changed shape, and she would have bought Tobiah's body with Tamsin's throat and would taste that trade every morning of the rest of her life.

Or she could do what she had already, without deciding it, done: nothing. Say nothing. Sew nothing. Sit in her chair while the storm came up the valley, and let two grown people walk into the river road with their secret, and let her son stand at his post with his stone in his boot, and trust—to God, to luck, to whatever power ran the tides—that mercy, if it existed anywhere on this coast, would pass close enough to grab.

The beam swept the window. White, white, dark.

Near dawn the storm reached its full voice, and Marta thought of Tamsin alone up there in the lighthouse with her secrets, keeping her lamp lit against a darkness that was, this time, entirely of her own choosing. And she thought of Tobiah, who would be at the landing by now in this weather, chapped and cold and counting crates, and she got up and found paper, and sat a long while with the pen dry in her hand.

In the end she wrote only: *Son — the early apples are ripe. Mind how you pick them. Some bruise if you look at them wrong. — Mother*

She sealed it and set it on the shelf by the door for the first rider, and told herself it meant nothing, a mother's foolishness, weather-talk, the kind of thing women wrote to sons in winter since writing began.

But her hands shook as she sealed it, and outside the storm leaned its whole weight on the house, and down the valley, unseen, unheard, the river ice began—very quietly, the way all ruinous things begin—to sing.
