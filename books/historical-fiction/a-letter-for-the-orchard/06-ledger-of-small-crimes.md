## 6. Ledger of Small Crimes

The ledger sat between us on the table like a third guest who had come uninvited and would not leave.

I had not opened it since carrying it home from the orchard, cradled against my chest beneath my oilskin while the second storm clawed at the coast like something trying to get in—or out. It had spent two nights on the shelf above my bed, wrapped in Daniel's old shirt, and I had slept beside it as I had once slept beside him, aware of its weight without touching it.

Now Elias Wren sat across from me with his coat still dripping from the walk up from the village road, and there was nothing left to do but open it.

"You could wait," he said. He had a way of speaking that made waiting sound like a kindness rather than a delay. "After you've eaten. After you've slept."

"I have slept seven years," I said. "It hasn't helped."

I untied the oilcloth. The ledger was smaller than I remembered—Daniel had always been a large man, and everything of his had seemed larger for it—and bound in green leather gone stiff and dark at the corners where his hands had held it. The spine was cracked along its length. Inside, the pages were ruled in faint blue lines, and his handwriting marched across them in that upright, careful script he used for anything that mattered, the same hand that had written our marriage lines in the parish book, *Tamsin Okonkwo, née Verran*, the letters tall and certain as fence posts.

The first entries were dated eleven years back. Before we married. Before the lighthouse. Before any of it.

I began to read aloud, because silence in that kitchen with a stranger listening felt worse than speech, and because I wanted to hear Daniel's voice again even secondhand, even in figures.

*Received from J. Merrick, agent, five pounds for certification of cargo manifest No. 441. Manifest amended per instruction.*

I stopped.

Elias did not move. He was good at not moving; I had noticed that about him the first day, when he stood dripping in my doorway asking his question-that-wasn't-on-the-form. A man who knew how to be furniture when furniture served him.

"Read on," he said quietly.

*Received from Corrigan's office, via Merrick, ten pounds. Two vessels cleared without inspection. Names withheld by request.*

The rain came against the windows then, hard, a squall off the sea that rattled the shutters the way it had rattled them every winter of my keeping. I read on. There were pages of it—small sums mostly, five pounds here, twelve there, occasionally thirty for something described only as *the usual consideration*. Ferry manifests. Customs clearances. A garrison requisition signed off without count. The small change of corruption, recorded in a hand so neat it might have been a sermon.

"He took bribes." My voice came out flat, factual, a keeper reporting weather. "Before. At the customs house. Before he ever worked for the light."

"Yes."

"He was twenty-four years old." I said it as though age were an argument, as though being young excused anything, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left me.

"He was old enough to know what a signature costs other people," Elias said. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it—he said it the way a surgeon says what the blade must do.

I turned the pages faster now, wanting to get past this part, wanting to reach the man I had buried, the witness, the husband who had died—no, who had been drowned—for the crime of honesty. And the honest entries were there too, further in, beginning four years after the first bribe. Testimony gathered. Names of men lost at sea whose vessels should never have sailed. Dates. Amounts paid out by Corrigan's office to keep inspectors quiet, keepers blind, ferrymen generous. Daniel had built his case the way he had built everything, brick by brick, line by line, and the later pages were full of the evidence that would have toppled the magistrate if Daniel had lived to deliver it.

But the early pages did not stop existing because the later ones were better. That was the thing about ledgers. They kept everything. That was their whole nature.

"Here." Elias leaned across and touched one entry near the middle, dated the spring before Daniel's death. *Note: R. Calloway, boatman, refused payment. Subsequently drowned off the Teeth, 9 March. Vessel insured through Corrigan's syndicate. See folio 61.* "This is the boy I told you about. Robert Calloway. Nineteen years old. His mother stood outside the assize court every sitting day for two years holding a placard no one read."

"I know who he is." I had known before Elias finished. I had read the name in these very pages the night before, alone, with the lamp trimmed low, and had sat with it the way you sit with a stone in your shoe all day because you cannot decide whether to take it out.

"And here—" Elias turned back toward the front, toward the early pages, the bribes. "Here is why Corrigan was able to drown him. Because Daniel wasn't a righteous man they silenced. He was a bought man who turned. That's the whole architecture of it. That's what makes the case."

"What makes the case," I repeated.

"Tamsin." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "When this goes before a court—if it goes before a court—Corrigan's counsel will have these pages inside an hour. They'll say: this witness was corrupt, his testimony is the testimony of a man saving himself, every word he wrote is self-exculpation. They will use his own hand to hang his ghost. Unless we bring it forward ourselves. Unless we say plainly: yes, he took their money, and here is exactly when, and here is exactly when he stopped, and here is what he did with what he knew afterward. You cannot defend a man by hiding him."

"I'm not defending him."

"No?" He gestured at the table, at my hands flat on either side of the ledger like a woman sheltering a candle flame. "You've been reading it upside down whenever I lean close. You've turned past the Merrick entries twice. You're reading it like a wife, which is your right, but the court will read it like a weapon, and one of us has to know which book we're carrying."

The storm pressed in. The lamp guttered and steadied itself, and in its little circle of light the kitchen was suddenly very small—the table, the two of us, the dead man's arithmetic spread between us like a wound opened for examination.

"You didn't know him," I said.

"No."

"He carried a drowning boy three miles through surf when he was seventeen. Off Porthallow. The boy lived. The family sent him a letter he kept in his coat pocket until it fell apart." I heard my own voice rising and could not make myself care. "He tithed when we had nothing. When the herring failed and we ate bread and salt for a month, he gave the church its tenth anyway, and laughed about God doing his own arithmetic. This—" I put my hand flat on the early pages. "This is a young man in a port town run by Corrigan, taking the money everyone took, because the alternative was to be Robert Calloway. And then he stopped. Do you understand? He stopped. He looked at what he'd become and he spent four years building the thing that would destroy it, knowing it would destroy him too. That's not a corrupt man. That's a man who climbed out."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind found the gap under the door and breathed cold across the floor.

"My sister," he said at last, "took a bribe too."

I looked up.

"Not money. She took a favor. A magistrate's clerk arranged her husband's discharge papers out of turn, and she thanked him, and she said nothing when the same clerk falsified a deposition a year later, because you don't bite the hand, do you, when the hand has fed you. She said nothing, and the deposition sent a man to the penal colony, and the man was innocent, and my sister carried it for six years and then drank herself into the harbor." His voice stayed level the whole way through, which somehow made it worse than shouting. "So I will tell you what I know about the truth of people, Mrs. Okonkwo. It does not come clean. It comes muddied, and partial, and ashamed of itself, and if you try to wash it before you show it, you are not preserving the truth—you are curating it. And curated truth is just another kind of lie, with better manners."

"Memory isn't a courtroom."

"No," he agreed. "Memory's worse. Memory is contested ground. Both armies camp on it forever."

We sat with that. The lamp hissed softly. Down in the rocks below the tower the sea worked at its endless labor, and I thought of Daniel walking those rocks at dusk with his hands behind his back, thinking whatever thoughts a man thinks when he is building a case against his own past self. Had he hated those early pages? Had he wanted to tear them out? I turned to the back of the ledger and saw that he had not torn anything out. He had indexed the bribes. He had numbered them. In the margin of the first entry, in his upright fence-post hand, he had written a single word:

*Begun.*

Not *sin*. Not *shame*. *Begun.* As though he understood that a ledger records what is, and what is, in a man, includes both the taking and the turning.

"He knew," I said slowly. "He left these pages in on purpose. He could have cut them out and no one would ever have known. He numbered them instead. He built his own prosecution into his own case."

"Then he was braver than most witnesses," Elias said, "and wiser than most lawyers. Which means the best way to honor him is to do exactly what he did. All of it, Tamsin. The bribes and the testimony together, in order, with dates. Let the court see the whole arc of the man. Because I promise you—" he tapped the page with one finger, gently, almost tenderly, "—if we hide the first half, Corrigan's people will find it, and present only the first half, and the second half will die in a drawer somewhere and every one of these names will stay buried with it."

I pulled the ledger toward me and looked at the first entry again. Five pounds. J. Merrick, agent. Cargo manifest No. 441. Somewhere downstream of that five pounds there might have been a vessel that sank, a family that starved, a boy on a boat called *Providence* or *Sweet Return* going down in weather she should never have sailed in. Bribery was never as small as its price.

"All of it," I said.

Elias let out a breath. "All of it."

"But I copy the fair version myself. My hand, not yours. And when the court reads it, I want it read in order. Beginning to end. Let them watch him become."

"That," said Elias Wren, and for the first time since he had walked dripping into my life he smiled—a small, tired, genuine thing, "is more than I hoped for."

* * *

He stayed to help with the copying. There was no pretense anymore between us about why he had come or what we intended; the pretenses had burned away like fog in wind, and what remained was stranger and sturdier. He ground the ink while I set out paper. We worked by lamplight while the storm walked its rounds outside, and neither of us spoke much, and the scratch of nibs on paper filled the kitchen like insects in summer grass.

It was near midnight, I think, when my pen stopped over a date.

"Elias."

"Mm."

"This entry. Three days before he died." My mouth had gone dry. "*Ledger complete. Copy lodged. Location known to one other. If anything happens to me, tell her to look where we first—*" And there it broke off. The sentence simply ended, mid-line, the ink trailing into a small blot where the nib had rested too long.

We stared at it together.

"Where we first," Elias read. "First what?"

I shook my head. "There's no record. He didn't finish it. Or—" and the cold moved through me, slow and certain, the cold of deep water, "—or he thought he had said enough. Or he thought I'd know."

"Do you?"

I looked at the lamp burning above us. I looked at the tower rising through the dark beyond the glass, seven years of my life poured into keeping its light, and somewhere in the walls of that light, in some crevice of the man's last days, a completed copy of everything lay waiting where *we first*—

"The orchard," I said, and my voice sounded far away to me. "No. No, I've searched the orchard. I searched it yesterday."

"Then where else did you begin?" Elias asked softly. "Together. Where did the two of you begin?"

And I found that I did not know, and that not knowing frightened me more than Corrigan's men, more than the storms, more than anything the sea had ever thrown at this coast—because the answer was somewhere in my own memory, contested ground, and time was coming up the river road to claim it.
