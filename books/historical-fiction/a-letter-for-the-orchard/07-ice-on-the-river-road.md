## 7. Ice on the River Road

Elias Wren had spent eleven years learning to read rivers the way other men read ledgers, and by the last week of February the river below Tamsin Okonkwo's headland was telling him a story he did not want to hear.

He stood at the edge of the ice road at first light, breath smoking, hands buried in his coat pockets where a census man's fingers had no business being blue with cold. The river lay flat and white from bank to bank, a mile of frozen road that farmers had crossed all winter without thinking twice—sledge tracks stitched in every direction, a dead fox frozen mid-stride near the far shore, the whole surface groaning now and then like an old house settling. But beneath the groaning there was another sound, lower, wetter, and Elias had heard it before, in the year of the Kessel flood, when the ice went out early and took three bridges and a boy with it.

The river was waking up.

"You'll want to mind the shallows past Miller's Bend." The voice came from behind him, and Elias turned to find old Anders Halvorsen standing with his sledge dogs, a man shaped like a fence post and about as talkative. "Ice thins there where the current runs under. Come spring you couldn't pay me to walk it."

"When does it usually go out?"

Anders spat, considered. "Depends what the storms give us. Early March some years. Late April others. But we've had two big ones already this winter and the third's coming—you can smell it in the wind off the water. When that one hits, the ice won't wait for April."

*The third storm.* Elias filed it away the way he filed everything: names, dates, weather, the small accumulations of fact that made up a case. He thanked Anders, bought nothing, asked nothing else, and walked back along the shore road toward the village with his collar up and his mind working like a mill wheel.

The spring court convened on the fifteenth of March. He had known that date for four years—had planned around it, dreamed around it, built a paper architecture of affidavits and precedents around it—and every year the date had come and gone with the case no stronger than a rumor. This year, for the first time, there was a ledger in the world with Corrigan's signature trail running through it like a vein of ore. This year, for the first time, there was a widow on a headland who had touched that ledger with her own hands and had not burned it.

And this year, the river intended to go out between him and the courthouse.

* * *

Tamsin met him in the kitchen, because she always met him in the kitchen, as though the rest of the lighthouse were sea territory and the kitchen alone belonged to the living. She had the kettle on and bread warming, and she did not look up when he came in, which was her way of telling him she already knew why he'd come.

"The ice is talking," Elias said.

"I've heard it for three nights." She set down two cups without asking whether he wanted one. "Daniel used to say the river keeps its own calendar. It doesn't care about courts."

"No," Elias agreed. "It doesn't care about anything. That's what I like about it."

That earned him a look—half suspicion, half something warmer she would have denied under oath. Seven days had passed since the second storm, since she had come back to the lighthouse with river mud on her boots and apple blossom grafting sap on her sleeves, pretending she'd been nowhere. Seven days since she had set the ledger on the kitchen table between them, wrapped in oilcloth, and said, *Read it, then. All of it. And tell me if it's worth what it will cost.*

He had read it. Three nights by lamplight, cross-referencing against the papers he carried sewn into his coat lining—the court transcripts, the shipping manifests, the letters Daniel Okonkwo had written to a correspondent named Aldous Vane in the years before Vane himself drowned. The picture that emerged was not clean. It was not the simple thing Elias had wanted when he was twenty-six and burying his sister with a verdict of *death by misadventure* stamped over what everyone in that courtroom knew had been something else entirely.

But it was enough. Bribes dated and initialed. Fines levied and pocketed. A consignment of bonded timber released without duty, twice. And in the back pages, in a hand that shook only once, a list of names—men who had tried to testify and been ruined, transferred, drowned—for that was the word, written plainly in Daniel's careful clerk's hand: *drowned*, beside the name Peter Lusk, aged nineteen, whose body had come up in the nets off Gull Point six weeks after he swore an affidavit.

Elias set down his cup. "We move it downstream. Before the break-up. Before the fifteenth."

"Downstream where?"

"The county seat. Judge Aldous's old circuit clerk still holds office there—Merrick. He's honest, which in that town makes him either brave or stupid, and I've corresponded with him long enough to know he's both. If the ledger reaches his hands before the spring session, he can enter it into evidence directly. No checkpoints after that. Once it's in the court's custody, even Corrigan can't burn it without burning himself."

Tamsin sat across from him with her arms folded, and Elias watched her do the arithmetic he'd watched a hundred people do in a hundred kitchens over eleven years—the arithmetic of risk, of what a thing weighed against what it might buy. She did it faster than most. She had been doing sums like these alone for seven years.

"The ferry," she said finally.

"The ferry. There are crossings left—two, maybe three weeks of them, until the ice goes soft and Tobiah's father ties the boat up for the season. After that it's forty miles around by the coast road, and the coast road passes through the garrison yard." He let that sit. "So it's the ferry, or it's waiting for thaw and taking the long way with no ice to carry us, and the long way arrives too late."

"And the ferry means Marta's son."

"Tobiah. Yes."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise. She had known, Elias realized. Had perhaps known from the beginning that any road off this coast ran through somebody's child.

"Marta has fed me bread every Sunday for five years," Tamsin said quietly. "She sat with me the week after Daniel drowned and never once asked me a question I didn't want to answer. And you're asking me to put her boy in the middle of it."

"I'm asking you to look at the map," Elias said. "There isn't another one."

* * *

They spent that evening and half the night building the plan the way he had learned to build cases: from the smallest true things upward.

The ferry crossing at Tobiah's landing was legal traffic until the ice went out—that much Elias had confirmed through the shipping notices, read aloud each market day at the cooper's hall. Crates, livestock, post, passengers. A woman with freight was nobody. A woman with freight was *everybody*, on this coast, where half the widows within ten miles shipped salt fish or wool downstream to make their winters' ends meet.

"So she's freight," Tamsin said. She had pulled Daniel's old chart of the river toward her across the table and was studying it with the same unflinching attention she gave everything, as though the chart might confess something the river wouldn't. "Apples. From the orchard. There are still crates of storage fruit in the cold shed—windfalls, mostly, but sound enough. Nobody questions a woman moving apples in February; they pity her."

"They should question the apples," Elias said. "Because the ledger travels inside them. Bottom of a crate, under two layers of fruit, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas. If they open the crate, they find apples. If they take the crate—" he hesitated, because the next part of the plan was the part he liked least, "—they find apples."

Tamsin looked up sharply. "Decoy."

"A second crate. Same markings, same stenciling, packed to weigh the same. It goes on the sledge where anyone watching can see it. The real one rides underneath the false floor of the cart, where nobody looks unless they're told to look." He tapped the chart. "Corrigan's men are already on the coast—I had a letter from Merrick's office Tuesday. A customs rider named Brandt has been asking at the landings about strangers. About *a stranger*, singular. A census man who's been seen at the lighthouse more often than a census requires."

"He's describing you."

"He's describing me. Which means we don't travel together. You take the ledger down on the morning ferry, plain as Sunday. I go around by the marsh path and meet the sledge on the far side—or I don't meet it at all, if it's cleaner that way. You deliver the decoy crate to the freight agent at the county wharf, you collect your receipt like any widow in the world, and you come home." He paused. "The real crate goes to Merrick by a different hand. I've engaged a courier—a carter named Sorrel who runs goods downriver and asks no questions because I pay him not to."

Tamsin was quiet a long moment, her finger resting on the chart at the point marked *Ferry Landing*, where the ink had faded almost to nothing. "Three checkpoints," she said at last. "Ferry. Garrison. Customs house. Daniel's letters named them, didn't they."

It wasn't quite a question. Elias nodded anyway.

"Then we know exactly where we'll be tested." She drew a slow breath. "At the ferry, they'll be looking for smugglers. At the garrison, deserters. At the customs house—" her finger moved down the river to the last marked station, "—for whatever Corrigan has told them to fear. And he knows Daniel kept records. So somewhere between here and the courthouse, someone is going to be told to search for a ledger."

"Yes."

"Then we should assume they find the decoy."

"If we're lucky, they find the decoy and believe it's the whole of it. If we're unlucky—" Elias chose the words carefully, the way he chose words in depositions, knowing they would be remembered, "—if we're unlucky, whoever seizes it becomes a witness against us. And the people at those checkpoints aren't strangers, Tamsin. You said it yourself once. They're friends. Or they were."

She flinched. Only slightly—a tightening at the corner of her mouth—but seven days of reading this woman had taught Elias to see the small movements, the ones she'd trained herself out of making. He thought of his sister then, unexpectedly, the way he always did when he saw someone carrying grief like ballast: steadily, silently, so long they'd forgotten it was there.

"My sister," he said, before he could decide not to say it, "was called before Corrigan's court in 'eighty-four. Debt claim. Manufactured—the whole file was manufactured, I proved it later, or proved enough of it. She lost the farm. Six months later she was dead. The record says fever." He looked at his cup. "I was twenty-six. I spent the next four years learning law I had no business learning, and the seven years after that collecting paper. Every affidavit. Every manifest. Every name. And every year the spring session came and went, because paper alone convicts no one. Paper needs a witness. Paper needs someone who stood in the room."

Tamsin said nothing, but she had gone very still, the stillness of a woman listening with her whole body.

"Your husband stood in the room," Elias said. "For years he stood in the room, and wrote it down, and hid it where he thought it would survive him. He was right. It did. Now it needs to travel thirty miles of rotten ice past three men who knew his face, and it needs to arrive before the fifteenth of March, and after that I can't help it anymore. Courts do what they do." He spread his hands. "But the clock, Mrs. Okonkwo—the clock starts now, and it doesn't run on our comfort."

Outside, the wind had come around to the southeast, and beneath its noise, faint but unmistakable, the river groaned again—that deep wet complaint, like something turning over in its sleep.

* * *

They settled the details by lamplight while the kettle went cold and the bread went untouched.

The ferry ran Tuesdays and Fridays while the ice held. Friday, then—four days hence—to allow for packing the crates, salting the fruit, oiling the cart. Tamsin would drive it herself; a hired driver was a loose tongue. The decoy crate stenciled with the orchard mark, the real one beneath the false floor with a layer of turnips over it, because no customs man alive searches under turnips. Sorrel the carter would take the real crate from her at the far landing, casual as a neighbor helping unload, and be in Merrick's chambers by Saturday night.

"And if Sorrel talks?" Tamsin asked.

"He won't. But if he does"—Elias had turned this over all night, honestly, unhappily—"if he does, then Corrigan learns the ledger exists for certain, learns it's moving, and learns nothing about where it started. Your name stays out of it. That's the shape of the thing: every layer buys silence for the layer under it."

"Every layer costs somebody," she said.

He didn't argue. It was true, and they both knew it, and arguing truths was a waste of lamp oil. Instead he took out his notebook—the census man's notebook, the one with the official stamp embossed on its cover, the finest hiding place he had ever owned—and wrote out the schedule in his small square hand: *Friday. Ferry, dawn crossing. Garrison road by midmorning. Wharf by noon.* Then, beneath it, in cipher, the name Merrick and the words *enter directly, do not wait for me.*

"What happens to you?" Tamsin asked, watching him write. "After. If it works."

"Then I go wherever the case goes. Depositions, hearings, the trial if it comes to trial. Eleven years, Mrs. Okonkwo—it won't end quietly, whatever ends." He closed the notebook. "And if it doesn't work—if Brandt's riders catch the sledge, if the ice goes out under us—then I'm a census taker who got caught in bad weather, and you're a widow who shipped some apples. That's the beauty of the plan. Its whole beauty. Everyone in it looks harmless."

"Everyone in it *isn't* harmless," Tamsin said. "That's rather the point, isn't it."

Elias smiled for the first time that day, and found it strange on his face, like a key that fit a lock he'd forgotten he had. "Yes," he said. "It is."

He rose to leave. At the door she stopped him with his hand on the latch—not touching him, just standing between him and the night, and outside the wind had thickened and turned, and he could smell it now too, the iron-and-river smell of a storm gathering beyond the horizon.

"Elias." First time she'd used his given name. She didn't seem to notice she'd done it. "Marta's son. Tobiah. If he's at that ferry landing, and if he looks at my crates the way his mother looks at my face—he'll know something's wrong. What do we do if he asks?"

Elias considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even when honesty was useless.

"We hope," he said, "that he loves his mother more than he fears Corrigan. Because we cannot buy that, and we cannot plan for it, and it's the only thing between us and the water."

She let him out then, into the dark and the rising wind, and Elias walked the cliff path down toward the village with the notebook against his ribs and the river booming below like a struck bell. Behind him the lighthouse lamp swept its steady circle—once, twice—and ahead, in the village, windows were being shuttered against the coming weather, and in one of them, he would learn soon enough, a customs rider named Brandt was drinking paid-for whiskey and asking a cooper's apprentice, very pleasantly, whether the lighthouse woman had been seen hauling anything out of her orchard lately.

Four days, Elias thought. Four days, and then the clock either started or broke.

Above him the clouds came up the river valley like a tide, and the ice spoke again, longer this time, a cracking groan that ran the whole mile of the crossing—the sound, old Anders had told him, that the river made when it began counting the days it had left.

Elias counted with it.
