## 8. First Checkpoint: The Ferryman

The river wore its ice like a man wears a coat two sizes too large—loose at the shoulders, splitting at the seams, groaning in the night when the cold bit deepest. Tamsin had lived beside it long enough to read its moods the way she read the sea: the flat grey of a river holding its breath, the white seams where pressure was building, the low complaint beneath the ferry landing that meant the season's patience was nearly spent.

She rose before dawn on the day of the crossing, and for a long while she stood at the kitchen window with her hands around a cup of tea gone cold, watching the dark loosen over the water. Below her, the lighthouse threw its last sweep across the channel—the lamp she would not extinguish until this was done, though Elias had argued for it. *A lit lamp says someone is home,* she had told him, and he had accepted the logic without liking it.

In the corner by the door sat three crates of apples, packed the night before with straw from Marta's barn and a cunning false bottom that had cost them most of a candle's worth of argument. The real ledger lay sewn into oilcloth beneath the apples of the middle crate, wrapped tight as a wound. On top, filling out the weight so the crates wouldn't ring hollow if anyone lifted them, sat fruit from the old orchard—small, hard, winter-bitten apples with skins like tarnished copper. They were sour eating but honest cargo. Nobody questions a widow selling apples in the dead of winter; they only pity her, and pity opens doors faster than suspicion closes them.

Tamsin dressed the part before the cracked mirror in Daniel's old room. She had worn widow's black for seven years—it was no disguise—but today she wore it differently: the collar high, the shawl drawn close, her hair scraped back under a bonnet that shadowed her face. She rubbed ash from the stove into the creases of her knuckles until her hands looked like a workingwoman's hands, chapped and indifferent. A woman who kept a lighthouse did not look like a woman who smuggled sedition. But a woman who hauled fruit to market looked like nothing at all—like weather, like furniture, like one more burden the world had set down without noticing.

*You are Mrs. Okonkwo of the orchard road,* she told her reflection. *Your husband grew apples and drowned, and you are poor now, and you want only to be through the checkpoint before your feet freeze.*

The reflection looked back at her with her own eyes and said nothing, which was its way of agreeing.

* * *

Elias walked her as far as the bend above the landing, then stopped where the gorse gave cover. He had wanted to come all the way to the water; she had refused, and he had finally seen the sense in it—a lone woman draws less notice than a pair, and his face had been seen asking census questions along every mile of this coast.

"The manifest," he said for the fourth time, pressing the folded paper into her mittened hand. "Ferry office stamps it at the crossing. If Brandt's men check it downstream, it must be clean."

"And if they check the crates?"

"Then we learn what we learn." His breath came out white and hung between them. "Tamsin. If it goes wrong—if they take you—you say nothing. You let them have the apples. You are a grieving widow shipping what her husband left her. You know nothing about any paper."

"I know I'm a poor liar."

"You're an excellent liar. That's what frightens me." He almost smiled. "I watched you lie to me across my own census form, and I believed every word until I knew better."

The wind came off the river then, carrying the smell of ice and wet rope and coal smoke from the ferry house. Somewhere below, a bell clanged twice—the signal that the morning crossing would run, the last easy one before the ice made liars of everyone.

"An hour," Elias said. "If you're not across in an hour, I go to plan B."

"Plan B is a bad idea."

"Plan B is a terrible idea. Which is why I'd rather you cross the river." He stepped back into the gorse and became, quite suddenly, nothing—a shape, then a suggestion, then absence. The man could vanish like smoke going out a window. It occurred to her, not for the first time, that whatever grief had built him, it had also taught him things she didn't want to ask about.

* * *

The ferry landing crouched at the foot of the cliff road like something that had crawled there to die and changed its mind. Ice furred the pilings. The river ran dark and fast in the channel, carrying broken plates of ice downstream with a sound like crockery dropped in another room. On the far bank, maybe two hundred yards of grey water away, the customs road climbed toward the garrison checkpoint and everything beyond it—and beyond that, in a town she hadn't seen in seven years, the courthouse with its spring sessions and its magistrate whose name she could not yet bring herself to think in full.

Corrigan. There. She'd thought it. The name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The ferry itself was a flat barge worked by cable and current, broad enough for a cart and four passengers, its deck boards silvered with frost. Two men waited ahead of her: a drover with sheep-smell coming off him in waves despite the cold, and a young clerk with a leather satchel who studied his boots as though they might contain instructions. Neither looked at her. Widows were invisible. She counted it a mercy of the age.

And there, at the cable post, checking the windlass with hands that moved like they'd been born around the handle, stood Tobiah.

Marta's son had been fourteen the year Daniel drowned—a big-boned boy forever hauling nets, forever laughing, forever underfoot. Seven years had turned him into a man with shoulders like a doorframe and his mother's exact eyes set in his father's heavy jaw. He wore the ferry company's canvas coat, and pinned to it, small and official, the brass badge of the checkpoint: ferryman, licensed inspector of cargo, sworn officer of the river district. Sworn, which meant sworn to Corrigan, because Corrigan held the ferry company's debts the way he held everything else on this coast—in his fist, gently, smiling.

*He knows me,* Tamsin thought, and her heart performed a slow, deliberate stumble. *He has known me since he was small enough to sit on my kitchen floor and eat my bread.*

It was too late to turn back. The clerk had noticed her now, and turning back was the one thing a widow with crates did not do.

"Morning, missus." Tobiah's voice was a baritone she didn't recognize, pitched to carry over water. "Manifest?"

She handed it up. Her hands, thank God, behaved themselves.

He read it slowly. Too slowly. The paper trembled very slightly at its edge, though whether from wind or from him she couldn't tell. *"Okonkwo,"* he said, and the name came out carefully, each syllable placed like a stone in a wall. "Orchard road."

"My husband's land." Keep it simple. Grief is a shield; use it. "First shipment since he passed. Apples keep poorly, but there's a buyer in Millbrook pays fair for cooking stock."

"Aye." He folded the manifest and stamped it against his knee with the ferry office seal—a practiced motion, a legal motion, a motion that made her cargo official the moment the ink bit. "Three crates?"

"Three."

He came down off the cable platform and lifted the first crate as though it weighed nothing, turning it in the grey light. His eyes went to the corner of it—to the burnt brand there, the little mark Daniel had cut into a potato and pressed into every crate the orchard ever shipped, a circle with a line through it, meaning *whole, sound, true.* Tobiah had grown up seeing that mark on Marta's table. Any child of the coast road would know it.

Any child of the coast road would know *her*, too.

He set the first crate aboard and reached for the second, and Tamsin watched him arrive at the recognition in real time. It happened in his face the way weather happens—nothing you could point to, and then all at once the whole sky had changed. His jaw tightened. Something moved behind his eyes, quick and hunted, and then was gone, shuttered behind the blank competence of a workingman with a job to do.

But his hands paused on the second crate. Just a heartbeat. Just long enough.

"Straw's from my mother's barn," Tamsin said quietly, before he could decide anything. It was not a plea. She had promised herself it would not be a plea. "Packing straw. Nothing more."

"No," Tobiah agreed, too loudly, and the clerk glanced over. He lowered his voice to a rumble barely above the river. "Nothing but packing straw. My mother sells it to half the coast." He heaved the second crate onto the deck and straightened, and now his eyes found hers and held them, and what was in them was not simple. There was fear in it—she recognized fear, she lived beside it—but there was something else, something harder and sadder: the look of a man measuring exactly how much trouble he was in, and finding it deep, and deciding to stand in it anyway.

Because of his mother. Because of seven years of her bread on his family's table. Because Daniel Okonkwo had once pulled him, half-drowned and blue-lipped, out of the shallows below the orchard when he was nine years old and greedy for the first apples of the season.

*Tobiah,* she wanted to say. *Do you know what's in these crates? Do you understand what you're touching?* But the answer was that it didn't matter. That was the terrible elegance of it. Whether he understood or merely suspected, he was already past the line, and both of them knew it, and neither of them could say so aloud with a clerk six feet away and a badge on his coat that answered to Corrigan.

He took the third crate—the one with the ledger sleeping under its false bottom, under its bed of sour copper apples—and lifted it, and for one endless moment he simply held it, weighing it, his thumbs pressing into the slats as though he could read the contents through the wood.

"Good heavy crop," he said, to the clerk, to the river, to no one. "For a bad year."

"Widow's luck," Tamsin said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, that they were both speaking in the same coded tongue, the language of the coast where everything meant something else: *good heavy crop* meaning *I know this is heavier than apples,* and *widow's luck* meaning *you know why I'm doing it.*

He stowed the crates amidships under the tarpaulin, weighted the corners, and turned back to her with his official face on. "Fare's tuppence. Crossing takes a quarter hour, no more—I'll not linger, the ice is moving in the channel." Then, lower, as he took her coin: "Sit by the rail, missus, port side. Starboard rail ices over. People slip."

People slip. Or people don't get looked at closely on the port side. She nodded and paid her tuppence and boarded.

* * *

The drover snored against the far gunwale. The clerk buried himself in his satchel. The river took the barge out on the ebb of the current, the cable humming overhead like a struck string, and the landing shrank behind them into a smear of frost-grey wood and smoke.

Halfway across, the ice spoke. A long report rolled up the valley from upstream, deep as cannon fire—the sound of a floe the size of a field giving way somewhere in the dark upper reaches. The drover woke, muttered, slept again. The clerk gripped the rail. And Tobiah, working the windlass with steady strokes, began very softly to sing—an old hauling song, wordless, just a low rising note and a fall, the kind of thing ferrymen sang to keep their stroke even and their minds off the water.

She understood. He was covering the creak of the deck, covering their silence, giving the crossing the texture of ordinary labor. Marta's son, protecting them both with a song.

Near the far bank he brought the barge in gentle as a hand closing, and the bow kissed the ice-furred pilings, and the clerk jumped off to secure the line. Tobiah came to unload. He handed her the crates down one by one, and when he passed the third one—the ledger crate—he held it a moment longer than the others.

Then, deliberately, where the clerk could see or not see, it hardly mattered, he reached under the tarpaulin's edge, took one apple from the top of the third crate, and put it in his coat pocket.

He looked at her while he did it. Level. Unsmiling. And Tamsin felt the meaning of it settle over her colder than the river wind: *I could have stopped you. I am the law at this crossing, and I let you pass, and I will hold this apple as the proof of what I chose not to do.* Mercy, yes—but mercy that had taken a hostage. Mercy with teeth. If Corrigan's men ever came to Tobiah with iron and questions, that apple was his confession and his shield both: *here is how I failed, here is the proof I was complicit,* or else—God forbid, and she prayed it otherwise—*here is how I caught her, here is my reward owed.*

She would never know which. That was the price. He was keeping the ambiguity the way he kept the apple, in his pocket, against his heart.

"Mind the road, missus," Tobiah said, loud enough for the record. "Ice past the bend. Walk wide of the garrison wall—they throw their washwater out at this hour and it freezes where it lands."

"Thank you," Tamsin said. "You've been—" She stopped. Almost said *kind.* Couldn't, with the badge shining on his coat.

"Tobiah," she said instead, very low. "Tell your mother the orchard thanks her for the straw."

His face did something complicated and quickly hidden. "I'll tell her," he said. "She'll want to know the apples went to good use."

She took the hand truck waiting at the landing, stacked her three crates, and walked up the ice-rimed ramp without looking back. Behind her the cable hummed, the windlass turned, and Tobiah's low hauling song resumed—steady, even, unbroken—as though nothing had crossed his river today but apples and grief.

* * *

The customs road climbed away from the water through bare birch scrub, and Tamsin walked it with the crates rattling on the hand truck and her blood beating strange and slow in her ears. Ahead, around the bend, she could hear the garrison before she saw it: boots on frozen mud, the jingle of tack, a voice calling numbers. The second checkpoint. Sergeant Hale's ground, if the letters were right—Daniel's old comrade, the man whose life her husband had pulled from a burning sloop in '86, now wearing the uniform of the magistrate who had destroyed him.

She rounded the bend and saw the barrier, the sentry box, the wood smoke standing straight up in the still air, and a sergeant in a greatcoat stepping out to meet her with his hand already raised.

And Tamsin Okonkwo, widow and keeper and liar, tightened her grip on the handles and prepared to be nobody at all—while eight miles behind her, at a lighthouse on the storm coast, a door was opening for a visitor she did not yet know had come: a carriage flying the magistrate's colors, rolling patient and polite up the cliff road, bringing Corrigan himself to her empty house, to her banked stove, to the sea chest she had not thought to hide.
