## 9. Corrigan Comes Calling

The carriage could not make the last mile of the coast road, so Corrigan walked it, and he was glad of it. A man learned more about a place from its mud than from its magistrates' bench. The ground here was black and giving, stitched with gorse that had gone over to wood-ash grey in the winter, and the wind came off the water with the smell of iron and old salt—the smell, he thought, of every promise the sea had ever made and then drowned.

He let his clerk fall behind. Brandt's man—no, not Brandt; Brandt was for the customs house—his man Pell walked twenty paces back carrying the courtesy basket as though it were ordnance. Good. Let the basket be seen. A gift arriving at a lighthouse was a story the whole coast would tell by nightfall: *the Magistrate himself climbed to Tamsin Okonkwo's door, in the wind, on foot, like any penitent.* Stories were levers. He had built thirty years of jurisprudence out of stories, most of them true, which was what made them load-bearing.

The lighthouse rose ahead of him out of the murk, white paint gone the colour of old teeth at the base where the storms scoured it, and above, the lantern room burning even in daylight, because keepers kept their habits the way other people kept their sins. He stopped on the path and looked up at it for a long moment, hands folded behind his back, letting the spray come off the rocks and touch his face.

Seven years ago he had stood at this same railing of grief—or near enough to it—at Daniel Okonkwo's inquest, and said the words *misadventure* and *regrettable* and *the sea keeps its own accounts*, and had watched the widow take each word like a coin she did not dare spend. He remembered her hands. Not her face—faces were cheap—but her hands, flat on her knees the whole session, absolutely still, stiller than grief makes people. Grief fidgets. That stillness had been something else, and he had noted it the way a merchant notes a flaw in otherwise good silver, and then, because the case was closed and the ledger was presumed at the bottom of the channel with the man who wrote it, he had let it go.

He was no longer entirely certain the ledger was at the bottom of anything.

*Pell says there was a census taker.* The thought arrived again, unbidden, the way unwelcome thoughts do when a man is climbing a hill in cold air. Elias Wren. A name that sounded invented, and Corrigan had spent three days confirming that it was—invented, or at least borrowed; the real Wren had died of lung fever in the county poorhouse eleven years past, and someone had taken his papers and his name and his little government authority and walked them up and down the coast asking questions. Questions about correspondence. About Aldous Vane.

Vane. Corrigan turned the name over with distaste, the way one turns over a stone to see what has been feeding underneath. Vane had been a nothing—a scrivener, a collector of grievances, the sort of man who believed paper was a weapon because he had never held anything heavier. And yet Vane had written letters, and letters were seeds, and Daniel Okonkwo—honest Daniel, decent Daniel, Daniel who had wept actual tears in Corrigan's chambers the first year and taken his second payment without them—Daniel had apparently been corresponding with the weed before the sea pruned him.

The sea pruned everyone eventually. That was the arrangement. Corrigan had never broken the arrangement; he had only ever been its very diligent clerk.

He reached the door and knocked—not the knock of office, two sharp raps, but something softer, three knuckles, almost apologetic. Power, properly worn, should always seem slightly embarrassed by itself. He had learned that from watching his father, a customs assessor of legendary mildness, ruin forty families without ever raising his voice or losing a Christmas invitation.

Footsteps on iron stairs inside. The door opened, and there she was.

Tamsin Okonkwo had gone thinner, and her hair had surrendered its colour early, but the eyes were unchanged, and it was the eyes he had prepared for. Sea-grey and level and patient as tide tables. She looked at him the way a keeper looks at weather: not with fear, but with an assessment already underway, and a decision about it nearly made.

"Magistrate Corrigan," she said. "This is a long walk for a social call."

"Every worthwhile call is." He inclined his head. "Mrs. Okonkwo. I've brought bread that isn't mine and wine I intend to drink half of myself, if you'll have me out of the wind."

For a moment—he counted it, he always counted things—he thought she would refuse. He watched her weigh it, and he understood exactly what she weighed: that refusing a magistrate at your door was itself a message, and messages travel; that admitting him was also a message; that the only safe answer was the one that meant nothing, and no answer meant nothing, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it, and this mutual knowing was in fact the entire conversation before the conversation.

"You'd better come in before the wind takes the basket," she said, "and you with it."

* * *

Her kitchen was clean the way only lonely rooms are clean, every surface scrubbed to a shine that was less housekeeping than argument—*I am not neglected; I am precise*. She set out two cups without being asked, which pleased him, and poured tea that was strong enough to stand a spoon in, which warned him. This was not a woman who would be charmed. Very well. Charm was for juries.

"I'll say plainly," she began, "that I'm surprised. We've spoken perhaps four times in seven years. Inquests don't breed intimacy."

"Inquests breed acquaintance," Corrigan said, "which lasts longer." He unwrapped the bread himself, an informality calculated to unsettle, and tore a piece. "I've been reviewing the coastal appointments. Your light is the best-kept on this stretch—I checked the shipping reports. Not one grounded hull in your watch since the Marrow incident, and the Marrow was the previous keeper's doing, God rest him. Seven years without a wreck. Do you know how rare that is?"

"It's the job."

"It's more than the job. It's character." He let that sit, then leaned back. "I've argued before the Assembly that keepers of your record deserve recognition. A service pension. There's a fund—discretionary, unfortunately, which means it requires my recommendation, which means most keepers never hear of it. But I hear of everything on this coast."

She turned her cup once on its saucer. Half a turn. He watched the small motion the way a card player watches a thumb.

"That's generous," she said.

"It's overdue."

"And it comes now, in the middle of winter, seven years late, because—?"

"Because I am growing old, Mrs. Okonkwo, and old men begin settling accounts while they can still remember them." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached his eyes, because eyes were where people checked for forgery. "And because the coast is changing. New men in the capital, new inquiries, commissions—they love a commission, the capital. They'll come poking into old files looking for sins, and they won't find mine, but they will find paperwork, and paperwork frightens people who don't understand it. I'd rather the honest folk of this coast were settled comfortably before the locusts arrive."

"Honest folk," she repeated, without inflection, and he admired that—she gave him nothing to grip, neither agreement nor offense. Most people, offered the word *honest*, either claimed it or blushed. She simply set it down between them like a stone she wasn't picking up.

He changed tack, gently, the way one changes horses mid-stream.

"I knew your husband," he said. "Better than the inquest allowed me to show. Daniel was a man of—considerable conscience. It made him difficult. Conscience usually does." He paused, and let his gaze drift to the window, to the grey violence of the water, and when he spoke again he lowered his voice, and this too was calculated, but calculation done well is indistinguishable from sincerity, which is why he had never needed to lie often. "I liked him. I want you to know that. Whatever else gets said, whenever it gets said."

"Whatever else gets said," she echoed softly. "By whom?"

"Commissions," he said. "Inquiry men. The census taker who isn't a census taker."

There it was. He watched it land—not a flinch, she was far too good for a flinch, but a stillness, the same terrible stillness from the inquest, her hands going quiet on the table, and he felt the old satisfaction rise in him like warmth from a stove: *there you are.*

"The census taker," she said. "Mr. Wren. He corrected our household count in October. A thorough young man."

"He asked you about letters."

"He asked whether I'd ever corresponded with anyone outside the parish. Standard practice, he said. Names go in the margins of the district roll." Her voice was perfectly smooth. It was, in fact, so perfectly smooth that it confirmed everything, because truth has texture and this had been sanded.

Corrigan nodded slowly, and sighed, and performed—for it was a performance, and a good one—the weariness of a man burdened by what he knows. "Mrs. Okonkwo, I'm going to speak to you as one keeper speaks to another. You keep a light. I keep a court. Both of us are in the business of warning people off rocks." He turned his cup, mirroring her earlier gesture deliberately, a small theft of intimacy. "Daniel, in his last months, was gathering papers. Statements. Accounts. He believed—sincerely, I think—that certain arrangements along this river were corrupt, and that exposing them was his duty. He showed me some of it. I told him he was playing with a tide he didn't understand." His voice softened further. "A month later the sea took him. I ruled it misadventure because it was misadventure. I have wondered for seven years whether I owed his memory more than a ruling. Whether I owed you the truth: that the man was in danger, from parties I could not then name, and that his death may not have been the sea's work at all."

It was a masterpiece and he knew it. It confessed to nothing—it confessed to *compassion*. It positioned him as the reluctant custodian of a dangerous secret rather than its beneficiary. And it planted, in the mind of a woman who might be sitting on those very papers, the image of what happened to men who kept them: drowning. It said *they are coming, and I am the only shelter*, without ever saying it.

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind found a loose shutter somewhere below and worked at it, patiently, like a prisoner with a spoon.

"You're telling me my husband may have been murdered," she said, "seven years after ruling that he wasn't."

"I'm telling you that rulings are made from evidence, and evidence is not the same as truth. I was wrong to close it so quickly. Or—" he spread his hands, the picture of humility— "perhaps I was right, and the inquiry men will decide I was wrong, and dig, and make everyone miserable. Including widows. Especially widows."

"And the pension."

"Is separate." He met her eyes. "Or adjacent. I confess I'm offering both at once, because I'm an old man and I haven't time for subtlety, whatever my reputation says. Here is the whole of it, plainly: there may exist papers bearing names. If such papers reach the wrong hands, the coast burns—ferry licenses revoked, garrison postings shuffled, customs men arrested, families ruined on the strength of a dead idealist's arithmetic. If they reach *my* hands, I can read them honestly, bury what deserves burial, and ensure that the innocent—and there are innocents, Mrs. Okonkwo, there are always innocents, Marta Kessler's boy Tobiah among them—are protected. And the woman who helped me protect them retires with a pension and the gratitude of a magistrate who does not forget."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the great lamp turned through its slow arc, sweeping its blade of light across the ceiling, across their faces, away again, like a search that kept finding nothing.

"You're asking me to bring you papers," she said, "that I've told you I don't have."

"I'm asking you to consider," he said, "what a wise woman does with a box of matches in a wooden house during a drought." He rose, took his hat from the peg, and paused at the threshold with the door open to the wind, letting the cold pour in around him like punctuation. "I'll leave the basket. The wine's better than it looks. And Mrs.—" he stopped, corrected himself with visible tenderness— "Tamsin. Daniel trusted the wrong instinct at the end. He thought truth was a weapon you could hand to strangers and expect them to aim it well. Truth is a flood. It takes the guilty and the innocent downstream together, and afterwards everyone stands in the mud saying *but we only wanted justice*. I have watched that flood. I have pulled bodies out of it." 

He put on his hat.

"Think about who drowns," he said, "before you open any sluice gates. That's all an old man asks."

* * *

The walk back down the path was worse than the walk up, wind full in his teeth now, and Pell materialized beside him with the umbrella that was useless against horizontal rain, and Corrigan waved it off and walked on, breathing hard, his chest complaining in the way chests complain after fifty-eight years.

"Well?" Pell shouted, when they gained the carriage.

"She heard me."

"Did she give anything?"

Corrigan hauled himself up onto the seat and sat a moment, getting his breath, watching the lighthouse shrink behind them into the murk, its beam swinging out over the water with that idiotic, magnificent regularity—*here I am, here I am, here I am*—as if honesty were a thing you could achieve by repetition.

"No," he said. "That's what troubles me."

"Troubles you, sir? Surely silence is—"

"Silence is guilt or caution, Pell, and I couldn't tell which, and I can usually tell which." He drew his coat tighter as the carriage lurched onto the road. "Do you know what she did the entire time I spoke? She watched my hands. Not my face—everyone watches faces, faces lie professionally. My hands. Like a woman reading a ledger." He laughed shortly, without humour. "Seven years keeping a light alone on this rock, and she's become a better judge of men than half my bench."

"Then she suspects something. Wren—"

"Wren is a symptom. Wren I can buy, break, or drown; I've handled a hundred Wrens." He was silent for a mile, wheels grinding, rain needling the oilskin, and when he spoke again it was mostly to himself, which was the only conversation he fully trusted. "It's the pension I'm unsure of. I watched her when I named it. She didn't look tempted. She looked—" he searched for the word, and disliked the one he found— "*sad*. As if I'd confirmed something she'd hoped was otherwise. As if she'd already decided, and my visit was merely the weather report."

"Then we move on the ferry road," Pell said. "Tobiah Kessler owes us enough. Tighten the checkpoints, stop every crate—"

"Tobiah stays exactly where he is and exactly as comfortable," Corrigan snapped. "You don't tighten a net when the fish has already read the water. You wait. You make the river seem safe." He closed his eyes against the grey. "No. We do nothing loud. We put a man on the orchard path, quietly, and another at the customs gate with instructions to be stupid. And we wait for winter to do what winter does."

"Which is?"

Corrigan looked out at the channel, black and heaving under the running cloud, at the white water standing off the shoals where the current fought the ebb, and felt the old, cold patience settle over him like a familiar coat.

"Ice breaks in March," he said. "Everything that wants to cross, crosses before it does. So we watch the crossings. And when a widow with a light and a grudge tries to move a dead man's paper down that river—" he turned his collar up against the rain— "we will be the most reasonable men on the bank."

Behind them, on its rock, the lighthouse swept the dark: *here I am, here I am, here I am*—and Corrigan, who had spent his whole life making sure that no light ever fell where it truly mattered, began composing in his head the courteous letter he would write her within the week, thanking her for her hospitality, enclosing the pension application forms, and reminding her—as a friend, purely as a friend—that the spring assizes convened the first week of April, and that witnesses, unlike ice, could be compelled.
