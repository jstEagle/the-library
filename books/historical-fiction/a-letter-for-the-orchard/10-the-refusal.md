## 10. The Refusal

The magistrate's carriage had not been gone ten minutes before the smell of him remained.

It was a particular smell, Tamsin thought as she stood in her own doorway listening to the wheels grind away down the shingle road—the smell of a man who traveled with a foot-warmer and a decanter and an opinion of himself polished smooth as whalebone. Bay rum and wet wool and something underneath it, faint but unmistakable, like the air from a cellar where things have been kept too long in the dark. She breathed it out of her house deliberately, the way one blows on a spoonful of soup, and then she shut the door and turned the key, though the lock was a courtesy at best against what Corrigan could send if he chose to send it.

The storm was rising again. She could feel it before she heard it, that pressure behind the ears like a held breath, the gulls gone silent and strange on their rocks. By evening it would be on them. Corrigan had timed his visit well—courtesy calls were best made when the roads would soon be impassable, when the woman visited would have no easy way to carry her grievances anywhere. He had sat in her kitchen with his gloved hands folded and praised her seven years of faithful service to the light, and he had spoken of Daniel with a warmth that made her skin crawl, *such a conscientious man, such a pity about the weather*, and he had mentioned—casually, the way a man mentions the price of flour—that he understood keepers sometimes came across old papers, old logbooks, things washed up or left behind, and that the Crown paid handsomely for the orderly disposal of such clutter.

A pension, he had called it. A pension for a widow's quiet memory.

She had poured him tea and smiled and said she kept no papers but tide tables, and he had believed her, or pretended to believe her, which amounted to the same courtesy between liars, and now the carriage was gone and the lie was sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Tamsin lit the stove and put water on for coffee she did not want, because her hands needed occupation or they would begin to shake, and she was not ready to let them shake yet.

* * *

The ledger lay where she had hidden it after the orchard—in the false bottom of the flour bin, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a fish. She had told Elias it was safe. She had told herself it was safe. But safe was a word that belonged to other lives, to women whose husbands drowned honest or died honest or simply went away honest, leaving nothing behind them but a coat and a debt and a grave marker with decent words on it.

She set it on the table and did not touch it for a long moment. The wind had begun now, low and steady off the northeast, combing through the grass on the headland with a sound like someone dragging a rake through gravel. Above her, in the lamp room, the lens turned its slow patient circles. The light went out over the water and came back around, went out and came back, faithful as breathing. Seven years she had kept it. Seven years of winding the clockwork and trimming the wicks and wiping salt from the glass, and she had told herself all that time that she was keeping the light for the fishing boats, for whatever poor hull might be out there in the black.

But standing here now with Corrigan's cellar-smell still in the room, she wondered if she had been keeping it for something else—for herself, a small bright proof that she could hold one thing steady when everything else in her life had gone under.

The ledger's binding was cracked leather, swollen with damp years in the sea chest before she'd moved it. Daniel's initials tooled into the cover, faded almost to nothing. D.O. She touched the letters with one finger, the way she might have touched a scar.

*You were a bribe-taker,* she said to him silently. *You took their money and wrote it down.*

That was the part that would not sit still inside her. Not the bribes themselves—she had grown up in port towns; she knew how grease moved through official channels the way bilge moved through a hull, and a man could take a little grease and still keep his keel true. No. It was that he had written it down. That some night in this same kitchen, or in the lamp room, or out in the orchard under the graft tree with the apples dropping around him like slow red rain, he had opened this book and recorded, in his careful surveyor's hand, every payment, every favor, every name—and kept recording even after the payments stopped coming. Kept the record of his own rot alongside theirs.

Why? she had asked the pages a dozen times since the orchard. Why does a man write down his own shame?

And the only answer the pages gave her was the answer she did not want: *because he meant to use it.* Because somewhere along the way the grease had stopped being grease and started being evidence, and Daniel Okonkwo had decided—too late, always too late—to turn witness. And look what witness-bearing had bought him. A ruling of accidental drowning. A closed casket she had never been allowed to see open. Seven years of widowhood spent keeping her eyes down while the man who signed the drowning verdict rode past her window in carriages that smelled of cellars.

The kettle began its thin whistle. She took it off without pouring anything.

* * *

Outside, the first real gusts arrived, slamming against the north wall of the tower hard enough to make the lamp room glass hum. She climbed the spiral stairs more from habit than decision, carrying the ledger against her ribs like a child that would not stop crying.

In the lamp room the world narrowed to glass and turning light and the roar of the building sea. She could see the white lines of breakers out past the point, and beyond them the darkness where the water went on being water whether anyone watched it or not. The clockwork ticked beneath the lens platform, steady, indifferent.

There was a small iron stove in the corner of the lamp room, for the cold nights. It stood there now, cold itself, a curl of last winter's ash in its belly.

Tamsin looked at the stove. Then at the ledger.

The thought came to her the way storms come—not gradually, not with warning flags, but all at once, whole, as if it had been waiting offshore for days. *Burn it.* Open the little door, crumple the pages, feed them in. The bribes, the names, the drownings, Daniel's careful hand recording his own corruption page after page—all of it gone in ten minutes of orange light, and no one the wiser. Corrigan's pension would come. The spring court would convene and hear nothing. The coast would settle back into its long amnesia, and she would grow old in this tower with her clean grief intact: a good husband, unjustly destroyed, remembered whole.

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. That was what the fire offered. Not safety—she understood, staring into the cold iron mouth, that burning the ledger would not buy her safety; Corrigan would simply stop needing to be careful with her, and careless men with power were more dangerous than watchful ones. What the fire offered was *Daniel*. It offered her the husband she had buried. The man who carried her across the flooded creek at the orchard the first autumn they were married, laughing at his own clumsiness. The man who taught her the names of the stars the keepers used and got half of them wrong and refused correction with magnificent dignity. The man whose voice she could barely remember anymore, whose face lived now mostly in the mirror and in dreams that dissolved by breakfast.

If she burned the book, he stayed that man forever.

If she let it stand—if she handed those pages to Elias Wren and let them be read aloud in a courtroom—then everyone would know what she had learned in the orchard with her knees in the frozen mud: that Daniel Okonkwo had taken money from smugglers and slavers' agents and men who moved people like cargo, had taken it for two years, had drunk with them and dined with them and called some of them friend. That her husband, her drowned saint, had been a small corrupt official of exactly the kind whose corruption fed the larger kind, the kind that wore robes.

Loyalty said burn it. Loyalty said a wife owes the dead her silence; the ground has enough weight on it already.

But—

* * *

She went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, the ledger still against her chest. In the kitchen she built the lamp up properly, trimmed the second wick, set the room glowing gold, and then she took down the trial records.

Not the trial—there had been no trial, that was the obscenity of it. The *inquiry*. Three days in February, six years past, held in the customs hall with Corrigan presiding in his best coat, and she had sat in the third row with Marta's arm around her shoulders and heard the whole machinery of it click smoothly past her husband's body like a train through a station. Accidental drowning. Rough weather. No witnesses. Verdict entered, file closed, condolences issued.

Elias had gotten her copies of the inquiry papers months ago, when they were still circling each other like cats. She had read them once, wept once, and put them in the drawer beneath the spare candles, and had not opened them since. Some reading is like drinking seawater—one time tells you everything you need to know.

But tonight she took them out, and the wind threw itself against the house, and she read them again.

Mostly it was what she remembered: the ferryman's statement, garbled; the garrison sergeant's statement, careful to the point of uselessness; the customs clerk's neat little paragraph about Mr. Okonkwo seen walking the river path at dusk in deteriorating conditions. And then the appendix, the depositions taken as background, the general character of trade on the river, and there—a name she had skimmed past the first time, because grief makes narrow readers of us all.

*Aldous Vane, boatman, aged seventeen.*

Seventeen. A boy, then. Not a man. Elias had said the name to her in her own kitchen during the first storm, asking his question that wasn't on any census form, and she had lied to him smoothly and hated herself for the smoothness, and all this time she had imagined Vane as some rival officer, some colleague of Daniel's fallen out over shares. She had built him a middle-aged face in her mind and given him a middle-aged treachery.

But the deposition was there, dated eleven weeks before the drowning. Aldous Vane, boatman, seventeen years of age, engaged to carry certain goods upriver for certain parties, stating for the record—he had wanted it *for the record*, the fool boy, the brave stupid loyal fool—stating that he had ferried crates for Magistrate Corrigan's agents on four occasions, and that on the fourth occasion the crates had not held brandy as represented, and that he wished to state further that one Mr. Daniel Okonkwo, keeper of records for the river traffic, had warned him to say nothing, and had promised him—

Tamsin stopped reading. Her hand was flat on the page as though she could hold the words down, pin them like moths.

Had promised him protection. Had promised the boy protection, and then—

The next line was in the coroner's later note, appended after the inquiry, a single sentence in smaller script: *Deposition of A. Vane subsequently withdrawn; subject unavailable for further statement.*

Unavailable. Subject unavailable.

They had written *unavailable* about a boy of seventeen, and three months later they had written *accidental drowning* about her husband, and both times the same hand had held the pen, and she knew whose hand because she had watched it sign the condolence letter, *yours in shared grief, R. Corrigan, Magistrate*, the ink still wet enough to smear.

She sat very still. The wind found some gap high in the tower and played it like a flute, a thin rising note that seemed to go on and on.

Here was the arithmetic, then. Here was the sum she had been avoiding since the orchard. If she burned the ledger, she burned it for herself—but she also burned it for Corrigan, and for the men whose signatures marched down those pages, and most of all for the memory of a boy named Aldous Vane who had done the single bravest thing available to a person of his smallness, which was to speak the truth onto paper, and who had been made *unavailable* for it. Burn the ledger and the boy's testimony stayed burned. Burn the ledger and Daniel's record of his own guilt—which was also, God help her, the only surviving record of theirs—died with him, and the drownings stayed accidents, all of them, forever, tidy as filed receipts.

Her loyalty to Daniel asked her: *what did the boy matter to you? You never knew him.*

And some colder, truer voice in her answered: *he mattered the way all of them matter, the ones who go into the water and stay there while respectable men rule it accidental. He matters because someone has to count him. Someone has to say his name in daylight with the file open.*

She thought of the lamp above her, sweeping its circles out over that same dark water. Every night for seven years she had thrown light onto the sea so that ships would not die on rocks they could not see. That was the whole of a keeper's covenant: you do not get to choose which ships deserve saving. You light the water. All of it. Even the water that has already taken what you love.

Could she do less with paper than she did with flame?

* * *

The near thing happened then, and afterward she would remember it with a kind of clinical wonder, the way one remembers the moment a horse shies at nothing on a dark road.

She was on her feet. She had the ledger by the spine. She had crossed to the stove and opened the damper and was reaching for the matches on the shelf above—and her hand actually closed on the matchbox, felt its small cardboard weight, familiar as a psalm—and in the instant before she struck one, the wind shifted, as coastal winds do, swinging round from northeast to east with a groan like a ship changing its mind, and through the sudden lull she heard the fog bell on the point buoy, far off, tolling its dull bronze syllable into the dark.

One stroke. Then silence. Then another.

Counting the seconds between them, the way Daniel had taught her, the way every keeper's wife learns: four seconds, five, the buoy a mile out in the rising sea, ringing to warn boats off the shoals it could not see and did not understand, ringing because that was what it was *for*, ringing whether anyone heeded it or not, ringing for ships that deserved wreck and ships that didn't alike, ringing into weather that would swallow the sound a hundred yards from its source and ring anyway—

She put the matchbox down.

She stood with her hand still hovering over the cold stove, and the fog bell tolled, and tolled, and she began, without deciding to, to cry—not the way she had cried at the funeral, which had been a performance grief demanded of her, but the way she had not cried in years, ugly and silent and doubled over the iron stove with the ledger hugged to her like the last warm thing in the house. Because the choice had just passed through her hands and out the other side, and she understood that she had never really been choosing between loyalty and duty. She had been choosing between two kinds of love: the love that keeps a beautiful likeness of the dead, and the love that lets the dead be fully, terribly human so that the living can be saved. And she had married a man who kept lights, and she had become a woman who kept lights, and lights are not sentimental. Lights show the reef and the safe channel in the same sweep. They do not flatter the water.

When the crying spent itself, she straightened, wiped her face on her sleeve the way she did after gutting fish, and closed the stove's damper with a click that sounded, in the quiet between gusts, like a door shutting on a whole country.

"Not tonight," she said aloud to Daniel, to the ledger, to the empty kitchen. "I'm sorry. Not tonight."

Then she did the thing she should have done hours ago, the thing her hands had been begging to do since the carriage left: she climbed back up the tower stairs, and in the lamp room she wound the clockwork that was due its winding, and she took up the polishing cloth and cleaned the lower panes where the salt spray had hazed the glass, working with the economy of long habit while the storm built itself into a full fury outside. And when the great lens swung past its focus, throwing its beam down the coast toward the river road—toward the ferry, the garrison, the customs house, all the checkpoints where her friends waited like pieces on a board—she watched it go, and she said to the glass, quietly:

"Light it all. Show them everything."

Downstairs again, she did not put the ledger back in the flour bin. Instead she wrapped it fresh in oilcloth, tied it with tarred twine, and set it on the table beside the inquiry papers, together, bound for the same future. Then she took out paper and ink and wrote by lamplight while the storm leaned on the house:

*Elias Wren. Come at first fair weather. I have read it all, and I will testify to all of it—his part and mine and theirs. Bring your questions. I find I am done being careful.*

She sealed it and set it upright against the salt dish, ready for the first soul heading down-coast.

Above her, the lamp turned. Out on the point, the fog bell counted the dark. And far down the coast, though she could not know it yet, ice was beginning to move on the river—groaning, splitting, shouldering downstream toward the courthouse landing—carrying the season, and everything it meant, toward spring.
