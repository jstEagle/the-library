## 12. Tobiah's Dilemma

The ice on the river had begun to talk in its sleep.

Tobiah Ferren heard it first thing every morning now, standing on the ferry landing with his hands jammed in his armpits and his breath smoking out of him like a small stubborn ghost. The sound came up through the pilings, through the soles of his boots, into the bones of his ankles—a long groaning creak, as if the river were a great door being forced open by something patient on the other side. His mother said the ice always spoke before it broke. She said it the way she said most true things: quietly, while kneading bread, not looking at you, so that you could pretend afterward you hadn't heard.

He had been pretending he hadn't heard a great many things lately.

The morning was grey as dishwater, the kind of light that made the far bank seem further away than it was, and Tobiah was mending the frayed eyelet of the ferry rope when the clerk's horse came down the river road at a trot that meant business without quite admitting to it. Mr. Quill—though that wasn't his name, everyone knew it wasn't his name, but no one had ever been brave enough or foolish enough to ask what was—rode a dun mare with a coat like wet sand, and he sat her the way he did everything: precisely, correctly, and with an air of finding the whole arrangement slightly beneath him.

Tobiah's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

There was only one reason a magistrate's clerk rode out to a ferry crossing in February, and Tobiah had spent four nights lying awake under his blankets rehearsing for it anyway, which turned out to be worth exactly nothing, because rehearsal is a poor armor against fear. He watched the rider approach and thought, absurdly, of the apple. Of the single apple he had taken from the widow's crate—the one with the graft scar running down its flank like a healed wound—and how he had set it on the shelf above his cot, meaning to eat it, meaning instead to keep it, meaning God knew what, and had done neither. It sat there still, going soft at the crown, smelling faintly of orchard in a room that smelled otherwise of rope tar and river damp.

"Master Ferren." Quill dismounted without hurry, though his eyes were anything but unhurried. They went over the landing, the rope, the flat-bottomed barge riding its moorings, the ledger chained to its post. "Cold work."

"It's February," Tobiah said, and immediately hated himself for it, because it sounded like a boy's answer, defensive and thin.

"It is indeed." The clerk smiled. He had a smile like a well-kept account book: everything in its proper column, nothing given away. He unwound his scarf with two fingers, precise as a man defusing something. "I wonder if I might trouble you for a few minutes by your stove. Magistrate Corrigan takes an interest in the crossings this season. Traffic, manifests, the usual housekeeping of commerce. You know how he is about order."

Everyone knew how Corrigan was about order. Order was the word he used the way other men used *please*.

"Stove's lit," Tobiah said. "Ain't much else."

* * *

The ferry house was one room and a loft, and it had never felt small until it held two people and a silence. Tobiah fed the stove while Quill removed his gloves finger by finger and held his palms toward the fire as though inspecting them for damage. On the shelf above the cot, the apple sat where it had sat for six days. Tobiah did not look at it. Looking at it, he was certain, would be the same as confessing to it.

"You've held this post two years," Quill said. It was not a question, so Tobiah did not answer it. "Your mother holds the bakery lease on Church Lane. Your father—" a small pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—your father's debts were settled by the Magistrate's Fund after the mill failed. Generous arrangement. The interest alone would have eaten that family alive."

"I know what it cost," Tobiah said. "Sir."

"Oh, I'm sure you do." Quill turned from the fire then, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without warmth, the eyes of a man who had learned that patience does more work than cruelty and costs less. "So let us not waste each other's time with the theater of surprise. Six days ago a woman crossed here with fruit crates bound downstream. A widow. Grey coat, black scarf, hands that knew rope. You logged her as *Mrs. Okonkwo, household goods, one crossing.*"

The name landed in the room like a dropped anchor.

Tobiah had known it would come—he had known since the moment he'd written it, since the moment before that, since the moment the crate boards had come apart in his hands and he'd seen the stenciled mark underneath the paint, the little lighthouse-and-star that Tamsin Okonkwo had burned into her crates since before he was tall enough to see over the gunwale. He had known, and he had let her pass anyway, and he had taken the apple, and all of it had seemed at the time like a small thing, a mercy the size of a fist, easily hidden.

Now the room was very warm and very small and the clerk was watching him the way a cat watches a wall where it knows the mouse lives.

"A widow shipping apples," Tobiah said. "That's all it was."

"That's all what was?" Quill asked pleasantly.

"That's all she was."

"Mm." The clerk drew off one glove again, though he'd already removed both, and Tobiah understood it was a performance, a piece of theater meant to make him watch the hands, and he watched the hands anyway because he couldn't help it. "And yet the Magistrate wonders why a lighthouse keeper's widow—no orchard, no press, no buyers on record—would be shipping forty pounds of winter apples downstream in the dead season, when apples fetch nothing and spoil fast and the roads are watched. He wonders it aloud, Master Ferren. In chambers. Where wondering has consequences."

"She had trees," Tobiah said. "Old ones. Up past her land."

"Did she." Quill let it sit there, unwatered, unpicked. Then, gently, terribly: "You opened the crates. Ferry regulations require inspection of all freight. Did you inspect?"

The stove ticked. The ice groaned out on the river, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.

"I looked," Tobiah said.

"And?"

"And apples."

Quill smiled again, wider this time, and shook his head slowly, the way a man shakes his head at a child who has lied about a broken cup with the pieces still in his pocket. "Master Ferren. I am going to tell you something, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide what kind of morning this is going to be. Yesterday, a man named Brandt at the customs house seized a shipment off a courier sledge. Apples, as it happens. Forty pounds, give or take. And the courier—a hired man, no loyalty in him, just hunger—is currently explaining to Sergeant Hale's opposite number exactly whose crates those were, and whose idea the journey was, and who held the ferry for her at the first crossing." He paused. "You are the only person between here and the marshes who hasn't yet been asked directly. Consider that a courtesy. Consider also that courtesies expire."

Tobiah's mouth had gone dry as August. He thought of his mother's kitchen, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, the way she'd passed him the salt that evening and held his wrist a half-second too long—*bread and salt, my boy, remember who feeds you and who doesn't*—and he thought of his father, who had once been a big laughing man with flour to his elbows and was now a quiet one who didn't meet the eyes of magistrates' clerks, and he thought: *they have us by the throat, they've had us by the throat for nine years, and this man is telling me the price of the throat.*

"The manifest says household goods," Tobiah said. "I log what the manifest says. That's the regulation."

"The regulation," Quill agreed. "And did the manifest say apples?"

Silence. The stove popped. Somewhere out on the water a crow called once, ragged, and gave up.

Because here was the thing—the thing Tobiah discovered in that silence, discovering it the way you discover a hole in the ice only when your boot goes through: the manifest said *household goods*, and household goods was a lie, and he had written the lie himself, in his own hand, in ink, in a book chained to a post, and the book could be read by anyone the Magistrate sent to read it. He had crossed the line six days ago with a stencil and a stamp and a steady face, and he had told himself it was small, and now the line was behind him and the snow showed his tracks plain as writing.

"No," he said. "It didn't say apples."

Quill exhaled, satisfied, and Tobiah understood with a sick lurch that he had just handed the man a confession wrapped in honesty—that truthfulness itself had become the trap, that the clerk hadn't wanted the lie, he'd wanted the admission, because an admission was a hook and hooks hold better than lies. Lies can be denied. Admissions sit in ledgers.

"There," Quill said softly. "Was that so difficult?" He rose, drawing his gloves back on, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch, and for one moment his voice lost its polish entirely and became simply tired, which frightened Tobiah more than everything before it. "The Magistrate will want your statement by Friday. Written. Signed. I'd think carefully about what a signed statement costs a man, and what failing to sign one costs him more. Your mother bakes fine bread, Master Ferren. It would be a shame if the ovens went cold over a crate of apples."

The door shut. The hoofbeats faded up the river road. And Tobiah Ferren stood alone in the ferry house with his heart going like a trapped bird and the apple on the shelf above his cot, softening, sweetening, rotting by degrees into evidence.

* * *

He did not go back inside right away. He walked out onto the landing instead, because the walls had become unbearable, and stood looking at the river with his fists on the rail.

The ice stretched bank to bank, white-grey, seamed with dark pressure ridges where the floes had rammed each other and frozen mid-collision. Downstream, maybe a mile, the current ran faster under the crust—you could see it, a long dark vein where the water moved too strong to freeze—and along that vein the ice had gone hollow, thin, treacherous. Every year it went the same way. Every year the river pretended to be a road right up until the moment it wasn't.

His mother's voice, in his head, kneading and not looking at him: *A crack is just the river deciding which side it's on.*

He thought about Friday. About the statement. About what a signed statement would do—not to the widow, he understood suddenly that the statement wasn't really about her at all; she was already caught, already named, already a matter of record—it would do to *him*. It would make him the Magistrate's. Permanently. There was no coming back across that particular ice. Men in this county had signed statements for Corrigan and spent the rest of their lives being sent for, being asked for small things, then less small things, their names kept in a drawer in chambers alongside their fathers' debts and their mothers' leases, and every one of them had the same look, sooner or later: the look of a man listening for hoofbeats.

And if he refused? If he wrote *apples, one crossing, nothing further* and signed it and meant it?

Then the ovens went cold. Then the lease got reviewed. Then his father's old debt, settled and sleeping, woke up hungry.

Or—

Tobiah looked downstream, at the dark vein of living water, and felt something shift in his chest that he would spend years afterward trying to name. It was not courage. Courage was what Sergeant Hale had, or what the widow herself must have, walking into checkpoints with a dead man's name stitched into her crates. This was smaller and harder and colder: it was the simple arithmetic of a man realizing that he was already damned by one side's ledger, and that the only question left was which side's ledger he'd rather be damned by.

He had held the ferry an extra minute for her. He had taken an apple as proof he could have stopped her—proof to whom, he'd wondered even then, and never answered. Now he knew. Proof to himself, someday, that he had seen the choice and chosen.

He went inside. He took the apple down from the shelf. It was going brown at the crown, wine-dark, nearly past eating, and he ate it anyway, standing up, all of it, down to the scarred skin, and it tasted of orchard and of October and of a decision he had made six days ago without knowing he was making it.

Then he took out the ferry log, and a fresh page, and began to write.

* * *

The code was his mother's invention, from the bad years, when letters between the bakery and the mill had needed to survive being read by other eyes. Nothing in it that any clerk could call sedition. Weather, mostly. Bread. The state of the road.

*Heavy thaw expected early,* he wrote. *Ice talking day and night. Vein below the landing gone dark and moving—crossing unsafe after Thursday. Recommend all traffic come down before the break. Mother sends her regards and asks after the grey-coated lady's health; hopes she keeps indoors till the weather settles.*

He read it three times. To any clerk's eye: a ferryman fussing about the season, a son relaying maternal pleasantries. But Marta Ferren read weather the way priests read scripture, and *the grey-coated lady* would stop her like a hand on the chest, and *keeps indoors till the weather settles* would tell her everything: *they know, they're asking, warn her, move her, Friday comes with a pen in its teeth.*

He sealed it, addressed it to the bakery on Church Lane—his own mother, the safest post office on the river—and carried it out into the grey afternoon to give it to the carrier sledge making its last run before the break. The driver, old Pell, took it without comment, slapped it under the oilskin with the rest of the mail, and cracked his whip, and the sledge slid away downstream along the river road, runners hissing, growing small against the white.

Tobiah stood on the landing and watched it go, and felt the strangest sensation, like a bone setting. Not relief—there was no relief in it, not with Friday waiting and Quill's pale eyes and the drawer full of names in chambers. But something had happened to him out there in the cold, something irreversible, the way the ice going out is irreversible: you can stand on it, you can curse it, you can pray over it, but once the vein runs dark underneath, the breaking has already begun whether you admit it or not.

He had crossed his line. He was on the other side of it now, and the snow showed his tracks plain as writing, and for the first time in his careful, careful life, Tobiah Ferren found he did not much care who read them.

Behind him, upriver, the ice groaned again—longer this time, deeper, a sound like a door giving at the hinges. He turned and looked north along the white seam of the river, toward the garrison checkpoint, toward the customs house, toward whatever was coming down that road with the thaw.

And far off, at the edge of seeing, a rider appeared on the far bank. Then another.
