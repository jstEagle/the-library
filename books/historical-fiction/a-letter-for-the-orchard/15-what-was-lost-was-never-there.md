## 15. What Was Lost Was Never There

The gulls came back before the news did.

Tamsin noticed it first from the gallery, three days after the storm had spent itself against the coast like a man weeping himself empty. The birds that had vanished inland before the blow—roosting in the orchard's bare branches, sulking in Marta's eaves—were wheeling the light again, screaming their complaints at the tower as though it owed them something. The sea had gone a strange bruised green, glassed and calm in the way of water that has finished breaking things and is not sorry.

She took it as an omen, or perhaps only as weather. After seven years she had learned there was little difference.

Elias had left on the morning of the second day, when the wind dropped enough for the river road to be passable to a determined fool. He had stood at her door with his coat still damp from three days of captivity in her kitchen, and he had looked at her with those careful eyes—the eyes of a man who counted everything twice—and said, "If I'm not back by Thursday, don't come looking."

"I wasn't going to," she'd said.

He'd almost smiled. "You were. You're a keeper. Keeping is what you do." And then he'd gone down the cliff path with his satchel under his arm, walking into the gray like a man stepping into cold water all at once rather than inching in.

That had been two days ago. Thursday was tomorrow.

So when she saw the boat—a small one, a fisherman's skiff, riding low and coming fast across the green water toward the landing below the tower—her first thought was: *he came back by sea*. Her second thought, arriving a heartbeat later and colder, was: *that is not Elias*.

* * *

The man who climbed the stairs was young, maybe twenty, with the raw-knuckled hands of a net-mender and a face that kept trying to decide whether it was frightened or merely cold. She knew him distantly—one of the Pell family from down the coast, the ones who fished the winter runs when sensible men stayed ashore. He stood in her doorway dripping and would not come further in until she pulled him inside by the sleeve, because that was what you did with half-drowned boys regardless of what they carried in their pockets.

"Mrs. Okonkwo." His voice cracked on the name. "I was told—I was told to tell you the courier talked."

The words went through her like a harpoon line, barbed at both ends. She kept her hands busy—she always kept her hands busy; Daniel used to say she could pour tea during a hurricane and not spill a drop—and set him in the chair nearest the stove and put a mug of the black tea she drank too much of into his shaking fingers.

"Slowly," she said. "From the beginning."

"The customs house." He wrapped both hands around the mug but did not drink. "Brandt's men took your crates yesterday morning. The decoy shipment, the one that went out with—" He stopped, swallowed. "With the courier. Jem Aldous. They took Jem too."

"And he talked."

"He talked." The boy's eyes came up to hers, and she saw in them the particular misery of someone delivering news they wished were theirs to soften and could not. "Mrs. Okonkwo, they opened the crates on the customs house floor. In front of witnesses. There was nothing in them but apples."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, in the lamp room, the great lens turned its slow patient circle, throwing its beam out over the emptied sea. Tamsin stood very still and let the sentence finish arriving.

"Apples," she repeated.

"Two dozen crates. Good ones, too—winter stock, late-keeping varieties." The boy almost smiled despite himself, the smile of a man watching absurdity happen to other people. "Brandt turned one over in his hands in front of everyone. Held it up to the light like a jeweler. And then he asked Jem where the ledger was, and Jem—" the boy's voice dropped, went flat with secondhand shame, "—Jem told him he didn't know. Because he didn't. Because nobody knew. And Brandt believed him, because why wouldn't he? A woman ships fruit past three checkpoints in the worst ice in ten years and forgets to include the thing she's smuggling?"

He laughed then, a short ugly sound with no humor in it, and Tamsin found that she was laughing too—or making the sound that stood in for laughing, which in that kitchen, in that hour, amounted to the same thing. It was either laughter or screaming, and screaming would frighten the boy.

"They let Jem go?" she managed.

"This morning. With a warning." The boy's face sobered. "But Mrs. Okonkwo—they know now that the ledger exists for certain. Wren's been asking after it for two years; Corrigan's people have suspected as long. But this—" he shook his head, "—this confirmed it. Something was moving downstream before the spring court. Something worth risking the ferry and the garrison and Brandt himself. And whatever it was, it isn't in the crates, which means it's still somewhere upstream. Still somewhere near you."

The word *near* settled over the room like fog.

"They'll search the lighthouse," she said. It was not a question.

"They'll search everything," the boy said. "I'd give you a day. Maybe less, if Brandt can get riders through the marsh road once the water drops."

* * *

She walked him back down to the landing herself, because the path was iced and he was young and she had buried one man who died on wet rock and did not intend to watch another. At the bottom, before he pushed off, he caught her hand—quick, rough, embarrassed—and pressed something into it. An apple. From the seized crates, passed hand to hand down the coast like contraband scripture until it reached the one person it belonged to.

"For luck," he said, not meeting her eyes. "It seemed right. It being yours."

She watched the skiff shrink against the green water until it was a fleck, then a suggestion, then nothing. Then she stood on the rocks with the apple in her palm, turning it over, feeling its cold waxy skin, and understood that she was afraid in a way she had not been since the day they told her about Daniel.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain as tide tables:

They knew the ledger existed. They knew it had not left the coast. Which meant it was within reach of Corrigan's men—within reach of a search party, a warrant, a torch applied to dry timber if warrants proved inconvenient. Everything she owned sat inside these walls: her clothes, her books, Daniel's sea chest with its false bottom scraped hollow, seven years of a life arranged carefully around an absence. If they came, they would turn it all out onto the floor, and they would find nothing, and their finding nothing would be worse than finding something—because a magistrate who searches and fails does not conclude the ledger doesn't exist. He concludes it is hidden better. He concludes the keeper knows where.

And a keeper who knows where is not a witness anymore. She is a vault. And vaults can be persuaded.

She climbed the stairs slowly, counting them without meaning to—ninety-six steps, she had climbed them ten thousand times and still her feet counted—and let herself into the lamp room as the sun went down behind the hills and the time came to light the wick.

* * *

It happened the way these things always happen: while her hands were busy.

That was the mercy of work, she thought later. The mind goes quiet and the hands go on, and sometimes what the hands remember is more than the mind ever knew. She trimmed the wick. She filled the reservoir. She polished the lower prism panels, breathing on the glass and wiping in slow circles the way Daniel had taught her—*never a dry cloth on cold glass, Tamsin, you'll scratch it, treat the lens like a woman you're courting*—and it was there, in the motion itself, in the ritual his own hands had built, that the memory surfaced.

Not the orchard. Not the graft tree where she had dug in the frozen ground with Marta's eyes possibly on her back. Not the sea chest or the false bottom or any of the hiding places she and Elias had argued over in the small hours, mapping routes and decoys and contingencies like generals who had forgotten the war might be fought entirely inside one building.

Daniel's logbook.

The old one, the first one, the one from his first year as assistant keeper, its binding cracked and salt-stained, retired to the shelf in the lamp room when the new logbooks arrived from the Board of Trade. She had dusted it every week for seven years. She had held it in her hands a hundred times. And she had never once thought to open it—not because she feared what was inside, but because it was so ordinary, so much a piece of the furniture of grief, that looking inside it had seemed as pointless as looking inside a stone.

But Daniel had re-bound it. She remembered that now—remembered it with the strange clarity of a thing remembered too late. One winter, years ago, he had sat at this very table with thread and wax and awl, sewing new stitching into the spine, complaining cheerfully that the Board's bindings fell apart if you so much as sneezed on them. She had brought him tea. She had watched him work the needle along the spine, in and out, patient as a surgeon.

And she had never wondered why a man re-bound a book that was going to be replaced anyway.

Her hands stopped on the glass.

The beam swung out over the darkening water, swept the horizon, and came around again. Ninety-six steps below, her kettle would be going cold. None of it mattered. She crossed to the shelf with a steadiness that felt borrowed from some calmer woman, and took down the logbook, and set it on the table beneath the lamp, and for a long moment simply looked at it.

It was smaller than she remembered. They usually are—the vessels of our largest secrets.

She ran her thumb along the spine. The stitching was uneven near the base, she saw now, in a way that no craftsman's work would be, in a way that only deliberate work would be: a slight thickening, a ridge beneath the leather where the thread doubled back on itself. As if something flat and folded lay sewn into the very structure of the book. As if the binding itself had been made to hold more than binding.

*Treat the lens like a woman you're courting.* How many times had he stood exactly here, trimming the wick, polishing the panels, with the record of his own ruin sewn into the spine three feet away? How many evenings had they shared this room, this silence, this slow turning light—she grieving a husband she thought she'd known, and he keeping company with a version of himself he'd locked in leather and thread?

She picked up the lamp scissors.

And then she stopped, because terror arrived all at once, whole, like a wave.

If she cut the stitches, it was real. All of it. Every choice Elias had argued for, every risk Tobiah and Hale and poor foolish Jem Aldous had already paid for, every mile of frozen road between here and the courthouse—it would all become irrevocably about what was in this binding. She could not uncut it. She could not put it back behind the ordinary face of an old logbook and go on dusting it weekly for another seven years. The moment the thread parted, she would be a smuggler in fact and not merely in intention, and Corrigan's men were coming, and the only place in the world safer than a secret no one believes in is a secret no one knows exists—and she had just spent the last hour teaching herself where it was.

They will search the lighthouse. A day, the boy had said. Maybe less.

Her hand trembled. The scissors caught the lamplight.

And then—grimly, unexpectedly, like sunlight finding a crack in a wall—came the relief. It should have shamed her and did not. Because beneath the fear, beneath the arithmetic of checkpoints and riders and warrants, was this: it was never lost. All winter she had mourned it as lost, or buried, or dug up and carried and nearly burned and argued over—and all the while it had hung above her head every night of her married life and every night of her widowhood, turning with the lens, lighting the water for strangers while holding its tongue. What was lost was never there. It had been here. It had been home the entire time, closer than the sea chest, closer than the orchard, closer than memory itself.

Daniel had trusted the light to keep it. Perhaps that was the last honest thing he did.

* * *

She cut the stitches at midnight, when the coast was darkest and the beam swept longest between landfalls.

The thread parted with small sounds, four cuts, five, and the ridge along the spine loosened like a knot giving way, and then the leather gaped just enough, and she drew out what it had held: pages. Folded twice, sewn flat, perhaps forty of them, written close in Daniel's hand—columns of figures, dates, names she recognized with a sick lurch, and interleaved among them sheets of a different paper, letters, copies of letters, and at the center, folded smallest of all, a single page whose heading she read before she could stop herself, three words in ink gone brown:

*What I have done.*

She did not read further. Not tonight. Tonight there was no time and no courage to spare, and both were needed elsewhere. She wrapped the pages in oilcloth from the stores, and then she sat with them on her knees at the table and made herself think, because thinking was the one tool no one could confiscate.

A day. Maybe less. Riders on the marsh road. A search of the lighthouse that would find nothing, because she would not be here when it happened—because the ledger could not spend another night under this roof, and neither, she understood suddenly, could she. Whatever Elias had planned, whatever remained of the plan after Brandt's ambush, it ended at the courthouse, and the ice on the river was groaning apart, and the spring court would convene whether she was ready or not.

She rose, and banked the lamp, and began packing.

At the door she paused, and looked back at the great lens turning in the dark—faithful, indifferent, keeping its long watch over water that had taken her husband and given her back nothing but questions. Seven years she had tended it out of duty, then habit, then fear. Tomorrow she would leave it, and the leaving would feel like a kind of drowning.

She took the apple from the windowsill where the boy's gift had sat, and put it in her coat pocket, and went down the ninety-six steps into the dark to wait for morning—and for whoever came with it, friend or rider, the light burning steadily above her the whole way down.
