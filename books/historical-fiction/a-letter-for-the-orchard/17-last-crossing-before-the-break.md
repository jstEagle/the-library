## 17. Last Crossing Before the Break

*Tamsin*

The river had been talking all night.

Not the ordinary talk of moving water—Tamsin had lived beside that voice for seven years and could sleep through it as easily as her own breathing—but something older and more dangerous, a groan that came up through the bedrock of the point and into the floorboards of the lighthouse, as though the whole world were a ship taking strain. The ice on the river was breaking. Not breaking apart, not yet, but deciding to. She had heard it in dreams before she heard it awake: a long, low complaint, like a giant shifting in its sleep, like timber flexing before it fails.

She dressed in the dark because there was no reason for light. The ledger rode against her chest, sewn into a canvas sling she had made from Daniel's old oilskin cap, strapped tight beneath her coat where it rose and fell with her breath like a second heart. In the lamp room above, the great lens turned its slow circle, throwing its blade of light out over the water and across the ice fields, so that every few seconds the frozen river flashed white and then went dark again, white and then dark, like something blinking in its sleep. Like something being counted.

Elias was waiting at the foot of the stairs, holding two cups of chicory he had no business making in her kitchen, steam rising off them into the cold. He looked worse than she felt, which she took as a small victory. Three days of storm had silvered his temples further and taught his face new lines.

"You slept," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"An hour." She took the cup. It was terrible. She drank it anyway. "You didn't."

"I listened to the river." He set his cup down untasted. "Tamsin, I want to say something before we go down to the bank, while there's still time to say nothing at all."

"Don't."

"It's the crossing," he said. "The ice won't hold a man's weight past the narrows—we both know it. If we can't make the ferry, if Tobiah's been replaced or watched or bought since Brandt took our courier—"

"Tobiah held a ferry against orders once already this winter."

"And Marta will have told him not to do it twice."

"Marta," Tamsin said, "will do what Marta does, which is stand exactly where she needs to be seen standing." She finished the chicory and set the cup beside his, rim to rim. "That woman has been telling me things without saying them for twenty years. Let her keep doing it."

Outside, the wind had dropped to almost nothing, which frightened her more than any gale. Storms were honest. This stillness was the held breath between one thing and another. The stars were out in their hard winter multitudes, and beneath them the river lay like a road of dirty glass, cracked in a hundred places, heaved up in plates along the middle channel where the current ran strongest. Even now, even in the starlight, she could see the plates shifting—one edge riding up over another with a sound like a door dragged slowly open, then grinding closed.

They walked down the cliff path with the lantern hooded, Tamsin leading, her boots finding the worn steps without needing to see them. At the bottom, where the shingle gave onto the ice shelf, the whole river lay open before them: a mile of broken white running downstream toward the ferry landing, toward the garrison bend beyond it, toward the courthouse landing where the spring court would convene in eleven days with or without them.

Somewhere behind, upstream, a dog began to bark. Then a second dog. Then men's voices, thin with distance but carrying anyway over the ice—the way sound carries when the world is holding still.

"They've ridden through the night," Elias said quietly. "Brandt doesn't waste riders unless he knows."

"He knows we exist. He doesn't know what we carry." Tamsin pulled the sling strap tighter. "There's a difference between hunting a widow and hunting a ledger. One makes a story. The other makes a trial."

The dogs came closer. The voices resolved into three, maybe four men, and the crunch of hooves on frozen mud along the top road, which meant they'd have to come down the same cliff path she and Elias had just used, which meant minutes, not hours.

"The ice," Elias said. "It's the only way they can't follow. A horse on that surface is a dead horse."

"A man on that surface might be too."

"Yes," Elias said. "Might be."

He did not dress it up, and she loved him a little for that—not love as anything either of them had named, but the plainer thing underneath, the respect of one survivor for another. He offered his arm for the ice and she took it, and together they stepped off the shingle and onto the river.

The first ten yards held like stone. The next thirty flexed—a slow, sickening give, like walking on the deck of a grounded boat—and beneath their boots, deep down, something boomed and settled. Tamsin kept her eyes on the far bank, on the black line of alders above the ferry landing, and put one foot after the other. The ledger beat against her chest. *Step. Step.* The lens light swept overhead once, white, and the whole river lit like a held-up mirror, and she thought absurdly: *I am the only keeper who has ever walked on her own beam.*

Behind them, on the shingle, the riders came down. Torchlight bloomed. Someone shouted—a name, her name, *Mrs. Okonkwo*, in a voice trained to command and cracking slightly on the vowels—and then another voice answered, a woman's voice, flat and carrying, the voice of a woman who had sold bread and salt to half the coast for twenty years.

"You'll want to think careful about shooting toward my landing," Marta called out, conversational as Sunday. "There's a dozen folk come down to watch the ice break, and every one of them standing right in your line. Go on, then. Explain to the assizes why you shot into a crowd of respectable witnesses."

Silence on the bank. The torches wavered.

Tamsin did not look back. Looking back was weight, and the ice charged by the ounce. But she heard it—the small sounds of a crowd that hadn't existed an hour ago assembling itself on the shore: boots on shingle, murmurs, someone laughing low and nervous, the particular rustle of neighbors arriving one by one out of the dark to stand where they could be counted. Nobody shouted slogans. Nobody made a speech. They simply stood, in their nightclothes and coats thrown over, a loose dark row of bodies between the torches and the river, and their standing said everything that needed saying.

*We are watching,* the row of bodies said. *Whatever happens on this ice happens in front of us.*

"Twenty yards to the channel crack," Elias breathed. "Then the ferry ice-road if it's still marked."

Ahead, the black seam of open water split the river bank to bank—or nearly; at the ferry landing the current slowed around the pilings and a skin of young ice had bridged part of the gap overnight, gray and thin and trembling. And tied at the landing, lantern lit at bow and stern in open defiance of every regulation, sat the ferry.

And standing on the deck of the ferry, arms crossed, watching them come, stood Tobiah.

* * *

*Tobiah*

Tobiah had been told to go home.

The order had come by rider an hour past midnight—Corrigan's clerk, pale and precise, with a paper bearing a seal: *ferry service suspended until ice conditions certified safe.* Signed, stamped, official. The clerk had read it aloud twice, as if Tobiah might have trouble with words, and Tobiah had nodded the way his mother had taught him to nod at magistrates' men: agreeable, empty, a nod that promised nothing and remembered everything.

Then the clerk had ridden off toward the garrison, and Tobiah had looked at the paper, and at the river, and at the lamp of his own ferry burning at bow and stern, and he had done the arithmetic his mother had also taught him, though she'd never said it plain.

A sealed order was a piece of paper. A widow carrying crates across a breaking river was a fact. And facts, once witnessed, could not be un-witnessed—which meant the smart move, the move the clerk intended, was for there to be no witnesses at all.

So Tobiah had lit both lamps instead of putting them out. Lamps drew people. People were witnesses. His mother had come down first, saying nothing, bringing bread as if it were an ordinary morning, standing on the landing where the torchlight from the riders couldn't miss her. And then, one by one, the others—drawn by the lamps, by the strange stillness, by whatever animal sense tells a village when history is passing through it. Old Pieter with his bad hip. The Halloran girls. The customs clerk's own cousin, which Tobiah noted with some satisfaction.

Now the widow Okonkwo and the census man were coming across the ice toward him, small and dark against the white, moving in that careful gliding way of people testing every step, and behind them the riders were milling on the south shore like wasps against a window.

Tobiah cast off the stern line.

"Against orders?" said old Pieter, beside him on the landing.

"Orders suspended the ferry," Tobiah said. "Ferry's suspended. This isn't the ferry." He patted the gunwale. "This is a boat, floating on water, which is legal at all hours."

Pieter considered this. "Your mother teach you that?"

"My mother taught me better lies than that. That one's mine."

The pair on the ice reached the seam of open water. The man went first, testing the young ice with a boathook, finding the old stakes of the winter ice-road by feel. The woman followed, and where the young ice flexed under her she did not hesitate—she stepped harder, deliberately, as if daring the river to argue. Tobiah found himself gripping the rail. He had ferried soldiers and smugglers and magistrates and corpses. He had never watched anyone walk toward him across a dying river with such a look of pure administrative patience, as if the ice were a form that had been filled out incorrectly and she intended to correct it.

They came aboard—her first, then the man hauling the boathook in—and the ferry dipped and steadied. Up close, the widow's face was calm and gray as the ice itself, and her coat hung wrong at the chest, weighted, and Tobiah looked at that weighted coat and then very carefully looked away, because there were things a ferryman saw and things a ferryman filed.

"Push off," she said.

"Riders," Pieter warned.

On the south bank, the torches had begun to move—downstream, toward the shallows below the landing where a horse might pick its way to the waterline. A voice carried across the ice: *"Stop the ferry! By order of the magistrate—"*

Tobiah looked at his mother. Marta stood on the landing with her basket on her arm, directly between the torches and the boat, close enough that any shot meant for the ferry would pass through the space she occupied first. She did not raise her hands or cry out. She simply adjusted her shawl, the way she did when settling in for a long market morning, and the message of it went through Tobiah like cold water: *they will have to shoot through me, and everyone here will watch them decide.*

The riders reached the waterline. One dismounted. Another argued. Torches swung.

Tobiah put the pole against the piling and pushed, and the ferry slid out over the black water with its two lamps burning, and the gap between the boat and the landing widened from six feet to ten to twenty, and the voice on the bank cracked out again—*"I said stop!"*—and a pistol came up.

"Pieter," Tobiah said mildly, "you're standing in front of me."

"So I am," said Pieter, and did not move.

Nobody fired. Later, in court, there would be testimony about this minute—the minute the ferry crossed open water under lit lamps before forty witnesses while an officer of the customs house stood on a bank with a drawn pistol and holstered it, because the alternative was explaining to a spring assizes why he'd murdered a bread-seller's neighbor in front of the bread-seller and half the parish. But Tobiah, poling the ferry out into the current with the widow's eyes fixed on the far bank, understood it more simply. Power was loud until it was seen. Then it got quiet, and did its sums, and usually decided against.

The current took them. The river carried them downstream between walls of grinding ice, plates rearing and subsiding on either side with sounds like slow thunder, and the ferry lamps threw their small warm circles on the black water, and nobody spoke. The widow unwound her scarf, and beneath it the canvas sling showed for just a moment before she covered it again—just a moment, just for him, deliberate as a signature. Tobiah nodded once. Filed it.

At the downstream landing, where the ice-road to the courthouse began, he held the boat against the current longer than he needed to.

"One more minute," he said. "For luck."

"Luck," said the census man, stepping onto the ice, "is a community that shows up."

"No," said Tobiah, pushing off again, alone now, turning the ferry back toward his mother and the lamps and the torches on the far shore. "Luck is a boat somebody bothered to keep lit."

* * *

*Tamsin*

The ice-road ran along the eastern bank, staked in winter by the ferrymen with alder branches, and most of the stakes still stood, black twigs against gray ice marking a path a man could trust—or had trusted, a week ago, when the river was still a road. Now the path wound between heaves and fissures, and the river talked to them the whole way: cracks propagating somewhere ahead with a sound like tearing linen, long sighs of settling, and underneath everything the deep boom of the main flow shouldering the underside of the world.

Elias walked ahead with the boathook, testing. Tamsin walked in his tracks. Neither spoke. There was nothing left to plan; the plan was now just the next stake, and the stake after that. Behind them, faint and growing fainter, she could hear the ferry bell ringing—Tobiah, ringing the arrival signal at an empty landing, ringing it over and over, so that anyone listening would believe the crossing was routine, was traffic, was nothing at all. A small man making large noise for her benefit. She would remember that sound as long as she lived: the bright indifferent clangor of a boy lying magnificently on her behalf.

Two miles on, the garrison bend appeared—a headland, a ruined watchtower, and beyond it the last reach of river before the courthouse landing. And there, on the high road above the bank, torches again. Riders. Coming fast.

"They cut across at the ford," Elias said. His voice was steady but his breath was ragged. "They know the ferry's run. They'll ride the bank road and be at the courthouse landing before us."

"Then we don't take the bank road."

"There is no other road. There's the ice or there's nothing."

Tamsin looked out at the middle channel. All winter it had been the one place no one crossed—a mile-wide field of pressure ridges and refrozen leads, heaved and shattered, the river's own fortress. But tonight, with the break beginning, the big plates were drifting, opening leads of black water and closing them again, and between the leads the new ice lay thin and gray but continuous, and the drift was moving *downstream*. Toward the courthouse landing.

"That's not a road," Elias said, following her gaze.

"It's a current," Tamsin said. "Riders can't follow a current. And I've spent seven years reading water." She tightened the sling straps, checked the ledger's knots with fingers gone numb and certain. "The ice breaks, we're on it. The ice holds, we're across. Either way, Brandt arrives at the landing to find us already there—or already gone under, which he'll find equally inconvenient to explain."

"That's not courage, that's arithmetic."

"My husband used to say courage was arithmetic," Tamsin said. "Counting what matters more than what it costs." She stepped off the ice-road and out onto the moving field, and after a moment—she heard it plainly, the small surrender of his breath—Elias followed.

The drifting ice carried them. It was the strangest passage of her life: standing on a slab the size of a church floor, poles in hand, sliding down the dark river between walls of grinding white, the courthouse lights growing by degrees on the headland ahead while behind them the torches of the riders shrank and scattered along the bank road, furious and helpless, pacing a river that would not carry them. Once a lead opened directly beneath their feet with a crack like a musket shot, and they leapt it together, hand to hand, and landed on the next plate laughing—actually laughing, breathless and appalled at themselves, two middle-aged conspirators skipping across a breaking river like children playing stones.

The last hundred yards, the ice thinned to lace, and they went the final stretch crouched, distributing their weight, and then the shingle of the courthouse landing scraped under Tamsin's boots, and it was solid earth, and the earth seemed to pitch like a deck because her body had forgotten what ground was supposed to do.

She knelt. Pressed her palm flat against the frozen gravel. Felt the ledger against her heart, intact, dry, whole.

Above them, the courthouse stood dark except for one lit window—the clerk's room, where the spring records were already being sorted—and beyond it the town, asleep, unaware that its reckoning had just arrived on a raft of broken ice.

Elias pulled her up. For a moment they stood looking at each other, iced to the shoulders, steaming like horses, and neither managed anything grand to say.

Then hoofbeats, close—too close—on the bank road above, and a torch flared at the headland, and a voice she knew from the customs gate called down through the dark, breathless with rage and triumph:

"*Stop them! Stop them at the door!*"

The courthouse window went dark.
