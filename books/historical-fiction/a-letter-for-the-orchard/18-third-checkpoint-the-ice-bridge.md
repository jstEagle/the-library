## 18. Third Checkpoint: The Ice Bridge

The road ended the way all roads end in this country—not with a wall or a gate, but with a slow giving-up of itself. The ruts thinned to wheel-tracks, the wheel-tracks to footprints, the footprints to nothing but wind-scoured snow and a signpost whose board had rotted away years ago so that only the post remained, leaning like a drunk against the grey morning. Beyond it, the river.

I had lived beside water my whole life. I had married a man who read tides the way other men read scripture, and I had spent seven years feeding a lamp that existed for no other purpose than to tell the sea *not here, not here*. But I had never once considered the ice as a road until it was the only road left.

It stretched from our bank to the courthouse landing perhaps four hundred paces distant—a pale seam stitched between two dark shores, groaning under its own weight with sounds like a great door being opened very slowly in another room. The break had begun at midstream. Even from here I could see where the ice had fractured and refrozen, fracture upon fracture, a scarred thing pretending to be whole. Water showed black in some of those seams, moving, alive.

"Four hundred yards," Elias said behind me. He said it the way a man reads his own death warrant aloud to hear how it sounds. "Maybe less if the middle's gone soft."

"Then we'd best not stand here admiring it."

"Tamsin." He caught my sleeve, and I turned. His face was grey as the sky, not from cold alone. He'd been riding ahead of me since before dawn—since Tobiah's coded warning had reached us at Marta's, scrawled on the back of a ferry manifest: *BRANDT RIDES. TWO MEN. LEAVES GARRISON AT FIRST LIGHT.* We had left the sledge and taken the shore path instead, trusting our legs over wheels, and still he had gained on us. You cannot outride a man who knows every shortcut because he has been paid to know them.

"We could go back," Elias said. "Wait him out. The court sits in nine days."

"The river breaks in three. And Corrigan's clerks are already in the town, already asking why the lighthouse keeper is traveling without her crates of apples." I pulled my sleeve free, gently. "We ran out of *later* somewhere around the garrison, Elias. This is what's left."

He didn't argue. That was the strange mercy of Elias Wren—he argued like a lawyer when arguing was useful and fell silent like one when it wasn't, and he knew which moments were which better than any man I'd ever met. He began unbuckling the oilskin packet from inside his coat, and I stopped him with a hand on his wrist.

"No. It stays with me."

"It was never meant to cross on your back—"

"It was always meant to cross on someone's back, and everyone else who might have carried it is dead, arrested, or bought." I held out my arms. "Strap it on."

* * *

He did it properly, because he did everything properly—the ledger wrapped first in oilcloth, then in wool torn from Marta's old blanket, then bound flat against my chest with the sledge straps pulled tight enough to feel like ribs. Daniel's handwriting, seven years of small crimes and large ones, pressed against my sternum with every breath. It should have felt like a burden. It felt more like a hand.

"If I go through," I said, while he knotted the last buckle, "it goes through. Don't come after me. The ice won't hold two, and the ledger's worth one of us, not both."

"Don't talk like that."

"I'm a lighthouse keeper's widow. Talking about drowning is the only fluent language I have left." I looked up at him. His eyes were wet at the rims—from wind, he would have said, and I would have let him say it. "Elias. Your sister's name. Tell me it once, so I know what I'm carrying besides paper."

He blinked. For a moment the census-taker's careful face slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired man who had been walking toward this morning for eleven years.

"Sera," he said. "Sera Wren. She was nineteen."

"Sera," I repeated, and put the name into the ledger with everything else, into the space between Daniel's bribes and the drowned boy from the trial records, into the place where the truth lived now, whole and ugly and mine to deliver. "Then let's give Sera her day in court."

* * *

Brandt caught us at the edge, as I had known he would. Not because we were slow—we had moved fast, fast as the shore path allowed—but because Brandt was the sort of man who was never chasing you. He was simply already where you were going, standing with the patience of furniture, his two riders dismounted behind him and their horses steaming in the cold.

He had not hurried. That told me everything. A man who hurries fears you; a man who strolls believes the world will wait for him, and Magistrate Corrigan had spent twenty years teaching the world to wait.

"Mrs. Okonkwo." Brandt touched his hat brim. Frost had built up in the fur of his collar and made him look oddly festive, like something out of a cruel children's story. "And Mr. Wren. The census office will be pleased to know their man is keeping such thorough records."

"I keep thorough records," Elias said pleasantly, "of everything."

"So I've heard." Brandt's gaze went past us both, to the ice, to the landing beyond, and I watched him do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic I had done an hour ago—and arrive at the same sum. His mouth tightened. "You're not crossing that."

"Is that an order?" Elias asked. "From customs? Because customs has jurisdiction over goods entering the port, Mr. Brandt, and last I checked, neither of us is cargo. Now, if you'd like to swear out a warrant—"

"You know precisely what I'd like."

"—then you'll need a magistrate's signature," Elias continued, warm as brandy, relentless as tide, "and there's only one magistrate within forty miles, and I wonder, I truly wonder, whether Magistrate Corrigan would care to put his name to a document stopping two private citizens from walking across public ice to attend a public court. Because that document would exist afterward, Mr. Brandt. Paper has a long memory. Longer than men. I've made rather a study of it."

I had watched Elias work before—at the ferry, at Marta's table, in the three storm-bound days at the lighthouse when there was nothing to do but talk and sharpen each other like knives on a stone. But I had never seen him spend himself so completely. Every sentence was a coin flung down to buy a second of time, and I understood, watching Brandt's jaw work, that the coins were running out even as they glittered.

Because that was the plan. The plan, such as it was, the one we had made in the dark at Marta's kitchen table with Tobiah's warning burning between us: *Elias talks. Tamsin walks.* A customs officer could not shoot a woman on open ice in view of both shores—not with spring coming, not with the town waking, not with witnesses on the far bank where the courthouse chimneys already smoked. And he could not arrest her without touching her, and touching her meant stepping onto the ice, and Brandt, whatever else he was, was not paid enough to drown.

But plans are made by people standing safely on land, and the ice does not consult anyone's plan.

"Mrs. Okonkwo." Brandt stepped forward, and his voice changed—dropped its bureaucratic boredom and found something almost gentle underneath, which was worse. "Whatever you think you're carrying, whatever he's told you it's worth—it isn't worth the river. That ice took two drovers last week. I fished one of them out myself." He paused. "Your husband was ruled accidental, wasn't he? Drowning. I remember the file."

For one white moment the world narrowed to that word—*accidental*—and Daniel's face, the living one, young and laughing in the orchard light, and I thought: *you would use him even now, you rotting, reasonable men, you would use the dead as doorstops.*

"He drowned," I said. My voice came out steady. Seven years of steady; it was the one skill grief had perfected in me. "Yes. Which is why I don't frighten."

I turned to the ice.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it is not a feeling. It is an arithmetic. You stand at the edge and you count—one, the distance; two, the seams; three, the sound the ice makes when it means it—and you discover that the numbers do not care whether your hands are shaking. The numbers simply wait to be counted. And then you step, because the alternative is to stand forever at the edge becoming the kind of person who stands forever at edges, and I had already been that person for seven years, and I had promised myself, kneeling in the lamp room with Daniel's confession open in my lap, that I was finished.

The first hundred paces were almost easy. The near-shore ice was thick, river-fed, honest. My boots crunched and held. Behind me I heard Elias's voice going on and on—something about jurisdiction, something about the postal act, magnificent nonsense delivered with total conviction—and Brandt's answering bark, and the two riders' horses stamping, and I fixed my eyes on the courthouse landing and walked.

At a hundred and fifty paces, the character of the ice changed.

It went from white to a bruised grey-green, and beneath my boots I could see it—actually see it—the current sliding past below, black and muscular, the way a snake moves under glass. The sounds rose around me. Not the door-in-another-room groaning of the shore ice but sharper things: cracks that started somewhere ahead and traveled *toward* me, quick as a struck match, finishing under my heels with a snap that went up through my bones.

I kept walking. One pace. Another. The ledger against my chest rose and fell with my breathing, and I found I had put my own hand flat over it, the way you'd quiet a frightened child, or hold closed a wound.

Two hundred paces. Midstream.

The seam opened six feet ahead of me without warning—a crack that split the surface with a report like a rifle shot, running bank to bank, and where it crossed my path the ice had separated by a hand's width and the black water showed through, moving fast, flecked with grinding floes. On either side of the gap the plates rocked gently, bobbing, as if the whole river were breathing.

I stood at the edge of it and my body did what bodies do—every muscle voting to stop, to turn, to go back to the lighthouse and the lamp and the seven years of safe smallness. And in that moment, absurdly, I heard Daniel's voice. Not the confession-Daniel, the stranger in the logbook. The orchard-Daniel, grafting stock in the autumn light, telling me the secret of it: *"You cut clean and bind it tight and trust the tree knows more than you do, Tam. That's all growing is. Trusting the wound to close right."*

Cut clean. Bind tight. Cross.

There was a floe in the gap—flat, wide as a door, rocking. I waited for it to swing near, timed the rock the way Daniel had taught me to time waves against the landing stairs all those years ago, and stepped.

The floe dipped under my weight, tilted, righted. Water slopped over its edge and soaked my boot to the ankle, cold enough to burn. Three seconds—I counted them, because counting was the arithmetic and the arithmetic was all there was—and then I stepped off onto the far plate of ice, and it held, and I was across the worst of it.

Behind me, shouting.

I didn't look back. Looking back is how you fall. But the sounds came anyway, carried flat across the ice: Brandt's voice gone sharp with real anger now, Elias's voice rising to meet it, the whinny of a horse, and then—clearer, closer, impossible—

A crack, and a heavy splash, and a man screaming curses in the specific register of someone who has gone into freezing water and discovered what winter actually costs.

One of the riders. Chasing me, or trying to flank along the shore path, or simply a fool on a frightened horse near broken ice—the reason didn't matter. What mattered was the scream, and the silence after it, and then Elias's voice cutting through everything, suddenly stripped of all its lawyerly silk:

"*Tamsin—don't stop—don't you dare stop!*"

So he had looked back, and seen me midstream on a rocking floe, and seen the rider go in, all in the same terrible instant. I could picture him: held at the edge by Brandt's men, straining against a grip he couldn't break, watching me walk away across the breaking river exactly as we had planned and nothing like what either of us had imagined.

I did not stop. I wanted to—with my whole body I wanted to, to turn, to help, to be the person who goes back—but the ledger was strapped to my chest and the ice was singing under me and somewhere downstream the courthouse clock was striking nine, and courage, I have said, is arithmetic. Two lives on thin ice versus one delivery. The numbers did not care about my heart. I made myself count again and walk.

* * *

The last hundred paces were the longest of my life.

The near-bank ice had been honest and the midstream had been violent, but the final stretch was something else entirely—thin, dark, translucent as church window glass, flexing under each step with a wet elastic sigh. Twice it cracked beneath me and held. The third time it cracked and kept cracking, a long splintering run that raced ahead of my boots toward the shore, and I did the only thing that made sense: I ran.

I ran across the last forty feet of frozen river with the ice failing behind me in a rolling series of reports like applause, like gunfire, like the end of something—and then my boots hit mud, real mud, blessed frozen riverbank mud, and I stumbled forward onto my knees at the base of the courthouse landing steps, gasping, soaked to the knee, alive.

Above me the courthouse rose grey and solid against the morning, its chimneys smoking, its windows catching the first weak sun. Ordinary. Brick and slate and justice, or whatever passed for it. From the landing behind me came the sounds of the river working—the groan and grind of the break beginning in earnest, floes shouldering and shattering—and beneath it, fainter, the voices on the far shore, and one of them, thinner with distance but unmistakable, still talking. Still spending his coins. Still buying seconds for a woman he couldn't see anymore.

"Elias," I whispered, and pressed my palm flat against the ledger through the soaked wool, feeling the hard rectangle of it, whole, dry, safe. "Hold on. Nine days. Just hold on."

The courthouse doors opened above me, spilling lamplight and the smell of ink and woodsmoke into the cold, and a clerk stood blinking down at me—a small man with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone whose ordinary Tuesday has just arrived wearing a soaked widow and a river of breaking ice.

"Madam?" he said. "Can I—what in God's name—"

"My name," I said, getting to my feet, "is Tamsin Okonkwo. I've come to testify." I put my hand on the rail and climbed, one step at a time, the ledger heavy and certain against my chest. "And you'll want to fetch the presiding judge. Tell him the evidence he's been waiting for didn't come by road."

Behind me, across the widening black water, the far bank disappeared into the morning mist—and with it, for now, Elias Wren, and Brandt, and everything I could no longer reach.
