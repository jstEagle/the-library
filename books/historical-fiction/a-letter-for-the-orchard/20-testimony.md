## 20. Testimony

The courthouse at Harrow's Landing had been built for grain disputes and boundary quarrels, and it wore the spring session like a borrowed coat that did not quite fit. The benches groaned under the weight of people who had walked miles along the river road to hear what would be said—fishermen with salt still in their beards, the ferry women, Sergeant Hale in a uniform he had pressed until the creases stood like fence rails, and Marta, who had taken the seat nearest the aisle as though she intended to leave quickly and knew she would not.

Tamsin waited behind the door of the witness room with her hands folded and her heart going like a lamp in wind.

She had dressed as she dressed for everything now: plainly, in the grey wool she kept for burials and court days alike. Her hair was pinned without ornament. She had considered wearing something of Daniel's—a scarf, a button—but decided against it before she finished the thought. This day was not about wearing him. It was about saying him, whole, and letting the room do what it liked with the saying.

Through the wall she could hear Elias Wren's voice, steady and dry as driftwood, reading from the ledger. He read numbers the way other men read scripture: *four pounds, ten shillings, received of A. Vane, for the matter of the boy Pell.* A murmur moved through the courtroom like water finding a crack. She heard Corrigan's solicitor rise and object to nothing in particular, and she heard the judge—a grey, patient man named Aldersey brought upriver for exactly this purpose—tell the solicitor to sit down.

Then the clerk's voice, calling through the door: *"Tamsin Okonkwo."*

* * *

The walk to the stand was eleven steps. She counted them because counting was what keepers did; it steadied the hands. Eleven steps across a floor that smelled of beeswax and cold stove iron, past faces she had known for years and faces she had never seen and might never see again, all of them turned toward her like flowers toward an uncertain sun.

The oath was administered on a Bible older than the town. She put her hand flat upon it and felt the leather's chill through her palm and thought, absurdly, of the lamp room, of glass gone cold at dawn.

"State your name and station for the record," said Aldersey.

"Tamsin Okonkwo. Widow of Daniel Okonkwo. Keeper of the light at Gullwrack Point these seven years."

"And you are the widow also of—" Aldersey paused, consulting a paper, though everyone in that room knew what the paper held, "—of certain correspondence and records entered into evidence this morning."

"I am his widow," Tamsin said. "I am not here to speak for him."

A rustle went through the benches. Aldersey looked at her over his spectacles with the expression of a man who has fished long enough to know when the line has gone taut on its own.

"Very well, Mrs. Okonkwo. Tell the court how you came to possess the ledger."

And so she began.

She began where it truly began—not with the sea chest, not with the storm, but with the orchard. She told them about the spring Daniel had planted those apple trees, grafting stock he'd carried home wrapped in wet sacking like a thing rescued from a wreck. She told them how he'd marked each tree with a knife-cut so he could tell his own work from the rootstock's wildness, and how he'd said, laughing, that a man ought to be able to tell what he planted from what merely grew.

"He marked everything that season," she said. "I thought it was only the trees."

The words came out of her slowly, and she let them. She was not a woman who performed grief, but neither was she a woman who fled it, and she understood now—standing in the heat of two hundred stares—that the truth required pacing, like a tide. You could not hurry it in. You could not hold it out forever either.

She told them about his letters. About the man named Aldous Vane, whose name she had lied to Elias Wren about the first night he knocked, soaked and shivering, asking questions no census required. "I lied," she said clearly, into the hush. "I am practiced at lying. Seven years of practice. I want the court to understand that I am not a reliable narrator of my own heart. I loved my husband, and love is a keeper too—it keeps things you did not ask it to keep."

Corrigan's solicitor, a soft-bellied man called Prewitt, stirred at his table like a man settling in for a long meal. Magistrate Corrigan himself sat two seats away, and Tamsin made herself look at him.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first cruelty of it, and she accepted it as such. Seven years ago he had filled a courtroom the way weather fills a sky. Now he sat with his hands folded, silver-haired, reasonable-faced, watching her with the mild attention of a man observing a widow misplace her keys. He had come to her lighthouse in winter under flag of courtesy and offered her a pension for silence, and even then—even holding the ledger against her ribs beneath her coat—she had almost believed him kind. That was the rot in him. Not cruelty. Kindness, worn like a uniform by a man who had forgotten there was anything underneath.

She did not look away from him when she spoke next.

"The ledger shows payments made to my husband between the years 'eighty-four and 'eighty-six. Bribes, the prosecution calls them." She heard Prewitt begin to rise and Aldersey's gavel come down once, softly, like a hand on a shoulder. "The prosecution is correct. They were bribes. My husband took money from Magistrate Corrigan's office to sign off manifests he had not inspected, to lose papers, to say the river fog was thicker than it was. I will not soften it. I have spent seven years softening it, and the softening nearly drowned the rest."

Something shifted in the room. She felt it the way you feel ice change its mind underfoot—a deep structural consent, a sound below hearing.

Prewitt found his feet at last. "Mrs. Okonkwo, the defense appreciates your candor regarding your late husband's—indiscretions—and would remind the court that a dead man cannot defend his name against—"

"Mr. Prewitt." Aldersey again, mild as milk. "The witness has not yielded the floor."

"She is character-assassinating the very evidence—"

"She is doing the opposite," Tamsin said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, with the grain of it showing, like timber with the paint stripped off. "That is precisely what I am doing. Your honor—if I may—I ask the court's patience for one minute more, because this is the part everyone in this room has been waiting for me not to say."

Aldersey inclined his head.

"There is a page near the end of the ledger," Tamsin said. "Dated the ninth of October, seven years ago. It does not record a payment. It records a debt. In my husband's hand. He wrote it because he was a bookkeeper before he was anything else, and a man who keeps books honestly eventually writes down the entries that ruin him."

She had rehearsed this part every night since the lamp room. She had rehearsed it walking the gallery, hauling the wick, standing at the rail watching the sea make and unmake itself. And still, when the moment came, her mouth went dry as August.

"On that page he confesses," she said, "that the drowning of the witness Pell—the fisherman whose testimony against this court was scheduled for the autumn sessions seven years ago—was not an accident of weather. My husband rowed out to meet him. There was an argument. There was—" her breath caught, and she mastered it, the way you master a valve, one turn at a time, "—there was a moment when Daniel could have pulled the man back into the boat, and he did not. He wrote that he told himself the sea would be blamed. And the sea was blamed. And I lived seven years beside a man the sea was blamed for, mourning him as a victim, while the truth sat in a logbook binding ten feet above my bed."

The silence afterward was total. Not the respectful silence of courts—the other kind, the animal kind, when a room full of people stops being an audience and becomes a single creature holding its breath.

Tamsin pressed on, because stopping now would have been the same as drowning halfway across.

"I am not telling the court this to bury my husband deeper. I am telling the court this because Mr. Wren asked me, weeks ago, whether I wanted the ledger read entire or read clean. And I told him—" here her voice did break, briefly, and she let it, and saw in the corner of her eye Marta lean forward as if to catch the words in her apron, "—I told him that if we trimmed the record to spare the dead, we would be doing exactly what Corrigan's court has done for twenty years. Choosing which truths are convenient. Love edits, gentlemen. Love is always editing. Somebody in this room has to refuse."

* * *

Prewitt cross-examined her for the better part of two hours, and it went worse for him than he could have known, because he kept trying to move her onto ground she had already burned.

He tried grief first. *Madam, you were devoted to your husband—* "I was," she said. *And yet you deliver his confession to this court—* "Because devotion that hides the truth is just loyalty with better manners."

He tried the bribes. *Your husband profited from the magistrate's office—does that not suggest the payments flowed the other way as well? That perhaps your husband invented this ledger?* Tamsin almost smiled. "Sir, I have kept a light for seven years. I know forgery the way you know your own signature—Daniel's ink fades where his hand hesitated, and it never hesitated on the numbers. Check the page against his ship's logs. The hand is tired in the confession and tireless in the accounts. Guilt slows a man. Greed does not."

There was laughter at that—startled, quickly swallowed, but laughter—and Prewitt's ears went pink.

He tried the widow herself, last of all. He came around the rail and stood close enough that she could smell the pomade in his hair, and he asked her, gently, terribly gently, whether a woman alone on a coast, dependent on the goodwill of the colonial office for her position and her pension, might not be vulnerable to persuasion by ambitious men. Whether Mr. Wren had not, perhaps, *sheltered* her through the storms. Whether her testimony might not be gratitude wearing the mask of truth.

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked out at the room—at Hale sitting rigid as a masthead, at Tobiah Ferryman with an apple in his pocket that he had carried forty miles like a relic, at old women who had sold her eggs and never asked why she kept her curtains drawn.

"Mr. Prewitt," she said, "you have offered me three stories about myself today. The grieving fool. The bought woman. The tool of ambitious men. All three have one thing in common—they require that I not know my own mind." She turned to Aldersey. "Your honor, I kept a lamp burning alone through seven winters. I know exactly what I burned and why. I buried my husband's truth in an orchard because I could not bear it, and I dug it up because bearing it was the lesser drowning. That is the whole of my testimony. Everything else in this room is weather."

* * *

They kept her on the stand while the afternoon light went amber in the high windows, and when Aldersey finally released her, her legs took the eleven steps back slower than they had taken them forward. But the room did something as she passed that no one had instructed it to do. It did not cheer—this was a courtroom, and a colonial one, and cheering was not in its nature. What it did was quieter and stranger. As she walked the aisle, the sound of it changed. The coughing stopped. The bench-creaks stopped. Two hundred people went still in sequence, the way water goes still when the wind quits, and it was not deference exactly, and it was not pity.

It was the silence you give a thing that has spoken true.

Marta caught her hand as she passed—briefly, hard, flour-roughened fingers squeezing hers—and let go before the clerk could frown. Hale stood. He did not seem to decide to; he simply rose, the way a man rises when the anthem plays, and then Tobiah rose, and then a ferrywoman, and then half a bench of strangers, and Corrigan watched it happen with his reasonable face and his folded hands, and for the first time Tamsin saw something move underneath the reasonableness—something quick and ratlike, turning, looking for the wall with the hole in it.

Elias Wren stood at the prosecution table with the ledger open before him, and he did not smile at her—he was not a smiling man—but he touched the ledger's cover once, lightly, the way you touch a shoulder.

Aldersey adjourned until morning, when Hale would take the stand, and after Hale, the boy with the apple.

* * *

Outside, the evening had come up mild off the water, the first truly warm evening of the year, and the ice on the river was done—gone to open leads and drifting pans, the spring court's own witness written in moving water. People lingered on the courthouse steps, not speaking much, nodding to her as she passed, and she understood that the nodding was its own verdict, preliminary and unbinding, rendered by the only jury that had ever mattered on this coast.

Marta fell into step beside her, and they walked a while without talking, boots ringing on the plank road.

"You said it whole," Marta said at last.

"I said it all. Whole is different."

"No," Marta said. "It isn't." And she squeezed Tamsin's arm once and left her at the corner where the road bent toward the harbor and the lodging house where she would sleep—or lie awake—until morning.

But sleep did not come easily, and when it finally came, near midnight, it came with a sound beneath it: the creak of the courthouse doors being opened early, by lamplight, by men whose business with the building was not the court's business. Tamsin woke in the dark with her heart hammering, listening to footsteps on the plank road below her window—several men, unhurried, moving toward the landing where the evidence was locked for the night.

And far down the street, a lantern swung once, twice, and went dark.
