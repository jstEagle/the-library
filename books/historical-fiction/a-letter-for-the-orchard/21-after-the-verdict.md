## 21. After the Verdict

The morning after the verdict, Marta Okonkwo—Marta Voss before that, Marta of the ferry road all her life—woke before dawn out of habit and lay in the dark listening for the storm.

There was no storm. There had been no storm for eleven days, which was longer than the coast had gone quiet since October. The house creaked its ordinary creaks. The fire she'd banked the night before breathed a thin line of smoke into the chimney. Somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

She lay there and waited to feel different.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. For a year she had thought: when Corrigan falls, I will feel *this*. Relief like a floodgate. Joy like bells. She had rehearsed the feeling the way you rehearse a prayer, saying it over and over until the words wore smooth and meant nothing. And now the man was remanded to the district jail in irons he could not talk his way out of, and the town's whole rotten scaffolding was coming down plank by plank, and Marta woke in her warm bed and felt—

Tired. Hungry. Forty-nine years old with dirt still under her nails from last autumn's digging.

Ordinary. That was the word, and it took her a while to find it because it seemed too small for the occasion. Ordinary, like a coat you put back on after standing a long time in the cold without one. It chafed at first. Then it warmed.

Downstairs, Tobiah was already up. He sat at the kitchen table with his boots off—he'd taken to coming home barefoot through the mud rather than ruin his good pair—and a cup of tea going cold in front of him, staring at the window where the light hadn't yet decided to arrive.

"You're up," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always up." She put the kettle on. "You sleep?"

"A bit." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table, then another quarter. "The clerk came by again yesterday. After court. Wanted to know if I'd swear the manifest was forged."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I'd swear it was mine. Every mark on it. My hand wrote those numbers since I was fifteen years old, and my hand doesn't forget them just because they've become inconvenient." He said it flatly, looking at the window, but she saw his jaw work once, twice, like a man chewing something too big to swallow whole. "He said there'd be consequences. He said—" A breath. "He said a ferryman who can't be trusted is a ferryman without a crossing."

The kettle began its low climb toward boiling. Marta stood with her hand on its handle and did not take it off the flame yet.

"Is that what you're afraid of?" she asked. "Losing the crossing?"

"No," Tobiah said. "I'm afraid of finding out I don't mind."

She let that sit between them the way you let dough rest—covered, undisturbed, doing its slow work. Then she poured the water over the leaves and set his cup full and fresh in front of him, and sat down across from her son in the grey half-light, and neither of them said anything else for a long while. Outside, the first proper birdsong of the spring started up in the alder by the fence, tentative, as if the bird itself weren't sure winter had truly been given notice.

* * *

By nine o'clock the whole coast knew, though knowing changed shape depending on whose mouth carried it.

At Halvorsen's store, where Marta went for flour and salt and the small mercies of ordinary weeks, the news moved through the shop like weather. Old Halvorsen himself stood behind his counter reading the printed account aloud to anyone who'd stand still, his voice trembling not with age but with something younger. *Corrigan remanded without bail. Fourteen counts read into the record. The ledger entered as evidence.* He got to the part about the drowned boy—the boy from the trial records, the one whose name had been spoken aloud in a courtroom at last, full and unabbreviated—and had to stop and press his knuckles to his mouth.

"Seven years," said Mrs. Aldridge by the pickle barrel, loud enough for everyone. "Seven years we watched that man sentence children to the workhouse and call it mercy, and we *curtsied* to him. I curtsied. I remember doing it."

"We all curtsied," said Halvorsen. "That's not the sin you think it is. The sin is what we did while we were bent over."

Marta paid for her flour and said nothing. She had done her own accounting of sins this year and found the ledger longer than she liked: the bread and salt warning she'd given Tamsin, oblique and cowardly; the seasons she'd seen Corrigan's men at the ferry and looked elsewhere; the way she'd taught Tobiah, without ever meaning to, that survival meant keeping your eyes down and your hands busy. But there was this too, written on the other side: she had stood on the riverbank in the wind with her shawl whipping around her, an old woman planted in plain sight so that no man on either bank would dare raise a gun with a witness watching. She had done one brave thing in fifty years and it had been enough. That was the lesson of the whole miserable season, if it had a lesson: you didn't need to be brave all the time. You needed to be brave once, at the right minute, and then keep breathing afterward.

Outside the store the sun was doing something shameless to the harbor ice. It had begun to rot, the locals called it—gone grey and honeycombed, groaning at night, shedding great plates into the current with a sound like doors closing in an empty house. By week's end the channel would run clear for the first time since December. The packet boat would come. Prices would drop. Letters would arrive from people who had no idea what had happened here and would have to be told, and told again.

Marta walked home along the ferry road with the wind at her back, past the garrison where Sergeant Hale stood at the gate in a uniform pressed sharp enough to draw blood. He touched his cap to her—not the old servile touch he'd given every woman on this road for a decade, but something slower, more deliberate, the way you greet someone whose opinion of you matters now.

"Marta Voss," he said.

"Sergeant."

"They'll ask me to testify again. At the assizes, when they move it upriver." His face was carefully empty, but his hands weren't; they kept adjusting his belt as if it didn't fit anymore. "About Daniel Okonkwo. About what I saw and didn't report."

"Will you?"

"I don't know yet." He said it plainly, and she found she respected him for the plainness more than she would have respected any easy yes. "But I know I won't lie. That's new. That has to count for something."

"It counts," Marta said, and walked on, and heard behind her the gate hinge squeal and his boots resume their rounds, and thought: *that has to count for something* might be the truest prayer this coast had produced in seven years.

* * *

The grievances, she decided that evening, would go into the ground with the winter.

Not forgotten—that was beyond her, and probably beyond wisdom anyway—but buried, the way you bury fish heads under a new-planted tree. Not thrown away. *Used.* Fed to the roots so that something could grow out of the rot of them.

She made a list, because lists were how Marta Voss had survived everything life had ever handed her. On one side of her slate, the debts owed to her: Tamsin's seven years of silence about what Daniel had been doing; the way the woman had let the whole coast believe her husband's drowning was simple misfortune while she sat on papers that would have burned Corrigan down years earlier; the lie of the fruit crates that had passed through Tobiah's ferry under Marta's own nose, using her son as a blind man uses a cane. On the other side, the debts she owed: the warning given too softly, too late, wrapped in courtesy when it should have been shouted; the years of silence of her own, the curtsy inside herself if not the one outside; and the plain fact that if Tamsin Okonkwo had not strapped that ledger to her chest and walked the rotting ice alone, they would all have gone on curtsying until they died of it.

She looked at the two columns for a long time. They balanced. Not evenly—they never balance evenly, that's the lie people tell about forgiveness—but they balanced the way a ferry balances, shifting, requiring constant attention, both sides necessary or the whole thing capsizes.

Then she wiped the slate clean and went to bed, and slept, and did not dream of storms or courts or men in chains. She dreamed of apple blossoms, which was absurd, because it was barely March and the orchard stood black and bare against the sky. But dreams are allowed their optimism. It's the only place most of us get any.

* * *

It was Tobiah who suggested the grafts.

He brought it up at supper a week later, in the roundabout way he'd inherited from his father—never arriving at a subject directly, always approaching it like a shy horse. "Went up past the old orchard yesterday," he said. "Checking the high path before the thaw mucks it proper. Some of the trees are dead past saving. But the ones along the south wall—" He stopped. Picked up his fork. Put it down.

"The south wall," Marta prompted.

"He grafted them himself. Mr. Okonkwo. Before I was born, my father said. Marked every one with his knife—two cuts, crossed, like an X." Tobiah looked at his plate. "Three of them are still alive. Budding already. You can see it, green under the bark, if you scratch with your thumbnail. Nobody's tended that ground in seven years and they lived anyway."

Marta set down her cup. Something moved through her chest that was not quite grief and not quite hope but had the weight of both.

"Apple trees are like that," she said finally. "Stubborn past all reason. My mother had one come up from a core she threw out the door. Never pruned, never fed. Gave fruit till she died." She looked at her son across the table—at this boy who had held a ferry an extra minute against direct orders, who had lied badly and then well, who had crossed some interior line and discovered his spine on the far side of it—and understood that he wasn't really talking about trees.

"You think she'd want them," he said. "Mrs. Okonkwo. You think she'd come up and tend them."

"She'd want to," Marta said. "Whether she lets herself is a different question. That woman's spent seven years practicing solitude like it's a trade." She stood and began stacking dishes, because feeling things was easier with your hands moving. "Ask her anyway. Worst she says is no, and she's earned the right to say it."

* * *

Marta went herself, three days later, carrying a loaf and a jar of the plum preserve she'd been saving for an occasion worth spoiling.

The lighthouse stood white in the hard spring light, and for the first time in seven years it looked less like a fortress and more like what it was—a tall house where a tired woman lived. The lamp was dark. Marta had heard about that too, how it traveled the coast faster than any official news: *the keeper's put the light out. Chose to. Season's over, she says, and the chart office agrees.* People repeated it the way you repeat anything that proves the world can change—a light going dark by decision instead of fear, a magistrate in irons, a widow testifying in her own clear voice about bribes and drownings and love, all of it, refusing to edit.

Tamsin answered the door with flour on her hands.

They looked at each other a moment across the threshold—the baker and the keeper, the witness on the bank and the witness on the ice, two women who had spent a year circling each other like weather fronts deciding whether to merge or shear apart.

"You're baking," Marta said.

"I'm learning to bake. There's a difference. Come in before the wind takes the smell out to sea and disappoints the gulls."

The kitchen was warm and cluttered and smelled of yeast and woodsmoke, and Elias Wren's coat hung on the peg by the door, which meant the census man had become something else entirely, though nobody on this coast had yet worked out what to call it. They drank tea and spoke of small things—the price of seed potatoes, whether the packet boat would run on Thursday, the scandal of the assizes being moved upriver where the newspapers would get hold of it. Then Marta set down her cup and said it straight, because she had promised herself she would do at least one thing directly this year.

"There's three of his grafts still alive. South wall of the orchard. Budding now."

Tamsin's face did something complicated and quiet. Her hands, wrapped around her cup, went very still.

"Three," she said.

"Tobiah checked. Green under the bark." Marta hesitated, then pushed on. "The ground wants clearing. Pruning. Feeding. Seven years of neglect doesn't fix itself, and it won't wait much longer—if you don't cut the dead wood back soon it'll strangle the living." She heard her own metaphor arrive a beat late and decided to let it stand. "I've got a good saw and nothing but time until planting. If a body wanted help with such a job."

For a moment Tamsin looked away, toward the window, toward the sea she'd guarded and the shore she'd hidden from, and Marta watched her wrestle whatever she was wrestling and win—or lose; it was hard to tell the difference sometimes, and maybe it always had been.

"Saturday," Tamsin said. "If the weather holds. I haven't been up there since—" She stopped. Started again. "Since I took the ledger. I dug under the tree in a storm and I never went back. I couldn't look at what I'd left open."

"Saturday," Marta agreed, and gathered her jar and her courage and her leave-taking, and at the door Tamsin caught her arm—not hard, just enough—and said, "Marta. On the bank. That day on the ferry. You know what you did."

"I stood in the wind. My feet were frozen for a week. Don't make more of it than—"

"I know exactly what you did." Tamsin's grip tightened once, then released. "So does everyone on this coast. So does Corrigan, wherever they've got him locked up. That's not a small thing, however you dress it down to spare yourself the compliment."

Marta went home the long way, along the water, because her eyes were doing something inconvenient and she preferred salt wind to explain it.

* * *

Saturday came up mild and smelling of wet earth, the first day that had felt like spring rather than like winter losing an argument.

The orchard was worse than Marta remembered and better too. Worse: fallen limbs everywhere, bramble marching up from the ditch, half the row along the north wall split and blackened by some long-ago lightning strike that no one had bothered to clear. Better: the light. With the dead wood thinned by storms themselves, sun reached places it hadn't reached in years, and the grass underneath was already greening, and the air had that particular sweetness of apple ground waking up.

And there they were. Three trees against the south wall, crooked and unpruned and alive, each trunk bearing the old crossed cuts grown smooth and wide with age—an X healed into the wood itself, Daniel Okonkwo's signature, legible only if you knew to look.

Tamsin stood before the middle one for a long time without touching it. She'd dressed for work—old trousers, her husband's canvas jacket, sleeves rolled—and her hair was tied back, and Marta, leaning on the gate, saw a woman standing at the grave of a marriage that had turned out to contain a stranger, trying to decide what to mourn.

"He marked them like this," Tamsin said at last, running her thumb across the scarred X. "Two cuts, crossed. He said it was so he'd know his own work in twenty years. I used to tease him—who else's would it be? Who else was going to come along and claim his apple trees?" Her voice caught and steadied. "Somebody did, in the end. Didn't they. Just not the way he meant."

"Trees don't care who claims them," Marta said gently. "They care who prunes."

They worked until the light went long and gold. Marta took the saw to the dead wood—seven years of it, brittle and pale-hearted, coming away in satisfying cracks—and Tamsin cleared the bramble by hand, gloved, methodical, piling the canes to burn later. They uncovered the base of the graft trees and found the soil starved and sour, and Tamsin knelt in it and worked it loose with her fingers like a woman taking a pulse.

Around noon, others came. That was the part Marta hadn't arranged and wouldn't have dared to. Tobiah appeared with a barrow of manure from the ferry stable, saying nothing, just dumping it and turning to go until Tamsin handed him a spade and he stayed. Then young Halvorsen, then Mrs. Aldridge's granddaughter, then—unexpectedly, unbearably—Sergeant Hale, off duty, in plain clothes, who took one look at the split north-wall trees and asked for the axe. By mid-afternoon there were nine people in the abandoned orchard of a dead man, working in the kind of companionable silence that doesn't need filling, and Marta understood that she was watching something being built that no verdict could have ordered and no magistrate could ever have permitted.

Near dusk, they planted.

Tamsin had cut scions from the three living grafts that morning—Marta showed her how, heel-cut, sealed with wax—and now they set them into prepared ground along the repaired wall, twelve slips of living wood from surviving wood, each one marked with two crossed knife-cuts, because some signatures deserve to outlive their signers.

Tobiah tamped the earth around the last one and stood back, wiping his hands. "Twenty years," he said. "That's what Mr. Okonkwo said his marks were for. So he'd know his work in twenty years. Well." He looked down the row of new sticks that didn't look like anything yet, that looked like nothing at all, like mistakes, like hope with the bark still on. "We'll know it for him."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Down in the harbor the rotted ice groaned and shifted, letting go of the shore one plate at a time, and the sound carried up the hill soft as surf.

Marta walked home in the blue hour with sawdust in her hair and blisters on her palms and the specific exhaustion that comes only from honest work, and she thought about the slate wiped clean, the grievances buried under the new roots like fish heads feeding a tree, and how ordinary it all was—planting, pruning, supper, sleep—and how it had taken a drowning, a ledger, a trial, and a walk across breaking ice to buy them the right to be this ordinary again.

At her gate she paused. Up the coast, faint in the last light, the lighthouse stood dark and calm against the brightening stars—and beside it, small and steady, a single window burned in the keeper's house, warm as a hearth, lit on purpose, for no one's safety but her own.

Marta smiled, went in, and began thinking about bread for tomorrow. There would be a crowd at the orchard again. There was always a crowd, now, wherever Tamsin Okonkwo let people gather—and tomorrow, Marta had heard, the woman intended to write a letter, and carry it up the hill, and bury it beneath the graft tree where the truth of everything had first gone into the ground.
