## 22. A Letter for the Orchard

The morning the verdict came down from the spring court, Tamsin Okonkwo did not go to the courthouse landing to hear it read aloud. She had already heard it—had spoken most of it herself, in her own voice, before a room full of strangers who had come expecting a widow's grief and gotten instead a widow's arithmetic: bribes entered neat as tide tables, a boy drowned off Blackrock Point, a magistrate's signature trailing through seven years of small cruelties like oil on water.

She had said all of it. And so when Elias came up the cliff path two days later with his coat smelling of courthouse dust and other people's tobacco, and stood at her door turning his hat in his hands like a man who had forgotten what news was for, she only nodded before he could speak.

"He's remanded," Elias said anyway. "Pending assizes in the capital. Hale testified. The ferry manifest. Your apple." He smiled then, thin and tired, the smile of a man setting down something he had carried so long his shoulders had grown around it. "It's done, Mrs. Okonkwo."

"Tamsin," she said, because he had earned the name twice over—once for the ledger, once for standing at the edge of the ice bridge while Brandt shouted himself hoarse and she walked out alone across the groaning white with Daniel's whole truth strapped against her heart.

"It's done, Tamsin."

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way it does on coasts where winter digs in its heels and has to be pried loose finger by finger. The river ran high and brown with snowmelt. The gulls returned first, then the smell of thawed earth, then—last and least expected—the green.

Tamsin walked out to the orchard on a Tuesday in late April, because Marta had told her the graft tree had woken, and because there was a thing she had been carrying since the trial that was not paper and would not fit in any crate.

The orchard had been Daniel's before it was anyone's grief. Seven years of salt wind and neglect had bent the rows into shapes like old men leaning into weather, but they had held. They always held. That was the thing about apple trees nobody told you—that they were stubborn past all sense, that they would put out blossom on a broken trunk, that they kept faith with the seasons long after everyone who planted them had stopped keeping faith with anything.

Marta met her at the gate, which was not really a gate anymore, just a gap in a wall the storms had been dismantling for a decade. She was carrying two spades and a burlap sack, and her face wore the expression it had worn through the whole of that terrible winter: nothing given away, everything understood.

"You said new stock," Tamsin said.

"I said it, and I brought it." Marta hefted the sack. Six saplings, bare-rooted, their pale limbs still sleeping. "From the nursery downriver. And these—" she nudged one bundle apart with her boot "—are the survivors. Cuttings off your graft tree. I took them in February while you were testifying. Figured somebody ought to be making new things while everybody else was busy unmaking old ones."

Tamsin looked at the cuttings. Thin whips of wood no longer than her forearm, each one bearing the pale scar tissue of the graft wound, healed over now into something stronger than either parent stock. Her throat did something complicated.

"Marta."

"Don't." Marta pressed the spade into her hands. "Don't thank me and don't apologize. We buried enough this winter. Let's plant something."

They worked through the morning. The ground gave reluctantly at first—it had not finished forgiving the frost—but by midday the six saplings stood in their holes like a small congregation waiting for a sermon, and Marta knelt at each root ball with her bare hands, packing the dark earth down, murmuring to the trees in the low way she murmured to bread dough and frightened animals and her son Tobiah when he came home from the ferry with things he couldn't say aloud.

Tobiah had gone back to work, after. There had been a hearing about the extra minute he'd held the ferry against orders, and Corrigan's clerk had stood up to accuse him, and the clerk had gotten exactly as far as *the widow's crates* before the new magistrate—there was a new magistrate already, young and nervous and scrubbed—had reminded him gently that the man whose orders were being invoked was currently awaiting assizes for drowning witnesses. Tobiah kept the apple, though. He kept it on the mantel at the ferry house, gone brown and wrinkled now, a relic of mercy with teeth. He said he meant to keep it until it was dust, and then keep the dust.

"Your graft tree's flowering early," Marta said, sitting back on her heels and wiping her forehead with her wrist. "Look."

Tamsin looked. The old tree stood at the orchard's heart, the one beneath which Daniel had buried the ledger in a tin box wrapped in oilcloth, the one whose bark bore the double scar of his grafting knife—a slash and counter-slash, his mark, the mark he'd cut into every tree he ever saved. It was covered in buds so tight they seemed to hum. A few had already broken open along the south face, showing white.

"He'd have liked that," Tamsin said. "Flowering before anything else dared."

"Daniel liked being first at everything," Marta said, and there was no venom in it, and no sweetness either. Just the flat, settled truth of a woman who had known him thirty years and had spent the winter learning that knowing someone is not the same as knowing them at all.

When Marta left—she had bread rising, she always had bread rising—Tamsin stayed.

She sat down against the graft tree's trunk with her back to the bark and took out the letter.

* * *

She had written it over three nights, which was two nights more than she'd needed for her testimony. Words for a courtroom came easier than words for a grave; the court had rules you could lean on, but a letter to a dead man had none, and every sentence threatened to become either an accusation or an absolution, and she wanted neither. She had promised herself neither.

In the end she had written it plain, the way she wrote the logbook, because plainness was the only honesty she trusted anymore.

*Daniel.*

*I am writing this at the kitchen table with the lamp trimmed low, which is how you liked to write, though you never liked me watching you do it. Now I know why. Now I know what you were drafting at that table while I trimmed the lamp and thought us the two most honest people on this coast.*

*I will not tell you I forgive you, because forgiveness is a debt and I don't know the sum. I will not tell you I condemn you, because I loved you, and love is also a fact, and the court made me swear to tell the whole truth and nothing but, and I have decided this letter is under oath too.*

*Here is what I know now, laid out the way you laid out your ledgers, neat and numbered, because you taught me that numbers are kinder than adjectives:*

*One. You took the money. Four years of it, starting small, a ferryman's bribe here, a customs favor there, until the small crimes had built you a road straight to the large ones.*

*Two. You drowned him. The boy from Blackrock Point, the witness, the one whose name is now carved in stone in the capital and spoken aloud in courtrooms. You rowed him out in weather you knew would kill him, and you rowed back alone, and you came home to me and ate supper and asked about my day.*

*Three. You turned. Whatever broke in you that night—and I believe something did break, I have read your confession eleven times and I hear it breaking—you turned, and you spent three years gathering the evidence that ended him. You died for it. Or rather, you lived for it another year and then drowned, and whether that drowning was accident or penance or murder, the sea knows and won't say.*

*Four. I kept your papers hidden for seven years because I thought I was protecting your memory. I wasn't. I was protecting myself from having to read them. That is the last true thing I have to learn about you, and I learned it late, and I'm sorry for the people who waited those seven years while I polished a memory instead of opening a chest.*

*Five. I loved you. That stands. The court didn't strike it from the record and neither will I.*

*The trees are waking. Marta brought cuttings from your graft tree and we planted six new ones this morning, and if even two live it will be two more than you deserved and exactly as many as you hoped for, because I found your planting notes, Daniel. I found them in the logbook binding, tucked behind your confession, and in among the dates and the stock names you wrote one line I have read a hundred times:*

*"Plant for the person who comes after."*

*I am the person who came after. This letter is for the orchard, and the orchard is for whoever comes next.*

*Tamsin*

* * *

She read it once more under the budding tree, her lips moving without sound, checking it the way she checked a lamp mechanism—not for beauty but for function. Did it hold? Would it bear weight?

It would. It was not kind and it was not cruel. It was just the truth delivered home, like a parcel returned to sender after seven years dead-lettered.

She dug.

The same spot where the tin box had lain—the earth there was still subtly different, darker, looser, as if the ground remembered being opened and had never quite closed its mind about it. She went down two feet with the spade and then knelt and used her hands, because some things should be buried by hand or not at all. The soil was cold and smelled of iron and rot and, underneath all that, faintly, absurdly, of apples—the ghost of a thousand fallen fruit composted into the very structure of the dirt.

She wrapped the letter in oilcloth, because Daniel would have appreciated the symmetry, and because the coast taught you that nothing unprotected survived.

"Here," she said aloud, to the roots, to him, to whatever part of a man stays in the place he worked. "Delivered."

She packed the earth down with her palms. It took only minutes. Seven years of dread and three days of composition, and the burial itself was the work of ten fingers and a quarter hour. That was the joke of it, she thought, sitting back on her heels in the warming sun. All that weight, and the ground barely noticed.

A petal came loose somewhere above her—one of the half-opened buds surrendering early—and drifted down and landed on the fresh-turned soil, white on black.

She left it there.

* * *

The lighthouse stood at the end of the point the way it had stood for sixty years, white tower, black gallery, the lantern room at top like a held breath. Tamsin climbed it that evening for the last time of the season, taking the hundred and twelve steps slowly, her hand on the rail worn smooth by a century of keepers' palms, hers and Daniel's and the two keepers before him whose names were in the oldest logbooks.

The season was ending. Not by rule—the commission's calendar said the light burned until June—but by choice, hers, declared in a letter she had posted that morning to the district office. The fishing fleets had moved on. The river road was open. The traffic the light existed to warn and guide had thinned to nothing, and the oil was dear, and she was tired in a way that sleep did not touch, and she had decided—for the first time in seven years, for the first time since the night she'd lit the lamp out of fear that darkness would look like guilt—that the light would go dark because she chose it, not because anything forced her hand.

There was a difference. She had learned the difference the hard way, and she intended to keep it.

In the lantern room the great lens stood gleaming, brass and crystal, Daniel's care still visible in it, her own care layered over his like the graft wood over the stock. She opened the valve and let the flame gutter down, watched it shrink from a beacon to a candle to a blue bead to nothing, and stood a moment in the sudden strangeness of the unlit glass—the sea visible through the panes for the first time in months, actual water, actual horizon, instead of fire and reflection.

Then she shuttered the lantern, banked the works, oiled what needed oiling, and wrote the entry in the logbook—the new logbook, clean-paged, Daniel's confession long since surrendered to the court along with everything else.

*April 29th. Lamp extinguished for the season. By choice of the keeper. All in order.*

She signed it. Below her signature, after a moment, she added a line no regulation required:

*Coast clear. Nothing left hidden.*

* * *

That night she slept in the cottage with the window open to the sound of the sea, which had spent all winter as an enemy and was only, when you got down to it, a large animal going about its business. The wind came off the water mild, smelling of thaw and kelp and the far-off green of the orchard, and it moved the curtain in and out, in and out, like breathing.

She slept without dreaming of storms.

She slept without dreaming of much of anything, and woke once in the grey middle of the night not to fear but to a sound she couldn't place, and lay listening, and understood after a while that it was the orchard—the trees talking to each other in the wind, the way they did, a dry soft rustle like pages turning, like a ledger being read from beginning to end by someone who already knew how it went and was reading it anyway, for the pleasure of the plain true words.

She pulled the blanket up. On the table by the window sat a letter that had arrived with the evening post, forwarded through three hands, its envelope soft with travel. The handwriting was Elias Wren's, careful and slanted, and the postmark was the capital.

She did not open it that night. Some letters want daylight, and she had spent enough of her life reading important things by lamplight, in the dark, alone.

Morning would do. Morning, and tea, and the long slow work of finding out who she was when she was no longer a keeper of secrets—or a keeper of lights—but simply Tamsin, of the coast, of the orchard, a woman with dirt still under her fingernails from burying the truth where it could finally grow into something.

Outside, the graft tree held its white buds up to the dark like small lamps, waiting for the sun to light them.
