## 1. The Escapement

The summons came on a Tuesday morning, which Rosalind Nystrom would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were always the day things went wrong.

She knew this because she kept a small ledger of her own—not of accounts, but of failures. Every clock that came into Soren's shop broken got an entry: the date, the complaint, and in her neat, narrow hand, the verdict. *Stopped since Sunday. Mainspring set.* *Chimes struck eleven at noon; cam wheel slipped.* And beneath nearly every Tuesday entry for the past three years, some variation of the same note: *Owner wept.*

People did not bring you clocks when the clocks were working. They brought them when something had gone out of joint in their lives—when the kitchen had gone quiet in a way that felt like grief, when the mantel looked bare, when the whole rhythm of a household had stumbled and no one could say why. They came in on Tuesdays especially, after a weekend of noticing.

So when the boy in regimental blue appeared at the shop door just past nine, stamping snow off his boots and holding out an envelope sealed with the crest of the Vellmark garrison, Rosalind thought: *Tuesday. Of course.*

"Message for the clockmaker," the boy said.

"That's my master," said Soren from his bench by the window, not looking up. He was seventy-one years old and had been not looking up at interruptions for roughly sixty of those years. His loupe sat screwed into one eye like a second pupil, and his hands—liver-spotted, trembling faintly, and yet steadier than any surgeon's in the city once they touched metal—were deep in the guts of a carriage clock that had belonged to somebody's late aunt and had apparently decided to join her.

The boy shifted his weight. "It says for the clockmaker. The observatory clockmaker."

Soren did look up then. So did Rosalind. The observatory kept its own man—a fussy, dignified creature named Herr Vollmer who wore a white coat and spoke of the master clock the way bishops speak of cathedrals. He did not send for outside help. He did not, as far as anyone knew, admit that outside help existed.

"Open it," Soren said, and because the envelope was addressed to *the clockmaker* without qualification, and because Rosalind was standing closest, she opened it.

She read it twice. The first time for the words. The second time for what was between them.

*To the journeyman responsible for horological works at the establishment of Soren & Sons. The Observatory Master Clock has ceased regulation. Herr Vollmer is unable to determine the fault. You are requested to attend today. By order of the Military Governor's office.*

"Unable to determine the fault," she read aloud, mostly to hear how it sounded.

"Hm," said Soren.

"It doesn't say apprentice."

"Hm."

"It says *journeyman responsible*." She was aware she was smiling and made an effort to stop, and failed. "Soren, they think I'm a journeyman."

"I expect they think whoever answers the door is a journeyman." But there was a crease at the corner of his mouth that meant he was pleased, and he went back to the dead aunt's carriage clock, and Rosalind stood there holding the governor's seal warm in her hand, feeling something she would not have a name for until much later: the particular, dangerous sweetness of being taken seriously by people who had never bothered to learn your name.

* * *

The observatory sat on Kessel Hill like a stone onion—the dome on top, the instrument rooms below, and at the very base, in a room most visitors never thought to enter, the master clock.

Everyone in Vellmark obeyed this clock whether they knew it or not. It regulated the regimental bells, which regulated the drill yards, the mess halls, the changing of the gate guard, the firing of the noon gun. It regulated the trains, or what remained of the train schedule in the third winter of the war. It regulated, if you wanted to be exact about it—and Rosalind wanted always to be exact about it—the pulse of the entire city. When the master clock ran true, Vellmark ran true. For months now, by every account, Vellmark had been running anywhere from four minutes slow to seven minutes fast, drifting like a ship with a tired helmsman, and nobody could say why.

Rosalind climbed the hill through snow that squeaked underfoot, her tool case heavy on her shoulder, and presented herself at the service door as instructed. A sergeant with a mustache like a wet brush admitted her, looked at her as though she might be carrying contraband inside the tool case—which, in a sense, she was; she was carrying competence, and the garrison had learned to be suspicious of anything unaccounted for—and led her down a stone stair that grew warmer and quieter with each step, until they arrived at a door marked AUTHORIZED PERSONS ONLY in letters that had been repainted so many times the ONLY had begun to eat the surrounding plaster.

Herr Vollmer was waiting inside, and he was not pleased to see her.

"You," he said. He was a tall man gone stooped, with the aggrieved expression of someone whose authority has been questioned by reality itself. "They sent *you*. I asked for the guild."

"The guild sent me."

"The guild sent a—" He stopped himself, which took visible effort, recalibrating his sentence the way one recalibrates a balance wheel. "How old are you?"

"Old enough to read your work order, which says the clock loses four minutes a day, variably." She set her case down on the floor—it landed with the solid, honest clack of good tools—and began unbuckling it. "Variable loss isn't a power problem. If the weights were short or the spring slack, it'd lose steady. Variably means something is interfering with the escapement intermittently. Something that comes and goes."

Vollmer drew himself up as far as his spine permitted. "I have maintained this instrument for nineteen years."

"Then you'll know I'm right," Rosalind said pleasantly, "in about twenty minutes."

She heard him inhale behind her—the long, gathering breath of a man preparing to explain to a young woman precisely where she stood in the hierarchy of the world—and she did not wait for it. She crossed to the great clock.

You did not see a master clock all at once. That was the first thing apprentices learned and civilians never understood: a clock was not an object, it was a decision, made over and over, thousands of times an hour, by parts too small to see from across a room. This one stood nine feet in its pine case, weights hanging patient as churchgoers, pendulum swinging with the deep unhurried calm of something that believed itself eternal. Up close, through the case glass, she could hear it—that soft, metronomic *tick... tock... tick*—and underneath it, faint as a whisper in another room, something else.

A hesitation. A tiny catch, maybe one beat in four hundred, like a speaker clearing their throat.

"There," she said.

"There what?" Vollmer snapped. "I hear nothing."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

An hour later, Herr Vollmer was silent, and Rosalind was lying on her back on a trestle table with her head inside the clock's movement, her left arm buried to the shoulder among wheels and levers, her right hand holding a jeweler's lamp at an angle that was making her shoulder scream. She had stopped feeling the screaming some while ago. Craft required a certain willingness to be uncomfortable; her father used to say a clockmaker's body was just a stand for the hands.

Her father. She put the thought away, the way she did. There was a drawer in her mind, walnut-faced, brass-cornered, and she was very good at closing it gently.

"Escaped tooth," she said, her voice echoing oddly off the movement plates. "Eleven teeth back from the locking face. See—" she withdrew her arm, sat up, hair full of dust, and held out the pallet assembly for Vollmer to inspect. On the pallet stone's working face, invisible to the naked eye, she'd laid down a film of marking blue and let the escapement run against it. The transfer showed a bright line where metal met metal that should not have met at all. "The escape wheel's tooth profile has worn a few ten-thousandths proud here, and the pallet stone has worn to match it, badly. Most cycles it locks clean. Every few hundred beats it catches, releases late, steals a fraction of impulse from the pendulum. Multiply a fraction by eighty-six thousand seconds a day and you get four minutes, give or take, depending on temperature, oil viscosity, and mood."

"Clocks do not have moods."

"All clocks have moods. This one sulks in cold weather—you'll notice it runs worse at night, when this room cools, because the wear gap opens up as the steel contracts." She turned the pallets in the lamplight. "Someone re-oiled this escapement within the last year with the wrong grade. Too thin. It ran beautifully for three months and quietly destroyed the pallet faces doing it."

Vollmer stared at the bright line on the stone. When he spoke again, all the cathedral-bishop grandeur had drained out of him, leaving something older and more tired. "I oiled it," he said. "In the spring. The supplier changed—I ordered the old grade and they sent the new one in the same tins, and it ran so sweetly afterward I congratulated myself." He sat down heavily on a stool meant for a much younger man. "Nineteen years. And the fault was my own hand."

Rosalind considered several replies. *It happens to everyone* was kind but false—she'd seen his maintenance logs, and his hand had been careless before. *Then you should have called sooner* was true and cruel. What she actually said, kneeling to pack her tools, was:

"You couldn't have found it. Not without stripping the escapement and laying the blue, and nobody strips a working master clock on a hunch. You needed the clock to fail badly enough to justify opening it, and by then the evidence had worn itself smooth." She glanced up. "Clocks are honest, but only if you catch them early. Same as people."

Vollmer made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. "And can it be repaired? The garrison will want it repaired. The governor—" He paused, and something flickered across his face, quick and unreadable, there and gone like the escapement's catch. "The governor's office has asked twice this month why the bells drift. They are not asking idly."

"I'll need to fit new pallet stones and dress two teeth on the wheel. Three days' work, if I can take the pallet assembly back to the shop. Four, if the stones in stock don't match." She stood, brushing dust from her apron, already running the job list in her head, already feeling the pleasant forward lean of a problem she knew how to solve. "I'll want the movement opened tonight to remove the assembly. Can you stay?"

"I can stay," said Vollmer, and then, unexpectedly: "Your name. For the log."

"Rosalind Nystrom."

His pen paused above the page. She watched him decide not to ask. Everyone in Vellmark knew the name Nystrom; it had been spoken in every officers' mess and every market square three years ago, wrapped in black cloth—*Captain Aldous Nystrom, lost with the column at Harrow Fen, a fine officer, a terrible waste.* She watched the recognition move through Vollmer's face like weather, and she watched him choose, courteously, to write only her given names.

She was grateful, and hated being grateful, and noted both in the ledger of herself without comment.

* * *

By lamplight, with Vollmer holding the lamp and asking questions he'd have been ashamed to ask in daylight, she opened the movement. It went the way good work goes: the case panels off, the dust covers lifted, the great going-train exposed like the skeleton of some patient whale. She loosened the pallet cock, eased the assembly free of its pivots, and cradled it in both hands—the heart of the city's time, light as a bird's ribcage.

"Extraordinary," Vollmer murmured. "All of this. Every bell, every schedule, every soldier waking and sleeping on the hour—all of it hangs on these two little stones."

"On everything and nothing," Rosalind said. "That's the trade entire. Nobody sees us. Nobody's supposed to. A clockmaker's whole ambition is that the world runs along for years without once thinking of her." She wrapped the assembly in flannel. "The best compliment my work ever gets is silence."

She did not add what she sometimes thought at night, working alone in the shop: that history was a great clock, too—that wars and governors and treaties were its striking train, loud and dramatic and impossible to ignore—but that somewhere inside it, unseen, small and worn and essential, an escapement was deciding beat by beat what happened next, and nobody ever wrote songs about the escapement.

She signed the work order. Vollmer walked her up to the service door, and the sergeant let her out into the dark, where the snow had thickened and the city below lay scattered with lamplight, all of it obedient, all of it keeping her fatherless time.

She was halfway down Kessel Hill, tool case on her shoulder, planning tomorrow's stone-fitting, when she noticed the figure standing in the lee of the observatory wall.

He stepped out as she passed—soaked to the knees, unshaven, with the look of a man who had been walking for days—and said, low and urgent, in a voice cracked with cold:

"Miss Nystrom. I need to know if you've ever seen this handwriting."
