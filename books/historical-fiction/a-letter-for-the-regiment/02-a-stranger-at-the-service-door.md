## 2. A Stranger at the Service Door

The rain had been falling on Vellmark since Tuesday, and by Thursday evening Rosalind Nystrom had stopped pretending she was going to do anything about it except listen to it.

She sat alone in the back of Nystrom & Soren's Clockworks—or rather, Soren & the Late Mr. Nystrom's Clockworks, though nobody had ever gotten around to repainting the sign—hunched over a carriage clock no bigger than her two fists, coaxing a bent balance spring back into true with tweezers that trembled only slightly from the cold. The shop's stove had gone out an hour ago and relighting it would mean leaving the bench, and leaving the bench was, in Rosalind's private philosophy, a moral failing.

"Three more teeth on the escape wheel," she murmured to the clock, because talking to clocks was another habit she saw no reason to break. "Three more teeth, and then you'll behave, and then we'll both go home."

The clock said nothing, which was one of its better qualities. Clocks were honest company. They never lied about the time, they never asked why a grown woman still lived above the shop where she'd grown up, and they never—unlike certain apprentices at the guild hall whose names began with Henrik—said things like *a girl clockmaker, how charming* as if charm were the entire point of a well-fitted pivot.

Outside, Vellmark settled into its wet winter dusk. Somewhere across the city the observatory's master clock was failing, she'd learned today—the great regulator that set every regimental bugle call, every sentry change, every bell in every church tower—and three separate men had been sent up to fix it and three separate men had come down shaking their heads. The guild had argued about it all afternoon through the workshop door, which was thin, like most things in the shop that weren't clocks. Eventually they'd decided on Rosalind, mostly, she suspected, because sending an apprentice meant admitting the problem might be small.

It wasn't small. She'd been up there once already, just to look, and something about the way the whole mechanism had gone subtly, stubbornly wrong had lodged under her skin like a splinter. The escapement wasn't merely worn. It was worn *wrong*—in a pattern she kept turning over in her mind the way one turns over a phrase in a foreign language, sure it means something, not yet sure what.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem was the balance spring, which chose that exact moment to slip her tweezers and vanish into the dark under the bench.

"Oh," said Rosalind, with feeling.

She was on her knees among the dust and dead watch parts when the knock came.

Not a customer's knock. Customers knocked at the front door, twice, politely, and if you didn't answer they came back in daylight like civilized people. This was the service door—the narrow one down the alley steps, the one used for deliveries of brass stock and for Soren slipping out to smoke his pipe where his wife couldn't count the pipes. It was past seven. Nobody delivered brass after seven in November.

The knock came again. Three raps, quick and uneven, like someone whose hands didn't entirely belong to him.

Rosalind stayed crouched under the bench for a long moment, listening. The rain filled the silence. Then, quietly, she took the poker from beside the cold stove—it was the nearest thing to a weapon the shop owned besides a very heavy mallet intended for case fittings—and went to the door.

"Shop's closed," she called through the wood.

"Please." A man's voice, cracked at the edges, pitched low. "Please, I need—I'm looking for someone who repairs clocks. I was told—" A pause, wet coughing. "I was told this was the best shop in Vellmark."

Flattery. Well. At least he had his priorities right.

"Best shop is closed. Come back at eight tomorrow."

"I can't come back tomorrow." The voice dropped further, urgent now, pressed close against the door. "Miss—Fraulein—if you're there, please. Five minutes. I'll stand in the doorway. I won't touch anything. I've been walking since dawn and I—" Another pause, longer, and when the voice came again it had changed, gone quieter and stranger. "I have letters. Letters I think someone here will want to see."

Rosalind stood with her hand on the latch and did not open it.

Every sensible thing she knew arranged itself in a neat row and spoke in unison: *strange man, alley, night, war's end coming apart at the seams, deserters and desertions and patrols, open nothing.* And beneath the row, quieter but older, her father's voice, from years ago, teaching her to hold a file: *A locked case protects what's inside, Rosie. But sometimes the trouble isn't inside. Sometimes it's whatever's knocking.*

He'd meant it about watches. He'd died before she could ask him what else he'd meant it about.

She opened the door.

The man who fell more than stepped into her lamplight was perhaps thirty, tall in the way of someone who'd been taller before the army got done feeding him badly, with a week's beard and a coat soaked through to a color the manufacturer would never have admitted to. Water ran off him onto the floorboards in a spreading dark map. His eyes were the worst part—not wild, exactly, but lit from somewhere behind, the way a lamp behind a curtain looks. The eyes of a man carrying something he cannot put down.

"You're not Soren," he said.

"Soren retired to his supper hours ago. I'm the clockmaker. Who are you?"

"Brandt." He said it like a man surrendering a weapon. "Elias Brandt. Late of the Ninth Regiment." A beat. "Very late."

Deserted. The word arrived without being spoken, the way some words do. Rosalind looked at him—at the shivering, at the ruined boots, at the lamplit desperation—and felt the familiar Vellmark arithmetic begin: *patrols are thick, penalties are harsh, my father's regiment, my father's—*

"Letters," she said. "You said letters."

His hand went inside his coat and came out with an oilcloth packet, soft-edged from handling, spotted with damp. He held it out. She took it. Her fingers were steady. She was rather proud of them for that, later.

She unwrapped the oilcloth on the bench, under the good lamp, while Elias Brandt stood dripping and watching her face with an intensity that should have been uncomfortable and instead felt oddly like being waited on.

There were four letters. Folded small, carried often—the creases worn to cloth, the paper gone soft as linen at the folds. And the handwriting—

Rosalind stopped breathing for a moment. It was a trick of hers, learned at her father's elbow: when something matters, stop everything else. The handwriting slanted forward, urgent even in rest; the capital N was drawn in two strokes with a little flourish on the second, like a man signing off a joke; the numerals were old-fashioned, the sevens crossed, the fours open-topped, the habits of a boy taught penmanship forty years ago and never persuaded out of them.

She had seen this handwriting every day of her childhood. On labels for parts drawers. On notes tucked into her schoolbooks. On the last letter she'd ever received, dated eleven days before the official notice of Captain Aldous Nystrom's death arrived, full of a joke about the mess coffee and a sketch of a gear train in the margin.

Three years dead. Three years of a grave with his name on it in the garrison cemetery, three years of her mother's quiet going-out like a lamp, three years of Rosalind learning to say *my father was a soldier* in the flat voice that ended conversations.

"This is a forgery," she said.

"It isn't."

"People forge dead officers' hands." She heard her own voice go cold and precise, the guild voice, the armor voice. "It happens. Sentiment sells. There's a trade in it, if you know where to look."

"Do I look like a sentiment merchant?" Brandt almost laughed; it came out broken. "Look at the dates, miss. Look at the postmarks. Look at—here." He leaned over the bench, dripping, pointing with a finger bitten raw at the corners. "That one. Read the last page."

She read it. She wished she hadn't.

*My dear Brandt—if you're reading this then either I am dead in truth or dead in name, and I find I can't tell yet which will be required. If the latter: forgive me the lie. It's a large one, and I've spent my life telling small truths so no one would expect the large ones. Don't let Rosie carry it. That's the whole of my asking. Whatever you learn, however it ends—don't let her carry it.*

*Rosie.* Nobody had called her Rosie since the funeral. She had retired the name herself, folded it away with his uniform buttons, buried it with proper honors.

Her hands were no longer steady. She noticed distantly, as if reporting on someone else's bench.

"He's alive," Brandt said softly. "Captain Nystrom is alive. I served under him two years, miss, and I carried these, and I know what I'm saying costs me my neck to say it, and I'm saying it anyway. He's alive."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere across the sleeping city the broken master clock missed the quarter hour, and every church bell in Vellmark rang slightly wrong, and nobody noticed but her.

"No," Rosalind said.

Brandt blinked water off his lashes. "...No?"

"No." She closed the oilcloth packet with exaggerated care, the way she handled a movement she suspected of being sabotaged. "My father is dead. I attended the funeral. I have seen the grave. I have spent three years being *the captain's daughter* in a city that says it gently, the way you speak to the bereaved, and I have built a life on top of that grave the way you build on any foundation—carefully, and without digging." She pushed the packet back across the bench toward him. "And you—a deserter, soaked through, appearing at my service door at night with four letters in a dead man's hand—want me to believe that foundation was a lie? That he let me mourn him? That he wrote *don't let her carry it* and then simply—left me carrying everything?"

"That's not—"

"Why?" It came out louder than she intended, and it surprised her, the size of it. "If he lived—why let me grieve?"

Brandt was silent a moment. Rain. Ticking. When he answered, his voice was gentle in a way that made it worse.

"I don't know, miss. I only know what he told me, and what he showed me, and that men were willing to kill over it. I know he chose the lie because the truth needed him dead to be believed. And I know he loved you—" He stopped himself, visibly recalibrating. "I know he spoke of you the way other men speak of God. With reverence, and mostly when drunk."

Something in Rosalind wanted terribly to laugh at that and something larger refused to permit it.

She wrapped the packet again. She slid it across the bench. She picked up the poker, not brandishing it, merely holding it, the way one holds a tool one intends to use.

"The shop is closed," she said. "You should go. There are patrols on Foundry Street after nine; take Cutter's Lane, the lamps are bad there but so are their horses' shoes. And take your letters, and your story, and don't come back."

"Miss—"

"*Don't come back.*" Her voice cracked on it, traitorously, and she hated him for hearing it, and hated her father more. "You want the sentimental daughter? The one who takes a stranger's word over a headstone? She died too, Herr Brandt. Same day as her father. What's left does escapements."

For a moment he just looked at her, rain still running off him, and there was something in his face—not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*—that she decided then and there never to think about again.

"He said you'd be like this," Brandt said quietly. "He said, *if she throws you out, wait anyway. She'll throw you out twice. The third time she'll listen.*" He gathered the packet, tucked it away inside his ruined coat, and moved toward the door. At the threshold he paused, silhouetted against the alley's wet dark. "The observatory clock, miss. The master clock. You've been up to see it—they talk about it at the guild. About how nobody can say what's wrong with it."

The cold that went through Rosalind had nothing to do with November.

"What about it?"

"Nothing," said Elias Brandt. "Only—he always did hide things in mechanisms. Said a clock was the one place in the world nobody ever looks, because everyone assumes it's only telling time." He managed something that was almost a bow. "Goodnight, Fraulein Nystrom. I'm sorry for the floor."

Then he was gone, down the alley steps into the rain, and she bolted the service door behind him with hands that shook now, thoroughly, past any pretense of steadiness.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She sat at the bench until the lamp burned low and then lit another, and the carriage clock with its three bad teeth sat unfinished in front of her, and the shape of the evening turned itself over and over like a part under inspection.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the flaw in her own fine work: she had thrown him out, yes. But she had looked at those letters first. And in the thirty seconds she'd held them, before the anger arrived to save her, some part of her—some part with her father's own slanting hand—had known. Not hoped. *Known*, the way she knew a wheel was true by its sound.

The capital N in two strokes. The crossed sevens. The joke hiding in the margin.

She thought about the observatory clock, and its wrongness, its worn-wrong pattern, and the phrase Brandt had left behind like a burr in wool: *everyone assumes it's only telling time.*

At half past midnight, Rosalind Nystrom blew out the lamp, climbed the stairs to the rooms above the shop, and did not go to bed. Instead she dragged a chair to the window overlooking the alley, wrapped herself in her mother's old quilt, and kept watch on the dark and the rain—telling herself firmly that she was guarding the shop from a possible thief, and knowing, in the honest part of herself that she usually kept locked like a case, that she was waiting to see if a soaked deserter would come back a second time.

He had said she'd throw him out twice.

She fell asleep at the window somewhere near three, and dreamed of a margin full of gear trains, and woke at dawn with the quilt around her shoulders and frost ferning the glass—and with the distinct, unwelcome certainty that tomorrow, or the day after, she was going to walk back up to the observatory and start taking the master clock apart to find out what her father had hidden inside it.

Assuming, of course, that she could get there before anyone else did.
