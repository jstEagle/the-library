## 3. What the Governor Wants

The patrol passed below the bridge at half past eleven by the church bells, which Elias Brandt had learned to trust more than any watch he'd ever owned, because a church bell had no reason to lie to him and every watch in Vellmark seemed to have been purchased by someone with an interest in his capture.

He counted them. Six soldiers, muffled to the eyes, boots crunching the frozen ruts of the Towpath Road. A lantern at front, one at rear, swinging in that unhurried way that meant they were walking a circuit, not hunting a man. Hunting men was faster.

Elias lay flat on the bridge's underside, arms hooked over a beam slick with frost, and watched them through the gap between two planks. Snow sifted down from the roadway above and settled on his neck with the patience of something that intended to bury him eventually and saw no hurry about it.

*Third circuit tonight,* he thought. *They know someone's moving after curfew.*

When the last boot-crunch faded, he let out a breath he'd been holding so long it had gone stale, dropped eight feet into the snow beneath the bridge—knees, then shoulder, then dignity—and lay there a moment looking up at the stars, which were indifferent, and the clouds, which were worse than indifferent.

"You're getting good at this," he told himself quietly. "I want you to know I've noticed."

Getting good at desertion. There was a sentence he could never have imagined composing three years ago, back when Private Elias Brandt of the 7th Vellmark Rifles still believed that orders came down from heaven by way of the quartermaster's office, and that the quartermaster's office was therefore a sort of chapel.

---

He had not meant to become a deserter. He wanted that written down somewhere, in a ledger if possible, because ledgers were what had ruined him and ledgers ought to have the decency to explain themselves.

He had been a clerk. That was the whole of his soldiering: a clerk in a regimental office, with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for neat columns and a conviction, held the way a boy holds a religion, that numbers did not lie. Numbers couldn't lie. They were the honest bones under all the meat of military language—the *provisions rendered*, the *stores expended*, the *wastage due to spoilage*. You wrote the truth down in figures and the figures kept it safe.

Then, on the fourth of November, Captain Verey had handed him a stack of ledgers—three years of them, the whole supply record for the regiment—and ordered him to burn them.

"Reorganization," Verey said. He had a face like a closed drawer. "New system coming. Old records are obsolete."

Obsolete. Three years of grain requisitions, coal deliveries, boot contracts, medical stores. Obsolete the way a witness is obsolete.

Elias had stood there holding them, feeling the weight of the paper, and had done the arithmetic without meaning to—he always did the arithmetic without meaning to—and found that the numbers in those books did not match the numbers in the copies he himself had made each month for the governor's audit office. Someone upstream had been reporting one set of figures and living by another. Someone had been feeding the regiment paper bread while selling real bread somewhere else, and the difference, compounded over three winters, was enough to keep a battalion fat while men at the front froze in boots whose leather cracked like biscuit.

He knew because he'd repaired those boots. He'd been detailed to stores one week in his second winter, sitting among crates stamped *LEATHER, SOLE, 200 PAIRS*, opening them to find straw and stones and, in one memorable case, bricks wrapped in boot-leather shavings, arranged with a care that almost amounted to craftsmanship. Bricks. Somebody had packed bricks by hand, pair by pair, and signed a manifest saying they were boots, and some clerk above him—or beside him, or exactly like him—had countersigned it, and the bricks had marched off to war in place of men's feet.

So when Captain Verey said *burn them*, Elias said "Yes, sir," carried the ledgers to the incinerator shed like an obedient man, and then sat down in the cold among the coal sacks and did not burn them.

It had felt, in the moment, less like courage than like a failure of obedience. As though some part of him had simply refused to fire, the way a rifle refuses—a small internal click where the spark should be.

He hid the ledgers in the false bottom of a ration cart and deserted that same night, which was cowardice or sense depending on who told the story, and he had stopped asking which. The captain was alive because the ledgers existed; the ledgers existed because the captain had known, years ago, that they would need to exist. Everything since had been Elias following a dead-seeming man's foresight through the dark, one hand on the wall.

---

The city received him now as it received all its ghosts: without comment. He moved along the drainage channel behind the tanneries, where the smell covered a man's trail better than any snow, and thought about warmth the way other men thought about God.

Vellmark in December was a city holding its breath. Banners for the armistice delegation hung frozen stiff on their poles, curved like question marks. Every wall wore new notices in the governor's tidy typeface: CURFEW AT NINE. REPORT STRANGERS. THE CITY'S SECURITY IS EVERY CITIZEN'S HONOR. Elias read that last one every time he passed it and marveled at the craft of it. Not *your* honor. *Every citizen's honor*—so that informing on your neighbor became a kind of civic hygiene rather than a betrayal, and betrayal became cleanliness.

Governor Halvard. Even the name sounded like a door closing.

Elias had seen him twice, both times at review parades, a broad silver-haired man with a voice built for parade grounds and eyes built for account books. The men loved him. That was the terrible thing. He remembered their names, asked after their mothers, distributed medals with both hands. And all the while—Elias was nearly certain, as near as a clerk could be without the final page—the governor's own office was where the second set of figures went to die. The stolen grain, the phantom boots, the coal that evaporated between depot and front: it flowed uphill, and everything uphill in Vellmark flowed to Halvard.

Which was why the notices on the walls were about strangers, and why six-man patrols walked the towpath at midnight, and why the observatory clock—the great master clock that set every regimental schedule in the garrison, reveille to retreat, every drumbeat of the city's day—had been allowed to fall out of true until no one trusted it.

That last part was the strange note in the music. Elias had puzzled over it for weeks. A garrison city does not let its master clock fail. Time is discipline; discipline is power; Halvard breathed power the way other men breathed air. Yet the clock had drifted—minutes, then quarter hours, then worse—and the governor's response had been not repair but *seizure*. Rumor said he intended to take the observatory before the armistice, "for security." Security against what? Against time itself?

Unless the clock wasn't broken.

Unless the clock was *hiding something*, and Halvard suspected it, and wanted the mechanism in his own hands—not running, but opened. Dismantled. Searched.

Captain Aldous Nystrom had been a clockmaker's son before he was a soldier. Everyone in the regiment knew it; he'd repaired the officers' watches as a hobby, sat in the mess with a loupe screwed in his eye like a cheerful pirate. And in the last letter he'd ever sent through channels—before the official dispatch announcing his death at the Kessel crossing, a death with no body returned, no grave, no effects beyond a kit bag that weighed wrong—there had been a line that had seemed like sentiment at the time:

*A man can hide things in plain sight if he builds the hiding place into the very order of things. What is more orderly than time?*

Elias had carried that letter folded against his chest through two winters of hiding. It was the reason he'd come to the observatory first, the night he reached Vellmark. It was the reason he stood shivering in a drainage channel now instead of a warm cell with a trial pending, which would at least have featured soup.

Because the captain had hidden copies in the tower. Copies of everything—the ledgers, the manifests, the correspondence, the whole rotten architecture of it, brick-by-brick documented. Elias was sure of it the way you're sure of a load-bearing wall: knock anywhere else and the house falls, but that wall holds.

And the captain had also left letters. Letters that had begun arriving, impossibly, in the hands of couriers who vanished before questions could be asked—letters in a hand Elias knew intimately, having filed three years of the man's reports, a hand like iron railings, upright and precise with sudden flourishes. Letters that named the observatory. Letters that said, in the captain's dry way: *If you are reading this, Brandt, you have finally done something useful. Go to the clock. Tell no one. Trust the person who keeps it running, if anyone still does.*

*Trust the person who keeps it running.* Which implied someone did. Which implied the clockmaker of Vellmark Observatory was either ignorant, complicit, or—most dangerous of all possibilities—an ally.

---

A dog barked somewhere near the granary, and Elias pressed himself into the shadow of a buttress and reviewed his sins while waiting, as was his habit. Fear, he'd discovered, sharpened best when whetted on inventory.

Item: he had abandoned His Majesty's service. True, but the service had abandoned the men first, in crates of bricks.

Item: he was abroad after curfew in a city under martial law, punishable by detention without charge, and the governor had recently demonstrated that *without charge* was not a figure of speech.

Item: he had broken into the observatory once already—two nights past, through the coal cellar, finding the tower locked and patrolled and himself too frozen to pick a lock with fingers like sausages. He'd left nothing but melted snow on the stairs and a growing conviction that he needed help.

Item, and this was the item that mattered: he had shown a letter to a stranger.

Not entirely a stranger. He'd learned her name from the guild rolls posted outside the hall—*Rosalind Nystrom, apprentice, clockwork*—and the surname had gone through him like a bayonet. Nystrom. The captain's daughter, apprenticed to the very trade the captain had inherited and abandoned, keeping the very clock where the captain had hidden the proof. Either the most astonishing coincidence in the history of supply corruption, or the captain had planned even this—had placed the evidence within reach of the one person alive who could read both the mechanism and the man.

Elias had knocked on her workshop door after midnight like a fool, soaked through, holding out the letters, and had managed to say approximately four sentences before she threw him into the street. He could hardly blame her. From her side of the door: a wild-eyed man at night claiming her dead father lived, waving papers. In any city on earth that ended with a constable. In Halvard's Vellmark it ended with a constable and possibly a ditch.

But she had looked at the letters. Before the door shut, he'd seen her face change—the particular collapse of a person whose floor gives way—and he had seen her look at the handwriting, really look, the way a craftsman looks.

She would come to the observatory. She had to. The summons would come for the clock—it was already coming, rumor said the governor's commission was being drawn up—and Rosalind Nystrom would climb those stairs to the mechanism her father had touched, and she would find, sooner or later, whatever he'd left inside it. The only question was whether Elias got there first, whether he could make her listen, and whether the governor's men arrived before either of them understood what they were holding.

He checked the sky. Clouds moving in off the marshes, low and gray, promising snow by morning. Snow was good. Snow erased. Snow was the deserter's friend and the deserter's enemy, depending which side of it you were standing on.

He set off toward the observatory hill through the back lanes, counting turns by the bells—midnight now, single slow stroke rolling across the rooftops, the whole city hearing the same beat and obeying it, bakers and soldiers and sleeping children all held in one great mechanical hand. That was the thing about a master clock that he'd only understood since coming here. It didn't tell the time. It *made* the time. Every schedule, every patrol rotation, every drum signal, every train movement—all of it derived from that one tower. Whoever controlled the clock controlled the shape of everyone's day.

Captain Nystrom had hidden the truth inside the instrument that governed the city. Not in a vault, not across the border, not buried in the marshes. Inside time itself, where no one would think to look, because no one looks inside the thing that tells them what time it is. It was either genius or the arrogance of a clockmaker's son, and Elias had long since concluded it was both.

The hill rose ahead of him, black against blacker cloud, the observatory dome faintly pale with frost like a skull thinking its long thoughts. Lights in the lower windows—someone working late, or the watch, or both. He circled wide, found the old stonemason's cut in the hillside that the maps called a footpath and the snow called a grave, and began to climb.

Somewhere below, faintly, a whistle blew. Once, twice—the patrol changing shift, probably. Probably.

Elias put his hand flat against the frozen rock, took a breath that burned going in, and climbed toward the clock.

*Trust the person who keeps it running,* the letter said.

"All right, Captain," he murmured to the dark. "But if your daughter throws me off that tower, I'm haunting you specifically."

Above him, through the wind, he thought he heard—though it might have been the wind, it was probably the wind—the tick of the great escapement, patient as conscience, waiting for someone to open the case.
