## 4. Handwriting

Rosalind did not sleep.

She told herself it was because the shop was cold — Soren kept the stove banked low after nine, and the workshop's north-facing windows let in a draft that had opinions about her ankles — but she knew better. The truth was sitting in her apron pocket, wrapped in oilcloth like something contagious: three letters from a dead man.

She had thrown Elias Brandt out into the rain with all the dignity of an apprentice defending her master's doorstep. She had called him a liar, a thief, and — when he'd hesitated on the step, dripping, looking at her with those terrible patient eyes — "a disgrace to whatever regiment was fool enough to take you." He had not argued. He had only pressed the letters into her hands as she shoved him backward, and said, quietly, so that the rain nearly took the words:

"Read them first. Then throw me out again if you still want to."

She had slammed the door. She had bolted it. She had stood with her forehead against the wood listening to his footsteps fade down the cobbles, and then, after a decent interval of perhaps four seconds, she had unwrapped the oilcloth under the lamp.

Now it was past two in the morning, and the lamp had burned through most of its oil, and the letters lay on her workbench beside her father's old notebook, and Rosalind Nystrom was doing what she always did when the world refused to make sense: she looked very closely at very small things.

* * *

Her father had been gone three years. Officially dead — that was the word the army used, *officially*, stamped in red ink on a form Soren had signed with a shaking hand before passing it to her without explanation. Killed in the retreat at Kolvary, the notice said. No body recovered, which happened, which was common, which meant nothing except that there had been no body to bury and so no grave to visit and so nowhere to put any of it. Grief with no address. She had learned to carry it the way one carries a clock that has stopped: heavily, uselessly, but because it was his, you did not simply set it down.

The handwriting on the envelopes — for they were envelopes, sealed ones, addressed in a slanting, impatient script — was addressed to *A.B.* No name. Just initials, and inside each envelope several pages folded to the size of a playing card.

Rosalind had not yet opened them. That was tomorrow's problem, or rather tonight's later problem, because first she intended to settle a simpler question: whose hand was this?

She opened her father's notebook to a page she knew by heart. He had kept it in the workshop drawer for years, back when he still worked at the bench beside Soren instead of marching off to be a captain of supply wagons — a strange promotion everyone in the shop had pretended made sense. The notebook was full of gear calculations, sketches of escapements, and marginal notes in a hand Rosalind could have identified blindfolded, by smell, underwater.

*Escapement too deep-cut. Soren says leave it. I never leave anything.*

That was the note she chose. It had always made her smile — her father, arguing with time itself through the medium of a pencil.

She laid one of the envelopes beside it and bent close.

The letters were small and forward-leaning, with that peculiar impatience in the crossbars — every *t* struck through as though the writer were annoyed at having to write it at all. The *y* descended in a straight line with no curl, ending abruptly, like a man who had decided mid-stroke that flourishes were for other people. And the numerals — she turned to a page of her father's calculations to check — the numeral 4 was open at the top, never closed, never once closed in forty pages of figures, and here in the address on the third envelope, in a house number or a reference code, an open-topped 4 sat as unmistakable as a signature.

Her chest went tight.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty workshop, in the reasonable tone she used on recalcitrant mainsprings. "No. Coincidence. Half the officers in the regiment learned their letters from the same schoolmasters."

It was a good argument. She gave it its due. She even tested it: she pulled out a letter from Lieutenant Voss — who had corresponded with Soren about a carriage clock — and compared. Voss wrote round, comfortable letters, closed his fours, curled his ys like a man patting a dog. Nothing alike. She tried a requisition form in another officer's hand from the same file. Different again.

She tried to think of some way a forger would work. A good forger, she reasoned, studied the model. But a forger copies what he sees; these letters contained things a casual copyist would never think to reproduce. The little hesitation-mark where her father's pen always stuttered on the word *regiment* — there it was, twice, in the same place, a tiny double-stroke like a pulse. The habit of crossing out a wrong figure by striking through it sideways rather than horizontally. Nobody invents those details. Those details are worn into a hand the way a key is worn into a lock — by years, by repetition, by a life.

Rosalind set down the magnifier.

Her hands were steady. Clockmaker's hands were always steady; that was the first thing Soren had ever taught her, *steady hands, girl, the work doesn't care about your feelings.* She was grateful for it now, because everything behind her hands felt like a mainspring wound far past its limit, coiled and trembling and waiting for release.

"If he's alive," she said slowly, working it through the way she'd talk through a mechanism, "then the army lied. Or the army was lied to. Either way—" Her voice caught on something mechanical and unhelpful. "Either way I spent three years—"

Three years of setting a place at the table out of habit and then feeling foolish. Three years of keeping his coat brushed in the back room, mothballs and cedar, telling herself it was sentiment and knowing it was hope wearing a disguise. Three years of being *the dead man's daughter*, which in Vellmark was a kind of title, a small pension of sympathy people paid out with awkward pats on the shoulder.

And he had let her.

If this was true — if Captain Aldous Nystrom had drawn breath somewhere in the world while she lit a memorial candle every winter's first frost — then he had chosen it. Chosen her grief like a man choosing a tool. Left it lying on the bench between them and walked away.

Anger arrived the way anger does when it has been waiting politely behind grief for a long while: all at once, and with excellent aim. She snatched up the nearest letter and nearly tore it open right there — stopped herself, barely, because tearing evidence was a fool's instinct, and because some stubborn craftsman part of her insisted that if you opened a thing you should open it properly, cleanly, along the fold, respecting the maker's work even when the maker was a liar and a coward and —

and her father.

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw gears in the dark.

"Steady hands," she whispered. "The work doesn't care about your feelings."

But it wasn't work. That was the whole trouble. For the first time in her adult life, the thing on her bench was not a mechanism, and mechanisms, at least, could be trusted to behave according to laws. People broke every law she knew.

* * *

She opened the letters at half past three.

Not all of them. The first one only. She slit the seal with her finest graver, the blade she reserved for balance springs, and unfolded the pages under the lamp with the reverence she might have given a document three hundred years old, though the paper smelled only of damp and pipe smoke.

It was not a letter, exactly. Not in the sense of *Dear Rosalind, forgive me.* It was a report. Columns of figures. Dates, wagon numbers, quantities of grain and boot leather and rifle oil — quantities that had been issued, and quantities that had actually arrived, and the difference between the two written in a firm hand in the margin with little arrows connecting the discrepancies. At the bottom of the second page, beneath the columns, a single line of prose:

*The trail runs higher than the depot. If anything happens to me, the rest is where a careful eye will find it. Trust the mechanism, not the men.*

Rosalind read it four times. The words swam and reassembled. *Trust the mechanism, not the men.*

Her father had said things like that at the bench. Once, when she was eleven and crying over a ruined fusee, he had knelt beside her and said, *the clock will tell you the truth even when people won't, Rosie — that's why I love them.* She had thought it was comfort for a child. Apparently it had also been instruction.

The second and third letters stayed sealed. She couldn't. Not yet. Her anger needed somewhere to stand, and unopened envelopes were solid ground; whatever was inside them might knock her over entirely. She stacked them neatly, squared their edges out of pure habit, and wrapped them back in the oilcloth.

Then she sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which for Rosalind Nystrom was the loudest possible activity.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the north windows. Down in the street the milk cart rattled past, and old Frau Behm next door began her morning argument with her shutters, and the city of Vellmark woke up around her as if nothing whatsoever had happened — as if the world were still the shape it had been yesterday, when her father was dead and her grief had an address after all, even if the address was nowhere.

Soren came shuffling down at six, as he always did, tying his apron on the stairs. He stopped in the doorway of the workshop and looked at her — at the lamp burned dry, at the papers squared on the bench, at her face, which she tried to arrange into something neutral and failed.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I worked."

"On what?"

"A difficult mechanism," Rosalind said.

He came closer, peering at the bench, and she saw the exact moment he recognized the notebook — his old partner's notebook, three years untouched in the back of the drawer — and she watched something move behind his eyes, quick and guilty as a mouse behind wainscoting.

"Where did you get those?" he asked quietly.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters a great deal." Soren lowered himself onto the stool across from her, slowly, the way he did everything now, and folded his hands. For a moment he looked very old, older than she had ever allowed herself to see. "Rosalind. Whatever you've found — whoever brought it to your door — you must think very carefully before you follow it. Some doors are locked for a reason."

"I know how locks work," she said. "I make keys."

"That," said Soren, "is precisely what frightens me."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she slid the first letter across the bench toward him, the one she'd already read, the columns and the margins and the single line of prose. "Look at the hand," she said. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me it isn't his. You knew his writing better than anyone alive — you shared a bench with him for twenty years."

Soren looked at the letter for a long time. His thumb moved along the edge of the paper, back and forth, a small repetitive motion like a man winding something invisible.

"It's his," he said finally. "Open-topped fours. Stutter on *regiment*. God help us, it's his."

"So he's alive."

"I don't know what he is." Soren's voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he cleared his throat and became brisk, which was how he hid. "I know that three years ago a notice came, and I signed it, and I grieved him, and I did not ask questions, because asking questions in Vellmark these days is a hobby with a short life expectancy. And I know that Governor Halvard's office sent a clerk round just yesterday asking after the observatory commission, and that men who faked their own deaths generally do it because someone wants them genuinely dead."

"Then someone should find out who."

"Yes," Soren agreed wearily. "Someone braver than me."

"And what am I supposed to do?" Rosalind stood, and the stool scraped back, and her voice came out harder than she intended, carrying three years of unsent letters of her own. "Keep my head down? Sign whatever they put in front of me? He taught me to open every case, Soren. Every case. He said a closed mechanism is either broken or hiding something, and both are the repairman's business."

"He also taught you that a spring under too much tension snaps," Soren said, "and takes your fingers with it."

They glared at each other across the bench, mentor and apprentice, and Rosalind realized with a small internal jolt that she had never done that before — never once, in eight years, raised her voice or her chin against old Soren. It felt terrible and necessary, like cutting a corroded screw free of its housing.

"I'm going back to the observatory tonight," she said. "The governor's commission stands whether I like it or not, and the clock needs me, and—" She hesitated. There was a second reason, and Soren deserved honesty even if the world didn't. "And the man who brought these said the rest of the proof is hidden in the tower. In the clock itself. *Trust the mechanism.*"

Something flickered across Soren's face — not surprise, she thought. Recognition. As if he had known, all along, exactly where Aldous Nystrom would hide a secret, because of course he would, where else, the sentimental impossible man, inside the one thing he loved more than his family.

"Then take the good lamp oil," Soren said at last, rising heavily and turning toward the storeroom. "And take the long-nosed pliers. And Rosalind—" He paused at the door without looking back. "Whatever you find in that tower, remember that your father loved you. Whatever else he is, whatever he's done — start from that. It's the only part that won't lie to you."

He shuffled out. Rosalind stood alone in the workshop with three sealed letters, a dry lamp, and a decision assembling itself inside her with the slow, inexorable precision of a weight driving a great wheel.

Tonight she would go back to the observatory. And if a certain deserter was waiting among the gears — soaked, desperate, and insufferably patient —

well. This time she intended to have questions.
