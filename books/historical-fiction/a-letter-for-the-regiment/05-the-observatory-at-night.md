## 5. The Observatory at Night

Rosalind climbed the observatory hill in the dark, and the dark was the honest kind—the kind that didn't pretend to be anything other than what it was. Snow squeaked under her boots. The wind came off the flats carrying the smell of frozen salt and coal smoke, and above her the observatory dome sat against the clouds like a great blind eye, patient, waiting for weather that would never clear enough for it to see anything again.

They had stopped using the telescope years ago. The army had no use for stars; the army had use for the clock.

The service door was unlocked, which was either slovenliness or an invitation, and Rosalind did not care to learn which. She let herself in, lit her lamp, and stood a moment in the stairwell while the flame steadied, listening to the building breathe. Old buildings breathed. This one breathed with a wheeze in its left lung, a draft somewhere on the second landing that she would have to find and kill before it found her solder joints.

Up the stairs. Through the door marked *TRANSIT INSTRUMENTS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL*, which she had decided, on her first visit, made her authorized, and through the second door, the one with no lock at all, into the clock room.

The master clock of Vellmark filled the far wall like a cathedral organ that had decided, after long reflection, to become furniture. Nine feet of walnut case, glass panels, a dial painted the white of a dead man's shirt. Behind the glass, the pendulum hung still. It had been still for eleven days, and in those eleven days the whole city had gone quietly mad—not the loud madness of riots, but the small madness of a thousand small arguments. *Was the noon gun late? Was the mail coach early? Is my watch fast or is the world slow?* A city without its clock was a city of ten thousand private times, and no two of them agreed.

"Right," Rosalind said to it. She set her lamp on the work ledge, unwound her scarf, and rolled her sleeves. "You and I are going to have a conversation."

Her father had taught her that. *Never talk to a mechanism as if it's deaf,* he'd say, chalking diagrams on the workshop floor while she sat cross-legged in the shavings. *It's not deaf. It's stubborn. There's a difference, and the difference is that stubborn things can be persuaded.* She had been seven. She had believed him absolutely. She believed him still, in the way you believe a dead man—without revision, without the possibility of his ever disappointing you by changing his mind.

She opened the case and put her ear to the going train.

Silence, but a particular silence. Not the silence of a broken thing. The silence of a thing holding its breath.

She'd diagnosed it from below, eleven days ago, in the first hour of her first visit: the escapement. Everyone else who had climbed these stairs—the regimental artificers, the governor's surveyor, a man from the telegraph office who claimed expertise on the strength of having once repaired a doorbell—had pronounced the fault to be the weight line, or the pendulum suspension, or, in the surveyor's case, "a general fatigue of the works," which was a diagnosis the way *it's raining* is a weather forecast. But she had put her fingers on the pallets and felt it: the anchor's right pallet arm worn past true, the locking faces rounded off like river stones. The escapement was letting the wheel escape by a hair and a half, and a hair and a half, multiplied by every tick of every day for forty years, becomes a lie.

She took out her file, her stones, her tin of fine oil, and began.

This was the part no one understood, the part that could not be explained at dinner parties even if anyone had ever invited her to one. The joy was not in the fixing. The joy was in the *knowing*—in being the one person in a city of forty thousand who could put her hand on the wound and say, *there, and there, and there.* History did not record clockmakers. History recorded generals, and treaties, and the dates of battles. But every one of those battles had been fought on time, more or less, because some nameless person had filed a pallet arm true. Rosalind Nystrom was twenty-two years old and she was nobody, and she was, at this moment, on this hill, in this room, the most necessary nobody in Vellmark.

She let herself have the feeling. She was entitled to it for as long as the lamp burned.

She had been working perhaps an hour—pallet arm clamped, stone whispering over steel in long even strokes, the sound so rhythmic she had half-forgotten she was making it—when she understood that the rhythm had a companion.

A breath. Behind the clock. In the gap between the case and the wall, where the weights hung in their channels.

She did not stop stropping the pallet. She let the stone finish its stroke, lift, return. Her heart was doing something unsporting in her chest, but her hands were hers, and her hands were the one thing in this world that had never once betrayed her.

"There's a poker by the stove," she said, without turning around. "I'd prefer you didn't make me fetch it. It's cold out and I'm behind schedule."

The silence went on long enough that she began to doubt herself. Then, from the shadow behind the case, a voice—rough, careful, the voice of a man who had been rehearsing this sentence for hours:

"You said I could search."

She turned.

He was sitting on the floor with his back against the clock's base casing, knees up, in the corner where the lamplight gave out. He'd made himself a nest of the tarpaulin that had covered the weights. His coat was still damp at the shoulders from three nights ago, from the rain he'd stood in outside her master's shop, from the door she'd shut in his face. He looked like he'd been living on the hill. He looked like he'd been living on very little of anything.

"You," Rosalind said, "are not supposed to be able to get in here."

"No," he agreed.

"The door was locked."

"It was." He said it without apology, without boast, the way you'd admit to having breathed. "The hasp on the coal hatch is older than I am. So is the coal hatch."

She looked at him for a long moment. He was perhaps twenty-five, with the kind of face that had stopped bothering to be handsome somewhere around the second winter of the war. There was a healing split in his lip. His hands, folded in his lap, had the specific stillness of hands that had learned not to fidget in places where fidgeting was fatal.

"You threw me out," he said.

"I did."

"I've thought about it. I've decided you were right to." He unfolded himself and stood, slowly, the way men stand when their bodies have opinions about standing. "You didn't know me. A stranger comes to your door at night saying your dead father is alive. That's either a cruel joke or a trap, and either way the correct response is the door." He paused. "I'd have thrown me out too. I'd have thrown me out *harder.*"

"And yet here you are. In my clock room. Having let yourself in through my coal hatch."

"Your father's clock room."

The words landed harder than she wanted them to. She turned back to the work ledge and busied her hands with the oil tin—there was nothing to busy them with, but hands could lie even when voices couldn't.

"He's dead," she said. "Three years in February. There was a letter. There was a coffin—empty, they told my mother, because the ground where he fell was—" She stopped. "There was a *letter*, in his handwriting, and I know his handwriting better than I know my own face."

"I know," Elias Brandt said. "I wrote the address on the envelope for the one that came to the regiment. He dictated it. He wanted it to be perfect." He came a step closer, into the light, and she saw that he'd been crying recently or had not slept recently, and that with this man the two conditions were probably indistinguishable. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I stopped asking anyone to believe me in the spring. I'm asking you to let me search the clock, and I'm telling you why."

"Which is?"

"Because your father hid something in it. And because the men who want that something are going to take this observatory within the fortnight, and when they do, they'll take it apart with crowbars and cart it to a furnace, and whatever he left in here will burn with it." He looked at the clock the way a man looks at a church. "He hid it where only someone who understood the mechanism would ever find it. That's not a hiding place, Fräulein Nystrom. That's a letter. And it's addressed to a clockmaker."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, the building's wheeze went on, patient as ever.

Rosalind looked at her pallet arm, clamped in its vise, half-trued. She thought about the surveyor's report, which had recommended, in its final line, that the clock be "assessed for decommissioning." She thought about Governor Halvard's name, which had been on the surveyor's letter of appointment.

"If I let you search," she said slowly, "you'll do it while I work. You'll touch nothing that moves. You'll tell me everything you find before you touch it. And when I say the word *enough*, we are finished, and you go out the way you came in, and I never see you again."

"That's fair."

"And if I find that you've lied to me—about my father, about any of it—I will walk down this hill to the garrison and describe you to the first sergeant I meet, and I will do it with a clear conscience, because you *will* have lied to me, and I will not have."

Something moved through his face that might, in a healthier man, have been a smile. "You'd be within your rights."

"I'm always within my rights. It's one of the few advantages of being nobody." She picked up her stone. "Sit down, Brandt. Start with the base casing. And if you so much as breathe on the escapement, I will teach you what a poker is for."

* * *

Elias sat with his back to the wall and watched her work, and thought: *she files like him.*

It was in the wrists. The captain had had a way of working—Elias had seen it only once, in the spring of '15, mending a camp stove with a bayonet and a piece of tin while the shells came over like freight trains—and it was this: total economy, no motion wasted, every gesture arriving exactly where it meant to. The daughter had it. Watching her stone the pallet arm was like watching a memory do carpentry.

He turned his attention to the base casing, because thinking about the captain was a luxury he couldn't afford, and grief, in his experience, was just fear that had stopped moving.

The base was walnut over oak over, he suspected, oak again. He worked by feel in the dark of the clock's underside, fingers walking the joints the way the captain had taught the scouts to walk a hedgerow—slow, patient, reading with the hands. The captain had been a quartermaster before he'd been anything else, and he'd had a saying: *Wood remembers. A joint that's been opened closes differently. Find the joint that closes like it's shy.*

The third panel from the left was shy.

Elias let his fingers rest on it and did not pry, because he had promised, and because more than he feared her poker, he feared—irrationally, completely—the idea of her deciding he was a liar. He had been called a deserter for eight months now. It was the only accusation that had ever fit him badly.

"Panel," he said. "Third from the left, bottom row. It's been opened and closed. There's a gap at the top edge you could take with a knife blade."

He heard her lamp set down. Heard her come around the case. Her shadow fell across him and he kept his hands flat and visible, the way you do at checkpoints.

She knelt. She took out a knife—a real one, a clockmaker's case knife with a blade like a sliver of moon—and worked it along the top edge, gently, the way you'd open a letter you suspected contained bad news. The panel came away with a sound like a sigh.

Inside: a cavity. Oilcloth. And in the oilcloth, nothing. The cloth lay there in its neat folded envelope shape, holding the memory of whatever had filled it, the way a bed holds the memory of a sleeper.

"Empty," Rosalind said.

Elias's stomach went down and kept going. "He wouldn't have emptied it."

"Someone else did." She lifted the oilcloth to the lamp. "Recently. See—the fold creases are sharp. This was opened within the year. Within the season." She was quiet a moment, and then she said, in a different voice, smaller: "He was here. Wasn't he. He came back. He stood in this room and took it away and left the cloth folded, because he's *precise*, because he's always been—" She stopped. Pressed the cloth flat between her hands as if she could press the answer out of it. "Or the governor's men found it. Or—"

"Or," Elias said, "he moved it deeper."

She looked at him.

"Your father," Elias said, "did not design hiding places. He designed *systems*. I carried his ledgers for two years—I know how his mind worked. He never kept one copy of anything where one copy would do. If there was a cache here, there's a cache elsewhere in the clock, and this one—" he touched the empty cavity, "—this one was either a decoy or a first letter. A letter that says: *keep looking. You're the kind of person who keeps looking.*"

Rosalind sat back on her heels. In the lamplight her face did something complicated—grief and fury and, underneath both, a terrible reluctant hope, the hope of a woman who had spent three years learning to be an orphan and did not appreciate being told she might not be one.

"If he's alive," she said, "and he's been alive, and he let me stand at his grave—"

"Fräulein."

"Don't. You don't get to *Fräulein* me. You don't know what that funeral was. You don't know what my mother—" Her voice cracked and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, by force of craft, and Elias thought: *there it is. That's the Nystrom steel. The captain used to talk about it like weather.* "If he's alive, he has a great deal to answer for, and I intend to be the one who asks the questions. Do you understand me? I'm not doing this for the regiment. I'm not doing it for the governor or the delegates or the war or the peace. I'm doing it so that I can stand in front of Aldous Nystrom and say *why*, and watch him try to answer."

"Yes," Elias said. "That's the best reason I've heard yet."

"It's the only reason." She stood, brushed off her knees, and went back to the escapement as if the last five minutes had been a regrettable interruption in an otherwise productive evening. But her hands, when she picked up the stone, were not quite steady, and she looked at them with distaste, as at tools that had failed her, until they steadied.

Elias folded the oilcloth, set it back in the cavity, and replaced the panel. Then he sat again in his corner, and for a while the only sounds in the room were the whisper of the stone and the wheeze of the building and, once, far off down the hill, the boots of a patrol on the frozen road, passing, passing, not stopping.

* * *

Near two in the morning, she called a halt.

"Not enough," she said, before he could ask. "I said I'd say when, and it isn't when. But the pallets are trued and the oil's set, and if I start the pendulum tonight I want to be watching it, not watching you." She closed the case panels and stood with her hand on the glass a moment, the way you'd stand at the bedside of someone sleeping. "Tomorrow I start on the great wheel. It's out of true by a hair. And tomorrow night—" she looked at him sideways, "—you'll search the movement. The train, the barrel, the fusee. The places only a clockmaker would think to look."

"You said I touch nothing that moves."

"The movement doesn't move. It hasn't moved in eleven days." Almost, almost, a smile. "Tomorrow night, it will have."

He gathered his tarpaulin. At the coal hatch he stopped, one leg already into the dark, and turned.

"Why?" he said. "You threw me out once. You could have thrown me out again. You could have gone to the garrison tonight and been a hero by breakfast. Why didn't you?"

Rosalind considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, and he understood that she would give him a true answer, because she did not seem built for any other kind.

"Because you said the surveyor wants it decommissioned," she said. "And I've read the surveyor's report, and it's wrong—deliberately wrong, wrong in ways a man has to *pay* to be wrong—and I want to know why a governor's man wants a working clock dead." She pulled her scarf up. "Also, you were right about one thing. It is a letter. And I have spent three years getting mail from my father that I never answered, because nobody answers the dead." Her jaw set. "If there's one more letter in that clock, Brandt, I'm going to open it myself."

She went down the stairs. He heard the service door, and her boots on the frozen lane, fading.

Elias stood in the dark clock room a while longer, in the smell of oil and cold stone, and let himself feel the thing he had not let himself feel in eight months, which was that he was not alone in this.

Then he went out through the coal hatch, down the hill, into the city of ten thousand private times, and did not notice—because the dark was the honest kind, and it hid such things—that a figure detached itself from the wall of the observatory gatehouse and followed him down, keeping to the shadows, keeping, always, just within sight.
