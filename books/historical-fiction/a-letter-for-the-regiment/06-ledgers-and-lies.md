## 6. Ledgers and Lies

The trouble with being a deserter, Elias had discovered, was not the shame. He had expected the shame, had budgeted for it the way a man budgets for rain. The trouble was that every other difficulty in his life now branched from it like roots from a stump.

He could not sleep at an inn, because inns asked questions. He could not buy bread from the baker on Cordwainer Row, because the baker's son served in the garrison and the baker had a memory like a ledger book. He could not even walk at an ordinary pace through the market square, because he had learned—eight months of running teaches a man these things—that soldiers read gait the way literate men read print. A man who walked too quickly was hiding something. A man who walked too slowly was hiding something else.

So Elias Brandt walked through Vellmark at exactly the pace of a man going nowhere in particular, which was its own small performance, and arrived back at the observatory hill two hours after dark with his heart already going like a drum corps.

* * *

Rosalind Nystrom had left him one candle stub, half a heel of bread wrapped in paper, and a note written in a hand so precise it looked typeset:

*The escapement is worn past repair. I have ordered a replacement blank from Soren's shop. It will take three days to cut. Do not touch anything you do not understand. This is not an insult. It is a general warning.*

Elias read it twice and smiled despite himself. *This is not an insult.* She had the particular courtesy of people who are very good at things and know it, and who extend politeness the way one extends credit—cautiously, and expecting repayment.

He ate the bread standing up, because sitting down felt like an extravagance, and then he took off his coat, rolled his sleeves, and went to work.

The captain's last letter—the one Elias carried against his chest the way other men carried a saint's medal—had been explicit, insofar as Aldous Nystrom was ever explicit about anything. He wrote in circles. He wrote in suggestions. He wrote like a man who suspected his letters were being read by everyone except their recipient, which, as it turned out, they were.

*"You will find,"* the letter said, *"that I always believed a clock is honest precisely where a man is not. In the case, if you take my meaning. The base especially. A clock stands on its foundations, and mine will stand on truth."*

Poetry, Elias had thought at first. The captain had been a great one for poetry when the situation frightened him, which had been often, near the end. But then there was the second letter, the one written in the hospital at Grausen in a hand gone thin and wavering, and in that one the poetry thinned out and the arithmetic showed through:

*"The base casing. Behind the weight board. Oilcloth."*

Two words of instruction buried in four pages of farewell. It was, Elias reflected, exactly like him. The man had hidden the most important documents in the city inside a clock, and then told no one plainly, because telling someone plainly would have made that someone a target—and instead he'd scattered the knowledge across letters like a sower who hoped some seed would land in furrow rather than stone.

One seed had landed in Elias. He intended to see what grew.

* * *

The clock room at night was a different country from the clock room by day. By day it belonged to Rosalind—her lamp, her tools laid out in ranks, her quiet voice narrating the machine's ailments as though it were a patient of long acquaintance. By night it was all shadow and tick, the great pendulum swinging overhead in its slow patient arc, and every gear in the tower contributing its own small voice to a conversation that had been going on, uninterrupted, for ninety years.

Elias stood beneath the movement and looked up at it for a long moment. Somewhere above, the escape wheel released its teeth one by one—tick, and tick, and tick—a sound he had heard every morning of his soldiering life without once wondering what produced it. Regiments lived by this sound. Men had marched to their deaths on time because of it.

"Well," he said aloud, to nobody. "Let's find out what you're standing on."

The base casing was a box of black oak the size of a coffin, paneled and corniced, built to look like furniture and doing a respectable job of it. Ninety years of dust lay along its top edge in a soft grey ridge. There was a door on the front—properly hinged, properly locked—for winding access, and Rosalind had the only key, and Elias was not about to ask her for it. So he went around the side, knelt in the cold, and began, with a borrowed screwdriver and a great deal of patience, at the panel behind the weight board.

The screws fought him. Of course they did. Everything in this building fought him—the doors, the locks, the weather, his own conscience—but Elias had spent eight months learning that most obstacles were only stubbornness wearing armor, and he worked each screw loose in its turn, easing them out with the care of a man defusing something, until the panel came free in his hands with a sigh of old wood.

Behind it: brick. Ordinary, mortared, indifferent brick.

His stomach dropped the way it dropped when an officer's eyes swept a parade ground and paused, briefly, on your face.

"No," he said quietly. "No, you're in there."

He talked to dead men sometimes. He had stopped apologizing for it around month three.

He sat back on his heels and looked again at the letter, holding the candle close. *"Behind the weight board."* Well. This was behind the weight board. Unless—

Unless "the base" meant something else. Unless the captain had moved things. Unless the captain had been lying, or mad, or dead before the ink dried. Unless eight months of walking and hunger and shame had been spent chasing a dying man's metaphor.

Elias closed his eyes and let himself feel all of that for exactly ten seconds, by the clock—which was fitting, since the clock was right there counting them for him. Then he opened his eyes and got back to work, because despair was a luxury and he'd given up luxuries along with everything else.

* * *

It took him the better part of two hours, working by candlelight with his sleeves pinned back and dust settling into his hair like grey snow, to understand that he had been thinking about the problem wrong.

A clockmaker builds a case to be opened. That is the whole point of a case—it is a promise that what is inside may be reached. But a man hiding something inside a clock does not want it reachable. He wants it reachable only by someone who understands the machine. Not a lock, which any thief can pick, but a puzzle, which only the right kind of mind can solve.

And Captain Aldous Nystrom had known exactly one person with that kind of mind. He had raised her. He had taught her to tell a lever escapement from a detent before she could spell *escapement*. He had built his hiding place not for a soldier with a crowbar but for a daughter with calipers.

Which meant, Elias realized, kneeling in the dust with his heart going strangely quiet, that the captain had never meant the hiding place for him at all. He was a courier. A carrier pigeon. He had flown eight hundred miles to deliver a message addressed to someone else.

There was a bitter joke in that, and Elias laughed at it—once, shortly, in the empty room, startling himself.

But the laugh turned over something. Because if the mechanism itself was the lock—if the hiding place answered to the clock's own logic—then perhaps he didn't need to be clever. Perhaps he only needed to be observant, which was a humbler skill but one that desertion teaches thoroughly.

He started at the bottom of the casing and went over it inch by inch, feeling with his fingertips the way he'd once felt for tripwires in the dark at Vosse Field, reading the wood like braille. Paneling. Cornice. Bracket. Bracket. Bracket—

The third bracket was wrong.

Not visibly. Visually it matched its brothers on either side, same profile, same age, same grime. But under his thumb it sat a hair proud, and when he pressed it—gently, gently, the way you press a bruise to learn its borders—it gave. A click, deep inside the oak, delicate as a knuckle cracking.

Somewhere within the base casing, something let go.

Elias held very still for a full minute, listening to the tower breathe above him. Then he pulled the whole lower front panel free—it came away easier now, as though the machine had merely been waiting for manners—and looked into the dark.

Oilcloth.

Folded, layered, tucked with a housekeeper's neatness into a hollow that had been cut out of the case's inner frame—not an afterthought but architecture, sawn and planned and finished, lined with oilcloth against damp the way you'd line a cradle. Someone had loved this hiding place. Someone had built it to last.

Elias put both hands in up to the wrists, the way a boy reaches into a river for a fish, and brought out—

Nothing.

Not nothing-nothing. The hollow was not empty the way a grave is empty. It was empty the way a drawer is empty after someone has tidied it. The oilcloth lining had been disturbed and refolded, carefully, but refolded by different hands than had folded it first—he could tell by the creases, crisp where the original folds had gone soft. Someone had come here. Someone had taken what was here. And whoever it was had left, in the corner of the oilcloth, one object, placed deliberately, the way you leave a calling card or a dare.

A key.

Small, iron, old-fashioned bit, the kind that fit a strongbox or a cabinet. Stamped on its bow—the round head—was a crest: a tower between two crossed cannon barrels, beneath the number 7.

Seventh Regiment crest. His regiment. Her father's.

Elias sat down slowly on the cold floor, the key in his palm, and stared at it while the pendulum swung its long slow arc overhead, patient as God.

"Someone got here first," he said to the clock. The clock, having no opinion, ticked on.

* * *

He did not sleep. Sleep, for Elias, was a country he could visit only when nothing was at stake, which meant he hadn't visited in months. Instead he sat in the corner of the clock room with his back against the stone and turned the key over and over, and thought.

Who? Who knew the captain had hidden anything here? The captain had trusted almost no one—that was the tragedy of him, that was why he'd died alone in a hospital bed writing riddles instead of testimony. Elias himself had learned of the hiding place only by accident, by carrying the captain's letters unopened through a retreat and finally, in disgrace and disobedience, opening them. If anyone else had read those letters, they'd have burned them. So either someone else had found the letters—

—or someone else had found the hiding place on their own.

And who could do that? Who could read a clock the way the captain read one?

Elias turned the key in the candlelight. The crest caught the flame.

The governor's office had engineers. Halvard kept specialists the way other men kept dogs. And the seizure order—the one Elias had seen pinned to the garrison noticeboard with his own disbelieving eyes—had named the observatory among properties "pending reassessment for security purposes." Pending. Which meant someone upstairs wanted this tower, and you did not want a tower unless you knew, or guessed, what was in it.

Had they already been inside? Had they searched and found the hollow and taken the papers, leaving the key behind as trash? No—the key wasn't trash. You didn't leave a key stamped with a regimental crest unless the key still mattered, still opened something, still pointed somewhere. Whoever left it had wanted it found, or hadn't cared who found it, and both possibilities were bad in interesting ways.

Or—third possibility, the one that made the back of his neck prickle—someone had gotten here first who was *not* the governor's man. Someone who had taken the papers to keep them safe. Someone who understood what they were.

Elias looked up at the great wheel-work, black against the observatory's high window, turning in the dark with its ancient unhurried patience, and thought about a girl with dust in her hair who diagnosed escapements the way priests diagnosed souls, and who had thrown him out of her workshop into the rain, and who had nevertheless come back tonight, alone, to a tower full of shadows, to finish her work.

*Do not touch anything you do not understand,* her note said.

Well. He touched plenty he didn't understand. But he was starting to suspect she understood more than her note admitted. Starting to suspect the apprentice clockmaker of Vellmark might be the addressee on all these sealed envelopes, and that he, Elias Brandt, deserter, coward by official record, was carrying mail for a ghost to a woman who hated ghosts.

The candle guttered. Outside, far below, the city slept under snow, ten thousand private times ticking away in ten thousand kitchens, all of them obedient to the machine above his head.

Elias folded the key into his shirt, next to the letters, where it lay against his skin cold as a coin from a drowned ship.

Three days, she'd said. Three days to cut the new escapement. Three days to decide how much truth a stranger could afford to hand a clockmaker's daughter whose father was supposed to be dead.

He settled back against the stone, closed his eyes, and listened to the clock count.

Somewhere down in the sleeping city, in the third hour of the morning, a patrol boot rang on cobbles and passed on. And somewhere closer—so close that Elias might have missed it entirely had he been asleep, as he was supposed to be—the service door at the foot of the tower shifted once against its frame, softly, like a breath drawn and held.
