## 7. The Governor's Clockmaker

The summons arrived on a Tuesday morning, which Rosalind would later think was fitting. Nothing truly dangerous ever happened on a Friday; even calamity preferred to keep respectable hours.

The messenger was a corporal with a face like a closed ledger and a voice that had been sanded down to a single note. He did not knock so much as permit the door of Nystrom & Soren, Clockmakers, to exist in his presence.

"Rosalind Nystrom?" he said, though he had already read the answer off the shop sign, off her oil-stained apron, and possibly off some internal list he carried in place of a soul.

"I'm she."

"Governor Halvard requests your presence at the citadel. Eleven o'clock."

"Requests," Rosalind repeated. In Vellmark, the word *requests* from the governor's office carried roughly the same grammatical weight as an arrest warrant, minus the courtesy of being written down.

The corporal produced, from somewhere in the folds of his greatcoat, a folded paper stamped with the city seal — the double-headed eagle over crossed keys that had watched over Vellmark for two hundred years and, in Rosalind's private opinion, had grown fat and smug about it. "You'll want to look presentable," he added, in the tone of a man delivering weather news.

"Presentable," said Soren from the back bench, without looking up from the watch glass he was polishing. He had been presentable once, decades ago, at his own guild examination, and had never forgiven the experience. "Tell His Excellency we regret that competence does not come in Sunday clothes."

The corporal's eye twitched. It was, Rosalind suspected, the closest thing to conversation he permitted himself before noon.

* * *

She wore her second-best dress — the grey one with the collar that behaved itself — and her best hands, which is to say she scrubbed them until the creases of her knuckles were pink and honest. A clockmaker's hands were her credentials; no governor could summon those away. Under the nails there remained, despite everything, a faint crescent of brass polish, like a small stubborn moon. She decided to let it stay. If Halvard wanted a lady, he could hire one. He wanted a clockmaker.

The citadel sat above the city like a judge above a courtroom, its granite shoulders dusted with the week's snow. Rosalind had walked past it ten thousand times and through it never. The guards at the gate checked her papers twice, then a third time as though hoping the paper might confess to something. Inside, corridors stretched away in both directions, hung with portraits of men whose expressions suggested they had all been photographed mid-sneeze and had decided to be furious about it forever.

An aide — younger than the corporal, better fed, worse at hiding his curiosity — led her up a spiral stair and announced her to a set of double doors.

"Miss Nystrom, Excellency."

"Ah." The voice behind the doors was warm the way a stove is warm: pleasant, controlled, and capable of burning you badly if you forgot what it was. "Send her in. And bring tea. Real tea, not the stuff we give the delegates."

Governor Aldous Halvard — no relation to any Aldous worth mentioning, Rosalind thought, and then hated herself for how much the name still stung — stood at a tall window with his hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man who has practiced looking thoughtful in mirrors. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with the kind of neat, unhurried body that suggested he had never once run for anything, including office. When he turned, his face arranged itself into welcome with the smoothness of a well-oiled escapement.

"Miss Nystrom. Thank you for coming. Sit, please — no, there, by the fire, you've come out of the cold." He said *you've come out of the cold* as if he had personally arranged the weather to justify the phrase.

Rosalind sat. The chair was softer than anything in the workshop and she distrusted it immediately.

"You repaired the observatory clock," Halvard said. It was not a question. He had, clearly, read the file. There was a file. She felt certain of it, the way one feels certain of a hidden weight inside a case — by the way things balanced.

"The escapement," she said. "It was worn past adjustment. I fitted a new one."

"A new escapement." He nodded slowly, savoring the words like a man tasting wine he intended to price by the bottle. "And the clock keeps time now?"

"To within four seconds a day. It will do better once the temperature settles."

"Four seconds." He smiled. "Do you know, Miss Nystrom, I am told three separate master clockmakers looked at that mechanism over the autumn and pronounced it beyond saving? One of them recommended replacement entire. A new clock, German works, six months' wait." He turned back to the window. "And then a girl of twenty-two walks in and fixes it in an afternoon."

"Nine hours," said Rosalind. "And I'm twenty-three."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, or the memory of amusement — and was filed away. "Precision," he said approvingly. "I'm told that's characteristic of your family."

The tea arrived: real tea, as promised, in porcelain thin enough to see light through. Halvard poured for her himself, which was either courtesy or theater, and in Rosalind's experience the two were cousins at best.

"Now." He settled into the chair opposite her, cup and saucer balanced on his knee. "You will know that the situation of the city is delicate. The armistice talks drag on. Delegates arrive within the fortnight. Whatever is signed will be signed here, in Vellmark, under this government's authority — and whatever happens in these weeks will determine whether this province passes into peace as an orderly part of the nation or descends into the sort of confusion that makes men do foolish things." He sipped. "Order matters, Miss Nystrom. Order is everything. And order, in a garrison city, begins with the clock."

"The observatory clock regulates the regimental schedules," Rosalind said carefully. "Signals, drills, guard changes—"

"And the trains, the telegraph offices, the courts, the schools." He ticked them off on neat fingers. "Every hour in Vellmark descends from that one instrument. Which is why it troubles me — deeply troubles me — that for months it kept poor time, and no one knew why, and no one could fix it, and anyone at all might have been inside it, doing anything at all to it." His eyes came up and met hers, mild as milk. "Sabotage is rarely dramatic, Miss Nystrom. It is usually very small. A tooth filed here, a weight shifted there. A clock that runs slow makes soldiers late and trains missed and, at the right moment, a bridge unguarded. One doesn't blow up a fortress. One merely arranges for everyone responsible to be elsewhere."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, a door closed with the soft authority of government.

"That's why I'm glad it was you," Halvard continued warmly. "A local girl. Guild-trained. No foreign connections, no politics — your file is remarkably dull, I congratulate you — and, most importantly, a daughter of the regiment." He let that phrase sit between them like a coin on a table. "Captain Aldous Nystrom's daughter. The whole city mourned him. Fine officer. Terrible loss." A pause, measured to the half-second. "You have my sympathies. Belatedly, I fear. These years have been busy ones."

"Thank you," Rosalind said, because the alternative was saying nothing, and silence in that room would be read as something else.

"So." He set down his cup and leaned forward, and for a moment the warmth in his face sharpened into something almost eager — the look of a man about to make a purchase he considers shrewd. "Here is what I propose. A commission. Official, salaried, with the full authority of this office behind it. You will refit the observatory entirely. New locks on the tower door — good ones, not the museum pieces they have now. A guard post at the base. Keys held by my office alone. The mechanism inspected weekly, by you, with a report delivered directly to me. No one enters that tower, touches that clock, or so much as breathes on it without your knowledge and my consent." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "You would be, in effect, the keeper of the city's time. Every apprentice dreams of a master clock, Miss Nystrom. I am offering you the master clock of Vellmark itself."

It was, Rosalind had to admit, beautifully done. The salary would triple her income. The title — *Keeper of the City Time*, she could hear the guild elders purring already — would make her career. Her father's daughter, honored by the state that had eulogized him. And underneath it all, wrapped in the velvet of the offer like a spring coiled in straw, the simple fact: *the keys to my office alone.*

She took a sip of tea to buy a moment and found it was excellent, which annoyed her further.

"It's a generous offer, Excellency," she said.

"It's a sensible one," Halvard corrected pleasantly. "Generosity is when one gives more than a thing is worth. This is precisely what the thing is worth. I dislike overpaying; it corrupts both parties."

"And the observatory staff? Dr. Lennart keeps the telescope—"

"Dr. Lennart will retain access to the sky," said Halvard, "at scheduled hours, accompanied. Astronomy, I'm afraid, must learn to keep appointments like everyone else."

"And the old keys?"

"Will be collected." He smiled again, and this time Rosalind saw the teeth in it plainly — not bared, nothing so vulgar, simply *present*, the way a wolf's teeth are present while it smiles at the sheep fence. "You understand my position, I hope. In eleven days the fate of this province may be decided at a table fifty yards from that clock tower. If, in that room, a delegate asks me whether Vellmark's timekeeping can be guaranteed — whether the guns will fall silent at the appointed minute, whether every man in this garrison will know the same hour at the same moment — I wish to answer yes without hesitation. That is all this is. Housekeeping. Good housekeeping before guests arrive."

Housekeeping. Rosalind thought of the nine hours she'd spent elbow-deep in the movement, of the fine gray dust of worn brass, of the strange little hollow she'd noticed in the base casing and dismissed as a casting flaw. Of Elias Brandt, soaked and desperate at the service door, swearing her father was alive. Of the letters in that impossible handwriting, hidden now in the false bottom of her tool chest like a splinter working its way toward her heart.

*One doesn't blow up a fortress,* Halvard had said. *One merely arranges for everyone responsible to be elsewhere.* She wondered idly what had happened to the last person who'd known too much about the observatory clock. Then she stopped wondering, because the answer had a name, and the name had been read aloud at a memorial service she'd attended in her blackest dress.

"I would need to inspect the mechanism freely," she said. "Unaccompanied. A clock tells its secrets slowly, Excellency. You can't interrogate it on a schedule."

Halvard studied her for a long moment. Outside the window, snow began again, fine and dry, sifting down over the parade ground where a squad was drilling with the crisp mechanical perfection of men who trusted their watches.

"Naturally," he said at last. "Weekly reports, but your hours are your own. Within reason." He rose, and she rose with him, and he extended his hand — dry, firm, brief. "You'll have the commission papers tomorrow. Welcome to the governor's service, Miss Nystrom. Your father would be proud, I think. He was always a careful man." A fractional pause, delicate as a hairspring. "Perhaps too careful, some said. But careful."

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and made herself turn around.

"Excellency — why me? Truly. There are senior masters in the guild. Men with forty years' standing."

"Because," Governor Halvard said, settling back into his chair and lifting his cooling tea as though the interview had never strained him, "the senior masters are old friends of mine, and old friends ask questions. You, Miss Nystrom, strike me as a young woman who understands that some questions are more expensive than their answers." He drank. "Good afternoon. Mind the stairs; the stone is treacherous in winter. So many things in this city are."

* * *

The walk down from the citadel took her past the memorial wall, where the regiment's dead were carved in columns so long the masons had run out of wall and started a second one. Her father's name was there — *Capt. A. Nystrom* — third row, chiseled deep, weathered already at the edges from three winters of pretending.

Rosalind stood in front of it with the wind pulling at her skirt and her mind running like a train.

He wanted the clock locked. He wanted it guarded, keyed to himself, inspected on his schedule, sealed before the delegates arrived. A man planning sabotage seals a building. A man planning *concealment* seals it harder. Either way, whatever was in that tower — whatever her father, alive or dead, had left sleeping among the gears — would be behind the governor's own lock within the week, and after the armistice, when the commissions and honors were handed out like sweets, no one would ever open that case again.

Unless someone opened it first.

She pressed her bare hand flat against the cold granite, against the carved letters of her own surname, and felt the truth of it settle in her chest with the heavy, unmistakable tick of a pendulum finding its beat: she had just been hired to guard the evidence. Paid, titled, and thanked for it.

The commission papers would come tomorrow. Which meant whatever needed doing in that tower had to be done tonight — and there was only one person in Vellmark who knew what to look for, and he was a deserter with a warrant on his head whom she had thrown out into the rain.

Rosalind pulled her collar up against the wind and turned toward the workshop, walking fast.

She hoped, for both their sakes, that Soren hadn't changed the locks since the last time she'd smuggled anything past him.

Though knowing Soren — and she was beginning to suspect she didn't know him at all — she had an uneasy feeling he'd known exactly what she was smuggling, all along.
