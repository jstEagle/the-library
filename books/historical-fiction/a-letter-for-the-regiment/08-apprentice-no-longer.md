## 8. Apprentice No Longer

The shop smelled of brass shavings and yesterday's coffee, which was to say it smelled like home—or what had stood in for home these eleven years.

Rosalind had come in early, earlier than the light, because she could not bear the silence of her own room and because her hands wanted work even when her mind refused to cooperate with them. She had lit the lamp over the bench, tied on her apron, and begun cleaning parts that did not need cleaning. A small alarm clock, disassembled for no reason but to be disassembled. Its gears lay in neat rows on the felt like soldiers awaiting orders that would never come.

She had been at it an hour before she heard the shuffle of slippers behind her and knew, without turning, that Soren had been standing there longer than she'd realized. Old men moved quietly when they wanted to. It was one of the many things people got wrong about them.

"You're oiling a movement that isn't dirty," he said.

"I know."

"Ah." He came around to the other side of the bench and lowered himself onto his stool with the sound of a man settling into a decision. He was seventy-one, mostly beard and eyebrows, with forearms still strong enough to make apprentices flinch when he demonstrated a proper tap of the mallet. This morning his hands were folded, though. Still. "So. You found him."

Rosalind's stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, into whatever lay beneath Vellmark itself. "Found who?"

"Don't do that." Soren's voice wasn't unkind. It was worse than unkind; it was patient. "You've been doing it since you were nine years old, lying with your whole face while your hands tell the truth. You've been winding and unwinding that poor clock since midnight, I'd wager, and you didn't sleep, and there's a letter in your apron pocket that isn't guild correspondence, because guild correspondence doesn't get read four times and folded along different creases each time."

She stared at him. "You've been going through my—"

"I've been *observing*," Soren said, "which is what I'm for. Sit down, girl. I'll make the coffee. You'll only burn it, and we're going to want good coffee for this conversation."

He made the coffee. That was the terrible thing, she thought later—the ordinariness of it. The clink of the pot, the scrape of the stove door, the two chipped mugs they'd used every morning for a decade. He handed her hers and sat down across from her, and outside the window the garrison bugle sounded the hour, slightly flat as always, and somewhere above the city the observatory clock answered it, three minutes slow, and Rosalind thought: *three minutes slow, and everyone in Vellmark arranges their lives around those three minutes without ever asking why.*

"Elias Brandt," Soren said.

Her mug stopped halfway to her mouth. "How do you know that name?"

"Because I taught it to you," Soren said, "or near enough. When you were six. Your father brought you here on a Tuesday—I remember it was a Tuesday because the mail cart always came Tuesdays and you were terrified of the horse—and he said, *Soren, if anything happens to me, teach my daughter everything.* And I said, *nothing is going to happen to you, Aldous, you're the most careful man in the regiment.*" He looked into his coffee. "And then something happened to him. Or appeared to."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the street a milk cart rattled past, and Rosalind was aware, with strange clarity, of every ordinary sound in a city that might be about to stop being ordinary.

"You knew," she said. "You knew he suspected something. About the supplies."

Soren set his mug down with great care, as though it were a movement part he couldn't afford to scratch. "I knew he was frightened. I knew he came here in the autumn of '15 smelling of lamp oil and fear, and asked me whether a clock could keep a secret. And I told him—" the old man's voice caught, just slightly, "—I told him a clock could keep any secret you liked, provided nobody opened the case. And then I told him to go home to his daughter and let the army mind its own rotten business, because I have a shop, Rosalind. A shop with a sign on it and a name over the door and eleven years of quiet. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you chose the shop."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "That I chose *you*. There's a difference, though I grant you it doesn't feel like one this morning." He leaned forward. "Your father comes to me with suspicions about quartermasters and the governor's office, and what are my options? I go to the authorities—his authorities, the ones he suspects—and Aldous disappears a year later anyway, and so do I, probably, and then who teaches you? Who keeps the roof over your head? I did the arithmetic every coward does, girl, and I did it honestly, and I have regretted it every single day since the telegram came. But I don't get to pretend the arithmetic wasn't real. Neither will you, someday, God help you."

Rosalind set her mug down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. Clockmaker's hands, her father used to say. Steady under anything, even grief.

"He let me mourn him," she said. "Three years, Soren. Three years of black armbands and the memorial stone and Marta holding my hand at the anniversary service while I pretended my eyes were dry. He was *alive* and he let me—"

"Would you rather he hadn't?" Soren asked gently. "If the alternative was the truth getting you killed too?"

"Yes!" It came out louder than she meant, loud enough to startle them both. She lowered her voice. "Yes. Because at least it would have been *mine*. He decided for me. Everyone decides for me. The guild decides when I'm ready. The governor decides what time the city eats breakfast. My own dead father decides how long I grieve him." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what the worst part is? The worst part is that I'm not even surprised. I sat in that tower last night with a stranger telling me my father was alive, and some cold little gear inside me turned over and said, *of course. Of course he is. Of course he built it into a clock.* Because that's what he does. That's what he *did*. He never once told me anything straight if he could hide it in a mechanism instead."

Soren was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Show me the letters."

She almost refused. Some stubborn part of her wanted to hold the last private thing she had against the whole world, including old men who made coffee and confessions in the same breath. But her hands were already moving, already reaching into the apron pocket, because her hands had never once obeyed her heart in her entire life and she suspected they never would.

Soren held the letter up to the lamp. His lips moved faintly as he read—not reading aloud, she realized, but reading the way he read a difficult diagram, tracing each line twice. When he finished, he didn't hand it back immediately. He turned it over, examined the paper stock, held it near his nose.

"Vellmark stationery," he murmured. "Governor's requisition grade. Where did Brandt get governor's-grade paper?"

"Elias? He was a clerk. Before he—" She stopped. "Before he ran."

"Mm." Soren laid the letter flat on the bench between them, smoothing its creases with one thick finger, the way he smoothed a drawing before cutting metal. "Eleven years ago, a man sat exactly where you're sitting and showed me a page of figures. Supply manifests. He'd found them in a mislabeled crate—he was forever finding things in mislabeled crates, your father, half the clocks in this shop exist because of his habit of opening boxes that weren't addressed to him." A ghost of a smile crossed the beard and died there. "The figures didn't add up. Four hundred blankets ordered, two hundred delivered, and the delivery receipts signed by a man whose signature changed depending on who was watching. Your father had copied the page. He asked me where he could hide a copy where no soldier would ever look."

"And you said—"

"A clock." Soren met her eyes. "I said a clock. I said it thinking it was a joke. A way to get him to laugh and go home. And he wrote it down. In that little notebook he always carried. He wrote it down like it was scripture." The old man's jaw worked. "Three days later he stopped coming by. Said the regiment had him busy. Said the war was heating up. And I told myself, *he's taken my advice, he's let it go, he's being sensible.* And when the telegram came I told myself, *see, this is what happens, this is why you keep your head down.* And I kept my head down for three years, Rosalind, and I slept at night, most nights, and I called it survival." He spread his hands, palms up, an old craftsman's gesture: *here is the work, judge it.* "Now you tell me. What do you call it?"

Rosalind looked at her master—at the man who had fed her, housed her, taught her that a mechanism never lies and neither should the person repairing it—and felt something shift in her chest that was not quite anger and not quite forgiveness and was certainly not the warm, simple love she'd carried into the room an hour ago.

"I call it what you called it," she said quietly. "Arithmetic. Honest arithmetic. And I think—" she took a breath, because the next part cost her, "—I think I understand it better than I want to. Because last night, when Elias asked me to help him search the tower, my first thought wasn't *my father*. My first thought was *my certificate*. My journeyman's exam is in the spring. If I'm caught harboring a deserter, the guild strips me, Soren. Everything. Eleven years."

"And what did you answer him?"

"I said yes."

Soren nodded slowly, and something in his face eased—not relief exactly, more like a man watching a knot finally come loose after years of worrying at it. "There it is, then. The same arithmetic, done differently. That's all growing up is, girl. Same sums, braver answers." He pushed himself up from the stool, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and went to the tall cabinet at the back of the shop—the one Rosalind had never been allowed to open, the one she'd assumed held nothing but Soren's private stock of brandy and grudges.

It held a notebook.

Small, leather-bound, water-stained, with a broken elastic band. Soren set it on the bench between them with both hands, the way one sets down something fragile, or something explosive.

"His workshop notes," Soren said. "From the winter of '15. He left it with me—said if anyone official ever came asking, I was to swear I'd never seen it, and I did swear it, to soldiers, twice, with my hand on my mother's grave." He tapped the cover once. "I told myself I was keeping it safe for you. Maybe I was. Maybe I was just keeping it buried. You'll have to decide which, because I've decided I'm too old to decide anything anymore."

Rosalind didn't touch it. Not yet. "Why now? Why give it to me now, after three years of—"

"Because last night," Soren said, "a soaked-through deserter hammered on my service door at half past eleven, and I looked at him, and I saw your father's handwriting on the letters sticking out of his coat, and I realized the arithmetic has changed." For the first time all morning, the old man smiled, and it was a tired smile, but underneath it was steel, the same steel that lived in every mainspring he'd ever wound. "The war's ending, girl. Whatever Halvard does, whatever the governor's men plant or seize or burn—by spring there will be peace, and peace means questions, and questions mean somebody's going to open every case in this city. I'd rather it be opened by us. While we still remember which screws go where."

Rosalind picked up the notebook. It was lighter than she expected. Most terrible things were.

She opened it to the first page and there it was—her father's hand, the same slanted, precise script from Elias's letters, the same little hooked *t* she'd spent three years trying to find in strangers' handwriting on street signs and shop ledgers and once, shamefully, in the steam on a window. The first line read:

*A clock can keep any secret you like, provided nobody opens the case.*

Below it, a sketch. Not a watch, not a regulator—a tower movement. The observatory's master clock, drawn from memory in her father's sure strokes, with annotations in the margins. And beside the escapement, circled twice, a note:

*Fusee cone — hollow it. Nobody checks the cone. Even Soren won't think to look there.*

Rosalind went cold all over.

"Soren," she said. Her voice sounded very far away. "Last night, in the base casing, Elias found a hiding place. Empty. Except for a key stamped with the regimental crest. Someone had been there first—we thought someone had gotten to the ledgers before us."

Soren frowned. "A key? To what?"

"Not the point." She was already pulling on her coat, already moving, the notebook pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "The point is my father hid things in *layers*. A decoy in the casing, the true cache in the fusee cone. And the key—" she grabbed the door handle, then stopped, and turned back to look at the old man who had raised her, lied to her, and just this morning handed her the truth wrapped in confession. "The key has to open something. Something regimental. And Elias was asking me last night about decommissioned buildings, and I was too angry to listen properly, but Soren—they're converting the old chapel on Marsh Lane into a munitions depot. Regimental chapel. Regimental crest."

Soren stared at her. "You're not seriously considering breaking into a munitions depot."

"I'm seriously considering," Rosalind said, pushing out into the grey morning, "that my father spent three years teaching me a lesson I was too stubborn to learn." She pulled the door shut behind her, and through it, muffled, she heard herself say:

"*Nobody ever opens the case.*"

Then she ran for Marsh Lane, and behind her, faint through the glass, she heard old Soren begin, very deliberately, to sweep the floor—as innocent a sound as any shop in Vellmark had ever made, and the loudest lie in it.
