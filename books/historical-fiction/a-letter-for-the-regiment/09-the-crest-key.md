## 9. The Crest Key

The chapel had been deconsecrated the way Vellmark did most things now: badly, and with paperwork.

Elias learned this from a boy selling chestnuts on the corner of Candlewick Row, who had no interest in the war but a great deal of interest in two coppers. The boy's name was Pim. He was perhaps eleven, missing one front tooth, and possessed of the particular genius for commerce that flourishes in garrison cities like weeds in a cracked wall.

"Old regimental chapel? Off Marsh Lane?" Pim counted the coppers twice, licked each one to be sure they were real, and pocketed them with the solemnity of a banker accepting a deposit. "Soldiers go in and out all hours. Wagons too. Big canvas over the windows now." He considered Elias with the frank appraisal of a professional evaluating a customer. "You're not a soldier."

"I'm a chimney sweep," Elias said.

Pim looked at his hands. They were ink-stained from copying ledgers in bad light, and there was clock oil under two fingernails that he had not been able to explain to himself, let alone a child.

"You're the worst chimney sweep I ever saw," Pim said. "But I'm not going to say anything, because you paid me, and because soldiers tip worse than anybody." He bit into a chestnut and burned his mouth and swore with impressive fluency. "They come at dawn and again after the evening bell. Between those times it's just old Fessler and a dog."

"Fessler?"

"The caretaker. He used to ring the bells. Now he sweeps up shell casings instead, he says, which is easier work because casings don't complain about the hymns." Pim shrugged. "Dog's called Bismarck. Bites."

"Bismarck bites."

"He bit the postman. Bit a lieutenant. Bit me." A pause. "I threw a rock at him first, though, so we're even."

Elias walked away from the corner with two facts he hadn't had an hour ago and one growing certainty that if he survived this war it would be entirely due to children and chestnut vendors. The evening bell rang at six. It was now— he checked the tower clock at the end of the Row, squinting through the grey drizzle —quarter past four. The master clock, running slow and wrong, its voice gone hoarse. *She'll have it right soon,* he thought, and was surprised by how much he wanted that to be true, and how much of the wanting had nothing to do with ledgers.

*Focus,* he told himself. *You are here for the key.*

---

He spent the next hour doing what deserters do best, which is nothing at all, conspicuously.

He bought bread at the bakery on Tallow Street and ate half of it on a bench where a man could sit without seeming to wait for anything. He read a week-old newspaper left behind by someone else. He watched the chapel.

It sat back from the lane behind a low iron fence, a squat stone building with a bell tower that had lost its bell—the mounting yoke stood empty against the sky like a gallows missing its guest. Canvas covered the windows, nailed tight, and through the gaps between boards Elias could see lamplight moving inside, steady and unhurried. Munitions depots kept odd hours. Men worked by night when the cargo was shells; light meant labor, and labor meant the crates were coming or going.

A sentry stood at the gate, stamping his feet against the cold. One man, bored, his rifle slung rather than held. That was good. One man meant a rotation, and rotations meant gaps.

The trouble was the dog.

Bismarck emerged around four o'clock, escorted by a small stooped figure in a caretaker's coat who walked him the length of the fence on a rope lead, exactly as Pim had promised, exactly as a clock keeps time. The dog was enormous, black and tan, with the heavy skull of something bred to argue with cattle. He stopped at the fence corner nearest the lane and stared directly at Elias with the unblinking conviction of an animal who has decided, on insufficient evidence, that you are guilty.

Elias turned a newspaper page he had already read.

*You don't know me,* he thought at the dog. *I am a chimney sweep. I am reading about grain tariffs.*

Bismarck barked once, a sound like a door slamming in a large house.

"Quiet, you great fool," said the caretaker, and hauled him onward, and Elias let out a breath he'd been holding since the armistice talks began.

---

The gap came as gaps always do: between shifts, in the failing light, when the world is busiest being tired.

At six the evening bell rang across the city—hoarse, dragging, a half-tone flat—and the sentry at the gate stamped off toward the guard hut for his relief and whatever passed for supper. For perhaps four minutes the gate belonged to nobody. The lamplight inside moved on. Somewhere a wagon team shifted and stamped in the yard beyond the chapel, and the smell of straw and horse came faintly through the cold air, and beneath it, unmistakable, the sharp mineral smell of explosives.

Elias went over the fence at the corner where the ivy gave handholds, dropped into the shadow of a yew tree that some pious gardener had planted a century ago without any thought for future felons, and crossed the open ground in the low crouch he had learned in the trenches, where crouching low was less a skill than a posture of permanent apology to God.

The side door was locked. Of course it was. But the coal chute beside it—a narrow brick throat angled down into what had once been the crypt—was covered by a board that had been nailed carelessly, by men who thought of shells as cargo and chapels as warehouses and never once imagined anyone would want *in*.

He eased the board aside. The chute exhaled cold air and candle smoke and gunpowder.

"I hate this," he informed himself quietly, and went in headfirst, because the alternative was thinking about it.

---

The crypt was low-ceilinged and pillared, old stone sweating in the winter damp. Candles burned in sconces along one wall—real candles, which struck him as either piety or negligence, given the inventory upstairs. Stacked against the far pillar were crates stenciled with the broad arrow of army ordnance, and beside them, incongruously, a wicker chair, a small table, and a teapot, because even the machinery of war runs on tea.

Above him, through the floorboards, boots crossed and recrossed. Voices, muffled. The scrape of something heavy being dragged.

The key in his pocket—he had carried it three days now, wrapped in cloth, taken from the hollow in the master clock's base—seemed to grow heavier the deeper he went. *Someone got here first.* Rosalind had said it flatly, the way she said everything, her grief packed down into sentences like powder into a cartridge. Someone had emptied the hiding place. Which meant either the captain's secret was dead, or it had been *moved*, and men only move secrets when they intend to use them.

The chapel had been his father's regiment's chapel. Elias had been christened in this crypt, according to his mother, screaming loud enough to be heard in the nave. He looked around at the ordnance crates and the candle stubs and thought: *well. Something in this family was always going to come back here making noise.*

Where would a captain hide a thing in a church? Not the altar—the altar floor was the obvious place, and Aldous Nystrom had not been an obvious man, or he would not have faked his own death with such thoroughness that his daughter had mourned him for three years. Elias climbed the crypt stairs slowly, testing each step, and pushed open the door at the top onto the body of the chapel.

Canvas over the windows turned the interior the color of weak tea. The pews had been shoved against the walls to make room for crates. The font remained, dry, holding a coil of rope. And at the front, where the altar had stood, a platform of new planks had been built—recently, the wood still pale at the cut edges—raising the whole east end by perhaps two feet.

A false floor.

His heart began the stupid hopeful drumming it always did at moments like this, and he made himself count the risks instead, the way the captain had taught him: *Count first, boy. Courage is just arithmetic done fast.* One sentry outside, changing soon. The caretaker and the dog, due for their rounds. Men overhead, maybe six, maybe eight. And somewhere in this building enough high explosive to redecorate the parish into the next century.

He crossed to the platform on soft feet. The planks were nailed but not well—one corner lifted with a groan he felt in his teeth, and he froze above it, listening, until the boots overhead resumed their crossing.

Underneath: darkness, and stone, and—his fingers found it before his eyes adjusted—a cavity. A recess cut into the old floor, mortared edges smoothed, sized to hold a strongbox.

Empty.

"No," he said, very quietly, to the dark, in the tone of a man arguing with furniture. "No, no—"

His fingers searched the cavity anyway, all four corners, the way you pat your pockets for keys you know aren't there. And then, in the seam where stone met stone, his nail caught on something small and hard wedged deliberately into the mortar.

He worked it free. A slip of paper, folded to the size of a thumbnail, oilcloth-wrapped. Inside, in handwriting he knew better than his own:

*Chapel keeps its own time. Ask the bell.*

And beneath it, pressed deep into the paper as if written with anger or haste: *If you are reading this, R., I am sorry for the years. — A.N.*

Elias sat back on his heels in the dark and laughed once, silently, the way you laugh at a joke told by a dead man who isn't dead.

*The bell.* The bell tower. The empty yoke where the bell should hang. Except—

Except the bell wasn't there. He had seen the empty mounting from the lane. So either the captain's "bell" was something else, something that had been *removed*—or removed by whoever had emptied the box—and the whole trail ran cold in a munitions yard full of soldiers.

Or the bell was still here, hidden, waiting, and the men upstairs had crates of shells stacked six feet from the answer to everything.

Boots on the stairs. Fast ones.

Elias was moving before his mind finished the arithmetic, dropping from the platform into the shadow between pew-ends, pressing himself flat against the cold wood as two soldiers came down into the nave carrying a lantern and an argument.

"—telling you the manifest says forty crates and I count thirty-eight, and I'm not signing for ghosts—"

"So sign for thirty-eight and let the quartermaster's ghost argue with the major—"

They passed within six feet of him. The lantern light swept the pews, the font, the platform. One of them paused, sniffed, frowned at the candle sconces.

"Smell that?"

"Candles. Smell like candles."

"It's not—" The soldier took another step toward the pews.

And from the crypt below, punctual as sin, Bismarck began to bark.

Both soldiers straightened. "That bloody dog's found the cat again," said the second, relieved, and they went down the stairs together to adjudicate the ancient war between dog and cat, and Elias went out through the coal chute backwards, heart slamming, and lay in the frozen mud behind the yew tree counting his breaths until his hands stopped shaking.

---

He made it four streets.

Four streets of slipping through the blue-grey dusk of Vellmark, past shuttered shops and a woman beating a rug and two boys rolling a hoop with the solemn intensity of engineers, four streets of feeling almost, almost safe—and then the lanterns came out of Tallow Street on both sides at once, and he understood with the sick clarity of long experience that he had not slipped the net. The net had *let him swim*, and was drawing closed.

Patrol. Six men, rifles, bayonets fixed—not for ceremony, not tonight. An officer with them, young, red-faced with cold and consequence.

"Papers," the officer said.

Here is what Elias Brandt knew in that moment, standing in the middle of Marsh Lane with the patrol closing and the chapel key burning in his pocket and the captain's note folded against his ribs: that his papers identified him as Corporal E. Brandt, 7th Regiment, listed these eleven months as a deserter. That the officer was already looking at his face the way men look at faces they've seen on a noticeboard. That the net had not closed by accident. That someone—Halvard's someone—had been watching the observatory, or the workshop, or the chestnut boy, or all three, and had known exactly which streets a desperate man would cross at dusk.

*Courage is arithmetic done fast.*

He could run. There was an alley, ten yards, and running men die tired in garrison cities. He could fight, and be shot, and the key found on his body by men whose job was finding things. Or—

Or the papers were the net. And a man can cut himself out of a net if he's willing to leave pieces of himself behind.

"Slowly," said the officer, hand on his holster.

Elias reached into his coat—and let the packet fall. Papers, pass, ration card, the whole paper self he had worn for a year, fluttering down into the slush between them. And as the officer's eyes followed them down—as every rifle in the lane dipped toward the falling white pages—Elias turned and ran for the alley after all, not away but *through*, over a wall he'd marked days ago, across a yard of frozen cabbages that crunched like gunfire under his boots, into the maze of Marsh Lane where the houses leaned together like conspirators.

Shouts behind him. A whistle. Once, wildly, a shot that took a chip off a drainpipe and taught the cabbages nothing.

He ran the routes Rosalind's city maps had taught him, the ones a clockmaker's apprentice knows because she delivers repairs to every street in Vellmark—the cut-through behind the cooperage, the steps down to the millrace, the low arch where a tall man must fold himself nearly double. His lungs burned. His hands, jammed in his pockets, closed around the key, the crest biting cold into his palm, and he ran on that grip the way a man runs on a rope bridge.

Behind him the whistles multiplied, spreading outward, calling the net wider. Ahead, the tower clock loomed against the last light, and even at this distance he could see it was wrong—could feel it, the way you feel a limp in a friend's walk. Quarter past seven, said the hands. The bells had rung quarter past seven ten minutes ago.

*She's working on you,* he thought, staggering into the shadow of the aqueduct. *Hold on. Both of you.*

By nine o'clock he was crouched in a boat shed under the millrace, soaked to the knee, listening to patrols cross the bridge above him at intervals too regular to be chance. They weren't searching anymore. Searching ends. This was *sweeping*, methodical, street by street, a grid drawn on the map of Vellmark and tightened nightly—and somewhere in that grid, at its center, sat a workshop on Marsh Lane where an old man swept floors and an apprentice clockmaker was planning, against all sense, to break into a munitions depot.

Elias put his back against the boat hull and took out the captain's note by the light of a match cupped in his palm.

*Ask the bell.*

The bell was gone from the tower. Unless it had never left. Unless a bell could be something that fit inside a chapel, inside a mechanism, inside a family—that could be carried out of a country in the hands of a girl who repaired clocks and didn't yet know she was carrying her father's voice.

"They've taken my papers," he whispered to the match flame. "I'm nobody now. No name, no number, no regiment." The flame trembled and died, and in the dark he found himself smiling, because it was true, and because being nobody was the freest he had felt in a year. "Which means, Captain, that tomorrow I can be anyone at all."

Above the shed, boots crossed the bridge. Below, the millrace muttered its one cold syllable over and over, keeping time.

Tomorrow, the chapel. Tomorrow, the bell—if he could reach Rosalind before the net reached her first. Because he had seen the patrol lieutenant's map, just a glimpse, just a corner, as the lantern swung.

There had been a mark on it. Red ink, fresh.

Marsh Lane. Number eleven.
