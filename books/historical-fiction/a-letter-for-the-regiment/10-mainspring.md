## 10. Mainspring

They posted a soldier at the observatory door at seven o'clock in the morning, which was, Rosalind had to admit, a considerate hour. At least he hadn't come at five. She had heard of shops in the lower city that were visited at five, and the owners never quite got the smell of boot-leather and official paper out of their floorboards afterward.

The soldier was young, red-cheeked from the cold, and clearly uncomfortable standing guard over an old woman's clock tower. He kept shifting his rifle from shoulder to shoulder like a man carrying something he'd been told was valuable but suspected was simply heavy.

"You're to have until dawn tomorrow," he recited, when she stopped in front of him. "Governor Halvard's orders. The clock must be running for the ceremony."

"And if it isn't?"

He looked genuinely distressed by the question. "Then I'm told there will be consequences." He said it the way a boy repeats a lesson he doesn't understand. "Ma'am," he added, as though that softened anything.

"Consequences for whom?" Rosalind asked pleasantly, and walked past him before he could answer, because she already knew: for everyone except the man who'd given the order.

Inside, the observatory was cold enough that her breath hung about her like a scarf. She lit the lamp at the workbench, set down her tool roll, unrolled it, and looked for a long moment at the great dark shape of the master clock rising through the tower's center — all brass and iron and patient weight, taller than two men, older than her father's career and possibly than her father himself.

"Well," she told it. "It's you and me again. And this time we're on a schedule, so do try to cooperate."

The clock did not answer. Clocks never did. That was rather the point of them.

* * *

The mainspring was the heart of the trouble, and hearts were never simple.

She had known since her first diagnosis that the original spring was finished — three years of neglect had done what no amount of cleverness could undo. The steel had taken a set, coiled into a shape of exhaustion, and near the inner end the metal had crystallized. She'd found the hairline crack with a magnifying loupe and a fingertip, the way Soren had taught her: *the eye guesses, the finger knows.* Run your nail along a suspect edge and wait. A crack sings back at you, faint as a plucked string.

This one had sung.

So: a new spring. Which meant she had to make one, because nobody in Vellmark stocked mainsprings for a clock of this size, and ordering one through the governor's requisition office would take a month and generate paperwork with her name on it in triplicate. She had the steel — good Swedish stock she'd bought two years ago on impulse, because it was beautiful and cheap and she'd told herself she'd find a use for it. This, apparently, was the use. She'd been preparing for this crisis since before she knew it existed, which was either foresight or luck, and Rosalind had learned not to argue with either.

Making a mainspring is not dramatic work. It is hours of annealing and winding and tempering, of watching color bloom across steel like a slow sunrise — straw yellow, then bronze, then the deep blue that means *stop, quench, now*. It is work done with tongs and patience and a small fire, in silence broken only by the hiss of oil. Her father had been able to judge temper by ear alone, holding the steel near the flame and listening for the moment the metal's hum changed pitch. Rosalind had tried to learn it and failed, and had decided long ago not to feel bad about it. Every clockmaker had one trick they couldn't steal from their teachers. Hers was something else: she could look at a mechanism for ten minutes and know what mood it was in.

The master clock, today, was anxious. She could feel it in the way the train wheels sat against each other, everything slightly over-tight, a machine bracing itself.

"I know," she murmured, feeding the new spring onto its arbor, coil by careful coil. "I know. Someone's coming to wind you who shouldn't be allowed near a kitchen timer. We'll manage."

She worked through the morning. The soldier outside stamped his feet. Once, around noon, a second pair of boots arrived, spoke to him briefly, and left; Rosalind heard only fragments through the door — *ceremony*, *dawn*, *no one enters* — and filed them away under things to worry about later. There would be time. There always seemed to be less time than there was, but there was some.

By mid-afternoon the new spring was tempered blue and beautiful, gleaming like a piece of sky someone had rolled up. She let it cool fully — impatience here meant a cracked spring and a wasted day, and she had already spent her allowance of cracked springs in apprenticeship — then fitted it into the barrel, greased it, and hooked the outer end to the barrel wall.

Then came the part she hated: winding the test. A mainspring this size stored enough force to take fingers off, and it held that force patiently, forever, waiting for carelessness. She used the winding crank with both hands and stood to the side, the way you stand away from a horse that has reason to be nervous.

The barrel turned. The spring took up tension, smooth and even, no creak, no catch. Through the case she could hear the escapement begin to tick — that dry, metronomic heartbeat she loved more than most people, if she was honest, than any people.

Something loosened in her chest. Three days of dread, unwound like a spring.

"All right," she breathed. "All right. Now the hard part."

Because a mainspring is only half the story. The other half is the fusee — that elegantly spiraled cone that sits between the spring and the gear train, compensating for the fact that a spring pulls hardest when it's full and weakest when it's nearly empty. The chain runs off the spring barrel onto the fusee's spiral groove, and the spiral's changing radius evens out the pull, so the clock keeps the same time on its first day wound as its last. It is, Rosalind had always thought, the most honest piece of engineering ever devised: a machine that admits its own weakness and corrects for it, silently, forever.

And the fusee cone of the master clock had been bothering her since the first night. Not mechanically — mechanically it was fine. But the chain showed wear marks that didn't match the groove's profile, as if the cone had been removed and refitted at some point, slightly wrong, and run for years with the chain rubbing a groove of its own making. Someone had opened the movement. Someone competent — the refit was clean, just a half-turn off — and then never touched it again.

Clockmakers are trained to notice when a thing has been apart. It's the first thing you check with any inherited mechanism: *has someone been here before me?*

She unbolted the fusee from the great wheel and lifted it free. It was heavier than it looked, solid brass, the spiral groove cut fine as a snail shell. Under the lamp, turning it slowly, she found what she'd half expected and fully dreaded: four screw heads around the base of the cone, and every one of them scarred by a blade — but old scars, browned with age, not bright. Years old.

Three years old?

Her hands went very still.

She thought of Elias Brandt in her doorway, soaked through, holding letters in her dead father's hand. She thought of the empty hollow behind the base casing — oilcloth lining, regimental key, nothing else. *Someone got here first,* Elias had said, and the look on his face had been the look of a man watching a door close on the last thing he wanted in the world.

But maybe not the last thing. Maybe only the first of two doors.

Rosalind fetched the proper screwdriver — the one ground exactly to those screw slots, because a slip here could mar the brass and she refused, on principle, to leave her fingerprints as damage on her father's clock — and fitted it to the first screw.

Her hand shook. She noticed it, frowned at it, and waited it out the way you wait out a tremor in cold weather.

"He hid something in the escapement housing once," she said aloud, to the lamp, to the tower, to whoever was listening. "Elias told me. Behind the count-wheel. I found the space empty. But Papa never trusted one lock when two would do." She thought of the workshop at home, of the false bottom in the tea tin, of the spare key taped inside the piano bench. "He never trusted *anything*."

The screw came free with a soft complaint. The second followed. The third. The fourth.

The base plate of the fusee cone — a disc of brass that should have been solid — lifted away on a hidden hinge so fine she felt rather than saw it, no thicker than a fingernail paring.

Beneath it, seated in a recess machined into the brass itself, lay a flat tin box the size of a cigarette case, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed thread.

Rosalind did not touch it for a full minute. She stood with the fusee cradled in one arm and her heart going like a striking train, and she made herself think about procedure, because procedure was armor and armor was how she survived things: note the position of the box, note the condition of the seal, photograph nothing because there was no camera and anyway photographs could be confiscated, memorize instead. Memorize everything.

Then she picked it up. The tin was cold and lighter than it looked.

The waxed thread had been tied in a particular knot — a loop-back with the ends tucked under, a knot no one uses because it's slower than every sensible knot. Soren used it. Her father had used it. She'd teased him about it once, aged eleven: *Papa, why do you tie things like a granny?* And he'd said, ruffling her hair, *because a granny knot tells me who's been in my toolbox, sweeting. Everyone else ties a bow.*

Her eyes stung. She let them, briefly, and then untied the knot.

Inside the tin: a folded page, torn roughly along its length. Half a ledger page. The other half was somewhere else — the chapel, Elias believed, under the altar floor, and God willing he was finding it even now, in the dark, with soldiers between him and daylight.

She unfolded the fragment carefully, as if it were a moth wing.

Columns of figures. Dates. Regimental designations she knew by heart from childhood dinner-table talk — supply convoys, grain allotments, coal, boots, medical stores. Quantities received. Quantities issued. And between them, in a column that had been added in different ink, quantities that existed nowhere except in this arithmetic: the difference. The skim. Three years of it, or however much fit on half a page — enough to feed a battalion, enough to buy horses that never arrived and winter coats that existed only on paper while men froze in trenches wearing whatever they had.

At the bottom of the fragment, a signature block. The approving quartermaster's name, written twice — once printed, once signed in a quick confident hand:

**Q.M. ALDEN VOSS — REGIMENTAL SUPPLY, VELLMARK GARRISON**

Rosalind read the name. Then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had slid past it the way eyes slide past things they are not ready to hold.

Alden Voss.

Alden Voss, who came to Sunday dinners sometimes, back when her father was alive, a big laughing man who brought honey cakes and called her *little cogwheel* and once let her sit on his knee and pretend to steer his carriage. Alden Voss, whose daughter was Marta. Marta, who had shared her bread through the hungry winters. Marta, who had held her hand at the memorial service when her father's empty coffin went into the ground, and cried harder than Rosalind did, and said, *he was the best of them, Rosie, he was the best of them all.*

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, wind moved through the observatory dome with a sound like slow breathing.

"No," Rosalind said quietly. It was not a word of disbelief. It was a word addressed to the universe, requesting reconsideration.

She looked down at the ledger fragment again, hoping the letters would rearrange themselves. They declined. Ink is loyal to what was written; it does not revise for the reader's comfort. *Voss.* The figures marched beside it in neat columns, and every column said the same thing: *this man signed for goods that never came, year after year, while the regiment went short.*

And her father had known. Had sat at this bench — she could see it suddenly, whole and unbearable, her father bent under this same lamp with these same tools, machining a hiding place into a fusee cone with hands that smelled of oil and honey cakes — and had known, and had hidden the proof inside the one machine in Vellmark too important for anyone to destroy, and had gone off to die a fake death rather than hand it to anyone who might be reached.

Had he known about Marta? Had he watched the two girls share sweets and wondered what he was about to take from them?

Rosalind wrapped the fragment back in its oilcloth, retied the granny knot with fingers that were almost steady, and sat down on her stool in the cold tower, holding the little tin against her sternum like a compress for a wound.

She had wanted proof. For weeks she had wanted proof — had told herself that proof would be clean, that truth was a thing you could hold without it cutting. Now she had half a page of a dead-seeming man's handwriting, and it named the father of the only friend she had left in the world.

Outside, the young soldier stamped his feet against the cold. Dawn was coming, and with it the governor, and a ceremony, and a crowd.

And somewhere across the city, Marta was probably awake by now, baking bread or banking her stove, not knowing that her best friend was holding a piece of paper that would burn both their houses down.

Rosalind put the tin in her apron pocket, over her heart, and went back to work — because the clock had to run by dawn, whatever else happened, and a clockmaker's hands keep moving even when the rest of her wants to stop.

But she caught herself, an hour later, staring at nothing, hearing a laughing voice say *little cogwheel*, and understanding with terrible clarity that tomorrow she would have to look Marta Voss in the face — and decide, before she opened her mouth, whether she was going to be the messenger or the mercy.
