## 11. The Quartermaster's Daughter

The ledger page sat in Rosalind's apron pocket all through Tuesday morning like a hot coal she couldn't put down.

She tried to work. She genuinely tried. Soren had left her a carriage clock with a sick balance spring and the expectation that it would be running by supper, and there was a certain mercy in that—a problem small enough to hold in both hands, unlike the other problem, which had no hands at all and kept slipping through her fingers whenever she reached for it.

*Quartermaster Henrik Voss. Requisition 447-C: two hundred blankets, delivered to Vellmark garrison. Received in full.*

Received in full. The blankets had never come. She knew that the way everyone in Vellmark knew it—the way you knew the river froze in January, without anyone having to prove it to you. The winter of '16, soldiers had shivered in the eastern barracks while requisitions marked *delivered* stacked up in some office three streets from where those same soldiers' wives queued for coal.

And the signature at the bottom of the requisition, copied out in her father's careful hand—*verified against delivery manifests, A. Nystrom*—was followed by the name that had stopped her breath when she'd first read it by lamplight in the observatory.

Voss.

Marta Voss's father.

"You've wound that clock four times," Soren observed from his bench, not looking up from his loupe. "It isn't going to run faster because you keep asking."

"I'm checking the mainspring tension."

"You're worrying a dead man's name." He set down his tools and looked at her over the rims of his spectacles, and for once the old man's face held nothing sly or evasive—only a tired kindness. "I can't tell you what to do about it, girl. I stayed quiet too long myself to hand out lessons on courage. But I'll tell you this much for nothing: secrets don't keep. They ferment."

"Thank you," said Rosalind, "for that comforting image."

"You're welcome." He went back to his work. Then, after a moment: "The Voss girl comes by at noon, doesn't she? Tuesdays?"

Rosalind's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "How did you know that?"

"I have eyes," said Soren, "and your friend has been bringing bread to this shop every Tuesday since before the war, and eating half of it on the doorstep, and talking at you until your ears steam. I notice things. It's the trade."

---

Marta arrived at noon precisely, as she had arrived at noon precisely for eleven years, carrying a loaf under her arm and a complaint ready-loaded. She was a broad, quick woman who worked at the telegraph office and could talk faster than most people could listen, which Rosalind had always considered less a flaw than an efficient use of a mouth.

"You look terrible," Marta announced, handing over the bread. "Worse than usual, I mean. Have you been sleeping?"

"A little. Here and there. In the professional sense of sleeping."

"That's a no." Marta let herself into the workshop the way she always did, claiming the stool by the window as though it had her name engraved on it—which, knowing Marta, she'd probably arranged at some point. "Good. Sit. Listen. You will not believe what Frau Kessler said about the governor's new curfew order. She says—and I quote, because I memorized it, because it was so perfectly ridiculous—she says, *'A city that goes to bed early is a city with a clean conscience.'* Can you imagine? The woman has never had a conscience in her life. If she had one it would be confiscated under the security regulations along with everything else."

"Marta."

"And another thing—" Marta stopped. She looked at Rosalind properly then, the way few people ever bothered to, and something in her face shifted from performance to attention. "Roz. What's wrong?"

"I need to ask you something," Rosalind said, "and I need you to hear the whole question before you answer any part of it."

Marta folded her hands in her lap. It was, Rosalind thought absurdly, the posture of someone bracing for a diagnosis. "Go on."

"When the blankets failed. Winter of '16. The eastern barracks. Your father was quartermaster then."

It was not a question, but Marta answered it anyway. "He was."

"The requisitions were marked received."

"They were."

"Do you know if they were?"

The silence that followed was unlike any silence Rosalind had ever shared with Marta Voss. Their friendship had survived eleven years on a principle neither of them had ever needed to state aloud: that between them, nothing required managing. No performance, no diplomacy, no careful handling. Marta talked; Rosalind listened; occasionally the arrangement reversed and Marta discovered she could listen too. There had never been a silence between them that wasn't comfortable.

This one had teeth.

"My father," Marta said slowly, "is a clerk. He has been a clerk for thirty years. He copies things into ledgers and stamps things and complains about the quality of the ink, and he comes home at six, and he eats his soup, and he does the cipher puzzle in the paper, and he is—" Her voice caught on something. She started again. "He is the dullest man in Vellmark, Roz. That has always been his great gift. Dull men survive wars."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Marta agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Rosalind took the page from her apron pocket. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be; there was something almost clinical about the moment, the craftsperson's calm that came over her when a mechanism finally lay open on the bench and she could see, at last, exactly which tooth was broken. She unfolded the ledger copy and laid it on the stool between them.

Marta read it. Rosalind watched her read it—the way her eyes went to the figure first, because figures were safe, and then traveled up to the name, and stopped there as though they'd walked into a wall.

"Where did you get this?" Marta whispered.

"It doesn't matter how I got it."

"Of course it matters how you—Rosalind, do you know what my father's signature is worth right now? What any of this is worth? The governor has people arrested for saying the bread is stale. This—" She pressed her palm flat over the page, not touching the writing itself, as if even the ink might testify against her. "Where did you get it?"

"A source I trust."

"A source." Marta laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "You sound like one of those pamphlets they burn people for. Is it the deserter? Everyone whispers about a deserter. The patrols are stopping people on the bridges now, asking after a tall man with a limp."

"He doesn't limp."

Marta stared at her. "You've *met* him."

Rosalind realized, with the particular horror of a person watching their own error assemble itself in front of them, that she had just confirmed the existence of a fugitive to the daughter of a garrison officer. "I didn't say that."

"You said he doesn't limp. You don't know the limp of a man you haven't met." Marta stood up from the stool, and the page slid off onto the floor between them, and neither of them picked it up. "God. Roz. What have you gotten yourself into?"

"What have *you* gotten yourself into?" It came out sharper than Rosalind intended, and she heard her own anger in it—three years of grief looking for somewhere to land, finding the nearest available target. "Two hundred men froze that winter, Marta. Two hundred men from Papa's regiment slept in unheated barracks because somebody signed papers saying blankets existed that didn't exist. And your father's name is on those papers."

"You think I don't know that?"

"Do you? Do you actually know it, or do you know it the way the whole city knows it—everybody certain and nobody saying?"

Marta's chin came up. Eleven years of friendship, and Rosalind had seen her cry exactly twice—once when her brother shipped east, once when the letter came back. This was neither. This was something harder. "My father cried at my brother's funeral," Marta said. "Did you know that? Big man, six foot, hands like hams, and he stood at the graveside and shook like a wire fence in wind. And afterward he told me—I remember it word for word, because I thought it was just grief talking—he told me, *'They sent them there with nothing, Marta. I wrote it all down. Every missing crate, every false manifest. I wrote it down and I gave it to Captain Nystrom.'*"

The workshop seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a room does when a clock stops and you feel the absence of its tick.

"He gave it to Papa," Rosalind said.

"He gave it to your father. And then your father died, and the investigation closed, and my father stopped talking about it forever, and we all went on being dull and surviving." Marta wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for the tears. "So don't stand there and ask me if I know what's on those papers. I've known since before you did. I've carried it every day for three years, and I said nothing, because saying anything would have gotten my father hanged and changed nothing else in the world except that I'd have lost him too."

The coal in Rosalind's pocket had nothing on the heat of that. She crouched and picked up the ledger page, smoothed it against her knee. "Papa didn't die."

Marta went very still. "What?"

"He faked it. Or someone faked it for him. I don't know all of it yet—I may never know all of it—but he's alive, Marta. He's alive and he hid the proof inside the observatory clock, of all the mad places, inside a clock, and I found part of it, and there's more, and it names names all the way up." She heard herself and winced. She sounded like Marta on a bad day at the telegraph office, breathless with other people's disasters. But there was no help for it. Some news refused to arrive calmly.

"Alive," Marta repeated. She sat back down on the stool, hard, as though her knees had filed their resignation. "Captain Nystrom. Your papa. Alive." She looked up, and beneath the shock Rosalind saw something else surface—something that looked almost like relief, and almost like hope, and almost like fear, all fighting for the same chair. "Then my father's testimony didn't die with him. It's still sitting in that clock, waiting."

"Yes."

"And you're going to finish getting it out."

"I'm going to try."

Marta was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a patrol passed along the street, boots ringing on frozen cobblestones, and both women listened to it go without moving.

"If you do this," Marta said finally, "my father's name is in it. Not just as a thief—as a witness. As the man who reported it. But he also *signed* the requisitions first, Roz. However honest his intentions were after, the signatures are real. When the delegates start asking questions, they're going to ask him questions, and he is sixty-one years old and he folds like wet paper under authority. You've seen him at dinner parties. Halvard's aide once asked him to pass the salt and he apologized."

Despite everything—despite the coal in her pocket, despite the patrol outside, despite the whole rotten apparatus of a city holding its breath—Rosalind felt a laugh escape her, small and startled. "He apologized to the salt?"

"He thanked it," said Marta, and then she was laughing too, wetly, helplessly, the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming, and for thirty seconds the two of them sat in the old workshop among the ticking clocks and laughed at Henrik Voss and his manners, and it was eleven years ago again and the war was something happening far away to other people.

Then it passed, the way such moments do, and Marta's face settled into something Rosalind had never seen on it before: resolve. It suited her badly and completely, like a borrowed coat cut for someone braver-looking and somehow fitting better than anything tailored.

"Here's what happens now," Marta said. "You tell me where you need the documents to go when they're copied, and I will move them through the telegraph office channels, because nobody searches a telegraph clerk, they're furniture, we're worse than furniture, we're *equipment*. And—" she held up a hand before Rosalind could object, "—don't argue. You argued me out of joining the nurses' corps in '15 and I've regretted it every day since. I am owed one act of usefulness and I am collecting it now, today, from you, at interest."

"There's a deserter involved," Rosalind said carefully. "Elias Brandt. He's the one who brought me the letters. He needs to get the copies to the peace delegates arriving Thursday."

"Brandt." Marta tested the name. "Second Company. Papa knows him—knows *of* him. Said he was the only soldier in the company who could do sums." She paused, and Rosalind watched her friend arrive, visibly, at a decision. "The patrols are asking after him on the bridges. They were asking this morning. My father mentioned it at breakfast—he's been strange lately, jumpy, taking walks at night he won't explain." Her voice dropped. "Roz, he said the warrant was being drawn up today. For the deserter. And—" she swallowed, "—and that the governor's men were talking about the workshop district next. About searching the shops."

The clock shop. Soren's benches, Soren's tools, forty years of patient work—and a wanted man who had, on at least three occasions, slipped in through the service door like smoke.

"Marta," Rosalind said, standing, already reaching for her coat, "thank you. Thank you, and go home, and lock your door, and whatever happens in the next two days, remember that your father wrote it all down—that he was, underneath everything, one of the honest ones, and honesty counts even when it arrives late and apologizing."

Marta snorted. "That's the epitaph he'd want. *He Was One Of The Honest Ones, Probably.*" But she hugged Rosalind then, hard, brief, flour-and-ink smelling, the way she had since they were girls, and said against her ear: "Bring your papa home, Roz. And bring mine through it. Both our fathers in one week. That's the job."

She left. The bell over the door rang and settled.

Rosalind stood alone in the workshop with the loaf of bread and the ledger page and the sudden, cold understanding that the net she had felt tightening around Elias for days was drawing closed tonight, and that it would draw closed here, at the shop, at the service door, at the exact seam where her old life met her father's unfinished war.

Soren looked up from his bench. He had heard none of it—or all of it; with Soren it was impossible to say. He only nodded toward the service door, where the latch hung slightly crooked, the way it did after Elias had come and gone.

"Girl," he said mildly, winding his own coat about his shoulders and reaching for the lamp, "I believe I'll take my supper late this evening. Very late. Shops want watching, these days." He paused at the door, and added, with the ghost of his old slyness returning like sun through cloud:

"And you might mention to your deserter that the back gate sticks. Always has. Terrible thing, a sticky gate, when a man's in a hurry."

* * *

Rosalind spent the afternoon doing the only sensible thing left to her: she worked.

She finished the carriage clock. She oiled the regulator. She swept the floor of the shop twice, which Soren would have called a waste of good sweeping and which she privately understood as prayer. At five the light went amber, at six it went grey, and at seven she was still there, trimming a lamp wick, when she heard the sound she had been listening for all evening and dreading all the same—

Boots. Many of them. Coming up the street in step, unhurried, the walk of men with paperwork on their side.

Through the front window she saw the lanterns swing past the chandler's, past the cobbler's, and stop.

At her door, someone knocked—not knocked; *rapped*, the official knock, the knock that is really a declaration—and a voice she didn't recognize announced to the darkened street:

"Open in the name of the military governor. We are looking for a deserter."

Behind her, from the shadowed stair to the loft where he'd been waiting out the daylight, Elias Brandt came quietly down into the lamplight, pale as paper, and looked at her.

"Well," he whispered. "I suppose this is the part where I jump out a window."
