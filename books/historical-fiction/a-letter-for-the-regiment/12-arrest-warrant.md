## 12. Arrest Warrant

Elias had learned, over three years of war and two months of hiding, that fear came in flavors. There was the thin, metallic taste of a shell screaming overhead—pure animal terror, gone as fast as it arrived. There was the slow sourness of waiting for orders you knew would kill men you loved. And there was this: the flat, gray dread of hearing boots on cobblestones outside a door you had no right to be behind.

Four pairs of boots. No, five. They walked the way soldiers walked when they were in no hurry because they already knew how the morning would end.

"You hear that?" he whispered to Soren.

Old Soren did not look up from the watch movement pinned in his jaws—he was fitting a hairspring, an operation requiring the stillness of a heron and the patience of a saint—but his eyes flicked toward the window, then back to Elias. Around the pins in his mouth, he said something that might have been *stay* or might have been *stew*. Given the man's priorities, it was almost certainly *stay*.

The knocking began. Not knocking—pounding, the kind that assumed doors were suggestions.

"Open in the name of the Military Governor of Vellmark!"

Soren took the pins from his mouth, set them down on a scrap of chamois with the care of a man laying down cards in a winning hand, and stood. He was seventy-one years old and shaped like a question mark, and he straightened himself with a series of small pops that Elias could hear across the room.

"Now," Soren said quietly, "you'll go up the ladder into the roof loft, and you will not sneeze."

"I don't sneeze."

"Everyone sneezes. It's dust up there. Bite your sleeve." Soren paused, and something dry moved through his face—not quite a smile, but its distant cousin. "And if they do find you, boy, you're my nephew from Kessel. Simple lad. Bad with clocks. We've all got one."

"I'm not simple."

"No one will believe you if you argue about it. Go."

Elias went, up the iron ladder and through the trapdoor into the loft where the shop's dead stock lived—broken regulators, orphaned cases, a stuffed owl that had once graced the window display and now wore forty years of dust like a second taxidermy. Through the gap between two floorboards he could see the workshop below, lit gray by a December morning, and Rosalind at the bench nearest the window, her back to the door, already working. She worked the way other people prayed. He'd watched her for three weeks now, ever since the night she'd thrown him out into the rain and then, three days later, appeared at his lodging doorway with a lantern and the words *"If you're going to lie to me, at least lie accurately."*

They were not friends. He was not sure what they were. But she had not given him up, and every day she did not give him up was a debt he could feel accruing interest.

Below, the door burst open without waiting for Soren to reach it.

Five soldiers. Elias counted them through the gap: four infantrymen with rifles, and an officer—a lieutenant by the single star, young, pink-cheeked from cold, wearing the polished confidence of a man whose worst experience of war was requisitioning firewood. Behind him, uninvited, trailed a clerk with a leather folio under one arm, the kind of man whose presence turned any room into a paperwork problem.

"Master Soren Vaile?" The lieutenant consulted a paper he clearly did not need to consult.

"The same." Soren folded his hands. "Though I'd take it kindly if your lads wiped their boots. I just oiled that floor."

"By order of Governor Halvard, we are authorized to search these premises for one Elias Brandt, deserter from the Ninth Regiment, wanted for theft of regimental property." The lieutenant's voice had the cadence of something memorized. "You are required to answer truthfully. Is Brandt present?"

"Brandt?" Soren scratched his jaw, producing a sound like sandpaper on oak. "Don't know him."

"He was seen entering this street on Tuesday last."

"Was he." Soren looked around his own shop with the expression of a man taking inventory. "Tuesday. That would've been the day the baker's boy delivered the wrong flour and Marta Lindqvist came in crying about her father's clock, which is a story, and the chimney smoked. Busy day. Can't say I recall every soul on the street."

Through the floorboards Elias watched Rosalind's hands go still above her bench. She didn't turn around. Her shoulders, though—her shoulders had stopped moving entirely, and he understood suddenly that stillness was her version of shouting.

The lieutenant gestured, and the soldiers began to search. They were not gentle and they were not thorough, which was worse; a thorough search might have been reasoned with, delayed, survived. These men swept their rifle barrels across shelves, sending a cascade of brass gears ringing onto the floorboards, and one of them picked up a carriage clock worth more than the soldier's yearly pay and held it to his ear as though listening for contraband.

"Careful with that," Soren said mildly. "It's older than the governor."

"Everything here is older than the governor," the lieutenant said, and there was a flicker of something in his face—not humor exactly, but the ghost of a man who might once have laughed at things. "Where do you sleep, old man?"

"Upstairs. Where does anyone sleep."

"Show me."

Soren led them up the back stair, past the loft ladder, and Elias pressed himself flat among the dead clocks, his heart doing work no escapement could regulate. Below, one soldier remained, and he wandered toward Rosalind's bench.

"Apprentice?"

"Journeyman track," Rosalind said, not looking up. Her voice was perfectly level. "Third year."

"See anything of a tall man, dark hair, limps on the left side?"

"A lot of people limp on the left side. It's winter. Everyone slips."

The soldier grunted. He reached out and turned over a half-assembled movement on her bench, and Elias saw her hand twitch—an involuntary thing, small as a heartbeat—and then steady itself.

"Mind the pallet fork," she said. "It's jeweled."

"It's what?"

"Small. Expensive. Easy to lose. Like soldiers' tempers, apparently."

The soldier stared at her. She stared back with the serene blankness of someone discussing weather. After a moment he put the movement down—carefully, Elias noted, more carefully than he'd picked it up—and moved off to overturn a drawer instead.

Above, the loft door opened. Elias had perhaps four seconds. He rolled behind the stuffed owl, which was either the most or least dignified hiding place of his military career, and held his breath until his vision sparked.

Boots crossed the loft. The lieutenant kicked at a crate. Dust rose, thick and gray, and filled Elias's nose and throat, and every nerve in his body screamed *sneeze*, and he bit down on his sleeve so hard he tasted wool and copper, eyes streaming, while somewhere below Rosalind kept talking about jeweled pallet forks in a voice like poured cream.

"Nothing up here," the lieutenant called down. Then, closer, so close Elias could have touched his boot: "Just a lot of dead clocks."

"They keep better time than the living," Soren's voice floated up from the stairs, mild as milk. "Less complaining, too."

The boots withdrew. The trapdoor closed.

* * *

They tore apart the rest of the shop for another hour. Elias watched through the floorboard gap as drawers were emptied onto the floor, as the soldier who'd handled the carriage clock pocketed a silver hunter case with the smoothness of long habit, as the clerk recorded everything in his folio in a hand that managed to look both meticulous and bored. Rosalind sat at her bench through all of it, working. Actually working. She fitted a click spring, adjusted a beat error, wound a test barrel—as if the raid were weather, as if five armed men ransacking her livelihood were simply a cold snap she'd dress warmly against.

Only once did she slip. When a soldier knocked over the jar of blued screws she'd sorted by shade—from blackest to brightest, a gradient she maintained with monastic devotion—the screws scattered across the floor in a spill of midnight blue, and she made a small sound. Not anger. Something worse. Grief, in miniature, for order destroyed.

"Sorry," the soldier said, and seemed surprised to have said it.

The lieutenant returned downstairs last, folio-clerk trailing, and planted himself in front of Soren.

"You'll sign a statement declaring you know nothing of Brandt's whereabouts."

"I'll declare I know nothing of his whereabouts," Soren said. "Whether that's the same as knowing nothing is between me and my conscience, which is a private arrangement."

"Master Vaile." The lieutenant sighed, and for a moment he sounded tired rather than official—young, really, younger than his star. "There's a war ending somewhere over those negotiations, and men in this city are being arrested daily for less than sheltering deserters. Sign the statement. Go back to your clocks. Nobody wants trouble with you."

"Then nobody shall have it," Soren said, and signed.

Elias let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since Tuesday.

But the clerk, folding away his folio, paused. He was looking at the bench by the window—at Rosalind—and then at a ledger open on the counter, the shop's repair book, and something in his face changed. He leaned closer. Touched one entry with a fingertip.

"This commission," he said. "The observatory master clock. Who authorized the repair?"

"The observatory," Rosalind said.

"The observatory answers to the governor's office now. All municipal instruments do, since the security decree." The clerk wrote something down. "This entry is signed Nystrom."

"My father's name is on the guild register. The commission passes through the family line." Her voice was still level. Still cream. But Elias, watching from above, saw her knuckles whiten around her tweezers, and thought: *she hadn't known they'd take the name. She hadn't thought that far.*

"Nystrom." The lieutenant frowned. "The captain? The one who died?"

"His daughter repairs clocks, Lieutenant. Grief doesn't disqualify her from gear trains."

The lieutenant looked at her for a long moment. Whatever passed behind his eyes, he filed it away rather than spending it. He clicked his heels—Vellmark fashion, three months out of date with the capital—said, "The premises will remain subject to reinspection," and marched his men out into the cold.

The door closed. The bell above it gave a single apologetic ring.

Nobody moved for ten full seconds. Then Soren sat down heavily on his stool, and the question mark of his spine bent further, and he put both hands flat on the bench as though steadying a ship.

"Well," he said. "That went well."

"That went well?" Rosalind was on her feet now, sweeping blue screws into her palm with shaking fingers. "They turned the shop inside out. They stole Mr. Halloran's carriage clock—that was the silver one, the one with the repoussé case, he's coming Thursday to collect it—"

"They stole it, yes. Note they didn't arrest anyone." Soren looked up at the loft hatch. "Boy. You can come down. And when you get down here, you can start sorting screws, because that girl has spent three years teaching me the difference between midnight and nearly-midnight and I refuse to learn it alone."

Elias climbed down. His legs were unreliable. In the gray light, with the shop wrecked around them—drawers gaping, gears strewn, the smell of disturbed dust everywhere—he felt the full weight of what he'd brought through their door.

"I should go," he said. "Tonight. If they come back—"

"If you go tonight, you'll be taken by morning," Soren said flatly. "There are patrols on every bridge now. You know that better than I do; you crawled past two of them to get here." He rubbed his face. "No. What happens now happens because of what I do next, and I've been thinking about it since before you knocked, so don't argue."

"Soren—" Rosalind started.

"Hush. Both of you." He stood, slowly, and began setting the shop to rights, picking up drawers, righting jars, his hands moving with the calm of a man who had decided something expensive. "Rosalind. The observatory. How long until the clock must run true?"

"The governor's ceremony. Eleven days. His office sent notice yesterday—they expect the striking train operational for the armistice signing."

"And Halvard needs it working," Elias said. "For the ceremony. For the optics. A stopped clock at an armistice would look like an omen."

"It would look like sabotage," Soren said, "and a man planning to hold power past a peace treaty loves nothing so much as a bit of sabotage to point at." He set the jar down and looked at them both, and for the first time since Elias had known him, the old man's mildness fell away entirely, and underneath it was something hard and clear, like the case glass over a fine dial. "I kept my head down for thirty years. Kept the shop neutral. Kept quiet when Aldous Nystrom came to me with questions I should have helped him answer, because I had apprentices to feed and a reputation to protect and a war on. I told myself neutrality was a kind of virtue." He shook his head once. "It isn't. It's just complicity with better manners. So."

He straightened his coat.

"When they come back—and they will come back, because that clerk wrote something down and clerks who write things down get promoted for it—I will be the one standing here. An old man alone in his shop, knowing nothing, remembering nothing. You two will be at the observatory, finishing what Aldous started, and neither of you will set foot in this room again until it's done."

"You can't—" Rosalind's voice cracked. "Soren, if they think you're hiding him—"

"They won't think it. They'll know it or they won't, and knowing requires proof, and proof requires finding him, and he will not be found." Soren picked up the fallen carriage-clock case from the floor and set it gently on the bench, aligning it square to the edge. "Besides. Someone has to feed the cat."

"We don't have a cat."

"We will by tomorrow. Cats appear when shops are troubled. It's known." 

Despite everything, despite the wrecked drawers and the stolen clock and the taste of dust still in his throat, Elias felt his mouth twitch. The old man was arming himself with jokes the way other men armed themselves with rifles.

A knock came at the door—soft, this time, civilian—and Marta Lindqvist slipped in, saw the state of the shop, and stopped as though she'd walked into glass. Her eyes went from the scattered screws to Rosalind's white face to the stranger by the ladder whom she had never officially met but whom, judging by the way her chin lifted, she recognized from description.

"What happened?" she asked. "Rosalind, what *happened*?"

"Governor's men," Rosalind said. "Looking for a deserter."

Marta looked at Elias. Elias looked at the floor. Marta looked back at Rosalind, and something complicated moved across her face—loyalty and arithmetic wrestling in broad daylight—and whatever won, she stepped fully inside and shut the door behind her.

"Then you'd better tell me everything," she said, "before someone else tells me first."

But Soren was no longer listening. He had gone very still, head tilted toward the window, and Elias followed his gaze down into the street—where, across the way, beneath the sagging awning of the shuttered tobacconist, a figure in civilian gray stood smoking a cigarette he never drew on, watching the workshop door with the patient attention of a man being paid by the hour.

Not a patrol. Not a soldier.

A watcher. Which meant the raid hadn't been the blow—it had been the measuring.

"They know he's here," Elias said quietly. "They're just deciding what it's worth."

Across the street, the cigarette glowed once, and did not glow again, and the man in gray turned up his collar against the cold and settled in to wait for dark.
