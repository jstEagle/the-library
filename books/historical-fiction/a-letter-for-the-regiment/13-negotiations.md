## 13. Negotiations

Governor Aldric Halvard had learned long ago that the most dangerous hour of any negotiation was not the one in which it failed, but the one in which it almost succeeded. Failure clarified. Almost-success made men greedy, and greedy men said things they could not unsay.

He sat at the head of the long table in the Governor's House with his hands folded on a blotter worn smooth by three governors before him, and he listened to Colonel Vess argue about grain quotas as though grain were the thing that mattered.

"The northern delegation will not sign while the requisition schedules stand," Vess was saying. He was a broad man with a soldier's impatience and a clerk's memory, which made him useful and tiresome in equal measure. "They say the schedules are theft dressed in arithmetic."

"They are correct," Halvard said pleasantly. "That is why they are called requisitions. If we called them what they are, no one would sign anything."

A ripple of uncomfortable laughter from the junior officers along the wall. Vess did not laugh. Vess never laughed, which was one of the reasons Halvard kept him — a man without humor could be relied upon to take everything seriously, including things that were better taken lightly.

The talks had been going for eleven days. Eleven days of delegates from four delegations shuffling papers across this table like men playing cards with a deck missing its face cards. The war, as far as anyone could tell, was finished; the fighting had guttered out in October like a lamp out of oil. But an armistice was not the end of a war. An armistice was a document, and documents required signatures, and signatures required agreement, and agreement required that every man at the table believe he had gotten slightly more than he deserved.

Halvard believed no such thing. Halvard believed he had gotten exactly what he deserved, which was everything, and he intended to keep it.

"Gentlemen," he said, rising, "we will recess until tomorrow morning. I suggest everyone sleep. Anger keeps poorly overnight; it sours, like milk. Reason, on the other hand, improves with rest." He smiled at the northern delegate, a thin, clever woman named Ostrevik who watched him the way a cat watches a cupboard where the mice have learned new tricks. "Madam Delegate. Until tomorrow."

"Until tomorrow, Governor," she said, in a tone that suggested she would spend the night counting the mice herself.

* * *

Afterward, alone in his study with a glass of the good brandy — the very good brandy, the bottle he did not share — Halvard permitted himself the luxury of thinking clearly.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a grocer's ledger.

The delegates wanted peace. The city wanted peace. The soldiers wanted peace so badly they had begun to want it loudly, which was the same as mutiny wearing a polite coat. In eleven days, perhaps twelve, some version of the document would be signed, because documents always were, eventually. And when it was signed, the emergency powers that had governed Vellmark for four years — his powers, granted by decree, renewed by fear — would lapse like a watch nobody wound.

He turned his glass and watched the lamplight swim in it.

Four years. Four years of supply convoys routed through warehouses that existed mostly on paper. Four years of requisition schedules signed by quartermasters whose signatures were worth more than their honesty. Four years of a war that had paid for itself, if one knew where to look, and Halvard had always known where to look. The corruption was not a scandal to him. It was a system, and systems, unlike men, could be trusted.

But there had been a problem. There was always a problem; the universe abhorred tidy ledgers.

Captain Aldous Nystrom.

Halvard swirled the brandy and thought the name the way other men said a curse word — silently, and with feeling.

Nystrom had been a good officer, which was the trouble. Good officers counted things. Nystrom had counted things for two years, quietly, in his neat hand, and then he had come to Halvard's office one gray afternoon in the third winter and laid a folder on the desk and said, in the mild voice of a man discussing weather: *Governor, either you are being robbed blind or you are the robber, and I have not yet decided which is worse.*

What a conversation that had been. Halvard still remembered the taste of it — the particular dryness of a mouth when a man realizes the person across the desk has just become either his executioner or his accomplice.

They had reached an arrangement, of sorts. Nystrom would be transferred. The folder would disappear. And then, three days later, the report came back from the front: Captain Nystrom killed in a shelling action, body unrecoverable, effects returned. A clean death. A convenient death. Halvard had attended the memorial service personally and spoken movingly about duty, and had gone home that evening and slept like a stone.

Body unrecoverable. He had wondered, even then. He had wondered the way one wonders about a loose thread on a coat — idly, and without pulling.

Now there were letters circulating. His adjutant had brought him one, intercepted at the mail depot, and Halvard had held it under the lamp for a long time. The handwriting was Nystrom's. Not similar. *Identical* — the same narrow capitals, the same peculiar way the man crossed his t's like a man signing a confession. Letters to regimental contacts, asking careful questions about requisition schedules. Questions with edges on them.

Either someone was forging a dead man's hand — possible, expensive, and pointless — or Captain Aldous Nystrom was alive, and counting again.

And now the observatory clock. That was the strangest item in the file, and Halvard distrusted strange items more than he distrusted obvious ones. The master clock had been failing for months — that was nothing, clocks failed, everything failed — but the repair order had been assigned to a Nystrom girl. The dead captain's daughter. An apprentice clockmaker, summoned to the one instrument in the city that regulated every garrison schedule, every troop movement, every bell.

Coincidence, said the file.

Halvard did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what incompetence called pattern.

* * *

In the morning the talks resumed, and by noon they stalled, precisely as he had arranged for them to stall.

It had been delicate work. One could not simply refuse to negotiate; that looked like tyranny, and tyranny invited intervention from delegates' governments abroad. No — one negotiated *beautifully*. One conceded small points with visible magnanimity. One agreed, in principle, to nearly everything, and then discovered, at the last moment, insurmountable difficulties of wording. Clause fourteen. The disposition of the river customs houses. The status of the garrison's stores pending audit.

"Audit," Ostrevik had said this morning, her voice flat as ice on a road. "You propose that your own officers audit your own stores."

"I propose," Halvard had replied, warm as fresh bread, "that we trust the institutions we have all bled to preserve. Unless the Madam Delegate proposes that trust is now obsolete."

She had proposed, in essence, exactly that. The session had broken up in acrimony, and Halvard had walked back to his study humming.

Because here was the second line of the arithmetic, the one beneath the first:

If the talks failed — visibly, publicly, catastrophically — then the city would need a strong hand. Delegates would storm home. Soldiers would grumble, workers would strike, and somewhere in the unrest there would be an incident. And a governor with emergency powers, facing chaos, could declare martial law and hold the city past any signature, past any treaty, indefinitely — *for its own good*, naturally. History was full of men who had done terrible things for the public good, and history had rewarded nearly all of them with statues.

All he needed was the incident.

He had considered several. A riot was unreliable — crowds had a way of dispersing instead of burning. An assassination was effective but wasteful, and assassins talked. What he needed was something symbolic. Something the whole city watched. Something that, when it broke, would break in front of everyone.

His eye went, as it had gone a dozen times in the past week, to the window. From the Governor's House one could see the observatory hill, and above the rooftops the tower itself, pale against the winter sky, its dial face catching the low sun.

Every regimental schedule in Vellmark answered to that clock. Every parade, every watch rotation, every train departure. When the observatory bell rang, ten thousand men moved. It was the city's pulse.

And it was failing. Had been failing for months — erratically, mysteriously, losing minutes and then finding them again, striking wrong hours, driving the garrison's timetable into confusion. The officers complained of it daily. The city muttered. A clock that lied was worse than no clock at all; it taught men to distrust the very idea of order.

*Someone should look into that,* Halvard thought, and allowed himself a small smile at his own wit.

The plan assembled itself in his mind with the satisfying click of well-made parts engaging.

On the eve of the next session — the final session, the one at which the document would either be signed or die — there would be an incident at the observatory. Sabotage. Fire, ideally, or the destruction of the master mechanism; something dramatic enough to be seen from the Governor's House. Saboteurs would be found — dead, regrettably, unable to answer questions, but identifiable as agents of the northern delegation. Evidence would be planted, thorough and damning: foreign papers, foreign coin, a map of the tower in a foreign hand.

And Halvard would stand before the city, grief-stricken and resolute, and declare martial law. The armistice talks would be suspended *for security*. The delegates would be confined to their quarters *for their protection*. The garrison would remain under his command *until order was restored* — and order, he was confident, could be prevented from restoring itself for years.

It was elegant. It was even, in its way, honest: he had never pretended to be anything other than what he was. Men like Vess called it ambition. Men like Ostrevik would call it tyranny, once it was too late to matter.

There remained only details, and Halvard was a great believer in details. The saboteurs themselves — he would use men from outside the garrison, mercenaries out of the border provinces, who took payment in gold and delivered silence. The fire — set after midnight, when the tower would be empty. The evidence — he would write the foreign hand himself; he had a fair talent for forgery, a skill he regarded as simply literacy with ambitions.

One complication: the clockmaker girl. She had been working in the tower for weeks. If she were present during the incident, she became either a witness or a corpse, and corpses generated questions, and witnesses generated worse ones. Better that she be elsewhere. Better still — his mind turned the problem over, found the smooth side of it — better that she be implicated. An apprentice found with sabotage tools, a daughter of the traitor Nystrom caught destroying the instrument she'd been hired to repair. Grief-driven vengeance, the newspapers would call it. The story wrote itself, and it buried two problems in one grave.

He rang the bell for his adjutant.

"Lieutenant Kessel. Two matters." He spoke in the mild, unhurried tone he reserved for instructions he wished never to be traced to him. "First, I want the observatory watched. Quietly — a man with a notebook, not a squad with rifles. I want to know who comes and goes, especially after dark. Especially the clockmaker."

"And the second matter, sir?"

Halvard looked out the window again. The tower stood against the sky like a patient sentence waiting for its verb.

"Second," he said, "I want a survey of the tower's cellars and stairways. Entrances, exits, where a fire would spread fastest and where it would be seen best. Have it drawn up discreetly. Tell the men it's a safety inspection."

"Safety inspection, sir?"

"Everything is a safety inspection, Lieutenant, until the moment it isn't. That is what government is." He waved a hand, dismissing him, then added, almost as an afterthought, the afterthought having been rehearsed since dawn: "And find out who assigned the Nystrom girl to the repair work. I want the paper trail. Someone signed that order, and I should like to know whether it was stupidity or sentiment. Both are actionable."

Kessel left. Halvard poured a second measure of brandy — the good bottle, still unshared — and stood at the window while the light went copper and then gray behind the tower.

Eleven days of talk, and tomorrow the delegates would return to the table angry and suspicious, primed to fail. Three days, perhaps four, and the document would collapse. Five, and the tower would burn. Six, and Vellmark would wake to martial law, and Aldric Halvard would still be governor when the snow melted, and the year after, and the year after that.

It was good arithmetic. It was, he reflected, almost beautiful — every term accounted for, every remainder carried forward, nothing left over except a dead man's handwriting and a clockmaker's daughter, and both of those, he was confident, could be made to divide evenly.

He raised the glass to the tower, a small toast between colleagues.

"To accurate timekeeping," he murmured. "May it end soon."

Below in the courtyard, a sentry's whistle marked the changing of the guard — off the observatory clock, as everything in Vellmark was, though none of them knew how much longer that would be true. Halvard drained the brandy, set down the glass, and sat at his desk to draft, in advance and in a hand not his own, the foreign papers that would hang three innocent mercenaries and save his career.

Outside, far up the hill, the observatory clock struck five — late, as it had been striking late all week, a fact the whole city remarked upon and no one yet understood.

Halvard noted the error in the margin of his papers, with a small, precise tick, as though marking a debt.

Soon, he thought. Soon he would stop that clock for good.
