## 14. The Chapel Vault

They waited for the moon to go behind the smoke of the foundries, which in Vellmark in winter was rather like waiting for a curtain to fall on cue — the furnaces never slept, and by eleven the sky above the eastern quarter was the color of dirty wool.

Rosalind crouched in the shadow of the chapel's boundary wall, her breath fogging in little impatient clouds that she kept trying to smother with her scarf. Beside her, Elias lay flat on his stomach in the frozen weeds like a man who had made his peace with the ground and intended to stay with it a while.

"You could at least pretend to be nervous," she whispered.

"I am nervous," Elias said. "I'm just lying down about it. It's more efficient."

She would have laughed if laughing hadn't felt so expensive. Three days ago she had been an apprentice with sore hands and a late clock; now she was lying in a ditch outside a decommissioned chapel that the army had quietly filled with crates of shells, next to a deserter whose face was worth a cell, all for the sake of a key stamped with a crest she'd grown up seeing on her father's coat buttons.

The key sat in her breast pocket, against her heartbeat, which struck her as appropriate. Keys ought to be kept near something that moved.

* * *

Elias counted the sentries again because counting was what he did when fear came calling — he had learned it in the trenches, where a man who counted his rounds, his paces, the seconds between flares, could keep his mind from running ahead into the dark. Two sentries. One at the gate, stamping his feet, smoking down a cigarette in short guilty drags; one walking the east wall on a circuit that took four minutes and twenty seconds, give or take the time he spent looking at the moon instead of the wall.

Four minutes and twenty seconds. Elias had crossed worse gaps with worse odds. But he had never crossed them with someone else's daughter beside him.

"The floor under the altar," Rosalind had said, back in the workshop, tracing the key's teeth against a sketch she'd drawn from memory. "If Papa hid papers in the clock itself, then this is the same thinking. A place everyone can see and no one looks at. The holiest spot in the building."

"Or the most guarded."

"Papa didn't think like a soldier," she'd said, with a small stubborn pride that had startled him. "He thought like a craftsman. He believed the best hiding places are the ones built in plain sight and finished beautifully."

Now the sentry at the gate flicked his cigarette away and turned up his collar, and Elias touched Rosalind's arm.

"Now or never," he said. "And mostly never. Stay low."

* * *

The chapel had been deconsecrated the way Vellmark did everything lately — carelessly, with paperwork. The pews were gone, sold or burned; the windows were boarded; and stacked along every wall were ammunition crates stenciled with lot numbers that Rosalind made herself not read, because reading them would mean understanding what they were standing inside, and understanding that would mean her hands would shake, and her hands could not afford to shake.

The altar remained. A slab of grey stone on two supports, too heavy to move and too awkward to bother stealing, draped now in a tarpaulin that smelled of gun oil.

Rosalind knelt before it in the dark, and something old and unbidden made her bow her head — not to pray, exactly, but to apologize for what she was about to do to a holy thing. Her father would have done the same. Her father had done the same, presumably, kneeling here with his ledgers and his oilcloth, doing arithmetic with his soul.

"Underneath," Elias breathed. He ran his fingers along the slab's base until they caught on a seam that shouldn't have been there. "Look. Dressed stone, but newer. Someone cut this and set it back."

Rosalind took out the key. It was small, iron, its crest worn nearly smooth — a tower and three stars, the mark of the Ninth Regiment, her father's regiment, the one the city still called honorable in the way people call things honorable when they've stopped checking.

The lock was set into the seam, flush, clever, invisible unless you knew to look. She fitted the key and felt the mechanism answer — a soft, oiled click, the sound of a well-made thing remembering its purpose after long disuse.

"Oh, Papa," she whispered. "You even greased it."

"What?"

"Nothing. Help me lift."

The false stone came free with a grunt and a scrape that sounded, in the silent chapel, like a shout. They froze. Outside, boots crunched past on the wall circuit. Four minutes and twenty seconds, Elias mouthed. They had perhaps three left.

Beneath the stone: a cavity lined in oilcloth, and in the cavity, wrapped in sacking and tied with cord, two bundles. Ledger-sized. Heavy.

Rosalind lifted them out and her arms trembled — not from the weight, which was modest, but from the weight of them. Three years of mourning. Three years of a grave with no body in it, of her mother's quiet going-away, of Soren's careful silences. All of it packed into sacking and hidden under God's floor while the men who caused it drank brandy in the governor's house.

"Elias." Her voice came out strange. "It's here. It's actually—"

The searchlight swept the boarded windows.

* * *

Later, Elias would try to reconstruct how it went wrong, and the only honest answer he ever gave was: the world is larger than your plan. A third sentry he hadn't counted — a runner, coming from the depot with a message — arrived at the gate at the wrong moment, saw the tarpaulin over the altar displaced, and did the one thing soldiers everywhere are trained to do.

He shouted.

What followed was not a chase so much as a negotiation between their bodies and the night. Out the sacristy door at the rear — Elias had scouted it days before, because hope is preparation wearing work clothes — across the yard where the frost held their bootprints like accusations, over the wall into the strip of waste ground beyond.

Behind them: a whistle, a lantern, voices cracking with that particular panic of men who have been bored for months and suddenly are not.

"The marsh," Rosalind gasped. "The frozen marsh — they won't follow onto the ice."

"They might."

"Then we'd better be faster than 'might.'"

The marsh began where the city's lamplight ended, a black expanse of reeds and shallow pools locked under ice that groaned faintly, like an old man turning in bed. Rosalind went first, because Elias insisted — no, she corrected herself later, because she simply went, and he followed, and neither of them discussed it, the way you don't discuss breathing.

Halfway across, the ice spoke louder. A crack ran out from her boot like a sentence being written, and she stopped, arms out, bundles clutched to her chest, absurdly careful of the ledgers even as the water beneath her considered taking her.

"Rosalind." Elias's voice, low and very steady. "Don't stop. Stopping is the weight. Keep moving, spread your steps."

"I'm holding the—"

"They're paper. Paper dries. Keep moving."

So she moved, step sliding after step, the reeds whipping her face, the crack chasing her with small hungry sounds, until the ice thickened and the far bank rose up and her legs, which had made their own decision some minutes earlier without consulting her, carried her up the slope into the trees.

Elias arrived moments later, breath tearing, and pulled her down behind a fallen trunk as the lanterns reached the marsh's edge and stopped there, sweeping uselessly over the white.

"They won't come out," he said. "Not without orders. Not onto the ice at night."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was a soldier, and I know exactly how brave I wasn't."

For a moment neither of them said anything. The lanterns withdrew. Somewhere far off, muffled by miles of frozen dark, the observatory clock struck the hour — late, as it had been striking late all week, and Rosalind thought, with a tired fierce clarity: *I will fix you. I will fix all of it.*

Then she looked down at the sacking in her arms, and untied the cord with fingers that wouldn't behave, and opened the first ledger by the light of Elias's shuttered lamp.

Columns of figures. Dates. Names — quartermasters, contractors, initials she didn't recognize yet but would. And on the flyleaf of the first volume, in handwriting she knew better than her own reflection, a single line:

*To whoever reads the mechanism.*

Rosalind closed her eyes. The exhaustion hit her all at once, complete and physical, the way dawn was beginning to hit the sky — grey, inevitable, cold.

"We have it," Elias said softly, as though saying it loudly might wake the whole world and spoil it. "Three years, and we have it."

"Yes," she said. She hugged the ledgers to her chest and found she was crying a little, and laughing a little, and too tired to be embarrassed about either. "We have it. Now we only have to survive long enough for anyone to believe us."

Above the treeline, the sky was turning the color of gunmetal. And somewhere behind them, in the warm rooms of the governor's house, a man was being handed a report about a broken chapel floor — and making, with a small precise tick of his pen, a note in the margin.

By morning, Rosalind knew — the way a clockmaker knows a spring is wound too tight — there would be soldiers at the observatory door.
