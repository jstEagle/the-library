## 15. What My Father Built

The ledgers weighed more than grief.

That was Rosalind's first coherent thought, standing in the back room of Soren's workshop with her coat still stiff from the marsh, Elias dripping on the flagstones beside her, and four oilcloth-wrapped volumes stacked on the bench where she usually cut pinions. Four ledgers, pulled from beneath a chapel altar by two people who had no business being alive at that hour, and they were heavy the way lead is heavy — not with size but with consequence.

She should have lit the stove. She should have wrung out her stockings. She should have done any of the hundred sensible things an apprentice clockmaker does when her hands are too cold to hold a file.

Instead she stood and looked at them.

"You're bleeding," Elias said. His voice was rough as a rasp dragged across soft brass. "Your hand."

She looked down. There was a shallow cut across her palm where the chapel floor's iron ring had bitten her, already clotting, already unimportant. "It's nothing. It'll scar in a nice straight line, like a seam."

"That's not — you can't just—"

"Elias." She said his name quietly, the way one sets down something fragile. "I have spent three years grieving a man who is apparently alive, two weeks suspecting it, one night crawling through frozen reeds with soldiers' lanterns behind us, and I have not slept since Tuesday, if indeed today is not still Tuesday. Let me look at my father's handwriting before I let myself feel anything about any of it. All right?"

He shut his mouth. To his credit, he looked like he understood. Grief deferred was a mechanism he knew well; she could see it ticking behind his eyes, all its wheels worn bright from use.

She untied the oilcloth.

* * *

The first ledger was regimental stock, dated three years back — the winter her father had died. Or had not died. The columns marched down the pages in Aldous Nystrom's hand: neat, upright figures with that peculiar little flourish he put on his sevens, like a man flicking ash off his sleeve. Rosalind knew that flourish the way she knew her own heartbeat. She had learned her letters tracing them from his workshop notes, sitting on this very bench with her feet dangling, while he soldered and hummed off-key and pretended not to watch her.

Grain requisitions. Boot allotments. Coal for the garrison hospital.

And beside each honest entry, in a smaller hand, squeezed into the margins like a confession whispered during a sermon: *received 400, issued 210.* *Hospital coal: 12 tons delivered, 3 tons recorded.* *Where does it go? Follow the signatures.*

"He was counting," she said aloud. Her voice came out strange, flat, the voice she used when telling a customer their heirloom clock was beyond saving. "He was counting everything, and writing down what didn't add up."

Elias came to stand beside her. He smelled of marsh water and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the chapel's cold stone. "Quartermaster Voss signed for most of it," he said. "But the countersignature — see, there, the second initials — that's H. Every page. Governor Halvard's own office authorized the shortfalls."

"I can read, Elias."

"I know. I'm sorry. I talk when I'm frightened and I'm very frightened right now and also extremely tired." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Keep going."

She kept going.

The second ledger was worse, because it was better organized. Someone — her father, or whoever helped him — had begun cross-referencing. Dates against dates. Signatures against names. A quartermaster named Voss appeared so often he might have been the ghost haunting the pages. And threaded through everything, in entries that made her breath go thin: shipments of grain and boots recorded as destroyed in transit, "lost to weather," "spoiled," "burned by partisans" — while the same weeks showed requisitions for wagons and drivers that had never left the city.

Her father hadn't just found corruption. He had built a case. Brick by brick, entry by entry, the way she would build a movement — not trusting any single part until every other part confirmed it.

"He was a soldier," Elias said softly. "But he thought like a clockmaker."

"No." Rosalind shook her head slowly. "He thought like a father who taught his daughter that a mechanism doesn't lie. You can paint the dial whatever color you like. You can engrave anything you want on the case. But open it up, and the wheels tell you what they've actually been doing." She touched the margin note — *follow the signatures* — and felt the cut on her palm sting. "Everyone looks at the dial."

"And your father built the case," Elias finished.

* * *

It was near dawn when she found the third ledger, and with it, herself.

This volume was different. The first pages were regimental business like the others, but somewhere past the middle the handwriting changed — loosened, hurried, the sevens losing their little flourish because a man writing fast for his life has no time for flourishes. The final entries stopped being records and became a letter, broken into ledger lines as though the form itself were a disguise.

*If you are reading this,* it began, *then you got past the altar, which means either the war ended or someone very stubborn found my key. Both possibilities please me.*

Rosalind sat down. Her knees simply decided the matter without consulting her.

*You will want to know why. Everyone always wants the why, as if understanding would make it hurt less. It won't. Here it is anyway: I counted the boots, and the boots did not exist, and men froze at the front in winter because a handful of gentlemen in warm rooms preferred arithmetic to conscience. I reported it through channels. Channels, I learned, are pipes, and pipes run wherever the plumber wants them. My reports went up and vanished. Then I understood they had not vanished — they had been read, and I had been read too, and the next convoy out of Vellmark was going to include me whether I boarded it or not.*

*So I did what I do best. No — that's wrong. What I did best was never soldering. What I did best was designing. In another life I'd have been a builder of towers.*

Rosalind laughed. It came out cracked and wet, and Elias pretended very hard to study the stove.

*The observatory clock was my design. Eleven years ago, before the war, when Halvard's office wanted a master regulator for the whole garrison and paid handsomely for the privilege of owning time itself. I gave them a magnificent instrument and I kept the drawings. A designer keeps his drawings. That is the first law.*

*A clock is a machine for hiding things, if you build it honestly. Think about it. A case nobody opens for years. A base full of weight and shadow. A fusee cone shaped exactly to a curve only I know. And above all — a mechanism that must be repaired by hand, by someone who can read wheels the way priests read scripture, or the whole city's time goes wrong and everyone comes asking questions. I did not need to hide my evidence where no one would look. I needed to hide it where only the right person could ever get close enough to find it.*

*I am told I am dead now. Good. Dead men are inconvenient to hunt. If you are reading this, I ask one thing, and it is the hardest thing I have ever asked anyone, harder than lying in a marsh for two nights: do not come looking for me. Finish the work instead. The ledgers name every name. Take them to the delegates, the newspapers, the guildhall steps if it comes to that. Truth is like a mainspring — useless coiled up in the dark. It has to be let out, slowly, in the right order, or it tears everything apart including itself.*

*And to whoever holds this book — I designed the hiding places so that only a craftsman could find them. Not a spy. Not a thief. A craftsman, with clean hands and patience and the guild's training. If that describes you, then I built this for you, whoever you are. But if your name is Rosalind Nystrom, then forgive me, my darling girl, for teaching you to read mechanisms and then making you read this one. I hid the message inside the trade I gave you, because it was the only inheritance I could be certain would reach your hands.*

*The clock was always meant for you. Everything else was just keeping it running until you arrived.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The last line trailed off in that flicked-sevenths handwriting she had traced as a child, and it simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when a man hears boots on the stair.

* * *

For a long time the only sound was the shop clock ticking — Soren's good regulator, the one that had governed Rosalind's entire childhood, its pendulum swinging with the patient indifference of a thing that has outlasted everyone who winds it.

She became aware, distantly, that she was crying, and that it was not at all like she had imagined crying over her father's return would be. There was no rage in it. The rage had been for the letters Elias carried, for the three wasted years, for the funeral with the empty coffin and the colonel's folded flag and her mother's grey face. This was something else. This was the feeling she got when she opened a case and found the maker's mark scratched inside the back plate, some long-dead journeyman saying *I was here, I cared, I did it properly* — except this time the mark was her own name, and the maker was alive, and the whole enormous mechanism of his disappearance resolved at last into a design she could finally trace wheel by wheel.

He hadn't abandoned her. He had aimed three years of silence at her like an arrow, and she had been walking toward it her entire life without knowing.

"He designed the observatory clock," she said. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "Eleven years ago. Before the governor's office commissioned it, he — Elias, I've been repairing his hiding place. For weeks. I've had my hands inside his message the entire time and I couldn't read it because I was looking for a fault in the escapement and the fault was *me*, I was the intended reader, the whole clock is a *letter*—"

"Breathe," Elias said.

"Don't tell me to breathe, you deserted from a regiment, breathing is optional for people like us—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and laughed again, horribly, wonderfully. "He wrote me a letter out of brass and steel and eleven years of patience. Do you know how long it takes to design a fusee cone with a false wall? Do you know how many sleepless nights go into machining a hollow inside a mainspring barrel so precisely that guild inspectors pass it twice a year? He spent *years* building the envelope before he even knew what he'd need to post in it."

Elias lowered himself onto the stool across from her. Up close, in the grey light leaking under the shutters, he looked ten years older than the man who had knocked on the service door soaked through. "In the regiment we used to say Captain Nystrom could find the flaw in anything," he said. "Men, maps, supply returns. Halvard himself supposedly said it once, at inspection — 'Nystrom counts everything.' We thought it was a joke."

"It wasn't a joke." She wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a streak of marsh mud across her cheek that she would discover later and treasure. "It was a job description."

She gathered the ledgers toward her, squaring their edges on the bench the way she squared a movement before casing it. Three volumes of proof. Names. Dates. The signature *H* marching down every margin like a column of soldiers. Somewhere in the fourth book — she hadn't opened it yet — she expected to find the rest, the copies her father had prepared for exactly this morning: fair-written summaries, perhaps, addressed to delegates and newspapers, ready for distribution. He would have prepared those. He prepared everything.

Which meant the work remaining was not discovery. It was delivery.

"The armistice delegation arrives within days," she said. "Maybe tomorrow. Maybe tonight. Halvard wants the observatory for the signing ceremony — he needs the master clock striking true when the papers are signed, because a governor who owns the clock owns the moment. That's why he summoned me in the first place. That's why he's been so *patient*." She tapped the third ledger. "My father hid the evidence inside the one instrument Halvard cannot help but stand beneath. He turned the governor's own ceremony into the delivery mechanism. You have to admire the engineering."

"I admire it and I'm terrified of it in equal measure," Elias admitted. "Halvard isn't a fool. If he suspects—"

"He doesn't suspect. He *knows something* is hidden — that's why he wants the observatory seized and 'refitted for security,' that's why his men searched the tower after we escaped the chapel. But knowing a secret exists and reading it are different trades entirely. He has soldiers." She looked down at her bandaged palm, then at the fine tools laid out in their velvet grooves, the lathe, the jeweler's lamps, everything Soren had put into her hands. "I have a craft. And the clock was built by my father, for my hands, and no one on this earth — not Halvard, not his officers, not the guild examiners themselves — reads that mechanism better than I do."

It was not pride. Well. It was perhaps forty percent pride. The rest was something steadier — the quiet certainty she had felt at nineteen, elbow-deep in the great clock's works for the first time, realizing that competence was the one armor no rank could pierce. They could arrest her. They could burn the shop. But they could not unteach her hands.

Elias watched her with an expression she was beginning to recognize: hope, held carefully, the way one holds a hair spring. "So what do we do?"

"We rest for two hours. We eat something. We copy nothing yet — copying comes after I've read every page, because my father will have ordered the information and I intend to follow his instructions *exactly*, he'd haunt me otherwise." She stood, joints protesting, and began banking the stove fire with the brisk motions of a woman who has decided to live through the week. "Then we take the ledgers somewhere Halvard can't reach, and we wait for the delegates, and when the clock strikes for the ceremony—"

The shop bell rang.

Both of them went rigid. It was barely six in the morning; the shutters were still closed; respectable customers did not exist at this hour, and neither did innocent ones.

Through the frosted glass of the door, distorted into a grey smear, came the unmistakable silhouette of a military officer's cap — and beside it, smaller, hooded, hurrying, a figure Rosalind knew even before the bell finished shivering.

Marta Voss had come to her door at dawn, alone, in the rain, wearing no gloves.

Rosalind looked at the ledgers. At the name *Voss* inked down a hundred margins in her father's small accusing hand. At the silhouette waiting on her step.

"Well," she said to Elias, very quietly. "Speak of the quartermaster's daughter, and she arrives before the quartermaster can be hanged."
