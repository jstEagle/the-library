## 16. Sabotage

Governor Aldric Halvard had never in his life been late to anything, and he regarded the habit of punctuality as the last honest virtue left in Vellmark. It was, he often reflected, a pity that honesty had so few other surviving representatives.

He sat at his desk in the governor's residence with the window open a hand's width despite the cold—a discipline, like cold baths and plain food, that he imposed on himself so that comfort would never learn where he lived—and listened to Captain Voss report on the observatory.

"The clockmaker girl is still working there," Voss said. "Alone at night, mostly. She's asked for another week."

"She may have it." Halvard turned his pen between his fingers. It was a good pen, silver-capped, taken from a delegate who had once tried to lecture him about supply lines. "A week is generous of us. Generosity disarms people. They spend their gratitude looking for a reason not to be suspicious."

"You don't intend to give her the week."

"I intend to let her believe she has it. There is a difference, Captain, and the difference is the entire art of government." He set the pen down and aligned it with the edge of the blotter. "Tell me what you've prepared."

Voss produced a leather folder and opened it on the desk with the reverence of a man presenting relics. Inside were photographs—grainy, angled, lit by magnesium flash—of tools: a cold chisel, a file, a small pry bar.

"Found these?" Halvard asked.

"Will be found. Tomorrow night, after she leaves. We place them in the clock room, along with filings from the escapement and this—" Voss drew out a twist of wire, copper, freshly cut. "Stripped from the chime circuit. Sabotage. Someone has been weakening the master clock so that it will fail—or appear to fail—at the moment of the signing. The evidence will point to her. The apprentice. Nystrom's daughter."

Halvard looked at the photographs for a long moment. Not because he needed to study them; he had approved the plan three days ago. He looked because a thing worth doing was worth looking at properly. Sloppy men lost empires by glancing.

"The chisel," he said. "Is it old or new?"

"New. Aged with acid and oil. It will read as months of use."

"Good. And the girl herself?"

"Gone before dawn. Arrested at the workshop as she arrives home. Her master Soren has already been detained—the deserter matter. No one will vouch for her."

"No one ever does," Halvard said mildly. "That is the tragedy of apprentices: they are only ever someone else's hands. When the hands are accused, the head that directed them has conveniently retired from the conversation." He rose and went to the window. Below, in the frozen square, a lamplighter made his rounds, and Halvard watched him with something close to affection. The man did one thing, did it well, and expected nothing from history. If only everyone could be a lamplighter, how simple the world would be.

"And the clock itself?" Voss asked.

"Stopped." Halvard said the word the way other men said *amputated*—without relish, but without hesitation either. "Permanently. Once the observatory is seized, I want the mechanism dismantled under supervision. Every gear accounted for. Every casing opened."

Voss hesitated. "The engineers say the clock regulates the whole city's schedule. The garrison, the trains, the—"

"The trains can run on the stationmaster's watch until order is restored. Order, Captain, is what I am providing. A city cannot have both a working clock and an armistice it didn't ask for. One of them keeps time. The other merely tells you when your authority expires." He turned back from the window. "Besides. There is a possibility—one I consider remote but refuse to ignore—that something is hidden in that mechanism."

"Hidden, sir?"

"Nystrom was a sentimental man. Sentimental men hide things in places they love. He loved that clock more than he loved his regiment, which is why he's dead, and why the clock must come apart piece by piece in front of witnesses I trust." Halvard smiled faintly. "If we find nothing, we have destroyed a symbol. If we find something, we have prevented a catastrophe. Either way I sleep well."

"And the girl?"

Halvard considered. In his experience, most subordinates who asked a question twice wanted permission to feel something, and were seeking cover for it. He gave no such permission.

"Detained. Questioned. Released eventually, if she's sensible. I bear her no particular ill will. She is simply standing where events require her not to stand." He picked up his pen again. "History is a crowded street, Captain. People get stepped on. It isn't malice. It's traffic."

* * *

The next evening, Halvard inspected the work himself.

He told no one this—not out of distrust exactly, though he distrusted nearly everyone on principle, but because a governor who visited his own sabotage in person was either a madman or a thorough man, and he preferred to be mistaken for the former than to risk being governed by the latter.

He came wrapped in a plain greatcoat, hood up, escorted by two men whose loyalty he had purchased not with money but with secrets, which held better in wet weather. The observatory at night was a cathedral abandoned mid-sermon: the dome black above, the great clock ticking in the dark like a patient heart, and the smell of oil and cold brass everywhere.

The sergeant in charge met him at the service door with a lantern shaded low.

"All placed, Excellency."

"Show me."

They climbed. The clock room was vast and dim, dominated by the case itself—tall as a house's end, glass-fronted, its pendulum swinging with a patience that struck Halvard, unaccountably, as insolent. *Tick. Tick.* As if time belonged to itself and merely tolerated them all.

On the floor beside the case: the chisel. The wire. The filings arranged just so, scattered with the studied carelessness of a stage magician. Halvard crouched, ignoring his knees, and examined each item without touching.

"Who positioned the wire?"

"Corporal Deyn, Excellency."

"It's too neat. See—it lies parallel to the case edge. Nothing falls parallel. Move it six inches toward the wall and turn it forty degrees. And the filings—" he studied the little glittering drift of brass dust. "Half of this should be under the case, not beside it. A saboteur works kneeling. Sweep half of it beneath. Do it now."

The sergeant blinked. "You want us to make it look worse done?"

"I want it to look *real*, Sergeant. Perfection is the fingerprint of fabrication. Any investigator worth his salt—and there are delegates arriving who employ several—will trust mess over order. People believe in their own untidiness." He straightened. "Also, break the glass on the left panel. Crack it, don't shatter it, and lay one shard inward. She'd have entered from the door, worked facing the case, and left hurriedly. The shard goes where her sleeve would have caught it."

The men exchanged a glance that lasted half a second. Halvard saw it, filed it, forgave it. Let them think him fussy. Fussy men won wars by refusing to lose battles over details.

At the top of the mechanism, near the chime train, Corporal Deyn stood ready with the cutting pliers.

"Not yet," Halvard said.

Deyn froze, pliers open around empty air.

"The wire stays whole tonight. Tomorrow, after the girl's arrest, my men will 'discover' the damage during the seizure inspection. If the clock fails *before* she's in custody, the story collapses—she'd claim she was fixing it, and might even prove it. But if we seize her, seize the tower, and then find the sabotage already in place—" he smiled at the pendulum, which swung on, indifferent, "—then every question answers itself. Cause follows effect. That is all law is, gentlemen: cause arranged to follow effect."

The pendulum swung. *Tick. Tick.*

Halvard stood a while longer in the tick of it, listening. There was a sound beneath the sound, he thought—some small irregularity deep in the train, a hesitation in the escapement like a speaker choosing words. He had noticed it on his previous visit and noticed it again now. The girl claimed the clock was failing and needed repair. Perhaps it was. Perhaps she was genuinely doing what she said.

It would make no difference. That was the mercy of his position: he did not need her to be guilty. He needed only for guilt to be available.

"Excellency," the sergeant ventured, "the chime barrels—we're to dismantle those first tomorrow? The engineers want the striking train out before anything else."

"Yes. Chimes first." He said it absently, watching the great wheels turn above him in the lamplight, slow as planets. "A clock that cannot strike is only furniture. And furniture makes no announcements."

He did not know why the phrase pleased him so much, but it did. He carried it back down the stairs like a coin found on the road.

* * *

In his study, past midnight, Halvard wrote the orders himself rather than dictating them. He trusted his own hand; he had seen too many clerks' hands develop opinions.

*Seizure of the Vellmark Observatory — dawn order of operations.* He listed it plainly. Cordon at four o'clock. Entry at four-thirty. Apprentice Rosalind Nystrom detained at the workshop simultaneously—no, he crossed that out and amended: *apprehended gently*. Words mattered in documents that might survive him. *Gently* would read well at any inquiry. The clock stopped and sealed. Mechanism dismantled beginning with the striking train. All materials removed to the citadel.

He signed it, sanded it, and set it aside, then sat for a moment in the quiet, listening to the distant bell of some parish church mark the quarter hour—wrongly, he noted. Two minutes fast. Even the churches had grown careless in the long winter.

There was a knock. Voss.

"The chapel detail reported, Excellency. The decommissioned chapel on Marsh Lane—they searched it again today. Still nothing. Whoever used the crest key took whatever it opened, or it was never there."

"Or it is somewhere we haven't thought to look." Halvard poured himself a finger of the thin red wine he allowed himself and did not drink it. "Nystrom hid copies of ledgers somewhere in this city three years ago, and the deserter Brandt has spent weeks circling the observatory like a moth with a grudge. The key opens a chapel floor. Fine. But keys also open clocks, Captain. Locks are a family; they resemble one another."

"You think the ledgers are in the clock."

"I think a man who designs a hiding place builds two, because a man who trusts one lock deserves to lose everything. It doesn't matter." He finally sipped the wine. Grim stuff. He drank it anyway; comfort, as established, was not a resident. "Tomorrow the clock comes apart under my eyes. Whatever is inside it—ledger pages, letters, the man's own heart if he managed to pack it—I will hold it before anyone else does. And the girl, the deserter, the delegates, the whole chorus of them will arrive at the signing to find the instrument of their little revelation already in pieces and already explained. Sabotage. Tragic. Regrettable. Investigation pending. Peace postponed—for security reasons, of course."

"For how long, sir? Postponed?"

Halvard looked at him over the rim of the glass. "Until it isn't necessary anymore," he said pleasantly, which answered nothing, which was the answer.

Voss saluted and withdrew. Halvard sat alone with his wine and his orders and the two-minutes-fast bell tolling across the frozen city.

He was not a cruel man—he had ordered the girl apprehended *gently*, had he not?—and he was not a vain one, and he was certainly not evil, a word he associated with poor self-discipline. He was simply a man who understood that peace, arriving on its own schedule, would sweep away not only the war but the men who had kept order through it. Armistice was a tide. He intended to be standing on high ground when it came in, holding a stopped clock and a very good explanation.

Dawn was four hours off. He finished the wine, washed the glass himself—servants remembered things, and servants talked—and went to bed, where he slept the way he did everything: promptly, completely, and without apology.

Across the city, in a dark observatory, the master clock ticked on with its small hidden stumble, keeping a secret in its brass throat, waiting—with the patience of all well-made machines—for someone willing to open the case.

And in a workshop below the hill, a lamp still burned where a clockmaker's daughter worked against an order she did not yet know had been signed.

She had asked for a week.

She had until dawn.
