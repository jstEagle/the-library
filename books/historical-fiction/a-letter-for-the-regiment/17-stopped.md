## 17. Stopped

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not the polite knock of a neighbor.

I had fallen asleep at the workbench again, cheek on a folded coat, the lamp burned down to a coin of oil. In my dream my father was showing me how to true a balance wheel, and he kept saying *gently, gently* in that voice I had spent three years trying to forget, and then the knocking started and the voice became boots on the stairs.

There were six of them in the workshop. Two held lanterns high enough to blind me; two stood by the door with rifles slung in that deliberately casual way soldiers have when they want you to know the rifles are there; one began, without asking anyone's permission, to open my drawers.

The sixth was an officer. He was young for the rank, or old for his face—hard to tell with men who had spent a war being promoted over the graves of their superiors. His greatcoat was dry, which meant he had come by carriage, which meant orders from above.

"Rosalind Nystrom?" he said. Not *Miss Nystrom*. My full name, spoken like a charge read aloud before a firing line.

"You're standing on my father's floor," I said. "It's older than all of us. Mind the loose board by the lathe."

The officer did not look down. "You are to accompany us to the observatory."

"At this hour?"

"The hour is not your concern."

"Everything is my concern," I said, reaching for my coat. "That's what happens when you're the only person in Vellmark who can keep the master clock alive. You may as well learn my name properly, Lieutenant, since you'll be repeating it to whoever gave you this errand."

He was a lieutenant, actually—I saw it on his shoulder strap as he turned—and something flickered across his face at being identified so quickly by lamplight and exhaustion. A man who expects to be feared does not expect to be *read*.

We went out into the cold. The city was the color of wet ash, snow turning to sleet, and the observatory hill rose black against a sky just beginning to think about gray. I could hear the clock from the bottom of the road—the deep uneven heartbeat of it, that stumble in the striking train I had been hunting for three weeks—and hearing it now, knowing what was waiting at the top, felt like hearing a friend call out from a burning house.

* * *

They had already begun.

Soldiers were in the clock room when we came up the stair—four of them with crowbars and a carpenter's mallet, working at the base casing where Elias and I had found the hollow lined with oilcloth. The great pendulum hung still. That was the worst part. In three years of failure and neglect, through every breakdown and frozen week, the master clock had never fully stopped. It had limped. It had lied about the time. But its heart had gone on beating, because a stopped clock in a garrison city is not a broken machine—it is a message.

Someone had stopped it deliberately. Someone had taken the escapement out of beat and let the weight run down, and they had done it cleanly, professionally, the way you'd close a vein.

A sergeant stood over the dismantled base casing holding something wrapped in sacking, and when he saw me he shifted it behind his back like a boy hiding sweets.

"Where's the officer in command here?" I asked the room generally.

"I am," said Lieutenant Brandt's escort, whose name I still hadn't caught and didn't intend to need. "You are to wait downstairs."

"Downstairs," I said, "is the transit room. Downstairs is full of instruments worth more than your commission, and if your men's crowbars slip through that floor while they're prying at things they don't understand, the cost comes out of somebody's ledger. Whose ledger, Lieutenant? Yours?"

I hadn't planned any of that. It came out the way good repair work sometimes does—assembled overnight in the back of the mind, presented finished in the morning. The lieutenant opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at the floorboards with new respect.

"The clock," I said, walking past him toward the movement. "Who ordered it stopped?"

"That's not—"

"It's a simple question. Either you know the answer, in which case you can say it, or you don't, in which case you're following orders from someone who knows you'd disapprove if you heard them. Which is more comfortable for you this morning?"

The sergeant with the sacking bundle made a sound that might have been a cough.

The lieutenant drew himself up. "By order of the military governor, the observatory is seized pending investigation into sabotage of regimental timekeeping. The mechanism is to be secured. That includes—" he hesitated over the word, knowing how it would land, "—dismantlement of components deemed compromised."

"Dismantlement," I repeated. I put my hand flat on the great wheel train, on iron so cold it burned. "You mean destroyed. You've been told to break it, Lieutenant, and you've been given a long word so it would sit easier in your mouth. It doesn't sit. I can see it doesn't sit."

"Miss Nystrom." He said it like a warning shot.

"Nystrom," I said. "Journeyman candidate, Worshipful Guild of Clockmakers, certified third class with distinction in striking trains, currently the only guild member in this city willing to certify the master clock for the ceremony you're all preparing for. Because that's what this is about, isn't it? The armistice signing. The governor wants the clock striking true at dawn tomorrow with the delegates watching, and he also wants the clock in pieces tonight, and those two desires are about to collide unless someone in this room has the sense God gave a mainspring."

Silence. The pendulum hung dead above us all, and I thought: *Elias, wherever you are, I hope you're copying faster than these fools are smashing.*

Because that was the truth I was circling, the truth I had understood somewhere between my workshop door and the top of this stair: Halvard could seize the tower, but he couldn't sign an armistice under a dead clock. Every schedule, every ceremony, every minute of the governor's carefully staged theater depended on the instrument only I could bring back to life. They needed me angry and compliant. Instead they were going to get me precise.

"Here is what will happen," I said. "You will send a message to Governor Halvard—not to his adjutant, to him—containing exactly these words. *The guild-certified clockmaker reports the mechanism sabotaged beyond repair by persons unknown, but believes restoration possible given one night of uninterrupted work. She requires the tower sealed, all personnel except herself withdrawn, and her personal guarantee of completion.*"

"And if the governor refuses?"

"Then at dawn tomorrow, in front of the delegates and half the diplomatic corps, the master clock of Vellmark will be silent, and every man in that hall will understand precisely who made it silent, and why, and eleven days before a peace signing is a poor season for that kind of arithmetic. Your choice, Lieutenant. Though I'd point out it isn't really yours."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then, to my genuine surprise, he laughed—a short, exhausted sound, the laugh of a man who had been carrying orders he hated since midnight.

"You're either very brave or very stupid, Miss Nystrom."

"In my trade," I said, "we call that *correctly tempered*."

* * *

They cleared the room within the hour. The lieutenant himself carried the message down the hill—I insisted on writing it out and sealing it, partly for protocol and mostly because it bought me twenty minutes alone with the mechanism to see what had been done to it.

What had been done was ugly and clever. The escapement had not merely been removed; it had been *mutilated*, pallets filed past recovery, and the filing marks were fresh. Whoever did it had worked by lantern light sometime in the last day, and they had known enough to choose the one component that could not be replaced in a night. Almost could not be replaced.

But I had spent three weeks inside this machine. I knew its tolerances the way you know a lover's breathing. And three nights ago, working the fusee cone by candle flame, hunting the second hiding place, I had found something else I had told no one—not Soren, not Elias, certainly no lieutenant: my father, it seemed, never trusted any single part of a mechanism to carry the whole burden. In the strike train, tucked behind the gathering rack, he had installed a spare set of escape pallets in hardened steel, wrapped in waxed linen, along with a note in his unmistakable hand:

*For the one who reads this far. You always did.*

I had cried, briefly, uselessly, and then I had washed my face and gotten back to work. There wasn't time for grief that didn't produce torque.

Now I unwrapped the pallets and set them on the bench beside their murdered siblings, and I began.

* * *

The answer came back at dusk, delivered by a different officer, older, with the careful blankness of a man who had learned to carry messages he despised.

"The governor accepts your terms," he read from a card. "One night. The tower will be sealed at midnight and unsealed at five. No person enters or leaves. If the clock does not strike true at the ceremony, Miss Nystrom, the governor wishes me to convey that he will consider your guarantee honored in the traditional manner."

"The traditional manner being?"

"He did not specify. He smiled when he said it."

"Then give him my compliments," I said, "and tell him a clockmaker always keeps her word. It's the whole trade. We deal in promises small enough to measure."

The officer left. The seal went on the doors below—I heard the bolts, heard the sentry's boots settle into the rhythm of a long watch. And then there was only the tower, the wind at the louvers, one lamp, and thirty-one hours of work compressed into five.

I rolled my sleeves and got to it.

* * *

You would think a clockmaker alone all night with a sabotaged machine would feel afraid. And I was afraid—my hands shook for the first ten minutes and I had to hold them flat against the bench until they remembered themselves. But underneath the fear was something else, something I had never once felt in Soren's shop or anywhere else in Vellmark: *room*. For one night, nobody was watching. Nobody was asking. The governor had sealed me in with his problem, and in doing so he had sealed me in with mine.

Because the ledgers were still in the tower. Half a page in the fusee cone, recovered days ago—but my father had designed this clock, had hidden his evidence in its bones, and a man that careful did not hide everything in one place. Elias believed the chapel vault held the originals. But the clock itself—there were spaces in that movement no inventory had ever listed, cavities a man builds into his own design the way you build silence into a conversation. If Halvard meant to dismantle the clock tomorrow regardless of what happened at the ceremony, then tonight was not only about repairing the escapement.

Tonight was about emptying the case before the executioner arrived.

So I worked both jobs at once, and it was the strangest labor of my life: with the left hand, restoration—a craftsman's honest work, fitting my father's spare pallets, truing the train, winding the weight, coaxing the dead heart back toward its beat. With the right hand, archaeology—probing the plates and posts for the false bottoms and slipped joints that only a Nystrom would think to test, opening each one, taking what it held, closing it clean.

At the second hour I found a cavity in the going-barrel housing: letters, seven of them, in my father's hand, addressed to me. I did not read them. I could not—not yet, not with five hours and a ceremony between me and daylight. I wrapped them in oilcloth and put them inside my bodice, against my skin, where they lay warm and impossible.

At the fourth hour, in the base of the striking train, I found the rest: a thin folio of papers, copies of copies, in several hands—quartermaster signatures, delivery tallies, dates. Names. Enough names that my hands went cold around warm paper.

And at the fifth hour, with the sky going from black to iron to the first bruised hint of morning, I fitted the last pallet, set the pendulum swinging, and listened.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

Steady. True. The most ordinary sound in the world, and I pressed my forehead against the cold case and let myself shake for exactly sixty seconds—one full minute of my father's clock measuring my grief—and then I wiped my face, dressed the mechanism, banked the lamp, and stood ready when the bolts came back at five.

The lieutenant unlocked the doors himself and looked at the pendulum, and then at me, and something in his young-old face suggested that he had spent his night wondering whose side history would record him on.

"It strikes," I said. "Tell the governor his ceremony is safe."

I walked down the hill with the ledgers sewn into the lining of my coat and my father's letters against my heart, past sentries and frost and the first gray light, toward a hall filling with delegates—and toward whatever my father had written to the daughter he had let mourn him.

I would read them after six o'clock. One way or another, everything would be different after six o'clock.
