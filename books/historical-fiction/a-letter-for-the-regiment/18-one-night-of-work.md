## 18. One Night of Work

The soldiers left at half past eleven.

Rosalind counted their boots down the observatory stairs—one hundred and six steps, she knew the number the way she knew her own pulse—and listened until the last door slammed and the last voice faded into the frozen streets below. Then she stood very still in the dark at the top of the tower, her breath hanging around her like a small private fog, and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she had just done.

She had bargained with an officer of the Governor's Own for one night alone with a clock. She had done it with her guild credentials held out like a talisman, her chin up, her voice steady, while inside she had been screaming in three registers. The officer—Lieutenant Vasse, young, tired, and visibly relieved not to have to arrest a girl with papers—had looked at her the way men look at a horse they suspect might bite.

"One night," he had said. "The clock must run for the ceremony. If it doesn't—" He hadn't finished. He hadn't needed to.

Ten seconds. Then Rosalind struck a match.

"Are they gone?" Elias's voice came from somewhere inside the clock's open flank, muffled by brass and iron, low and careful. For the past hour he had been folded into the base casing like a letter into an envelope, copying pages by the light of a shuttered lantern while soldiers tramped overhead and asked her questions about mainsprings.

"Gone." She lit the lamp and turned its wick low. "Though if you ever make me explain a remontoire to a man holding a bayonet again, I shall charge you double."

"I thought you handled him beautifully."

"I thought I was going to be sick on his boots."

"That too was beautifully managed."

She found him smiling at her from the shadow of the great wheel, ink on his fingers, a smudge of it across his jaw where he'd pushed his hair back. Three days ago he had been a stranger at her service door, soaked through, mad-eyed, saying impossible things about her dead father. Now he was the only person in Vellmark she trusted to hold the other end of a wrench. Life, she reflected, kept time in ways no clockmaker could predict.

"How many?" she asked, nodding at the stack of thin paper beside him—the ledgers themselves, salvaged from beneath the chapel floor, smelling still of cold earth and candle smoke.

"Eighty-two pages copied. Perhaps sixty to go." He flexed his cramped fingers. "I've written faster for less important reasons. Love letters, mostly."

"You?"

"I was young and handsome once."

"You're neither now. You're a fugitive with ink poisoning." But she was smiling as she said it, and she climbed into the clock's works to begin.

* * *

The clock had been stopped for two days, and it sulked like a living thing.

Rosalind worked upward through the train, cleaning and oiling, testing each wheel's mesh with her fingertips, listening with her whole body. Her father had built this mechanism—or designed it, at least, before she was born, before the regiment took him away every winter and brought him back thinner each spring. She had known that, distantly, the way you know a house has foundations. Only now, with the ledgers' truth spread across the floorboards below her, did she understand what kind of foundation it had been.

He had hidden a conspiracy in a clock. And then he had gone off to die—not quite—in a war, leaving the instrument for someone to find. *For someone who could read the mechanism.* She had found the fusee cone's secret because she knew how a fusee should look; she had found the hollow in the base because the base rang wrong under her knuckle. Every clue had been addressed, in effect, to a craftsperson. To a Nystrom.

"He meant it for me," she had told Elias two nights ago, and her voice had broken on it. "All this time I thought he'd abandoned the shop, abandoned me, and really he was writing me the longest letter anyone ever wrote."

Now she thought of it as she worked, and the grief sat differently in her chest—not lighter, but load-bearing. Like good timber.

Below her, Elias scratched on. The plan was simple and terrifying: by dawn, delegates from four powers would arrive in Vellmark for the armistice signing. Halvard intended the ceremony to crown him—martial law declared, the clock blamed for some manufactured sabotage, power extended past any peace. Instead, when the master clock struck six, its chime sequence would trip a compartment Rosalind had spent last night building into the gallery housing, releasing eighty copies of the ledger pages above the assembled officers. Let them fall like snow. Let everyone read.

It required, among other things, the clock to actually work.

"Rosalind." Elias's voice, suddenly sharp. "Lantern out."

She pinched the wick without question and went still. Below, through the floor, came the unmistakable sound of the service door opening.

Boots. Two sets, heavy, unhurried. A lantern beam swept the stairwell and threw bars of light up through the treads.

Rosalind lowered herself flat against the frame of the going train, her cheek against cold iron, and did not breathe. Below, she heard Elias fold himself into the gap behind the weight channel—a space barely wider than a coffin, chosen for precisely this possibility, which told her something about the optimism they were both running on.

The beam climbed. Boots on stone. A voice: "Check the tower. Governor wants the mechanism inspected before dawn."

They had come back. Of course they had come back. Halvard needed a working clock for his ceremony, and Halvard trusted no one, least of all the woman he'd grudgingly permitted to fix it.

The soldier reached the top landing. His lantern swung across the great dial's back, across the tools laid out on Rosalind's cloth in their careful rows, across the stack of ledger pages—

No. Not across the pages. Elias had covered them with the oilcloth from the base lining, weighted with a spanner. It would pass a glance. It would not pass a search.

The second soldier spoke. "Nothing here but gears. Smells like a church in here."

"It's a clock tower, idiot."

"It smells religious."

Rosalind lay against the iron and made herself think like a mechanism instead of like a terrified woman. If they searched, the pages were found, the night was lost, Soren had been arrested for nothing, Marta had risked everything for nothing. Unless—

Unless the search never happened. Unless something made these two bored, cold men want to be anywhere else.

She heard the first soldier step toward the base casing. Toward the oilcloth. Toward everything.

And then, faintly, from far below—from the foot of the stairs, from the service door that had opened twice tonight—came a voice. A woman's voice, bright and carrying and utterly unafraid.

"Hello? Is anyone up there? I'm looking for the clockmaker! Guild business!"

* * *

Elias heard the new voice and could not place it, which frightened him more than the soldiers had.

He listened, crushed into the weight channel with his knees against his chest, as the boots above paused, conferred in mutters, and descended. Through the gap between the frame members he caught a slice of the stairwell: lamplight, a young woman in a wool coat and a red scarf standing at the bottom with a document case held out like a passport.

"—apprentice papers, if you need to see them. Journeyman examination materials. Master Soren's shop sent me, only Master Soren's been arrested, which is why nobody came sooner, honestly, the guild is in chaos—"

"Marta." Rosalind's whisper reached him through the iron, wondering.

The soldiers hemmed and hawed. The woman—Marta, Marta Quarles, daughter of the quartermaster named on the ledger page burning a hole in Elias's conscience—talked over them with the tireless momentum of someone who had decided, sometime between supper and midnight, exactly who she was going to be. She complained about the stairs. She complained about the cold. She mentioned, twice, that her father was Quartermaster-General Quarles, in the tone of a woman mentioning weather.

Elias felt the name land in his stomach like a dropped weight. She knew. She had to know—Rosalind had gone to her, or she had guessed, or she had read the page herself. Either way, the quartermaster's daughter was standing in a freezing doorway at midnight lying to soldiers of the governor she'd grown up under, to buy time for documents that would end her father.

The soldiers gave up the inspection. He heard them clatter down, hear her promise to have the clockmaker's apprentice report to the guild by morning, hear the door close behind them all.

Then silence, long enough to count your heartbeats in.

"Rosalind," he called softly. "Is it clear?"

A pause. Then her face appeared above him, upside down over the edge of the frame, pale in the returning lamplight, and she was laughing—quietly, shakily, the laugh of someone whose fear has nowhere else to go.

"She's downstairs," Rosalind said. "She says she'll wait. She says—" the laugh wobbled— "she says someone owes her an explanation and a cup of tea, and she doesn't care which comes first."

* * *

Marta Quarles stood at the bottom of the tower stairs with snow melting on her shoulders, and when Rosalind came down to her, the two friends looked at each other for a long moment across twelve years of shared bread and secrets.

"You read it," Rosalind said. Not a question.

"The page you left. My father's name." Marta's chin came up, and Rosalind saw that she had been crying recently and had stopped on purpose. "I sat with it all evening. I thought about everything he's bought since the war started. The house. Mother's furs. The way he stopped looking at Uncle Aldous's memorial portrait." Her voice stayed level the way ice stays level—you could walk on it, but you could hear the water underneath. "I thought about you, sitting in my kitchen eating my mother's buns while my father stole boots off soldiers' feet."

"Marta—"

"No. Let me finish, because if I stop I'll turn around and go home and pretend, and I have spent my whole life watching people pretend, and I am so tired of it." She breathed. Snow dripped from her scarf onto the stone. "So. I'm here. Tell me what a quartermaster's daughter can do."

From the top of the stairs, Elias's voice drifted down, dry as graphite: "For a start, she can keep doing whatever she just did to soldiers, because it was magnificent."

Marta looked up the stairwell at the voice, then back at Rosalind, and her eyebrows performed a question that Rosalind chose, for the sake of the schedule, to ignore.

* * *

They worked until the windows went grey.

Rosalind lived in the clock's upper works, hands moving by muscle and lamplight, rebuilding the strike train her father had modified decades ago. The chime sequence was the key: at six, the fourth bell would sound a fraction early, tripping a lever Elias had helped her fit into the gallery housing, releasing the counterweighted tray of copies. Eighty packets, each tied with thread, each folded small. They would fall among the officers like leaves.

Below, Elias copied the final pages, his handwriting deteriorating from elegant to merely legible to something Marta described as "a spider's funeral." Marta ran messages—out to the print shop where a sympathetic compositor owed Soren a favor, back with fresh ink and news. The delegates arrived at seven. The signing was set for the observatory terrace at dawn, because Halvard wanted the sunrise behind him, wanted the city's highest point, wanted the clock striking six beneath his speech like an amen.

"He thinks the clock is his prop," Rosalind said once, around a mouthful of the bread Marta had brought. "He has no idea it's the whole argument."

Near four o'clock, disaster wore a friendly face.

Rosalind was fitting the release lever when she heard Elias say, very quietly, "Don't move," and then, after a silence, "Good morning, Lieutenant."

She went cold. She slid along the frame to the edge and looked down.

Lieutenant Vasse stood at the base of the clock, lantern raised, alone, his breath steaming. His eyes moved from Elias—ink-stained, exhausted, unmistakably the deserter whose description had been nailed to every post in the city—to the oilcloth-covered stack beside him, to the open ledger on top.

Nobody breathed. Somewhere below, a sentry coughed.

Vasse lowered the lantern slowly. He looked tired, Rosalind thought. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. He had stood in this room yesterday while she explained a remontoire, and he had watched her hands, and perhaps he had thought about what soldiers' hands do versus what clockmakers' hands do, and what gets built and what gets burned, and who gives the orders about which is which.

"The Governor believes there will be sabotage tonight," Vasse said at last, conversationally. "He wants the clock working for the ceremony. That's all I was told. That's all I intend to verify." He turned for the stairs. At the top step he paused. "My brother died at Kessel Field waiting on ammunition that the ledgers say was sold before it left the depot. I haven't read any ledgers. I wouldn't know where." He looked back once, at Elias, at Rosalind, at the clock towering over them all. "Make it strike true."

Then he was gone, and the three of them stared at each other in the ringing silence, all debts called in at once—Marta's courage, Soren's arrest, a stranger's grief, a father's long letter finally reaching its last line.

"Well," said Marta, into the quiet, and her voice cracked at last, just slightly, just humanly, "someone had better finish that clock."

Rosalind wiped her eyes on her sleeve, picked up her pliers, and climbed back into the heart of her father's machine. Above the dial, the sky was turning the color of gunmetal warming to rose. An hour, maybe less, until six.

The escapement ticked, once, experimentally, like a heart remembering its work.
