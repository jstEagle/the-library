## 19. The Strike of Six

### Rosalind

The observatory had never held so many coats.

They hung from the gallery rail in ranks—grey officer's wool, the darker cloth of the delegates, a few civilian overcoats that looked embarrassed to be there at all—and beneath them moved the men themselves, breathing steam into the cold dome like so many patient kettles. Rosalind stood at the base of the master clock with her tool roll under her arm and her heart going like a badly balanced flywheel, and she counted exits the way her father had taught her to count teeth on a wheel: methodically, without hope.

Two doors. Both guarded. The windows too high and too narrow for anything but regret.

"You'll want to step back when it strikes," she told the nearest sergeant, who had been assigned to watch her. "The counterweights run close to the wall. It's nothing personal against officers, but the mechanism doesn't distinguish."

The sergeant shifted his weight and said nothing. He had been saying nothing, competently, for two hours.

Above them, the ceremony arranged itself. Governor Halvard stood at the center of the floor where the light from the oculus fell, because of course he did; Rosalind had come to understand that Halvard treated daylight as a resource to be allocated, and he allocated most of it to himself. The delegates from the southern powers sat in a crescent of chairs that had been carried up all four hundred steps by soldiers who would remember it bitterly for the rest of their lives. An aide fussed with a small table, arranging pens in order of diplomatic precedence.

And somewhere in that crowd, moving slowly along the gallery with a tray of mulled wine he was distributing with the air of a man who had never committed a crime larger than jaywalking, was Elias Brandt.

Rosalind did not look at him. She had spent three days learning not to look at him, which was harder than any repair she'd ever done. You could rebuild a mainspring blindfolded. You could not glance at the man you loved across a room full of armed men without your face telling the whole story in letters ten feet high.

*He's copied everything,* she reminded herself. *Every page. Marta has half of them sewn into her coat lining right now, standing next to her father as though Sunday dinner were a normal thing they still had.* The thought of Marta made something twist in her chest—not fear exactly, but its cousin. Marta had chosen. That was the terrible gift of it. She had read the ledger page naming Quartermaster Voss, had gone white as unpolished brass, had disappeared for one whole day, and had returned with her jaw set like a rivet and said only: *Tell me what you need me to carry.*

The clock read five minutes to six.

Rosalind checked it anyway, because checking was what she did, because the ritual steadied her hands. The great dial above the assembly showed the hour hand crawling toward the numeral she had set it toward—or rather, that her father had designed it toward, decades ago, in a workshop that smelled of oil and burnt toast, while a little girl with grease on her nose watched him file a cam no bigger than a thumbnail.

*"What's this one for, Papa?"*

*"For six o'clock," he'd said. "Six is the honest hour. Everything before it is intention, and everything after is excuse."*

She had thought it a joke. Most of what her father said had sounded like jokes until you took it apart.

"Clockmaker." Halvard's voice carried through the dome, pleasant as ever, a voice like a well-lit shopfront with nothing behind the glass. He had descended upon her twice already tonight, each time to admire her work, each time letting his gaze rest a half-second too long on the clock's case. "You've performed miracles, I'm told."

"Routine maintenance, Your Excellency," Rosalind said. "Though I suppose from the outside it looks like magic."

"It looks," Halvard said softly, "like a machine I intend to have running perfectly at six o'clock tomorrow morning, when the armistice is signed beneath it, and every eye in Vellmark is turned upward." He smiled. His teeth were excellent. "Whatever else happens tonight, that clock will strike true. Won't it?"

"On my life," said Rosalind Nystrom, and meant several things by it.

He moved away. The sergeant resumed saying nothing. And the minute hand walked on.

Five minutes to six became four. In the gallery, Elias set down his tray. In the crowd, Marta Voss laughed at something a delegate's wife said, a warm genuine laugh, because Marta had decided that if she was going to betray her family she would do it cheerfully or not at all. Three minutes. Two. Rosalind put her hand flat against the cold iron of the case and felt, through her palm, the gathering of weights and wheels, the long patience of springs wound tight for days.

One minute.

She thought: *he built this for whoever could read it.* She thought: *he built it for me.* She thought, absurdly, of the toast.

At six seconds before the hour, the escapement released the train.

* * *

### Elias

Elias Brandt had faced artillery fire twice and desertion once, and none of it, he reflected, had required the particular courage of standing in a crowd holding an empty wine tray while waiting for a clock to commit treason on your behalf.

The chimes began.

Everyone knew the observatory's chime—or thought they did. For months the mechanism had struck wrong, cracked and dragging, the sound of a city limping. Tonight it rang out clean and full and true, the first note rolling around the underside of the dome like a coin spun in a bowl, and heads turned up everywhere, and faces softened, because people are fools for a bell done properly, and Elias counted on it.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

On the fifth stroke, hidden high in the tower where no one was looking, a cam filed forty years ago by Captain Aldous Nystrom came around at last to meet its follower. A lever lifted. A detent slipped. Somewhere inside the clock's chest, three hundred pages of copied ledger, folded into paper wings weighted with lead shot, began their fall.

Six.

The sixth stroke was different. Everyone heard it—a deeper note, almost a chord—and then, above the assembled government of Vellmark, the air itself seemed to shed papers.

They came down through the oculus light like enormous snow, turning, catching, drifting. One landed on the pen table. One settled onto the shoulder of a delegate from the south, who picked it off with the mild irritation of a man removing ash, glanced down, and went very still. Another spun past Governor Halvard's face close enough to fan him.

Elias set down the tray very quietly and began, unhurried, to move toward the stairs.

It lasted eleven seconds. He counted.

Then a staff officer said, in a voice pitched for the whole room despite himself: "What is—those are regimental ledgers. Those are *sealed* ledgers—" And the room broke apart like ice on a thawing river.

Officers surged. Delegates clutched their falling documents with the instinct of men who understood paperwork was ammunition. Halvard's voice cut through it all, still smooth, still smiling, saying something about forgery, about sabotage, about the traitors responsible—and his eyes, Elias saw from the gallery, were already fixed on the clock. On the girl beside the clock.

"*Seize her.*"

* * *

### Rosalind

They came for her fast—two sergeants and the staff officer, boots ringing on the stone, and Rosalind did the only sensible thing, which was to stand directly behind the largest piece of iron machinery in the building and look professional about it.

"Gentlemen," she said, as hands closed on her arms, "if you shake the clock while the striking train is engaged, the fly will overspeed and take someone's head off. Possibly yours. The mechanism makes no distinction. I mentioned this earlier."

"You're under arrest—"

"For repairing a clock? Splendid. Write that on the warrant. My guild will frame it."

But her mouth was doing the brave part while the rest of her did arithmetic: two sergeants, one officer, two doors, both guarded, Marta thirty feet away with a coat full of sedition, Elias—

Elias stepped out of the gallery crowd into the open floor, into the space between the soldiers and the girl, and raised his voice in the parade-ground bark of a man who had once been very good at being a soldier.

"Sergeant! You want the deserter? Here he is. Elias Brandt, Fourth Regiment, absent without leave these fourteen months, and proud of every day of it. Take me and write it down properly."

Everything stopped. Even the last of the drifting papers seemed to hang.

Because that was the thing about a confession shouted in a room full of officials—it demanded paperwork, and paperwork demanded time. The staff officer wavered between two arrests. The sergeants looked at each other. Halvard's smile acquired hairline cracks.

"The deserter," Halvard said, recovering, silk over gravel, "who has been hiding in this city for a year. How convenient that he appears now. Seize *both* of them—"

"And while your men fill out the forms, Excellency," Elias called back, walking forward with his wrists already offered like a man handing over a borrowed tool, "you might ask yourself why every delegate in this room is reading quartermaster signatures. Go on. Ask them. They're reading."

They were reading. That was the whole architecture of it—the plan Rosalind had hated and admired in equal measure: not escape, exposure. Papers don't stay fallen. Papers get picked up. A document pressed into a delegate's hand cannot be unpRESSED by any number of sergeants; it can only be denied, and denial, in front of sixty witnesses, is just confession wearing a uniform.

Marta appeared at Rosalind's elbow as if by clockwork, which, given the evening, felt appropriate. Her father was behind her, grey-faced, saying loudly to anyone adjacent that he had *no idea* how his signature had gotten onto those pages and that he intended to cooperate fully with any investigation, an announcement that fooled nobody and saved everybody, which was perhaps the definition of diplomacy.

"Your friend is surrendering himself," Marta murmured.

"I know."

"He's buying you time."

"I know that too."

"Then stop admiring the view and *move*, Rosie. There's a delegate from the southern delegation asking specifically for 'the young woman who keeps the clock.' By name. Someone sent word ahead." Marta's eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely steady. "Someone with your father's handwriting."

Rosalind's heart did something no escapement could regulate.

Across the room, the sergeants marched Elias toward the door—not roughly; even now, soldiers are reluctant to be rough in front of delegates—and he passed within six feet of her, and he did not look at her, because looking would spend time they couldn't afford, and instead he said, conversationally, to the air between them:

"Six o'clock, Miss Nystrom. Honest hour. Everything after is excuse."

Then the door swallowed him, and the cold came in, and Rosalind turned to find the southern delegate waiting for her—an old woman with snow in her hair and a ledger page held out in one steady hand.

"They tell me," the delegate said, "that you can read a machine."

* * *

### Elias

The corridor beyond the door was dim and smelled of lamp oil and old stone, and Elias walked down it between two sergeants with the strange lightness of a man who has finally put down the thing he has been carrying for fourteen months.

Let them lock him up. Let them write his name in whatever book they liked. Every second they spent on his name was a second the pages traveled hand to hand above, and every page was a nail in the governor's coffin, and he had helped build the coffin, and it was good work, joinery sound, and he—

"Brandt." The staff officer had followed them out. He stopped Elias with a hand on his shoulder, and for a moment the man's face did something complicated. "Fourth Regiment," he said quietly. "I served under Nystrom. We all believed he was dead."

"He wanted you to," Elias said. "It was safer for everyone. Including him. Mostly."

The staff officer looked back toward the door, toward the muffled roar of the hall, toward the sound—faint, rising, unmistakable—of many voices talking at once, which is the sound a conspiracy makes when it stops being one.

"Then God help me," the officer said, and let go of Elias's shoulder, and did not tighten his grip again, "I hope the girl knows what she's doing."

Upstairs, faint through the stone, the old woman from the south was saying something in a clear carrying voice, and the voices answering her were getting louder, and somewhere among the gears above them all the master clock ticked on—steady, impartial, keeping perfect time for the first time in months.

Elias Brandt smiled at the ceiling like a fool, and was led away toward whatever came next.

* * *

### Rosalind

Later—though later was only twenty minutes, though it felt like the length of a war—the hall settled into a new shape. Delegates clustered. Officers hovered, uncertain whose orders still applied. Halvard stood at the center of his diminishing circle of light insisting, beautifully, reasonably, that the documents were forgeries planted by enemy agents, and Rosalind watched the old southern delegate listen to all of it with the expression of a woman hearing a clock that everyone else had stopped noticing run audibly wrong.

"Madam," Rosalind said, when the moment opened, "if you'd care to verify the papers, I can show you the mechanism that delivered them. Designed and built by Captain Aldous Nystrom of the Fourth Regiment. The same hand that wrote those ledgers. The same hand—" her voice caught, and she let it; there was no more use hiding anything tonight— "that signed my apprenticeship papers. My name is Rosalind Nystrom. My father hid the truth of this city inside a clock, and I repaired that clock, and I will testify to every gear of it, before any tribunal you convene."

The old woman studied her for a long moment. Around them, Halvard's voice climbed another register, insisting, insisting.

"A clockmaker," the delegate said at last. "Of course. Of *course* it would be a clockmaker. The one trade that understands you can't lie about the hour forever—sooner or later the hands come round, and everyone sees."

Behind her, at the far door, there was a commotion: soldiers returning, escorting no one, and at their center a figure in a colonel's cloak who was not Halvard's man—who was, by his badges, a delegate's own guard—and who carried a folded paper and was asking, audibly, for "the clockmaker. The Nystrom girl."

Rosalind turned.

The soldier saluted her. Actually saluted.

"Message for you, miss," he said. "From a prisoner downstairs. He says—" the soldier squinted at the paper, clearly baffled by it— "he says, *tell her the toast is burnt.*"

And Rosalind Nystrom, apprentice no longer, laughed until she cried, right there in front of the delegates and the officers and the governor whose empire was coming down around his ears like a poorly maintained works, because some codes are meant for one reader only, and hers had arrived.

She wiped her eyes. She straightened her shoulders. She turned to the southern delegation, to the tribunal forming itself out of the wreckage of the evening, and she said:

"Now then. Shall we talk about names?"
