## 20. Reckoning

The papers did not fall so much as snow.

That was the image Rosalind would carry for the rest of her life—the ledger copies drifting down through the lamplit air of the observatory hall, turning slowly, catching on epaulettes and delegate's collars, settling on the great table where the armistice had been waiting all night to be signed. A clockmaker knows how a thing falls. She had spent three years learning the exact weight of small objects dropped from a height. The pages came down like the gentlest of her father's demonstrations: *watch, Rosalind—gravity is honest, even when nothing else is.*

The hall went quiet in that particular way a room full of important men goes quiet, which is to say not quietly at all. There was a scrape of chairs, a hiss of *what is this*, a colonel two rows back saying *sabotage, I said there'd be sabotage* with the satisfaction of a man whose only skill is having been afraid early.

Rosalind stood at the base of the master clock with ink still on her fingers and her heart going like an unbalanced flywheel.

Above her, Elias was already descending the maintenance stair with his hands raised—not in surrender, exactly, though it looked like surrender, though it *was* surrender, and she hated him a little for how calmly he did it. He had told her he would. *When they come for me, let them come while everyone's watching,* he'd said, somewhere around four in the morning, over cold tea. *A deserter who runs proves their story. A deserter who walks down the stairs in front of forty delegates makes them ask why.*

She hadn't argued. She'd been too busy re-tempering the strike train.

An aide-de-camp snatched a page from the table, read three lines, and went the color of tallow. He carried it to the delegate chairing the session—a grey, patient man from the northern delegation named Voss, who had struck Rosalind, during the long hours of waiting, as the sort of person who checked a watch twice before trusting it.

Voss read. Then he read again. Then he lifted his head and looked—not at the soldiers, not at Governor Halvard's rigid silhouette on the dais—but at the clock.

At Rosalind.

"Who maintains this instrument?" he asked.

Every eye in the hall followed his. It was, she thought later, the longest walk of her life, those eleven steps from the clock case to the foot of the dais, and she made them with her chin up because her father's letters had taught her one thing above all others: *the mechanism does not apologize for its function.*

"I do," she said. "Rosalind Nystrom. Apprentice journeyman, Guild of Horologists, Vellmark chapter." Her voice cracked on *Nystrom*, which she had not intended, and which turned out to be the most useful crack in the world, because a woman near the front—an archivist, grey-haired, sharp-eyed—said, "Nystrom? Aldous Nystrom's girl?" and the name moved through the crowd like current through a wire.

"Captain Nystrom is dead," said Governor Halvard.

It was the first time all night he had spoken, and he spoke it like a man closing a drawer.

"Yes," Rosalind agreed pleasantly. "That's what everyone was told."

* * *

They did not arrest anyone immediately, which surprised her. She had expected the soldiers to move at Halvard's word; instead the delegates moved first, and faster. Voss ordered the doors held. He ordered the pages collected, counted, and read aloud by rotation, "so that no single officer may be the last to touch them." It was, Rosalind thought, the procedure of a man who had sat through enough wars to know that documents die in pairs—one burned, one buried—and that the only safe place for a truth was in forty heads at once.

They read for two hours.

She stood through all of it. Elias stood beside her under guard, wrists bound with the regulation cord that left just enough slack for a man to hold a page if someone offered him one—which Rosalind did, more than once, and no one stopped her, because by the second hour the guards were listening harder than the delegates.

The ledgers told their story in the dry voice of columns and initials: grain requisitioned and never delivered, coal sold twice, boots invoiced for a regiment that had been disbanded eighteen months, uniforms contracted to a mill owned by the governor's brother-in-law at triple rate. And against each entry, the countersignature Rosalind had known since childhood—*A. Nystrom*—recording what was actually received, which was, in nearly every line, nothing.

Her father's hand marched down the margins like a metronome. Steady. Unimpressed. Refusing, even in numbers, to keep false time.

Halvard denied everything, of course. He did it well, at first—indignation, jurisdiction, procedure, the word *forgery* deployed like cavalry. But then a delegate from the western states produced his own papers, requisition copies from three years back, and matched them against the ledger lines without breaking stride, and Halvard's indignation developed a limp.

It ended the way such things end—not with a shout but with arithmetic. The southern delegates needed the peace signed; the northern delegates now required an investigation; and both sides understood, in the cold way of professionals, that a governor who had falsified supply returns for three years could falsify an armistice just as easily. Voss put it to the room in nine words:

"We cannot sign beneath a roof we cannot trust."

And Halvard's own allies—his own *allies*, the men who had dined at his table, the officers who had saluted him that very morning—took him into custody on the steps of the dais, gently, correctly, terribly, because it was cheaper than losing the peace.

Rosalind watched them lead him past her. He slowed. Of course he slowed. Men like Halvard always slow, needing one last look at whatever has undone them.

"You repaired my watch once," he said. "Winter before the war. You were fourteen."

"I remember," she said. "You didn't pay until the guild threatened to garnish your office budget."

Something crossed his face that was almost, almost humor. Then the soldiers walked him out, and the doors closed, and the hall exhaled, and Rosalind discovered that her knees had opinions about standing.

* * *

Marta found her in the clockroom afterward, sitting on the floor with her back against the great case, listening to the escapement tick with the wholehearted relief of a woman who has spent a week certain the sound would be taken from her.

"So," Marta said.

"So," said Rosalind.

"My father's name was in them." Marta did not sit so much as fold, down beside her, skirts and exhaustion together. "Quartermaster Brenner. Page after page. I've read them twice. I wanted—I don't know what I wanted. To find a mistake. To find that it wasn't really him."

"It was him," Rosalind said gently.

"I know." Marta stared at the pendulum's slow arc. "He signed the false ones himself. That's the part that—everyone else initials, but Papa *signed*. Like he was proud of the work." Her laugh came out broken. "You know what he used to say? *A good clerk keeps clean books.*"

The pendulum swung. Rosalind reached over and took her friend's hand, and neither of them said anything wise, because there wasn't any wisdom available at that price.

"He'll face a tribunal," Marta said finally. "Voss's people were clear. Testimony, evidence, all of it proper." She swallowed. "They'll want you to testify. Against him. Against all of them."

"I know."

"And you'll do it."

Rosalind thought about lying, and rejected it as unworthy of them both. "Yes."

Marta nodded, several times, like a clock deciding whether to run. "Good," she said, and her voice wobbled, and then steadied, because Marta's voice had always been better built than it looked. "Because I'll testify too. I've written it already. What I saw in the supply office, the winters the men froze in boots that were never bought. Papa taught me clean books, Rosalind. He just never imagined I'd keep them about *him*."

She stood, smoothed her skirt with hands that shook only a little, and managed something that was half smile and entirely Marta.

"Your father," she said, "would be insufferably proud of you. If he were alive."

"If," said Rosalind, and could not manage anything else, and Marta—who knew about ifs, now, better than most—squeezed her shoulder and left her alone with the ticking.

* * *

The tribunal convened nine days later in the assize hall, which had been swept, dusted, and hung with bunting that nobody believed in. Rosalind testified on the third day.

She had prepared the way she prepared everything: obsessively, mechanically, with diagrams. She brought the fusee cone with its hidden sleeve. She brought photographs of the hollow in the base casing. She explained, in the flat patient voice Soren had drilled into her—*explain to the dumbest apprentice in the shop, and never let them see you think they're dumb*—how a clockmaker hides a document inside a mechanism so that only someone who truly understands the mechanism will ever find it.

"A trap?" asked the tribunal's chairman.

"A test," Rosalind said. "My father designed it so that the documents could only be recovered by hands that respected the work. A soldier with a crowbar would have destroyed them and never known. I found them because I read the clock the way he wrote it."

"And the governor's commission to refit the observatory?"

"He wanted the clock stopped and dismantled before the armistice. Once disassembled under his supervision, the hiding places stop being evidence and become scrap. He wasn't buying security. He was buying silence with a wrench."

There was a murmur. Somewhere in the gallery, Marta's pen scratched steadily—she was keeping the record herself, unofficially, in a notebook she'd labeled, with terrible family humor, *Clean Books*.

Then came the hard hour. The quartermaster's entries. Rosalind named him—Edek Brenner, Quartermaster Third Class, regimental stores—and heard her own voice say the name of the man whose daughter had held the observatory door against a soldier's shoulder ten days ago, and felt every year of their friendship compress into a stone she had to carry to the table and set down in public.

"Do you have personal acquaintance with the accused?"

"I've known his daughter since we were six."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," Rosalind said. "But you were going to. So: yes, it costs me. And yes, I'm certain of the figures. Certainty and comfort are different instruments. I only claim the first."

The chairman looked at her for a long moment over his spectacles. "For the record," he said, "the witness distinguishes between certainty and comfort."

"For the record," Rosalind agreed, "someone ought to."

* * *

The verdicts came a week later. Halvard: stripped, convicted, remanded to the allied commission—his own allies again, signing the order, saving the peace again at the price of their pride. Three officers cashiered. Two civilians bound over. The quartermaster, Edek Brenner: guilty, sentenced to forfeiture and years, the number read out in the same bored tone as the weather.

Rosalind was in the workshop when Marta got the news, and she was there when Marta came in an hour afterward, pale, composed, carrying a wrapped parcel.

"Papa asked to see me," Marta said. "Through the solicitor. One visit allowed." She set the parcel on the bench. "He gave me this. For you."

Inside the paper was a small brass object, polished lovingly, unmistakably a watch case—empty, no movement inside—and engraved on the back in a clerk's immaculate hand: *To R. Nystrom. Clean books. — E.B.*

"He followed the trial," Marta said. "All of it. He said—" her voice caught, held, resumed— "he said you were the only witness who never once made it easy for herself. And he said to tell you the case was his father's, and his grandfather's, and that it should belong to someone who fills things instead of emptying them."

"Marta—"

"Don't." Marta held up a hand, smiling and crying in the unequal proportions of the genuinely brave. "Don't apologize. You testified true. I testified true. We're the only two people in Vellmark who can say we did both and mean the other one did too." She laughed wetly. "That has to be worth something. Write it in your books."

After she left, Rosalind sat a long time with the empty case in her palms, feeling its weight, thinking about what might go inside it. A movement takes a hundred parts, her father's old lesson ran, and not one of them is optional.

* * *

They let her see Elias on the tribunal's final afternoon, in the corridor outside the holding rooms, with a sergeant at a polite distance and ten minutes on a pocket watch that belonged to the sergeant and ran, in Rosalind's professional opinion, a full minute fast.

"They're offering him a pardon," the escort had told her beforehand, "conditional on service. His choice of garrison duty or—" the sergeant had hesitated, embarrassed— "or some kind of civilian posting. Clockwork-adjacent. Don't ask me, miss, the paperwork says *horological auxiliary.*"

"Copies the ledgers," Rosalind translated. "And keeps the archive mechanisms running."

"That'd be it."

Elias looked terrible and wonderful—three weeks of holding cells worn off him like cheap plating, but underneath, the same stubborn bright metal. He stood when he saw her, out of habit rather than permission, and the sergeant pretended not to notice.

"They gave me the pardon papers to sign tomorrow," he said.

"Are you going to?"

"I'm going to read every word first. Twice. Out loud, if they'll allow it, just to watch the clerk suffer." He glanced at the sergeant's watch. "That's fast, by the way. Minute and four seconds."

"I know," Rosalind said. "I intend to fix it. Eventually. When I have time."

He smiled, and then the smile settled into something quieter. "I owe you a debt I can't repay in a way that means anything."

"You can start," Rosalind said, "by telling me where they're keeping him."

The words landed between them like a dropped tool.

"Who?"

"You know who. My father. The ledgers prove the death report was filed from Halvard's office, not from any field hospital. No body, no grave, no burial detail in any record. A man doesn't vanish into paperwork unless someone is keeping him somewhere the paperwork points away from." She kept her voice level, craftsman-level, the voice for explaining a difficult repair. "The tribunal has three weeks of testimony saying Captain Nystrom exposed the corruption. Nobody has asked the obvious question. Where did Halvard put the man who knew too much—too valuable to kill, too dangerous to free?"

Elias was very still. Then, slowly, he said, "There were transfer orders. I burned most of them—that was my orders—but I read one first. Prison infirmary, coastal depot. It wasn't marked with a name. Just a number and a note in the margin." He met her eyes. "*Chronic condition. Keep comfortable. Indefinite.*"

"Indefinite," Rosalind repeated. The word tasted like a key turning. "Nobody writes *indefinite* about a dying man, Elias. They write it about a secret."

The sergeant cleared his throat. Ten minutes, the watch announced, had become eleven.

"The pardon hearing's tomorrow," Elias said quickly. "After that I'll have freedom of the city for the archive posting. Give me two days. There are depot manifests in the regimental records—infirmary transfers get logged for rations, even secret ones. Especially secret ones. Secret prisoners still eat."

"Eat," said Rosalind, and laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob, and she pressed the heel of her hand to her eyes for exactly one second, no longer, before lowering it. "Two days," she said. "I'll meet you at the records office."

"And if the number leads somewhere?" Elias asked softly. "If it leads to a room, and the room leads to a door, and behind the door—"

"Then behind the door," said Rosalind Nystrom, who had opened a hundred cases that other people had sworn were sealed forever, "is my father. And I am going to need someone to hold the light."

The sergeant's watch ticked its fast, eager lie toward noon. Somewhere across the city, in a prison infirmary by the grey water, an old man with a chronic condition and an indefinite sentence waited without knowing that the clock had finally begun striking his name.
