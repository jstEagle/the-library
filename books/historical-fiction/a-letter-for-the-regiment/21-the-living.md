## 21. The Living

They would not let me bring tools into the prison infirmary. I understood the reasoning—files make holes in bars, files make holes in plans—but standing at the checkpoint with my coat pockets turned out, my fingers felt naked. Three weeks of testimony, of hearings, of delegates' clerks asking me to repeat myself until my voice went thin as wire, and I had learned to live without many things. Sleep. Certainty. Marta's laughter across the workshop bench. But my hands had never once been idle in twenty-three years, and now they hung at my sides like instruments in a case that wouldn't open.

The matron who searched me was a broad, unhurried woman with a face like a well-used anvil. She found the small brass key on its cord around my neck—the crest key, the one Elias had pulled from a hollow clock base while I held the lamp—and turned it over twice.

"Evidence," she said.

"It's mine."

"Everything in this building is somebody's evidence." She weighed it in her palm, and for a long moment I thought she would confiscate it. Then she dropped it back against my collarbone. "Your father asked after you every day this week. Same question each time." She looked at me then, and something behind her eyes had gone tired and kind. "He wants to know what you built."

I did not answer. I could not have, not yet, not honestly. The corridor beyond the checkpoint smelled of carbolic and boiled linen and, beneath it, stone that had been cold so long the cold had become part of it. My boots rang on the flags. Somewhere ahead a man coughed the wet, ruined cough that three years of war had taught half the city, and someone else said *hush now* in the tone of a person who has said it ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more.

The infirmary ward was a long room with twelve iron beds and windows too high to see anything but sky. Winter light came down through them in pale slants and lay across the blankets like unspooled thread. In the third bed from the end, propped against pillows that were too flat for him, sat Captain Aldous Nystrom—or what remained of him.

I want to say I knew him instantly. The truth is meaner and stranger: I did not. The man in the bed was sixty if he was a day, gaunt as a winter branch, with white stubble on his jaw and hands folded on the blanket like two birds that had forgotten how to fly. My father had been forty-one when he died. He had been a big man who filled doorways, who lifted me onto his shoulders so I could see over the heads of parade crowds, whose laugh came out of his chest like the strike of a bell. This man's shoulders curved inward. His hair, what the infirmary barber had left of it, was grey going to nothing. Only when he turned his head toward the sound of my boots—only when he squinted against the window light the way he always had, screwing up his whole face as though daylight were a personal affront—did something in me lurch sideways and know him.

"You're late," he said.

His voice was rusted. It took me a moment to understand the words at all, they came out so thin, so careful, as if speech were a mechanism he had to wind by hand.

"You're supposed to be dead," I answered.

"Ah." He considered this. "Yes. That was the idea."

And there it was—not the bell-laugh, but underneath it, buried under three years and whatever they had done to him in the places they had kept him, the dry humor that used to make my mother throw dish towels at his head. I stood at the foot of his bed with my fists at my sides and discovered that I had rehearsed this moment perhaps four hundred times in three weeks and none of my rehearsals survived contact. In the rehearsals I shouted. In the rehearsals I wept. In the rehearsals he explained himself and I understood, and the understanding closed the wound like a well-fitted part seating home.

In the actual room, a cough rattled down the ward, a spoon clinked somewhere, and my father looked at me across six feet of iron bedstead with an expression I could not read, and I understood that no explanation was going to close anything. Some things do not get repaired. They get lived with. You learn their new weight and carry them differently.

"Sit," he said, and nodded at the wooden chair by the wall.

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes." A ghost of movement at the corner of his mouth. "You always did. Your mother said you learned to walk angry."

"My mother cried for a year."

The words came out flatter than I meant them, which is how the worst ones come out. His face did something complicated—a flinch caught halfway through becoming a nod.

"I know," he said. "Soren wrote to me. Before—" He stopped. Started again, slower, winding the mechanism. "Before the letters stopped being safe."

"Soren knew."

"He suspected. I never told him outright. Telling people is how they die." He said it without drama, the way you'd note that water is wet or that cheap steel snaps at the shoulder of the spring. "Halvard's quartermaster ring wasn't stealing boots, Rosalind. They were selling the regiment's grain allotment in the famine months and letting the garrison eat short rations. Men starved on paper that said they were fed. To prove it I needed to stay dead, because a dead captain can go where a living one can't, and a living one gets a quiet funeral of his own." He flexed his right hand, and I saw the tremor in it, and saw him fight it still. "It seemed a clean trade at the time. One death on paper instead of many real ones."

"And mine?" My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. "What was my death on paper? Because I stopped existing too, you know. I was the captain's daughter, and then I was nobody's daughter, and everyone in Vellmark watched me wear mourning for three years and thought, *poor girl*, and I believed them. I built a whole life inside your lie. Soren took me in out of pity for a dead man's child. Did that seem a clean trade too?"

The ward was very quiet around us. Even the coughing had paused, the way rooms do when grief raises its voice.

"No," he said.

Just that. No defense, no arithmetic, no *you must understand*. I had prepared myself for eloquence—for the silver tongue that had talked his way out of court-martial twice before I was born—and instead I got two syllables, delivered with the honesty of a man reading a gauge he cannot adjust.

"I told myself you'd be safer as an orphan," he went on, "which was true, and also convenient, because the truth served me. I have had three years to look at that sentence from every angle. It doesn't improve." He drew a breath that cost him. "I am not going to ask you to forgive me. Forgiveness is a thing you give a man who made a mistake. What I did wasn't a mistake. I'd do most of it again. I just—" The tremor took his hand and this time he let it. "I didn't know how much of you I'd be burying along with my name. That part I'd do differently. That part keeps me up at night in this excellent establishment."

A nurse passed at the far end, checking a chart, pretending deafness. I sat down finally, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me, and the chair scraped loud in the hush.

"They nearly killed you anyway," I said. "Elias told me. After the chapel. The men Halvard sent."

"After the chapel." He smiled, and it transformed his ruined face for a moment into my father's face entirely, and then it faded and left the ruin again. "Your deserter is worth ten of my old officers, incidentally. When they caught me I swallowed the key. Did he ever mention that?"

"He mentioned you bit a sergeant."

"The key was more important. The biting was incidental." He shifted against the pillows, and I saw how much even that cost him, and hated how carefully I was noticing. "They never found it. Three years in five different cells and they never found it, because I am very good at being empty-handed." He looked at the cord at my throat, where the brass key lay hidden under my collar. "You brought it."

"You knew I would."

"I hoped. Knowing and hoping are different trades. I was a soldier; hoping was never my strong suit." He studied me then, really studied me, the way I studied a movement when it first came across my bench—with attention that left nowhere to hide. "Rosalind. Ask me the thing you came here to ask. Whatever it is. I'm too old and too tired and too fond of you to survive you being polite."

I looked at my hands. There was a burn scar on my left thumb from a soldering iron, age fourteen, the day he taught me to sweat a joint and I'd rushed it. He'd wrapped it himself, clumsily, with a strip of shirt, and told me the scar would remember the lesson longer than I would.

"Why the clock?" I said. "Of everything in the world—banks, churches, the archive vaults—you chose to hide the proof inside a timepiece. You designed that escapement yourself. You spent years on it. Why?"

"Because of what a clock is," he said simply. "Every other strongbox in this city waits for someone to come open it. A clock does the opposite. It announces itself. Every hour, all day, all night, it speaks to everyone within earshot and no one listens, because everyone thinks they already know what it's saying." His eyes had gone bright, fever-bright, the brightness of the old workshop arguments. "I needed the ledgers somewhere that Halvard's men would search a hundred times and find only machinery. And I needed them somewhere that—if I died properly, if the plan failed—would eventually be opened by someone who could *read* it. Not break into it. Read it. Do you understand the difference?"

"The fusee cone," I said. "The ledger page was in the fusee cone. And the chime sequence—the count of six—it releases the compartment above the ceremony floor. You built the message into the striking train."

Something moved through his face then that I will be describing badly for the rest of my life. Pride is too small a word. Grief is wrong too. It was the look of a man watching a spring he coiled thirty years ago finally, perfectly, release.

"I built it for whoever repaired it," he said quietly. "I couldn't know who that would be. I could only make sure that whoever it was would have to be good enough to deserve the answer." He paused. "And then they sent word to the guild that the observatory clock was failing, and the guild sent an apprentice, and Soren wrote me one last letter that said only: *the girl has your hands.*"

"I don't have your hands," I said. "I have my own hands. They're better."

The laugh came out of him then—rusted, half-strangled, real. It bent him double against the pillows and turned the heads of two old soldiers in the far beds, and I found, to my considerable irritation, that my own face had cracked into a grin I had no intention of permitting.

"There she is," he wheezed. "There she is. God help the governor if you'd been on his side."

We were quiet awhile after that. The light had shifted; the slants of winter sun had crawled across the floorboards and now lay against his blanket, warming his hands, and he turned them palm-up in it like a cat.

"What happens to you now?" I asked.

"A tribunal. Delegates want to question me about the supply chain before the treaty terms are finalized. Then, I'm told, a pardon, possibly a pension, certainly a great deal of boring official tea." He grimaced. "I've been declared dead once already. I find I've rather lost my appetite for ceremonies."

"Elias is in the cells downstairs. They're holding him until the pardon papers clear. He surrendered himself at the ceremony so the documents could spread—he bought us the minutes we needed with his own neck."

"I know. I've been writing letters on his behalf. My hand carries weight again, apparently—being resurrected has its uses." His voice gentled. "He talks about you, in the letters he writes back. Did you know that? He says you argue like a fusee cone—everything you resist makes you turn faster."

"That's not how a fusee works."

"I know. I think that's why he likes it."

* * *

Visiting hours ended with a bell, because of course they did, and the matron appeared between us like a drawn line. I stood. My father reached out and caught my wrist—not gripping, just resting, the way you rest a finger on a balance wheel to feel whether it's alive.

"One more thing," he said. "The question I've been asking the matron all week. She promised she'd deliver it, but she's a cruel woman and enjoys making me wait." He glanced at her; the anvil-face did not change. "Tell me what you built. Not the clock. The other thing. In that tower, in one night, with soldiers at the door. Tell me what you built."

I could have given him the inventory. New mainspring, hand-fitted. Escapement re-poised. Chime train altered to release the compartment at the sixth hour. Two hundred copies of a dead regiment's honest accounts, distributed into four hundred hands before the officers finished their coffee. All true. All beside the point, and he knew it, and I knew he knew it, and so I gave him the true answer instead, the one I hadn't said aloud to anyone, including myself.

"I built the version of me that doesn't wait to be told what things mean," I said. "You hid the truth in a machine and trusted a stranger to find it, and I opened the case, and I read it, and then I decided what to do. Nobody ordered me. Nobody permitted me. I stood in front of the governor's officer and lied to his face with my guild credentials and my father's eyes, and I won." I swallowed. "I'm not the apprentice anymore, Papa. I'm not even your daughter-the-orphan. I'm Rosalind Nystrom, journeyman clockmaker, and I keep time in Vellmark, and I am going to be insufferable about it for the rest of my life."

His eyes had gone bright again, and this time it was not fever.

"The certificate," he managed. "Soren signed it himself?"

"Soren signed it. Marta witnessed it. Half the delegates' clerks applauded, which was embarrassing, but I've decided to allow it."

"Good," he said. "Good." He released my wrist and settled back into his pillows, and for a moment he looked less broken—not mended, nothing here would mend, but like a mechanism that had finally been set running in the right direction after years of running backward. "Come back Thursday. Bring the certificate. I intend to inspect it for faults."

"You'll find none."

"I'll find one," he said comfortably, closing his eyes. "I'm your father. Finding fault is the one mechanism I never let wear out."

The matron walked me down the corridor, past the checkpoint, past the turned-out pockets, and at the outer door she paused.

"He slept the week you testified," she said. "First proper sleep since they brought him in. Now you know, so you'll come back, and he'll sleep again Thursday, and that's how it goes." She held the door open into the cold. "Families," she added, in the tone of a woman filing a difficult report. "Nobody repairs them. They just keep running."

Outside, Vellmark lay under a sky the color of pewter, and the bells of the observatory rang out across the roofs—one o'clock, exactly one o'clock, true to the second—and I stood in the falling light of them and counted every stroke, and started walking home.
