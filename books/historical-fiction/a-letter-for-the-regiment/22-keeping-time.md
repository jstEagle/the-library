## 22. Keeping Time

Spring came late to Vellmark that year, the way it always did, creeping up the harbor road in fits and starts as if it too were checking whether the war was truly over before committing itself. By the time the cherry trees along the regimental parade ground bloomed—and they bloomed extravagantly, defiantly, as though making up for three years of holding their breath—Rosalind Nystrom had been a journeyman clockmaker for exactly eleven days.

The certificate hung in the workshop window where old Soren's had hung before his arrest, and then hers in his name, and then nothing at all through those gray weeks when the shop stood sealed with wax paper over the broken glass. It was Marta who had suggested the window placement.

"People should see it," she'd said, pinning the certificate to the frame with a thumbtack and her chin. "People should walk past and know that a Nystrom made this."

"A Vellmark guild journeyman made it," Rosalind had corrected, but Marta only looked at her with that expression she had perfected since February—the one that said *I held the door for you, I will not watch you hide behind modesty now*—and Rosalind had let the tack stay.

Her name. Her own name, in the guild register, in ink she had signed herself while the registrar's clerk watched with an expression suggesting he still wasn't entirely certain how a woman of twenty-three had come to be standing there. *Rosalind Aldousdatter Nystrom*, journeyman of the Clockmakers' Guild, examined and passed on work shown: one (1) mainspring, rebuilt; one (1) escapement assembly, restored; one (1) master regulator, observatory-grade, kept true for ninety consecutive days.

That last line had required some negotiation. The guild examiner, Master Ostrick, a man built like a barrel and nearly as roundabout in conversation, had initially balked at certifying a mechanism he hadn't personally inspected.

"Ninety days," he'd said, tapping the ledger. "You're asking me to take your word."

"You could come and listen to it," Rosalind had offered. "Any hour you like. It strikes true. You can set your dinner by it. You can set your *life* by it, which is more than most of us can say about anything these days."

Master Ostrick had come. He had stood in the observatory dome with his head tipped back like a man watching swallows, listening to the great clock breathe through its full train—wheel by wheel, click by click, the escapement letting go its measured heartbeats into the silence—and when it struck six he had closed his eyes.

"Well," he'd said finally. "I'll want to write down what you did to the fusee cone. For the guild records."

"Write whatever you like," Rosalind had told him. "It's all true. That seems to matter lately."

He had laughed at that—a surprised bark of a laugh—and written everything down.

* * *

The workshop smelled different in spring. In winter it had been solder flux and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a room heated by one stubborn stove, but now, with the door propped open onto the yard, it carried cut grass and the wet-earth sweetness of turned garden soil, and beneath it all the eternal brass-and-oil heartbeat of the trade. Rosalind had decided she liked the mixture. It was honest. Things grew; things were mended; both took daily attention and neither ever finished.

Elias was in the garden, which was still a strange sentence to complete in her head, even after two months of it being true.

He knelt among the bean rows with his sleeves rolled up, and the sun had begun—finally, grudgingly—to put color back into him. When he'd first arrived, pardoned and hollow-eyed and jumping at the sound of boots on cobbles, he'd had the look of furniture left out in the rain: still serviceable, but warped somewhere fundamental. Soren's arrest had done part of it. His own surrender at the armistice ceremony had done the rest—he had stepped forward in front of three hundred officers and delegates, hands raised, voice steady, and given himself up so that the ledger copies could move hand to hand through the crowd like contraband of the only kind that mattered.

The tribunal had taken four weeks. The pardon had taken two more. What neither process could give back was the man Elias Brandt had been before the ledgers, before the burning order, before three years of sleeping in ditches with a captain's letters sewn into his coat lining. What grew in that man's place was quieter, carefuler, and—as Rosalind had discovered with something between surprise and amusement—funnier than she'd expected.

"You've planted them wrong," she called from the doorway.

"I have not." He didn't look up. "Beans go in rows."

"They go in rows *north-south*. Yours run east-west. They'll shade each other by afternoon."

A pause. He sat back on his heels and squinted at the rows, then at the sun, then at her, with the expression of a man re-examining a battle he'd thought won. "Your father's notes say east-west."

"My father," said Rosalind, "faked his death, hid state evidence inside a fusee cone, and spent three years letting his daughter believe he was dead. I love him. I would not take his gardening advice."

Elias laughed—the real laugh now, not the careful one—and pulled his cap off to scrub a hand through his hair. "Fine. Show me."

She came down the steps and showed him. She moved his rows with her boot heel drawn in the dirt, demonstrating angles, explaining afternoon light the way she'd explain gear trains, and he listened with the same total attention he gave to everything—which was, she suspected, the thing she'd first trusted in him back in that terrible lamplit night among the gears, before trust had any right to exist: that he paid attention the way craftsmen did. Nothing escaped him. He simply hadn't known, until recently, what to do with a life where nothing needed escaping.

Soren weeded the far bed. He had come home in April—thin, coughing, with a stiffness in his left hand that would never fully leave—but home, and the first thing he'd done upon release was walk to the shop, stand in the doorway a long moment looking at the certificate in the window, and say, "Huh. Crooked." Then he'd straightened it, gone inside, and picked up a burnisher as if no time had passed at all.

He never apologized outright for the years of silence—for knowing her father's suspicions and choosing the shop's safety over the truth. But one evening in March, working late over a carriage clock, he had said, without looking up: "I told myself I was protecting you. That was mostly true. The rest was that I was afraid, and fear wears a very convincing uniform." He'd fitted a pallet jewel into place with a breath-held precision. "Don't be me, girl."

"I'm trying not to be anyone but myself," she'd answered, and he'd nodded once, and that had been the whole of the reckoning between them. Some repairs, she understood, were not restorations. Some things, once broken, are simply reassembled around the break, and the break becomes part of how they keep time.

* * *

Her father came on Thursdays.

It was not a visit, exactly—it had stopped being a visit sometime in May and become something more ordinary, which Rosalind counted as the truest kind of progress. He arrived on the morning coach from the infirmary annex, walked up the lane leaning on a stick he refused to call anything but "temporary," and sat in the workshop's one good chair with tea going cold beside him while the day happened around him.

He was older than three years should have made anyone. The prison infirmary had taken his hair to white and his hands to a tremor that meant he would never again hold a graver steady enough for pivot work—a loss Rosalind knew he felt more sharply than the prison itself. But his eyes were the same eyes she'd known across a workbench her whole childhood, quick and slightly amused, always three adjustments ahead of the conversation.

On this Thursday he was watching Elias and Rosalind fit a new balance spring into a lady's carriage clock, and after a while he said, "You're letting her lead."

"She's better at it," Elias said, not looking up.

"He's learning humility," Rosalind said. "Slowly."

"I'm learning *accuracy*," Elias said. "Humility comes free."

Captain Aldous Nystrom—no longer Captain, nothing anymore but Aldous, though old habits died hard and half the city still touched their hats to him in the street, which he bore with visible discomfort—made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough, and drank his cold tea.

There had been no grand reconciliation. Rosalind had learned, in the infirmary, that there wouldn't be—that forgiveness was a mainspring, not a switch, and had to be wound a little every day or it simply stopped. She had asked him the question she'd been carrying since the chapel marshes, since the ledgers, since the long winter of anger: *Why didn't you tell me? Why let me mourn?*

And he had looked at her with an exhaustion so complete it seemed almost peaceful, and said, "Because the moment anyone I loved knew, you became a lever someone could pull. I have watched men destroyed through their children, Rosalind. I chose to break your heart instead of your neck. It is not a choice I'd make twice, and it is not a choice I can defend, and I will spend whatever years I have left earning back the difference."

He hadn't asked for forgiveness. That, more than anything, was what allowed her to begin offering it.

Now he mostly talked mechanisms. It was their common language, safer than the past, truer than most present things. On this Thursday he leaned forward, trembling hand braced on the chair arm, and studied the carriage clock's escapement under Rosalind's loupe.

"Your beat's uneven on the third wheel," he said. "Listen."

She listened. He was right. She adjusted, listened again, found it true.

"When you designed the observatory clock's hiding places," she said—it was the closest they came to the subject, these oblique approaches—"how did you choose where? The fusee cone, the base casing. Was it craft or chance?"

"Craft." He said it without hesitation, without false modesty. "A hiding place has to obey the same law as any component. It must do its job without interfering with the mechanism's. Most people hide things in clocks by *stopping* the clock—cavity here, weight there, and the whole instrument runs crooked forever so nobody notices. I wanted the opposite. I wanted a clock that ran *truer* for carrying its secret, because the secret's mass balanced the train." He smiled, and it was a tired smile but a real one. "That's the whole philosophy, really. Truth properly placed doesn't burden the machine. It regulates it."

Rosalind thought about that for a long moment. Outside, Elias was re-planting beans in north-south rows, and Soren was arguing with a delivery boy about the price of shellac, and the church bells of Vellmark were ringing noon in seven different towers, six of them slightly wrong and one of them—not anymore, not since January, not ever again—perfectly, unremarkably right.

* * *

In June the delegates' commission arrived.

It was a formal document, seals and ribbons and all, thanking the Clockmakers' Guild and one Journeyman R. A. Nystrom specifically, for services rendered to the peace of the signatory powers, and enclosing payment that made Soren whistle low when he read the figure. There was also, folded separately, a letter in a secretary's careful hand, asking whether the Guild would consider undertaking maintenance of the observatory regulator as a standing appointment, given its, quote, *"historical significance to the proceedings of state."*

Historical significance. Rosalind read the phrase several times. It had a certain flavor, that phrase. Three winters ago the master clock had been significant the way a roof beam is significant—load-bearing, unnoticed, blamed immediately when anything sagged. Now it was *historical*, which meant people had finally noticed it, which meant, in her experience, that they would soon begin telling stories about it that weren't true.

Already the pamphlets had started. She'd seen one in Marta's father's shop window before Marta's father's arrest—quartermaster General Voss was awaiting trial now, and the shop stood shuttered, and Marta did not speak of him except once, in April, when she'd said quietly, over coffee, "I keep waiting to feel like an orphan. I don't. I feel like someone who finally opened a letter she'd been afraid of."—and the pamphlet had described the armistice ceremony in terms Rosalind barely recognized. There had been a *heroic engineer*, apparently, who had *single-handedly* exposed the conspiracy, and the chime mechanism had been a *ingenious trap*, and Governor Halvard—who was in custody awaiting his own trial, his own allies having delivered him to save the peace he'd nearly strangled—had been *a tragic figure undone by loyal subordinates*.

"History," Rosalind said to the empty workshop, reading the pamphlet, "is just repair work done badly and remembered worse."

But the standing appointment she accepted. She went to the observatory every second Tuesday, climbed the worn stair with her tool roll over her shoulder, oiled and checked and listened, and every second Tuesday the great clock told her it needed almost nothing, which was the highest compliment a machine can pay its maker. Sometimes she stayed past necessity, sitting on the floor of the dome with her back against the case, feeling through the wood the tiny continuous shiver of the escapement—tick, tick, tick, the patient argument between gravity and spring that never concluded, never needed to, only continued.

Once, in May, she had found a chalk mark inside the base casing that she didn't remember leaving: a small circle with a dot, the guild mark for *inspected and true*. She had stared at it for a long time before realizing it must be her father's—he had visited in April, during one of his walking days, and asked the caretaker to see the clock, alone, for five minutes.

He had inspected it. Found it true. Marked it so, in the old way, the way masters marked apprentices' work.

She had not mentioned it to him. Some messages are better received silently.

* * *

On a bright Saturday in late June, Marta came to the workshop carrying a basket and an announcement.

"The guild's opening the apprentice rolls in autumn," she said, setting the basket on the bench with the decisiveness of a woman laying down cards. "Properly open. New charter. Any applicant. And they need examiners' assistants, because half the old guard retired out of embarrassment." She looked at Rosalind. "I've applied."

Rosalind blinked. "To be an apprentice?"

"To be an *examiner's assistant*. I can't mend a clock to save my life—I've seen you try to teach me and we both feared for our lives—but I can read a ledger like scripture, and somebody trustworthy ought to be in that room when the guild decides who gets to learn the trade." She began unpacking the basket: bread, cheese, the small sour cherries they'd both loved since childhood, bought openly now from a stall whose owner no longer flinched. "Besides. Someone has to keep the histories honest, and you'll be busy keeping the clocks honest, and between us we might cover the whole city."

"And if my name goes in the history books anyway?" Rosalind asked. "The pamphlet version, with the heroic engineer."

"Then I shall write a corrective pamphlet," Marta said serenely, "detailing how you threw a soaking deserter out of your workshop door and sulked for a week afterward."

"That is slander."

"That is *history*, Rosalind. Ask your father."

They were laughing when Elias came in from the garden with dirt to the elbows and the last of the beans properly rowed, and Soren emerged from the back room complaining that everyone's laughter was putting him off his polish, and the afternoon arranged itself around them the way good afternoons do—without effort, without agenda, the way a well-built movement arranges itself around its mainspring.

Later, when the others had gone and the light lay gold and long across the benches, Rosalind stood at the workshop door and looked out at the yard. The beans stood in their neat north-south rows. The cherry petals had mostly fallen, drifting into the gutters like pink snow, and the evening bells were ringing across the roofs of Vellmark—seven towers, seven voices, six slightly adrift and one dead true, and tomorrow morning she would climb to the observatory and check it anyway, because checking was the work, checking was the whole of the work, and the work never ended.

History happens to everyone, she thought. That was the lesson of the whole grim, glorious business—of her father's faked death and Soren's silence and Marta's held door and even Halvard's cold arithmetic. History is the war that arrives in your city, the governor who seizes your observatory, the stranger soaked at your service door with letters in a dead man's hand. History happens to everyone, indiscriminately, the way weather does.

But it is repaired by someone. Always, in the end, by someone willing to open the case—to look directly at the worn escapement, the bent pallet, the lie worn smooth by years of turning—and say: *I know what's wrong, and I know how it should run, and I will stay here until it does.*

She turned back into the workshop, where her certificate hung straight in the window at last, and her tools lay in their proper places, and tomorrow's work waited on the bench with the patience of all true mechanisms: ready to run true, the moment someone gave it the care it asked for.

From the observatory hill, faint and clear across the cooling air, the master clock began to strike six. She counted the strokes out of habit, out of love, out of the simple craftsman's joy of a thing done right.

Six strokes. All true. She smiled, and reached for the lamp, and began the evening's work.
