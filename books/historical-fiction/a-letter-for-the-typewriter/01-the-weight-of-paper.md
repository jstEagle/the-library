## 1. The Weight of Paper

There is a way a letter sounds before you read it.

Most people never learn this. Most people take their post from the tray and tear it open standing in the hall, careless as animals. But I have carried other people's letters for three years now — up stairs and down, through rain, across counties — and I can tell you that paper speaks. A bill arrives flat and heavy, resentful of its own existence. A love letter comes soft-cornered, handled often before it was ever sent, and it seems to warm the hand. A letter written in grief has a looseness to its fold, as though the writer's fingers could not quite remember what they were for.

I know these things because Mrs. Adeline Marsh receives between forty and sixty letters a week, and because every one of them passes through my hands twice: once sealed, once opened. And because I have never once been able to stop myself from reading them.

I should explain that this is not wickedness. Or if it is, it is a small, domestic wickedness, the kind that keeps a body alive. I am Juniper Fairweather — driver, courier, general fetch-and-carry for the suffrage movement's most formidable woman in the western counties — and I am also nobody. That is my profession, truly, more than the driving. Nobody sees a chauffeur. Nobody sees the person who stands at the edge of a room holding a coat. Invisibility is not something done to me; it is something I have built, plank by plank, like a boat I live inside.

And into that boat I carry other people's lives.

* * *

October had come down hard on the town that year, the kind of autumn that smells of coal smoke and rotting apples, and on the morning this story properly begins I was waiting outside the Corn Exchange with the engine running, watching my breath fog the windscreen while inside, above me, Adeline Marsh was dismantling a man's argument with the ease of a woman shelling peas.

I could hear her without hearing her. That is another skill of mine. Through the tall windows her voice came in shapes rather than words — a rising shape here, a flat, terrible stillness there — and I knew from the stillness that someone had said something about women's minds being unfit for the franchise, and that he was now learning otherwise.

The letters were on the seat beside me. Eleven of them, bound with string, collected from the forwarding office in town, addressed variously to *Mrs. A. Marsh*, to *The Secretary*, and once, memorably, to *Madam — For You Know What You Are*. That last one I had held a moment longer than necessary. People who write anonymous letters always press too hard. The nib goes through the paper. Their fury leaves braille on the page, and you can read it with your thumb like a blind woman reading a face.

I had already read all eleven, of course. I do it quickly, in the ten minutes between collecting the post and delivering it, standing in doorways, in the lee of walls, once crouched behind my own motor car in a churchyard while the verger looked the other way. I read standing up, fast, the way some men drink. And then I folded each one back along its original creases — I am very good at creases — and I carried them upstairs, and I handed them over, and I watched Adeline Marsh open her own correspondence with no idea whatsoever that she was receiving it second-hand.

What do I get out of it? People would ask that, if people ever asked me anything. Not gossip — I couldn't repeat half of what I knew without destroying the work we did, and besides, gossip is a poor meal. No. What I got was company. When you are nobody, when your own life is a corridor with a door at each end and nothing hung on the walls between, other people's letters are pictures. A farmer's wife in Devon writing to ask whether civil disobedience would cost her her children. A girl of sixteen enclosing sixpence and asking, shyly, whether it was true that women could be arrested for asking questions. A man threatening Adeline with ruin, spelling *ruin* wrong. Each one was a window lit against the dark, and I stood outside every window, and I warmed my hands.

You will say I should have felt shame. Perhaps I did, somewhere under everything, the way you feel the weight of a coat. But shame requires being seen, and I had arranged my life so that I was not.

That morning, while the engine ticked and the exchange windows rattled with applause, I took the eleventh letter out of the bundle again. It sat apart from the others because it was different in every particular: heavy cream stock, not cheap linen; a wax seal, cracked now where I had opened it — I had a knife for the seals, a thin blade that lifted them whole when I wanted to be careful, but that morning I had been careless, or hurried, or both. The handwriting on the front was copperplate, old-fashioned, each letter formed with the deliberate grace of someone taught penmanship by a governess who believed character lived in the wrist.

And the address was not to *Mrs. Marsh*, nor to *The Secretary*, nor to any of the names Adeline wore in public like coats.

It said: *Mrs. Cordelia Marsh, Blackwood House.*

Blackwood House. Adeline's mother's house — the great grey estate across the river, shuttered these two years except for a skeleton staff, where an old woman I had seen exactly twice grew steadily more legendary and less present. Letters came *from* Blackwood House sometimes, in a secretary's hand. Almost none came *to* it. The world had agreed, without discussion, to let Cordelia Marsh slip beneath the water quietly.

So why was a letter to a dying matriarch travelling in her daughter's post? Why was Adeline intercepting it?

And why — this is the thing, this is the thing I have turned over every night since, the stone I cannot stop lifting — why did that copperplate hand look familiar?

Not familiar the way faces are familiar. Familiar the way a smell is. It went past thought entirely and arrived somewhere lower, somewhere I keep things from before I was eleven years old, before I learned to be furniture. The long descenders. The way the *d* leaned back as though reluctant to finish. I had seen that hand before. I had seen it on envelopes left on a hall table, on a note pinned to a kitchen door, on —

On a letter of dismissal.

I put the letter back in the bundle. My hands were not shaking yet; that came later. I set my jaw the way I do when the roads ice over, and I drove Adeline home.

* * *

She came out of the Exchange flushed and victorious, which for Adeline meant her mouth was a straight line and her eyes were bright as struck flint. She got into the car without help — she never took help — and arranged her skirts with two efficient movements, and only then did she look at me, which was rare, and worse than rare.

"Fairweather."

"Ma'am."

"The post?"

"In the case beside you, ma'am. Eleven items."

"Any trouble?"

There was a question with layers to it, the way she asked all her questions. Any trouble on the road. Any trouble with the police at the office. Any trouble, ever, anywhere, that I had failed to report. I kept my eyes forward.

"No trouble, ma'am."

"Hm." She opened the case. I heard paper, the small dry music of it, and then — silence. A silence with a specific gravity. When I glanced in the mirror her thumb was resting on the broken seal, on the crack I had made, and her face had gone very still in a way I recognised, because it was my own trick, the stillness of a person deciding what to show.

"Fairweather," she said at last, lightly, too lightly. "Did anyone handle the post today?"

"The office clerk sorts it. Same as always."

"Mm." She slid the letter away, into her coat, not the case, and looked out the window at the river, brown and swollen with rain, sliding past below the embankment road. "Drive on."

I drove on. Twenty-two minutes to the house, and she said nothing more, and I catalogued the silence as I catalogue everything, filed it under *later*. But underneath the filing, underneath the professional calm, something had shifted its weight. Because I knew whose letter that was. I knew the name on it. And I knew — with the sick certainty of a person who has spent twenty years pretending not to know things — that the handwriting belonged to someone who had once written five words to my mother on the back of an envelope:

*I know what you did.*

My mother had kept that scrap of paper in her sewing box until the day she left, and I had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Five words, copperplate, the *d*s leaning back as though reluctant to finish.

I had told myself for twenty years that I remembered it wrong. Children misremember. Children build monsters out of kitchen scraps.

But hands do not lie twice.

* * *

That evening the household settled around us like silt in disturbed water. Adeline at her desk with the door shut — she never shut the door. The cook humming off-key. Rain starting again against the glass, patient as a debt collector. And me in the boot room, cleaning mud off the mudguards, doing the small mechanical work that lets a mind pretend it is empty.

Except my mind was not empty. It was full of copperplate. Full of a hall table in a house that smelled of beeswax and boiled mutton, and a woman in an apron reading a letter and going grey, and me, aged nine or ten or however old I was, understanding without being told that this was a thing we would never speak of, and tucking it away in the same place where I now kept forty strangers' secrets, in the boat, in the dark, where I lived.

Adeline had taken that letter out of my hands today. Out of the post that was hers to open, addressed to a mother she had not visited in two years. She had lied about it, or near enough — *did anyone handle the post* — testing me, weighing me, the way she weighed everyone.

Which meant she thought I might matter. Which meant, God help me, that I had stopped being invisible to someone.

I set down the rag. In the boot room's single window my reflection hung ghost-grey against the black garden, and for the first time in years I looked at it properly — the face I kept blank for a living, the eyes I'd been told, once, by a dying drunk of a grandmother, were *Marsh eyes, God rot them*, though I'd assumed it was delirium, or spite, or both.

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstairs a bell rang — Adeline, wanting the car at dawn, no doubt, wanting the bridge road before the river rose further. There was talk at the county office of closing it for repairs come winter. If the bridge closed, the whole valley would be cut off until spring: one road in, one road out, gone.

I did not know yet how much that would matter. I did not know anything yet, really. I only knew that tomorrow I would drive my mistress wherever she wished, and hand her whatever she asked for, and be furniture, and be safe.

And that in my breast pocket, folded along its careful creases, I had kept one thing back from her.

The envelope. Just the envelope — the cream stock, the cracked seal, the copperplate address in a hand I had known before I knew my own name.

*To Mrs. Cordelia Marsh, Blackwood House.*

Written by the person who had destroyed my mother.
