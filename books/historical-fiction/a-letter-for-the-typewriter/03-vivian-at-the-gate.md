## 3. Vivian at the Gate

### Juniper

The car smelled of petrol and Adeline's lavender water, and by the time we reached the estate gates I had driven the last three miles in a silence so complete I could hear the tires reading the gravel like braille.

Adeline had not spoken since the station. She had watched the platform as though expecting her daughter to materialize out of the steam itself, and when Vivian Marsh instead descended from a third-class carriage with a single trunk and a face like a closed ledger, something in Adeline's jaw had set — the look of a general receiving news she had already priced into the campaign.

"She's thinner," Adeline said now, to the windscreen, to no one.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You needn't agree with everything I say, Juniper. It makes you useless as a barometer."

I said nothing to that either, which was its own kind of agreement, and she almost smiled.

Vivian rode in the back with her chin lifted and her gloved hands folded, looking out the window at the hedgerows as if they were evidence in a trial she intended to win. Twice I caught her reflection in the mirror above my head — twice her eyes passed over me without stopping, the way one's eyes pass over a hat stand, a coat rack, a thing placed in a room for convenience rather than presence.

I knew that look. I had spent twenty-four years perfecting it from the inside.

The household was waiting on the front steps when we turned up the drive: Mrs. Quill the housekeeper with her ring of keys held like a rosary, the girl Tansy bobbing on the bottom step, and behind them all, framed in the tall window of the morning room, Cordelia Marsh — propped upright in her chair, wrapped in a shawl the color of old snow, watching the car come with an expression I could not read and did not dare try.

I opened Vivian's door before Mrs. Quill could reach it. It is the driver's privilege, the small sovereignty of handles and hinges, and I took it gladly, because a door opened well buys you ten seconds of being necessary.

"Miss Marsh."

Vivian stepped out onto the gravel. Up close she was not thin so much as *edited* — every softness pared away, her dark hair cut short in a fashion London hadn't quite finished arguing about, a scar of powder over a healing blemish at her jaw. She looked at my offered hand, did not take it.

"Where is my mother?"

"Behind you."

"Then why are you talking to me?" And she walked past, heels striking the stone steps, past Mrs. Quill's murmured welcome, straight to where Adeline stood with her arms at her sides like a soldier awaiting inspection.

"Mother."

"Vivian." A pause with four years inside it. "You came."

"You sent a summons. I assumed refusal would simply produce another." Vivian's voice carried across the gravel the way her mother's did — the same instrument, tuned to a different key. "I'll stay through whatever this is. I'd like to know how long 'this' is expected to be."

"The bridge closes Thursday. Until spring."

The silence that followed had texture. Tansy dropped a salver somewhere behind us and pretended she hadn't.

"Spring," Vivian repeated. "You've trapped me in a house with you until April."

"I've asked you home. The trap is your word for it."

They went inside like two fronts meeting, and I stood by the open car door holding a leather strap that no longer needed holding, and thought: *the machine has begun to turn.*

### Vivian

The room they gave me had been mine at seventeen and had not changed, which was its own insult. The same faded roses on the wallpaper. The same water stain on the ceiling shaped like Portugal. Someone — the housekeeper, presumably — had laid a fire and put fresh flowers on the sill, and the flowers were the only new thing in the room, which meant someone had gone to trouble on my behalf, which meant someone here still believed I could be reached by flowers.

I unpacked nothing. I sat on the bed in my coat and let the anger settle over me like familiar clothing.

Four years. Four years since the argument whose details I had sanded down in memory until only the grain remained — *she chose the cause, she chose the cause over me* — and now a telegram in her unmistakable hand: *COME. MOTHER FAILING. BRIDGE CLOSES THURSDAY.* Nine words, no apology, no tenderness, and I had come anyway, because Cordelia was dying and because some traitorous organ in my chest still answered to that house the way a dog answers to a whistle.

I needed air and a cigarette, and there was exactly one place in that house where both could be had without an audience: the back steps, by the kitchen garden, where the servants' door stood and nobody of consequence ever looked.

She was already there.

The driver. Of course. He — or she, or whatever Juniper Fairweather was; I had heard Mrs. Quill use "they" once and then correct herself into flustered silence — stood at the rail with a cigarette going, watching the garden go gold and grey in the late light. They glanced at me, nodded once, and looked away again, granting me the steps as if I owned them.

Which, technically, I might.

I lit my own cigarette off theirs without asking, an old habit from a life my mother would have died of, and leaned against the rail a yard away. For a while neither of us spoke. The smoke rose straight in the windless cold. Somewhere beyond the wall, the river was doing whatever the river does when nobody supervises it.

"You drive her everywhere," I said finally. Not a question.

"Everywhere she asks."

"That must be exhausting."

"It's quiet work, mostly. She does her thinking in the car. I'm just the walls that move."

I turned to look at them properly then — and stopped, because there was something wrong with the face. Not wrong. *Unfinished.* It was one of those faces that refuses to resolve: young and old at once, plain until suddenly not, features arranged as though by someone who had run out of conviction halfway through and left the eyes to do all the remaining work. The eyes were grey-green, watchful, and they held a quality I could not place and could not stop trying to.

I had seen it before. Recently. Or long ago. In a photograph, maybe. In a mirror, possibly. It sat at the edge of recognition the way a word sits on the tongue.

"What?" Juniper said. Not defensive. Almost amused. "Have I got ash on my face?"

"No." I dragged on the cigarette. "You have a familiar face, is all. Which is strange, because I've never seen you before in my life."

"People say that about faces. It usually means I remind them of somebody they owe money to."

Despite myself — despite the whole architecture of my arrival, the anger I had built plank by plank on the train north — I laughed. Short, unwilling, but real. Juniper smiled at the far end of their cigarette like someone who had learned to accept laughter the way one accepts weather.

"Do you always answer questions with jokes?"

"Do you always ask questions you don't want answered?"

Fair. We smoked. A pheasant exploded out of the hedge below and made us both flinch, and that shared flinch — absurd, animal, identical — did more diplomatic work than four years of silence had managed.

"Why did you come back?" Juniper asked, quietly. "If you don't mind my asking. You clearly didn't want to."

"Mind your asking? You're furniture. Furniture doesn't ask."

The words left my mouth and I regretted them before they finished landing — not because they were cruel, but because they were *lazy*, secondhand cruelty, my mother's drawing-room voice coming out of my own mouth. Juniper took it without visible damage, which somehow made it worse.

"Furniture it is," they said, and stubbed out the cigarette against the rail with great care, and did not leave, and I understood that the non-leaving was a rebuke more elegant than anything I could have composed.

"I apologize," I said. "That was beneath both of us."

"It was beneath you. I don't set the bar very high for myself." They produced a tin from a pocket, shook a fresh cigarette loose, offered it. "But thank you. Most people don't bother apologizing to the furniture."

I took the cigarette because taking it meant staying, and staying meant something I wasn't ready to name. Our fingers didn't touch. But close.

"My grandmother," I said, nodding toward the lit window above the kitchen garden — the morning room, where a shape in a shawl sat motionless as a portrait. "Is she truly failing? Mother's telegrams are weapons, not reports."

Juniper was silent long enough that I looked over.

"She has good days," they said carefully. Each word placed like a stone in a stream. "And on her good days, she talks about the past. Quite a lot about the past."

Something in the phrasing snagged on me. *Carefully* — that was the quality. Everything Juniper said arrived pre-measured, as though language were rationed.

"And what does she say?"

"Things I probably shouldn't repeat."

"Oh, come on. You just told me you're furniture. Furniture hears everything and repeats nothing — isn't that the arrangement?"

Juniper looked at me then, fully, and again the face refused to finish itself, and again the eyes held that unbearable familiarity, closer now than before, like a name arriving letter by letter.

"Ask her yourself," they said. "On a good day. Ask her about the people who used to work in this house. About the ones who left."

The light was dying. From the kitchen came smells of onion and roasting meat and Mrs. Quill calling Tansy in from somewhere, and above it all the window glowed amber, and the shape in the shawl had not moved.

"Why?" I said. "What happened to them?"

Juniper stood, brushed ash from a knee that needed no brushing, and for one moment looked less like furniture and more like a locked drawer.

"That," they said, "is a very good question. I've been asking it my whole life."

And they went inside through the servants' door, leaving me alone on the steps with two cigarettes' worth of smoke and the sudden, unwelcome certainty that I had been brought to this house not as a daughter summoned, but as a piece — placed, like the flowers on my sill, by someone who believed I could be reached.

I looked up at the window. The shape in the shawl was looking down at me.

I raised my hand. After a moment — after long enough that I nearly convinced myself I'd imagined it — the shape raised its own.
