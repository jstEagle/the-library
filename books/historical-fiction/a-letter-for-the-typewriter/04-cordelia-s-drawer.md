## 4. Cordelia's Drawer

The summons came through Mrs. Halloway, the housekeeper, who delivered it in the flat tone she reserved for anything connected to the east wing — that part of Marsh Hollow where time moved differently, slower, thickened like honey left too long in a cold cupboard.

"Miss Cordelia wants her correspondence sorted. She asked for you specifically."

For me. The words landed strangely, like a coin dropped into a well whose depth I had never measured. I was the driver, the courier, the girl who stood at elbows and fetched coats and carried letters I pretended not to read. I was not summoned. Summoning was for secretaries and solicitors and daughters.

"She said," Mrs. Halloway continued, folding her hands with the finality of a woman closing a ledger, "you have neat hands."

I looked down at them. Neat hands. Hands that had spent three years learning to be invisible, folded behind my back or wrapped around a steering wheel or smoothing creases from Adeline Marsh's traveling coat. Hands that read other people's mail by lamplight because my own life contained so little writing that I had begun to hoard theirs, memorizing addresses and postscripts the way other people memorized hymns.

"I'll go up directly," I said.

* * *

Cordelia Marsh's room occupied the whole of the east tower, and entering it felt like stepping into the hold of a ship that had been at sea for eighty years. The light came through leaded windows in pale, watery sheets, falling across a bed the size of a lifeboat, across lace curtains gone the color of old teeth, across the towering walnut armoire and the vanity crowded with bottles whose contents had evaporated decades ago into perfume-ghosts. The air smelled of camphor and lavender and something underneath both — paper, I thought. The sweet, dusty breath of paper kept too long.

She was propped against pillows like a queen in exile, small and sharp-boned, her white hair braided into a crown so tight it seemed to hurt her. Her eyes were the surprise. Everyone spoke of Cordelia as already gone, a woman dissolving month by month into fog, calling for dead sisters and long-sold horses. But her eyes, when they found me in the doorway, were quick and dark and utterly present.

"You're the one who drives," she said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am. Juniper Fairweather."

"Fairweather." She tasted the name the way one tastes wine of uncertain vintage. "Sit. There, by the drawer. The bottom one sticks; lift and pull."

The desk in the corner was mahogany gone soft with age, its leather top cracked like drought earth. The bottom drawer resisted, then gave with a groan of swollen wood, and inside lay correspondence — hundreds of letters, bundled with ribbon and twine, sorted by some system only the dead would understand. My orders were to sort them: keep, discard, return to senders. A simple task. An hour's work, perhaps two.

I untied the first bundle and understood within three sentences that no task in this house would ever be simple again.

These were not recent letters. The paper had softened to the color of weak tea, the ink faded to brown ghosts. 1893. 1894. Dates from before my birth, before Adeline's marriage, before whatever catastrophe had estranged Vivian from her mother. Letters between Cordelia and someone named Rosamund, full of gossip about harvests and neighbors and a daughter called Addie who was, even then, "too loud for her own good, though God knows where she gets it."

I should have sorted. That was my function, my purpose, the shape of my usefulness. Instead I read. I told myself it was diligence — one must know a letter to classify it — but we are all liars about our appetites, and mine has always been other people's lives. In these pages Cordelia was young and funny and fierce; in these pages there was a husband mentioned once, briefly, like a door closed on a room no one wished to enter. I read until the light shifted and the room went amber, and my hands moved through the bundles like hands through water.

It was the fourth bundle from the bottom that stopped me.

The handwriting was round and careful, each letter formed with the deliberate patience of someone taught late and taught hard. I knew that hand. I knew it the way you know a scar on your own body — not by looking, but by the flinch it produces. It was the hand from the recipe card in my tin box, the one relic of my mother I carried from placement to placement: *for the cough, boil elderflower with honey.* It was the hand from the single letter she'd managed to send me at the Foundling Home when I was nine, returned unopened for want of postage, which I had steamed open anyway and read four hundred times.

*Dear Mrs. Marsh,* it began. *I write to ask after my position, and to say what I could not say at the gate.*

My mother. Ruth Fairweather, housemaid at Marsh Hollow, dismissed in disgrace in the spring of 1894 — that much I had pried from a matron's records, from half-heard gossip, from the arithmetic of dates. Dismissed in disgrace. Four words that had constituted my entire inheritance, my entire explanation, my entire history. And here was the fifth word, the sixth, the hundredth — here was her voice, twenty years old and still warm, asking after her position, saying what she could not say at the gate.

I did not read further. I could not. My hands were shaking so badly the paper chattered like teeth, and the sound seemed enormous in the quiet room, and I realized — distantly, as if from another country — that Cordelia Marsh was watching me.

"How curious," she said.

I fumbled the letter back toward its bundle. "Ma'am, I'm sorry, I was only—"

"Sorting." Her mouth curved. "Yes. You sort very slowly, child. And your hands shake over certain letters, which is an interesting method of classification." She pushed back her blankets with the imperiousness of a woman who had never once been obeyed quickly. "Give it here."

I gave it to her. What else does one do, in that house, in that world, when commanded? Obedience was the water I swam in.

She read it without expression, her dark eyes moving down lines she must have known by heart, and when she finished she held the page a moment in the window light, tilting it, studying the watermark as if the answer might be printed there.

"Do you know," she said, "that in nineteen years, no one has ever come into this room and shaken over a piece of paper?" She set the letter on the quilt between us, deliberately, face up. "People shake over brandy decanters and telegrams and doctors' pronouncements. Never paper. Paper is patient. Paper waits." She looked up at me, and the fog that everyone swore lived in her eyes was nowhere to be found. "Except you. You shake over paper. Which tells me paper has done something to you."

"Mrs. Marsh—"

"Cordelia," she corrected. "Everyone here calls me Mrs. Marsh as though the title might keep death waiting politely on the step. Call me Cordelia. It will annoy my daughter, which is a benefit at no extra cost." She tapped the letter with one translucent finger. "You know this hand."

It was not a question either. This family, I was beginning to understand, did not ask questions. They issued verdicts dressed as conversation.

"No, ma'am," I said, and it came out wrong, too fast, pitched too high, the lie of a girl who had built her whole self out of such lies and suddenly found the mortar crumbling.

Cordelia studied me for a long moment. Outside, wind worried the ivy against the tower stones, a dry scratching like something trying to get in — or out. Somewhere below, a door slammed; Vivian's voice rose and fell in argument with her mother, sharp as broken glass. The household assembling itself, piece by piece, into the machine I dreaded.

"Ruth Fairweather came to me in April of '94," Cordelia said quietly, "with her wages in her pocket and her trunk on the drive, and she asked me for one thing. Not money. Not a reference. She asked me to burn a letter she'd written, because she couldn't bear the thought of it existing." Her eyes never left my face. "I told her I don't burn things. I told her paper is patient, and patience is a kind of mercy — someday she might want it back." She paused. "She cried. She had your chin when she cried. Stubborn. Lifted, as if defying the tears to fall."

The room tilted. Or I did. Twenty years of silence rearranged themselves around me like furniture in the dark.

"I know whose eyes you have," Cordelia said.

Not a question. A verdict. And beneath it, unmistakable, something almost gentle.

"They're hers exactly — that gray that looks blue in certain light, always watching, always a beat behind the moment, cataloguing. She used to stand in doorways the way you do. Half in, half out. As if she hadn't yet decided whether the world deserved her whole weight." She reached out and turned the letter toward me again, and this time her finger rested on the signature at the bottom, the round careful letters spelling *Ruth Fairweather* like a spell or a sentence. "So. Are you going to tell me who you are, or shall we continue pretending you're sorting?"

Every instinct I owned screamed invisibility. Fold the hands. Lower the eyes. Become furniture, become shadow, become the girl at the elbow whom no one sees and therefore no one can wound. Three years of craft, of survival, of careful curated nothing — all of it telling me to deny, deflect, dissolve.

But my hands were still shaking, and the letter lay between us with my mother's voice rising off it like heat, and Cordelia Marsh was dying by inches in a tower full of paper, and she had chosen — of all the servants, of all the moments — to be lucid now, with me, over this.

"My mother worked here," I said. My voice sounded foreign, rusted from disuse in matters of truth. "Before I was born. She was dismissed."

"She was," Cordelia agreed. "In disgrace, they called it. Though disgrace is a word men invented to avoid saying what actually happened." Something flickered across her face — anger, old and banked, the coals of it still live after twenty years. "And you've spent your whole life wondering which side of that word you inherited. I can see it. It's written on you plainer than any letter."

"Then tell me." The words tore loose before I could cage them. "Please. What happened? Why was she—" I stopped, swallowed. "No one will tell me. There's no one left to tell me."

"There is one person left," Cordelia said. "And she is sitting in this room, holding your mother's letter, deciding how much truth an afternoon can carry." She drew a breath that whistled faintly, the sound of a body negotiating with itself. "But I am an old woman, Juniper Fairweather, and my memory is a house with rooms that lock and unlock without warning. If I give you this now, I may lose it by morning. So we will do this properly."

She pressed the letter into my hands and closed my fingers over it, her grip surprisingly strong, the bones of her like a bird's.

"Take it. Read every word tonight. And tomorrow—" her eyes went to the window, to the drive beyond, where headlights were sweeping the gravel, where a motorcar I knew intimately was bringing Adeline home early, ahead of schedule, moving fast the way she always moved when something frightened her — "tomorrow you'll take this to Adeline, and you'll ask her yourself. Because it was her letter that started it all, and it is her truth to finish telling. Mine is only the frame around the picture."

"What picture?" I whispered.

Cordelia smiled, and in that smile was twenty years of patience, and something else — something that looked terribly like pity.

"The picture of why my daughter has spent two decades being brave about everything except her own past," she said. "Read your letter, child. And mind the third paragraph. That's where the trouble lives."

Below, the front door opened. Voices rose — Adeline's, clipped and controlled; Vivian's, brittle as ice on a pond. The machine was assembling. And in my shaking hands, my mother's letter waited, patient as paper, holding its trouble in the third paragraph like a held breath.

I lifted it toward the fading light, and began to read.
