## 5. The Printer in the Cellar

My mother used to say that houses have cellars the way people have secrets — everyone knows they're there, and no one wants to go down alone.

I had been at Blackwood Grange four days, and I had already catalogued every room above ground. This was what I did instead of unpacking: I learned houses, mapped their corridors and dead ends, noted which floorboards announced a visitor and which doors had been locked so long the keyholes wore little shawls of dust. London flats surrendered themselves easily. The Grange was another animal entirely — a long grey body of a house sprawled against the hillside, all its rooms facing away from each other like relatives at a funeral.

I mapped it because I could not map my mother.

Adeline moved through the house as though she were its architect rather than its guest, issuing instructions to Juniper — the driver, who watched everything and said nothing, a quality I initially mistook for stupidity — while Cordelia lay upstairs dying by increments so small the doctors couldn't chart them. And I drifted through it all like smoke from someone else's fire, unwanted, unconsulted, furious about both.

On the fifth evening, I went down to the cellar because I wanted to be somewhere in this house where no one would look for me.

* * *

The cellar stairs were stone, worn to shallow curves by a century of feet, and cold rose up them like water filling a well. There was one lamp at the bottom and I did not light it. I knew the dark of country cellars; I'd spent childhood summers here before the estrangement, before Paris, before my mother decided I was a thing to be managed by post. I could navigate by memory: wine racks along the left wall, the old fruit store straight ahead, the coal chute on the right breathing the smell of winter.

I took two bottles of my grandmother's claret without guilt. If Cordelia wanted to drink her cellar dry before she died, that was her privilege, and if Adeline objected she could add it to the list of my failings she was evidently keeping somewhere.

It was as I turned toward the stairs that I heard it.

A sound so small I might have invented it: the tick of metal against metal. Not a drip — too regular. Not a rat — too deliberate. Somewhere behind the fruit store, in the black seam between the shelving and the wall, something in the dark was working patiently at something else.

I stood very still. My heart performed a small, undignified maneuver.

"Who's there?" I said, and hated how much my mother I sounded.

Silence. Then, low and roughened with disuse: "If you've come for the claret, take the '09 on your left. The '12's gone to vinegar."

Not a ghost, then. Ghosts, in my experience, were less practical.

I should have gone back up the stairs. I should have fetched Adeline, or the police, or at minimum the lamp. Instead I set down the bottles, felt along the wall until my fingers found the match tin on its nail, and struck a light.

The flame showed me a man sitting on an overturned crate in the gap behind the fruit racks — a space I'd have sworn, as a child, led nowhere but cobwebs. He was perhaps forty, gaunt in the way of men who had recently eaten badly and slept worse, with ink-stained fingers and a beard trimmed hastily, as though by his own knife. Beside him, wrapped in sacking like an invalid, sat a small iron hand-press, its type tray open, its letters gleaming dully in the matchlight.

He watched me the way animals watch: without blinking, calculating the distance to escape.

"You're Elias," I said. It wasn't a guess. I'd heard the name once, overheard down a telephone line in London, my mother saying *he's settled* in a voice she used for nothing else — not for me, not for Cordelia, not for any of her causes, which she discussed the way generals discuss weather.

His eyes narrowed. "And you're the daughter."

"I'm the one who could have you arrested," I said. "Sedition, isn't it? That's what they're calling it now when women ask for the vote too loudly."

"They're calling it that when printers won't name their sources." He said it evenly, without drama, which was somehow more alarming than bravado would have been. "You've got your mother's manner, do you know that? Interrogating a man in his own hiding place."

"This is my grandmother's cellar."

"And yet here I am." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere important. "Ask her why, if you want the truth. Though I notice you'd rather stand there holding matches than ask anyone anything."

The match burned down toward my fingers. I let it.

* * *

Here is what I had, at thirty-one years old, to show for myself: a flat in Paris paid for by money I told people came from translation work and actually came from Adeline, laundered through a solicitor so we could both pretend otherwise. A talent for languages I used chiefly to write letters to a woman named Colette who would never answer them as fully as I answered hers. And a conviction, deepening yearly, that I was a spectator of my own life — that courage was something that happened to other people in other rooms, and I would always be the one in the corridor afterward, hearing the raised voices through a door.

I did not go back up those stairs. I sat down on a second crate, across from the fugitive and his press, and I said:

"How long have you been down here?"

"Nine days."

"In the dark?"

"There's a lamp. I save it. Dark's cheaper than discovery." He nodded at the sacking. "She meant to move me on within the week, but then the bridge closed, and now—" He shrugged. "Now I'm part of the furniture. Like you."

That stung precisely because it was accurate. I changed the subject the way I changed subjects: like slamming a door. "What do you print?"

"Handbills. Pamphlets. The truth, in runs of five hundred." He lifted the sacking corner to show me the type — beautiful, old, each letter a small iron decision. "This week's edition is about the force-feedings. What they do to suffragists in Holloway. The government calls it medical care. It's torture with a spoon."

I knew about the force-feedings. Everyone knew about them, in the way one knows about distant wars — acknowledged, then folded away with the morning paper. But down here, with ink under the man's fingernails and the press waiting like a patient horse, it became suddenly, horribly particular. A tube forced down a throat. A woman held by four warders. My mother had been writing letters about this for twenty years, and I had been answering them with complaints about my lodgings.

"Why hide you?" I asked. "Why risk it? She has enough trouble without harboring a criminal in the wine cellar."

"She doesn't think of me as a criminal. She thinks of me as infrastructure." He said it without bitterness, and something in the phrasing made me laugh — a short, startled sound that echoed off the stone and embarrassed us both.

"You find her funny."

"I find her magnificent," he said. "Magnificent people are frequently funny. They can afford to be."

* * *

I began going down after that first night the way one begins anything ruinous: once, then again, then always.

I told myself it was curiosity. I told myself I was gathering ammunition — material for the eventual reckoning with my mother, evidence of the risks she took with other people's freedom while refusing to spend a farthing of her own attention on mine. Both true, in their ways. But neither was the truth.

The truth was that at three o'clock on my sixth afternoon at the Grange, I sat on a crate in a freezing cellar setting type by lamplight, and Elias put my hand on the composing stick and said, "No — from left to right, and upside down, mind, everything backwards, like the world seen properly" — and I felt something turn over in my chest that I hadn't felt since I was a child: the specific pleasure of being *useful*. Not ornamental. Not tolerated. Useful.

The words were stubborn things. Each letter had to be chosen and set and justified, and if my mind wandered the sentence collapsed into nonsense, so for an hour at a time I could not think about Paris, or Colette, or the letter I'd received that morning which I had read four times and hidden under my linen. There was only the work, and the work was good, and the work mattered, and when Elias pulled the first proof of the pamphlet and the wet ink smelled like iron and rain, he handed it to me and said, "You've got a compositor's eye," and I carried that idiotic compliment around for two days like a medal.

We printed five hundred copies of the Holloway account in three nights. I learned to work the press myself, my arms aching pleasantly afterward, my hands stained to the wrist. We hid the bundles in false-bottomed hampers, and I discovered I had a talent for the logistics of conspiracy — routes, timings, the plausible errand. I, Vivian Marsh, who had never organized so much as a dinner party without a crisis, found that smuggling seditious literature out of a sealed village suited my temperament exactly.

Because that is what the bridge closure had made us: sealed. A town cut off by the river's decision, a household holding its breath, and inside it a secret press running hot as a heart. The constable couldn't cross the water any more than the postman could. We were, for the moment, a country of our own, with laws Elias and I made up as we went.

"You know she'll take the fall for this if we're caught," Elias said one night, wiping the rollers. "Not you. Me, certainly. Her, probably. You — they'd hardly prosecute the daughter. Daughters get sent abroad."

"Then don't get caught."

"It's not a matter of *don't*, Miss Marsh. It's a matter of *when*, and how much warning we have." He looked at me across the lamplight, and his voice dropped into something gentler and more dangerous. "Which is why I'll say what your mother won't say to you. Whatever you think you owe her, whatever grudge you're nursing — you should know what it costs to stand near her. Ask the driver sometime. Ask her what she's seen. People orbit your mother and call it loyalty right up until they burn."

"I'm not loyal to her," I said.

"No," Elias agreed, turning back to the press. "That's rather what frightens me."

* * *

I went up the cellar stairs that night with ink on my cuffs and a bundle of pamphlets sewn into the lining of my coat, and I met Juniper in the passage outside the kitchen, holding a candle, clearly come downstairs for some legitimate purpose and finding none.

For a moment we simply looked at each other. Candlelight, ink stains, the smell of the cellar rising off me like a confession.

"You've been drinking," Juniper said. Not accusing. Testing.

"Claret," I agreed. "Grandmother's '09. It's excellent."

Juniper's gaze dropped, briefly, to my cuff. To the stain. I watched the driver's face do something complicated and quick — recognition, calculation, and then the shutters coming down, smooth as a shop closing. Whoever Juniper Fairweather was beneath the servility, the mechanism worked beautifully.

"The mistress will want her boots brushed before church," Juniper said, and stepped aside to let me pass.

But as I climbed past, I heard it, barely above a whisper, pitched so softly I might have imagined it:

"The third stair from the top creaks. Take the left side."

I paused. "How long have you known?"

Long silence. Then, from below, mild as milk: "Known what, miss?"

I walked on up the stairs, taking the left side, my coat heavy with contraband truth. Behind me the house creaked and settled around its secrets — my mother's, the printer's, the driver's, mine — and upstairs my grandmother lay listening to all of it, I was suddenly certain, with her eyes open in the dark, counting her descendants' sins like beads on a string.

In my room I unpicked the pamphlets from my coat lining and read the headline by candlelight, the one I had set myself, letter by backward letter: *WHAT IS DONE IN HOLLOWAY IN ENGLAND'S NAME.*

Somewhere south of here, a ship waited at a dock. Somewhere in this house, my mother was already planning who would sail on it. And I had begun, without meaning to, to care which of us it would be.
