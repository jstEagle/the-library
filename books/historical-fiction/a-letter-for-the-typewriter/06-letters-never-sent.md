## 6. Letters Never Sent

I did not sleep that night. I lay in the narrow bed above the coach house with Cordelia's drawer still open behind my eyes — the yellowed paper, the faded ribbon, and my mother's hand marching across the page in those tall, impatient letters I had known before I had known my own name.

*Miss Marsh* — no, not Miss Marsh then. *Mrs. Blackwood's girl.* That is how the letter began. *I am writing to you because there is no one else left to write to.*

The rest I had read four times by lamplight, my fingers trembling so badly the paper clicked like a moth against glass. My mother, Ruth Fairweather, housemaid at Blackwood House for six years until the winter of 1893, when she was dismissed in disgrace and walked out through the same gate I now drove through twice a day. Dismissed in disgrace. Those were the words the village used, the words my aunt had used when she took me in, the words I had carried like a stone sewn into the lining of every coat I'd ever owned. But the letter said something else entirely. The letter said *you know why I must go, and you know it was never what they will say it was.* And Adeline Marsh — then a young wife, already restless inside her marriage — had written back. There was a reply in the drawer. Two replies. Three. A correspondence lasting nearly two years, ending abruptly in the spring of 1895 with a final note from Adeline, stiff as starched linen:

*I can do nothing more for you. Please do not write again.*

Please do not write again. I turned those five words over all night like coins worn smooth. What had my mother asked of her? What could she have asked that made Adeline Marsh — a woman who chained herself to railings, who fed hunger strikers through tubes against their will, who had never in my three years of service let an injustice pass without sharpening herself upon it — close a door on a frightened girl with nowhere to go?

By morning I had my answer ready to demand, and by afternoon I had lost my nerve twice, and by evening I found it again, which surprised me more than anything else that day.

She was in the study. Of course she was in the study; Adeline Marsh inhabited that room the way a fire inhabits a grate, and everything around her existed only to be consumed or illuminated. The lamps were lit though the window still held a bruised grey light. Papers everywhere. Her pen moving. When I knocked she said "Enter" without looking up, and I stood there in my driver's coat, smelling of horse and river fog, holding a letter twenty years old against my chest like something stolen — which, in a sense, it was.

"Juniper." She glanced up then, and whatever she saw in my face made her set down her pen. She saw everything, Adeline. It was one of the things I loved about her, back when I permitted myself such a word, even silently, even only in the privacy of my own skull. It was also one of the things I had come, that evening, to hate.

"You went into my mother's correspondence," she said. Not a question.

"Cordelia asked me to sort it."

"My mother asks for many things she will not remember asking." She rose from the desk. In her dark dress, with the lamp behind her, she looked carved rather than born. "Which letter."

I put it on the desk between us. Ruth Fairweather to Mrs. Marsh, November 1893. My hands were steady now. I noticed that with a strange, floating detachment — the steadiness itself felt like someone else's hands.

Adeline looked at the envelope for a long time. Long enough for the clock on the mantel to clear its throat, long enough for me to hear the river running under the ice-thin silence. Then she sat down, slowly, the way old women sit, though she was not old, and read it. I watched her read my mother's handwriting and understood, with a lurch, that this was the first time in twenty years she had allowed herself to look at it.

"She wrote well," Adeline said finally. It was almost a whisper. "Your mother. She had a gift for it. I used to think she wasted it on service."

"You knew," I said. "You knew who I was. From the first day."

"I knew your name."

"You knew my *face*. You hired me off the road at Ashford with no references worth the paper—" My voice cracked and I hated the crack and kept going anyway, because I had learned from watching her that a crack in the voice is not a weakness if you drive straight through it. "You looked at me and you saw her. Didn't you. You've been looking at her for three years and saying nothing."

"Yes." She folded the letter along its original creases, precise, careful, a woman putting something back where it belonged. "Yes, Juniper. I saw her eyes in your face the moment you handed me your hat like a man and asked after the condition of my brakes. What would you have had me say?"

"The truth!"

"To what end?" And here it came — the lift of the chin, the marshalling of argument, the great engine of her conviction turning over. "To tell a child of sixteen that her mother had been sent away from this house in—"

"In disgrace. Say it. Everyone else has said it my whole life. You may as well have the privilege too."

"It was not disgrace." She said it flatly, without heat, and that flatness was worse than any heat. "It was circumstance. Your mother was young and loyal and she made a sacrifice for this family that this family repaid by forgetting her. That is the truth, and it is ugly, and I have carried it for twenty years alongside everything else I carry, and I chose — yes, *chose* — not to make a burden of it for you."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out of me sideways, bitter and small. "Not make a burden of it. Madam, do you know what I did instead of being told? I invented it. Every night of my childhood I invented a new version. She stole something. She was ill. She loved someone she shouldn't have. She killed someone. I built a whole gallery of mothers out of other people's silence, because nobody — not you, not my aunt, not God himself — would give me the true one."

Something moved behind her face. I had catalogued that face for three years the way I catalogued everything — the way I memorized letters, timetables, the contents of other people's pockets — and I knew every weather it produced. This was new weather. This was guilt arriving late to its own funeral.

"And the letters," I pressed, stepping closer to the desk. "There are years of them. Hers and yours. And then yours stop. *Please do not write again.* What did she ask you for, Mrs. Marsh? Money? A reference? Forgiveness?"

"She asked me for nothing."

"Everyone asks for something."

"She asked for nothing!" And then Adeline Marsh, who had faced magistrates and mobs and her own mother's slow unraveling without flinching, looked down at my mother's letter as though it might bite her, and said, very quietly: "That was precisely the difficulty. Do you imagine I don't recognize evasion, Juniper? I am speaking to the finest practitioner of it I have ever employed."

The word *employed* landed between us like a dropped glass.

"I learned it somewhere," I said.

"I know." She closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she was assembling herself again — I could see the assembly happening, plank by plank, and for the first time it did not look like strength to me. It looked like carpentry over a rotting hull. "Here is what I can give you, and I will give it whole: your mother discovered something in this house in the winter of '93. Something about my marriage. About Vivian's father. She could have used it — sold it, whispered it, held it over us. Instead she offered to leave quietly and never speak of it, and asked only that we let her go without a character ruined. I was young. I was furious at my husband and ashamed of my household and I agreed, and I told myself agreeing was mercy. And then she wrote to me afterward, and her letters were kind, Juniper. That was unbearable. Kindness from someone we had discarded. So I stopped answering. I called it clean lines. I called it moving forward. I have spent twenty years calling things by their campaign names so I would not have to call them cowardice."

I stood there while the lamp hissed and the clock ticked and the river ran on in the dark below the terrace, doing what rivers do, deciding nothing and everything.

"That isn't the whole of it," I said at last.

"No," she admitted. "It isn't."

"What's the rest?"

"The rest is not mine to tell." She pushed the letter back across the desk toward me, and her hand trembled — hers, Adeline's, trembling. "It lives in my mother's head, in whatever rooms are still lit in there, and some nights she wanders into them and calls your mother's name and weeps, and I sit with her and pretend I don't understand, because understanding would mean answering questions I have spent twenty years refusing to answer even to myself."

*Cordelia knows.* The thought went through me cold as well water. *Cordelia remembers.*

"So I'm to wait," I said. "Wait for her to die lucid enough to tell me the truth about my own mother. That's your offer."

"That is the only honest thing I have to offer, and I despise it." She straightened. The planks were all in place now, the hull sealed, the mistress reassembled — but I had seen the workman underneath, and there was no unknowing it. "You are angry with me."

"I am."

"Good." She picked up her pen, looked at it, set it down again. "Anger is the beginning of honesty, and honesty is the only weapon in this house that has ever actually fired. Keep it oiled."

I took the letter from the desk. At the door I stopped, because there was one more thing, and I found I wanted to wound her with it — no, not to wound. To mark. To leave something of mine in the room the way she had left something of hers in my life for twenty years without my consent.

"When Elias's press ran the piece about the Home Secretary," I said, "there was a paragraph about the wives who look away. About the households built on things no one will name." I turned the envelope over in my hands. "My mother's handwriting looks like yours does when you're tired. Did you ever notice that? All these years, and neither of us could escape the other's ink."

I heard her breath catch — actually catch, a small mechanical failure in that magnificent machine.

"Juniper—"

But I was already gone, down the corridor with the letter against my chest, past the portraits, past the door of the room where Cordelia slept and wandered and remembered, and it was only on the stairs that I understood what I had done: I had read her the way she read everyone. I had taken the half-truths she offered and found the shape of what was missing, the way I had always done, the way reading other people's mail had taught me — because the truth of a letter, my mother once wrote in a line I could not forget, *is never in what it says. It is in what it is afraid to say.*

And Cordelia Marsh, sleeping upstairs with her lit and unlit rooms, was afraid of something.

So was Adeline.

And tomorrow, I decided, standing in the dark of the hall with my heart hammering like a press running hot, I was going to stop waiting for either of them to volunteer it. Tomorrow I would go up to that bedroom, sit down beside the dying woman, and ask her directly whose eyes she thought she recognized — and pray to whatever governed houses like this one that her answer would come while her mind was still her own.

Behind me, faint through the study door, I heard the sound of a chair being pushed back, and then nothing at all — which was, I was learning, the loudest sound Adeline Marsh ever made.
