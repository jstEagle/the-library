## 10. A Letter for the Typewriter

The bridge reopened on a Thursday, and the whole town turned out to watch the first cart cross it, as though a cart were a miracle. Perhaps it was. For five months the river had made its decision for us, and now men in bowler hats stood at the new span slapping the railings like they'd built it themselves, and someone sold roasted chestnuts from a barrow, and the church bell rang eleven times for no reason anyone could name.

I watched from the drive with the motor idling, because some habits outlive their usefulness. Adeline stood beside me without her coat, letting the cold have its way with her, and I noticed she no longer flinched when Vivian touched her arm.

"Spring," she said, as if confirming it.

"Spring," I said.

The estate had changed in ways that would take years to itemize. Cordelia's room had been stripped and aired and then, at Vivian's insistence, not closed again but converted — a writing room now, with the good light kept for the desk. The wine cellar held no printer anymore; Elias had sailed in October with one berth and two crates of type, waving from the rail of a ship bound for Amsterdam like a man escaping a burning building he was proud to have set. But his press remained, disassembled and wrapped in oilcloth, waiting under the stairs like a promise someone intended to keep.

And I had changed too, though the evidence was quieter. My hands no longer shook around other people's letters. Not because I had stopped wanting to read them — that appetite is older than my honesty — but because there was nothing left in this house I hadn't already confessed to knowing. It is a strange liberty, being found out completely. The silence I had spent a lifetime filling with borrowed lives had finally been filled with my own.

* * *

Adeline came to me in the library on the last evening of March, carrying a folder tied with the green ribbon she used for campaign correspondence, which meant she considered whatever was inside official business. She sat across from me instead of behind the desk, and I understood before she spoke that something structural was about to change.

"I've drafted terms," she said. "You'll want to look them over."

"Terms."

"For your employment." She set the folder down between us but did not push it forward. "Except that's the wrong word, and you'll tell me so, so let me say it properly first." She drew a breath, and I watched the most formidable woman in three counties prepare herself the way I had once seen her do before hostile crowds. "I don't want an employee. I haven't wanted one since November, if I'm honest, and possibly longer than that. I want a partner. In the work — the organizing, the paper, the press when we get Elias's machine running again. And in this house, such as it still is. You know where everything is buried, Juniper. Literally, in the cellar. Figuratively, everywhere else. It seems wasteful to keep paying you to pretend otherwise."

I looked at the folder. "You wrote terms."

"I wrote terms because you're the sort of person who trusts a document more than a promise, and I've learned that about you from your own letters, which you gave me permission to read, and which I did, all of them, twice." Her mouth twitched. "There's a salary figure. There's a clause about the typewriter, which I suspect you'll find sentimental and object to. There's a clause about your mother."

My breath went somewhere and stayed there.

"The investigators' report," she said gently. "The last address. Glasgow, 1911, a lodging house by the shipyards. After that, nothing — which is not the same as nothing to be found. People go quiet, Juniper. They don't always go away." She slid the folder across the wood at last. "Read it whenever you're ready. Sign it or don't. But either way, the berth I bought last autumn — I'm buying another. Any port you name. If you decide to go looking, you'll go with money and my blessing and my address in your pocket, so you can write and tell me what you find."

"That's a great deal of blessing," I managed.

"I've been told I overcorrect." She rose, and paused at the door, and said, without turning: "Your mother kept house for mine for four years and was dismissed for a crime she didn't commit, to protect a man who didn't deserve it. I've spent five months learning to say that sentence out loud. You should know the terms include the truth, whole, in writing, wherever it needs to go. That's the part I care most about signing."

Then she left me alone with the folder and the fire and the sound of the river running free again below the reopened bridge.

* * *

Vivian stayed through winter, which surprised everyone except, eventually, Vivian. She had a ticket to Paris in January and burned it in the study grate on New Year's Eve, feeding it to the flames strip by strip while Elias played something mournful on the piano that wasn't really a piano tune. She claimed it was a gesture against sentimentality. Then she cried into her whiskey and dared any of us to mention it, and none of us did, because we had all learned that winter what mercy looks like: it looks like not saying the thing you could so easily say.

She found me in Cordelia's old room — the writing room now — on the first mild evening of April. The windows were open. The river smelled of thaw and mud and the particular green that means the year has decided to continue.

"You're actually going to use it," she said, nodding at the typewriter.

It sat on the desk where it had sat through Cordelia's last winter, black and patient, its keys worn to silver at the letters that occurred most often in the correspondence of dying women: *e*, *t*, *h*, *o*, *m*. Mother. Home. Them.

"It was going to sit there gathering dust otherwise," I said.

"No." Vivian leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, watching me the way she'd watched me on the back steps the day she arrived — like a puzzle she refused to admit interested her. "It was going to sit there as a shrine. There's a difference. Dust is what happens to shrines." She came further into the room and put her hand flat on the machine's carriage, the way you'd touch a horse you meant to ride. "She dictated half her letters on this thing, you know. Even blind near the end. She'd have us read back every word twice. Said a letter wasn't finished until it had survived being heard."

I hadn't known that. Or rather — I had read dozens of Cordelia Marsh's letters, copied passages of them into my hidden collection, memorized her circling, imperious, unexpectedly tender prose — and I had never known that. Reading everything, I understood then, is not the same as knowing anything.

"Juniper." Vivian's voice changed. "Write to her. Your mother. Stop rehearsing it."

"I don't know if she's alive."

"You don't know if she isn't." She straightened up. "I spent six years composing letters to a woman in Paris and sending none of them, because sending them meant finding out whether I was loved back. It nearly killed me, and it killed less than keeping them did." At the door she stopped, and said, softer than I'd ever heard her: "For what it's worth — whoever reads it will be lucky. You've been practicing your whole life."

* * *

They left me alone after that, deliberately, tactlessly obviously alone, the way this household had learned to give one another room. I heard Adeline close the study door below. I heard Vivian in the kitchen arguing cheerfully with Mrs. Prewitt about whether rhubarb counted as a fruit. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a family surviving being seen.

I sat down at the typewriter.

The ribbon was old and dry; I would need a new one, and there was a shop in town, past the bridge, past the chestnut barrow, in the ordinary world where letters travel. But there was enough ink left for beginnings, and I had learned that a beginning is most of the battle. I rolled in a sheet of the good cream paper Cordelia had favored and sat with my hands in my lap for a long moment, listening to the river.

All my life I had taken other people's words and kept them. Now I had to make words that were mine and let them go. It seemed impossible. It seemed exactly as impossible as giving away a berth on a morning tide had seemed, and I had done that, and the sun had risen anyway.

I began to type.

*Dear Mama,*

The keys struck harder than I expected — the machine had a voice, a clatter, an opinion — and the letters leapt onto the page slightly crooked, imperfect, alive.

*I don't know if this will reach you. Adeline Marsh believes you may be in Glasgow, and she is a woman who is usually right about things she refuses to be wrong about, so I am choosing to believe her.*

*My name is Juniper. You knew that once. I was born in the spring of 1893 in the room above the scullery at Marsh Hollow, and I was told you left, and I believed it for twenty years because believing it was easier than asking questions. I have spent those twenty years reading other people's letters — I won't pretend otherwise, even in a letter, especially in a letter — and I have only just learned that everything I was looking for in theirs was a version of this one.*

*Here is what I know now: you were dismissed unjustly. Here is what I am: employed no longer, partnered instead, living in the house that dismissed you, loved — I think the word can bear the weight now — by the daughter of the woman who did it, and by that woman's daughter, who burns tickets and cries into whiskey and stays anyway. Here is what I want: not an apology. Answers. And failing answers, then simply you. Alive, somewhere, with a door I can knock on.*

*The bridge has reopened. Everything crosses now.*

I stopped. Outside, the light had gone gold and long, and somewhere below Vivian laughed, and the river ran toward the sea where ships went out on tides toward Glasgow, toward Amsterdam, toward every place a letter might land.

One line more. I reached for the keys, and my hands — my steady, steady hands — shook anyway, the way they had shaken over the very first letter, months ago, in another life.

*Come and find me, Mama. I'll leave the lamp lit.*

*Your daughter,*

*Juniper*

I pulled the sheet from the platen and read it back aloud once, the way Cordelia would have insisted, letting it survive being heard. It held.

Then I folded it, sealed it, and wrote on the front, in ink this time, the address I had carried in my pocket since November:

*Mrs. E. Fairweather*
*Lodging House, Shipyard Lane*
*Glasgow*

And beneath it, because the truth, whole, was the only thing worth signing:

*To be opened by the addressee only — and if she cannot be found, return to sender, who will keep looking.*
