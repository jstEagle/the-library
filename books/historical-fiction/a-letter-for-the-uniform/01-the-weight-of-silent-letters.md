## 1. The Weight of Silent Letters

The type case for the letter *f* was running low again, and Casper Yarrow counted what remained with the particular patience that ten years in her father's shop had taught her — the patience of a woman who knew that a shortage discovered early was an inconvenience, and a shortage discovered late was a ruined edition.

Forty-one sorts. Enough, if she was careful, if she rationed the ligatures and let the compositor's thumb do the work of memory. She slid the little leaden letters into their compartment and stood a moment with her palm flat against the wooden lip of the case, feeling the grain of it, worn smooth by three generations of hands. Her grandfather had built this case. Her grandfather had built most of the furniture in the shop on Quill Lane, back when Vellmark's printers still signed their names to what they made.

Outside, the river fog was doing its October work, pressing gray fingers against the leaded windows until the light inside came down soft as tallow. The shop smelled the way it always smelled: ink and oil and the vinegar bite of the dampening water, and beneath it all the faint sweet rot of old paper — the smell Casper thought of, privately, as the smell of being home. She was twenty-six years old and had spent nineteen of them in this room, and she could set a line of eight-point Baskerville faster than most men could read one.

"Girl." Her father's voice from the front office, where he kept the ledgers and the good brandy and the door that locked. "We've the Vellmark Almanac to finish before Friday. Mind your spacing."

"I'm minding it," she said, though he couldn't see her, and pulled the composing stick toward her across the stone-topped table.

The almanac was safe work. Safe work was most of the work now. Tide tables and market days, the phases of the moon, the feast calendar with two saints' names quietly dropped from this year's edition because the Provisional Council had decided those saints had been too fond of the old Assembly. Nobody at the Council had ordered the saints removed. Nobody had to. Tam Yarrow read the wind the way sailors read the sky, and he trimmed his sails accordingly, and the Yarrow press had survived every change of government in Vellmark for sixty years by printing exactly what each government wished printed and nothing else.

Casper had learned the trade the way children learn language — without remembering a time before it. She had learned something else along with it, something that was never spoken aloud but was understood as thoroughly as the difference between em and en dashes: which words were welcome in the shop, and which were not. A man had come in four years ago wanting funeral broadsides for his brother, who had drowned in the custody of the harbor watch. *Drowned* was acceptable. *Beaten* was not. Casper had set the first word and refused the second, and her father had later told her she'd done well, and neither of them had said anything else about it, and that was how the lesson was given and received.

She was setting the column of market prices when she heard the office stove door clank.

It was a small sound. It should have been nothing. But there was a rhythm to the shop's day the way there was a rhythm to a press run, and the stove in the office was lit at seven in the morning and banked at noon, and it was not yet ten, and Tam Yarrow did not open that iron door between those hours unless he was feeding it something that was not coal.

Casper kept her eyes on her stick. Through the doorway she could see a slice of the office: the ledger shelf, the corner of the map of the river districts that hung crooked and had hung crooked for a decade, and her father standing at the stove with his back to her, a sheet of paper in his hand.

He was not burning it quickly. That was the thing she noticed, the thing that lodged under her ribs like a splinter. He was burning it slowly, holding it so that only the bottom edge caught, letting the flame crawl up while he read whatever was written there one last time. Men burned papers they feared. Men who burned them slowly wanted to be sure of what they were losing.

The paper caught fully. He fed it into the grate whole and stood watching it curl, and then he took the poker and broke up the black flakes with more care than he gave to any job in the shop, grinding them small, small, small, until there was nothing left that could be read even by a desperate man with a sieve.

"Father," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to seem deliberate. "Shall I pull a proof of the tide tables?"

He turned. He had a way of arranging his face, her father — she had seen him do it a hundred times with tax assessors and Council clerks and once, memorably, with a colonel of the harbor garrison who had come asking questions about a pamphlet someone else had printed. The face he arranged was mild, slightly tired, faintly amused, the face of a man whose only ambitions were typographical.

"Proof it twice," he said. "The Council's new surveyor swears the spring tides are off by a finger's width, and I'll not have the Yarrow name on figures some clerk disputes."

"What was in the letter?"

The question came out of her before she had decided to ask it, the way a knot comes out of a rope when you're not looking for it. She watched it land. She watched her father's hands — big hands, ink-stained at the knuckle, hands that could feel a misaligned form through a folded sheet — go still at his sides.

"Letter?" he said.

"The one you just burned."

For a moment the office was very quiet. Somewhere below the floorboards, the river worked at its pilings. In the street outside, a cart went by, and a woman called out the price of onions, and the ordinary morning of Vellmark continued as if the world were a place where fathers did not burn letters at ten o'clock and grind the ashes like a man burying a body.

"Nothing," Tam Yarrow said. "A circular. Prices of lamp oil from a factor in Grelsen's Wharf. Not worth keeping."

"You read it twice."

"I read everything twice. It's a printer's habit." He smiled the arranged smile. "You should cultivate it."

He came out into the shop then, moving with the deliberate ease of a man walking away from something, and put his hand briefly on her shoulder — a gesture that in another family might have been simple affection, but which Casper had long ago learned to read as a full stop. *End of sentence. End of paragraph. Do not begin a new one.*

But she had begun it already, and the knot would not go back into the rope.

* * *

She finished the tide tables and proofed them twice and found no errors, which annoyed her, because she wanted to find an error, wanted the small clean satisfaction of correcting something when everything larger felt uncorrectable. At noon she ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window, watching the fog lift off the Channel Reach in ragged sheets, and thought about the letter.

There were things she knew about her father without knowing how she knew them, the way she knew the weight of a galley or the smell of ink about to spoil. She knew that he never opened the shop's shutters fully on the side facing the Council House. She knew that he paid the district assessor promptly and in coin and never asked for a receipt, and that the assessor never offered one. She knew that twice a year, on no fixed date, a man named Corvan Hale came to the shop after dark, dined with her father in the office, and left without ever having been introduced to her — and that Corvan Hale wore the gray sash of the Provisional Council's civil bureau, and that her father always drank less than usual on those nights and slept badly after.

She knew that the shop had money in it that the work did not explain. The almanacs and wedding invitations and shipping manifests of Quill Lane did not buy the second press, or the house above it with its glass windows, or the winter coat her father had bought her two years ago without her asking. When she had once asked, gently, whether they were in debt, her father had laughed and said the Yarrows owed no man anything, and the laugh had been the arranged face made audible, and she had let it go, because letting it go was also part of the trade she had learned here.

Ten days. That was what the talk in the markets said, give or take. Ten days until the Council's army came down the Grelsen road, or didn't; ten days until the city either held or fell; ten days during which the price of flour doubled weekly and sensible people bought candles as if candles could hold back whatever was coming. The Council's criers had begun announcing the victory in advance, which Casper found a curious thing — celebrating a battle before it was fought, as though words could be marched ahead of soldiers to occupy the future. Her father had heard the same criers and said only, "They're confident," in a voice she couldn't read, and had gone into the office and locked the door.

At half past two, a customer. Old Mistress Anselm, wanting two hundred funeral cards for her sister, with a verse. Casper took the order, quoted the price, discussed the verse — the sister had loved the psalm about the rivers — and it was ordinary work, good work, the kind of work that reminded her why she loved the craft underneath the fear that had grown over it like moss over stone. Setting a dead woman's favorite psalm in twelve-point with a thin rule border was honest. It would comfort someone. No one would burn it.

Mistress Anselm left, and Casper was washing her hands at the basin when she heard her father speaking in the office — low, urgent, the voice he used only when the door was locked. She caught fragments through the wall. He was talking to himself, or rehearsing, or praying; she couldn't tell. One phrase reached her clearly, repeated twice as if he were testing its weight:

"—never came here. Never came here."

Then silence, and the click of the lock, and Tam Yarrow emerged looking ten years older than he had at breakfast, and told her cheerfully that they ought to think about goose for Sunday, since there might not be much cause for feasting afterward, whichever way the war went.

* * *

Evening came down on Quill Lane the way it came down everywhere in Vellmark that autumn: early, cold, smelling of woodsmoke and river. The curfew bell would ring at nine. Casper closed the shutters, banked the press, wiped the stones, and did the small closing rituals that ended every day of her working life — and found, as she did them, that her hands were steady but her mind was not.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: her father had lied to her. Not the great smooth lies of commerce, not the arranged-face lies that were practically the shop's dialect. He had lied badly. *A circular. Lamp oil prices.* Tam Yarrow, who could quote the cost of Dutch paper from six years past, who noticed a kerning error at arm's length, had claimed he couldn't remember the contents of a letter he'd read twice and destroyed. And men lied badly only about the things that frightened them, and her father was not a man who frightened easily, and therefore whatever had been on that paper was not a circular, and was not small, and had teeth in it somewhere.

She climbed the stairs to the loft above the shop where the type cabinets stood in ranks like a silent congregation, and she stood among them in the dark without lighting a lamp, listening to the building settle. Below her, through the floor, she heard her father moving in the office. The lock clicked. The stove door clanked — once, twice — and she understood that he was burning something else, or making sure of the first thing, feeding the fire past the point of sense.

Casper pressed her palm flat against the nearest cabinet, against the cool wood and the thousand sleeping letters inside it, and thought: *Every word I have ever set is still in this room. Every word he has ever refused is here too, in the shape of the space around them.*

The bell of Saint Ferren's rang nine. Down in the street, the lamps of the curfew watch swung past, and the city drew itself in for the night, and ten days became nine at midnight, though nobody marked it.

She was almost asleep in her room behind the loft when she heard it — so faint she might have made it out of wind and worry: a knocking. Not at the front of the shop, where customers knocked. At the back. The delivery door, the one that faced the alley, the one that was never used after dark.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more, softer, as if the hand making them was very small.

Casper lay still in the dark, her heart going hard against her ribs, and listened to the rain begin, and listened to the knocking come again — patient, stubborn, impossibly small — and knew that whatever her father had burned that morning had just arrived at their door anyway, wearing a different shape, asking to be let in.
