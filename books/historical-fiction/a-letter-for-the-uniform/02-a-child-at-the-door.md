## 2. A Child at the Door

The knocking came a third time before Casper moved.

She lay in the narrow bed above the shop, listening to the rain drum its impatient fingers on the slates, and told herself what any sensible person in Vellmark would tell themselves on the second night of curfew: it is the wind. It is a shutter. It is nothing that concerns you. The city had spent ten days teaching its people this lesson — *hear nothing, see nothing, be nothing* — and Casper had been an apt pupil. She had learned her letters at four and her silences at nine, and of the two educations, the second had kept her family fed.

But the knocking came again, three small blows, spaced apart like someone counting to keep from crying.

She rose without lighting the candle. Her dress went on over her shift; her boots she carried, because Tam Yarrow slept like a man standing guard even when he was asleep, and a creaking stair could wake him into one of his moods — the tight-lipped ones where he counted his money twice and said less than usual, which was saying almost nothing at all.

The shop below smelled of ink and lamp oil and the ghost of burnt paper. She did not need light to navigate it; she had walked these floors since she could walk at all, past the stone sink where the brayers soaked, past the imposing case with its ranks of type like a city seen from above, past the great iron hand-press her grandfather had bought secondhand from a bankrupted Bible house and which had outlived him, outlived her mother, and would likely, being Yarrow property, outlive them all. She unbolted the back door in the dark by touch, the way you do a thing you have done a thousand times and never once thought about until the night it matters.

Rain came in first, cold as a slap. Then, when she opened the door wider, a child came in after it.

A girl, small enough that Casper's first stupid thought was *someone has lost a doll*, except dolls do not shiver, and dolls do not look up at you with eyes that have already catalogued your face for later. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Soaked to the bone in a grey wool coat too big for her, its hem dragging wet stripes across the threshold stones. In one fist she clutched a letter wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string. In the other, a coin purse so heavy it pulled her arm straight down.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Rain filled the silence, hammering the yard behind the girl, running off the eaves in ropes.

"You're the printer's," the girl said. Not a question. Her voice was steady in the way voices are steady when steadiness has been rehearsed.

"I'm the printer's daughter," Casper said. "Who sent you?"

The girl's jaw worked. She looked over her shoulder at the dark yard, at the alley mouth beyond it, and something crossed her face — quick, practiced, terrible in a child — that Casper recognized because she had worn it herself for years in her father's shop whenever soldiers came to count the presses: the face of someone checking whether the world was still safe enough to say a thing aloud.

"I'm not supposed to say." The girl stepped inside without being asked, dripping on the floorboards. "I'm not supposed to say anything. I'm supposed to give you that—" she held up the letter, still gripped tight, knuckles white under skin gone blue with cold— "and you're supposed to read it and I'm not supposed to watch you read it. Those were the instructions."

"Whose instructions?"

"Please take it." And here the rehearsal cracked, just slightly, just at the seam. "My hands are going to drop it. They've been shaking since the bridge."

Casper took the letter. It was heavier than paper should be — several sheets, folded tight, and beneath the oilcloth something hard, a seal or a token, pressing through. The string was knotted with a care she recognized: the double knot printers used for parcels that must not come open by accident. Whoever had wrapped this had wanted it to survive rain, mud, and searching hands.

"Come away from the door," she said, and closed it, and shot the bolt, and stood a moment with her forehead nearly touching the wood, listening to the storm, feeling the absurd animal relief of a barrier between herself and the night — as if a bolt of iron were any match for whatever had put a seven-year-old alone on Vellmark's streets after curfew, when the Council's patrols shot first at shadows and apologized, sometimes, later.

The girl had already begun exploring. That was the thing about children; they had not yet learned that curiosity was dangerous. She drifted toward the imposing stone, trailing water, and reached up on her toes toward the cases of type, and Casper saw her fingers hover over the little metal letters the way other children hovered over sweets.

"Don't touch those."

"Why? What are they?"

"Words." Casper set the letter on the counter, then — no, not there, anywhere but lying out in plain sight — picked it up again and slid it inside her apron. "Every word anyone has ever printed in this city passed through hands like these. Words are heavier than they look."

"They're not heavy. They're tiny."

"That's how they get in everywhere." Casper knelt to the girl's level. Up close she could see details now: a scrape healing on one knee, a fine coat ruined but well-made underneath the grime, hair hacked short with scissors rather than cut — cut in haste, or cut to disguise. Someone had dressed this child well and then made her look like nobody's. "What's your name?"

The girl pressed her lips together. Then, visibly deciding that one more broken rule wouldn't matter now: "Wren."

"Wren what?"

"Just Wren. He said if I gave a whole name, people could find me with it." She glanced at the door, then up at Casper, and her voice dropped to the whisper adults think children can't manage and children, frightened enough, manage perfectly. "'Forget my face.' That was the instruction. He said it twice. He made me say it back."

"He?"

"The man who brought me. He couldn't cross the bridge — he coughs too loud, the patrols listen for coughing — so we walked the long way, along the tannery ditch, in the dark. He carried me over the bad parts." Wren's chin trembled once and was mastered. "He gave me the purse. He said pay the printer. He said the printer owes a debt and the debt is due tonight."

Casper felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it does in dreams. *The printer owes a debt.* Her father owed debts all over Vellmark — she'd heard the creditors' notes read aloud at supper — but debts of money were the least of what Tam Yarrow might owe. She thought of the letter burned that morning, the ash in the grate, her father's face when she'd asked. She thought: *it has found us anyway.*

"Where is this man now?" she asked.

"Gone. He went back before the bells. He said—" and here the girl recited, word-perfect, the way children recite things that have been carved into them— "he said if anything goes wrong, I am to say I ran away from my aunt in Harrowgate and I don't know anybody in Vellmark and I've never seen this shop before in my life. He made me practice it four times." A pause. "I don't have an aunt in Harrowgate."

"No," Casper said quietly. "I imagine you don't."

Above them, a board groaned.

Both of them froze. Casper knew that groan the way she knew her own heartbeat — the third stair, the loose one, the one her father had promised to fix for six years. Footsteps followed it, unhurried, heavy, descending.

"Casper?" Tam Yarrow's voice came down the stairwell ahead of him, roughened by sleep and suspicion in equal measure. "I heard the bolt."

He appeared in the doorway to the workroom holding a candle, and the candlelight did what candlelight does — it took the scene and flattened it, made it simple: his daughter kneeling on wet floorboards, a strange child dripping a puddle beside the imposing stone, and Tam Yarrow's face going through, in the space of two seconds, every expression Casper had ever seen him wear and one or two she hadn't.

"Saints' blood," he said softly. "What is that."

"She knocked. She has a letter for you."

"I don't want it."

"It doesn't seem to care what you want, Da. It's here."

He came closer, and Casper watched him look at the child — really look, the way he looked at damaged stock, assessing, calculating cost — and she watched the calculation arrive somewhere he didn't want it. Because Wren looked back at him without fear, and said, in her careful reciting voice:

"You're Tam Yarrow. He said you'd know why I came. He said you owe Aldous Renk, and the debt is due tonight, and you're to keep me till morning and burn nothing."

The name landed in the room like a dropped ball of lead type. Casper saw it strike her father. She saw him go still in the particular way he went still — not calm, never calm, but *arrested*, a man caught mid-step over thin ice — and the candle flame shivered in his hand because his hand was shivering.

"Aldous Renk is dead," Tam said.

"Yes," said the child, simply, as though reporting weather. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

They argued in whispers, which is somehow worse than arguing loudly — every syllable pressed flat, urgent, furtive, two conspirators in their own kitchen. Tam wanted the girl gone. Not unkindly, exactly; that was the horror of it. He wanted her gone the way a man wants a lit fuse carried out of his house, quickly, carefully, by someone else's hands.

"There are patrols on every bridge and the curfew bell won't ring again till dawn. Where would I take her?" Casper hissed. "Into the street? To the garrison? 'Excuse me, Captain, I've found a child, please dispose of her responsibly'?"

"Then she sits on the step till morning—"

"In this rain? She'll be dead of fever by noon, and her death at our door will be a far louder question than her presence in our loft."

"There should be no questions at all!" His whisper cracked. He caught himself, glanced at the doorway where Wren sat huddled on a stool wrapped in the shop's oldest blanket, drinking the warmed milk Casper had scrounged from a crusty jug, apparently unbothered by being discussed. Children learn early to sit outside arguments. Casper wondered whose kitchen this girl had learned it in, and what had happened to that kitchen. "Casper. Listen to me. Ten days. Maybe fewer. The Council's men are already walking the streets like they own them because in a fortnight they will. Every press in this quarter gets registered, inventoried, stamped. You think they won't ask who slept under a Yarrow roof? You think paper doesn't talk?"

"Paper talks," Casper agreed. "That's rather the whole trade, Da."

"This isn't a jesting matter."

"No," she said. "It's the most serious matter that has ever walked through that door, and it's seven years old, and it's shivering, and you want to put it back out into a curfew storm because it frightens you." She held his eyes. She almost never held his eyes. "Who was Renk to you?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a patrol went by — boots, the wet slap of a cape, lantern-light sliding across the shutters — and all three of them, father, daughter, and stranger-child, sat motionless until it passed, three animals in one burrow.

"We'll speak of it tomorrow," Tam said finally, and his voice had gone flat and final, the voice he used to close accounts. "Tomorrow, when the bell rings free passage, the girl goes to the parish house on Cooper Lane. They take foundlings. It's legal, it's distant, and it's none of our affair past that. And that letter—" he pointed at Casper's apron, where the oilcloth bundle pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat— "goes in the stove. Tonight. Before you sleep."

"You haven't read it."

"I don't need to read it. Nothing that arrives this way, at this hour, carried by a child with a paid-off purse, brings anything but ruin. Your grandfather taught me that. Ruin always travels light and knocks politely." He pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, and the dark came down over the room like a lid. "Bed, Casper. Both of you. The loft. And Casper—" his shape paused at the foot of the stairs, a darker darkness— "the stove. Promise me."

"Goodnight, Da."

It wasn't a promise, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so, and that too was a kind of family language, older than either of them.

* * *

The type loft was a low attic above the workroom where finished stock was stored against damp — reams of paper, bundles of pamphlets awaiting collection, and the old wooden cabinets of sorted letters that had come with the press and were too heavy ever to move again. Casper had slept up here as a girl on summer nights when the shop baked below; she knew every beam by the crown of her head.

She settled Wren into the nest of blankets between the paper cabinets, where the child sank down with the boneless gratitude of the truly exhausted, milk-warm and half-asleep in seconds. But her eyes — her eyes stayed open, tracking Casper as she moved around the loft, banking the shutter, folding a spare blanket.

"You're not going to burn it," Wren said drowsily.

"What makes you say that?"

"He said you wouldn't." The girl yawned enormously. "He said, *tell her the words are the only place he's still alive, and she'll understand, because she's a printer's daughter.*" Wren frowned sleepily, working at it. "Which man? There were so many names. He talked all the way along the ditch. I wasn't even supposed to listen but he kept talking anyway, like he couldn't stop, like he was emptying himself out so he'd be light enough to run."

Casper sat down slowly on a crate of devotional octavos. "What else did he say?"

"He said the letter tells you everything. He said—" the child's voice was fraying now, sleep pulling the words apart like wet paper— "he said the witnesses were getting old, or sick, or scared, and soon there'd only be paper left to say what happened. And paper needs somebody. Paper can't walk itself across a bridge in the rain." Her eyes closed. Opened halfway. "Are you the somebody?"

Outside, thunder rolled up the river valley, long and low, the sound the storm had been carrying all night like a message it couldn't deliver. Rain lashed the loft window. Downstairs, faintly, Casper could hear her father pacing — she knew his tread the way she knew the third stair's groan — up and down, up and down, a man wearing a groove in his conscience.

"I don't know yet," Casper said honestly.

"That's what he said you'd say." And Wren was asleep before the sentence ended, breath evening out, small fists curled against her chin, the coin purse — she would not let go of it even sleeping — tucked under her arm like a talisman.

Casper stayed sitting on the crate a long while. The letter pressed against her ribs where she'd stowed it, and she became aware that she could feel it the way you feel a coal through cloth — not burning, not yet, but warm, insistent, patient. *Burn nothing,* the child had said. *The debt is due tonight.*

Her father wanted it in the stove. Her father, who had burned a letter that very morning and lied about it with a fluency that had frightened her more than the lie itself. Her father, who owed Aldous Renk a debt he had never once mentioned in all her years of setting type — a name that had never crossed his lips, and now arrived at midnight wearing a drowned child's coat and speaking in a rehearsed whisper.

Fear was a familiar country; she had citizenship there, papers and all. But sitting in the dark above the silent press, listening to a stranger-child breathe and a guilty man pace, Casper felt the fear begin, slowly, grain by grain, to change state — the way type metal changes in the crucible, softening before it sets into something harder, sharper, capable of holding an impression.

By morning, she understood, her father would expect the letter to be ash and the child to be gone. By morning, the bell would ring free passage, and the parish house on Cooper Lane would be waiting with its legal, distant arms.

Casper looked at the sleeping girl. She looked at the shape of the letter through her apron.

Then she reached up to the shelf above the paper cabinets, where the misfit sorts lived — the broken ligatures, the worn characters retired from service — and lifted down the false-backed drawer her grandfather had built during the last upheaval, the one Tam believed held nothing but defective q's and obsolete long-s's, and opened it, and laid the oilcloth bundle inside among the dead letters, and slid it shut.

The loft went dark and ordinary again. Rain, thunder, breathing, pacing below.

Up and down. Up and down. Her father's footsteps wore their groove, and Casper sat in the dark above them, guarding a drawer full of words that could hang them all, and waited for morning, and for the first time in her careful, quiet, complicit life, she was not afraid of what the day would bring.

She was afraid — no, she was *resolved*, and the two feelings sat in her chest so close together she could hardly tell the seam — of what she would bring to the day.

Below, the pacing stopped. And in the sudden silence, Casper heard the small unmistakable sound of the stove door opening, and the scrape of a chair dragged toward the hearth, and she was moving down the ladder before she had decided to move at all.
