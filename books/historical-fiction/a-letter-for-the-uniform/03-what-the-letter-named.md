## 3. What the Letter Named

The letter sat in the type loft for three days before Casper touched it.

She told herself it was prudence. A sealed letter, delivered by a frightened child with orders to forget a face, was not a thing to be opened lightly — her father had said so twice, once gently and once with the flat finality he reserved for customers who asked him to print something he shouldn't. *It goes to the magistrate's office when the roads clear,* Tam Yarrow had said, tucking the letter into the ledger drawer where he kept the accounts no one else was meant to see. *That's the end of it, Casper. The girl goes to the parish house. We were never part of this.*

But the letter did not go to the magistrate's office, because the magistrates had stopped holding office hours on Tuesday, and by Thursday they had stopped being magistrates at all. The Provisional Council's criers were already working the Fishmarket, announcing new ordinances in voices pitched to be heard over the sound of their own boots. The roads out of Vellmark were choked with carts. And the child — Wren, she called herself, or perhaps that was all she could remember of a longer name — slept in the loft above the composing room, curled against a sack of worn nonpareil, breathing the smell of ink and oil like it was bread.

So the letter stayed. And Casper found that she thought about it the way one thinks about a tooth that aches only when pressed.

* * *

On the third night, after her father had banked the hearth and gone up to his rooms, Casper climbed the ladder to the loft with a candle stub cupped in both hands.

Wren was asleep. In sleep she looked younger than seven — smaller, the bones of her face not yet settled, her thumb hooked loosely near her mouth in a way she would have denied awake. She had been quiet in the way of children who have learned that quiet is a virtue that gets rewarded with food. Three days of it. Three days of helping Casper sort quoins without being asked, of eating what was put in front of her down to the crust, of flinching only slightly — so slightly Casper might have imagined it — whenever soldiers passed in the street below.

Casper knelt beside the sack of type and eased the ledger drawer open.

Her father had moved the letter. That was the first thing she noticed. It wasn't where he'd put it; it lay now beneath the false bottom of the drawer, wrapped in oilcloth as if it might spoil, and beneath it something else — a second paper, older, its edges browned and soft as cloth. Her hands went still over the drawer's mouth.

The oilcloth crackled when she unwrapped it. Wren stirred. Casper froze, candle tilted to shield the flame, and counted ten of her own heartbeats before the child's breathing evened again.

The seal had been broken already. Of course it had — her father would never send anything to a magistrate unread. The wax was good wax, dark green, stamped with an impression she didn't recognize: a quill crossed with a key. Someone who dealt in records. Someone whose business was paper.

She unfolded it under the candle and read.

*I write this knowing I will not live long enough to see it matter, and asking whoever receives it to forgive the trouble it brings them, because the trouble is nothing against the cost of silence.*

The hand was cramped and slanting, the letters of a man who had written too many documents too fast for too many years. Casper knew that hand from somewhere — not personally, but generically, the handwriting of clerks, of registrars, of men who spent their lives making other people's words official. She read on.

*My name is Ivet Sarn, clerk of the Third Registry. I am dying of the lung complaint and do not expect to survive the winter regardless of which army holds the bridges.*

*On the fourteenth of Frostmonth last, Aldous Renk was taken from his shop on Tanner's Row by six men in civilian dress. He was carried to the salt warehouse on Quay Lane and there, between the hours of midnight and dawn, beaten to death. His press was broken with hammers. His galleys were thrown in the river. The official record states that Renk died of a fever contracted in flight, having fled the city ahead of debts.*

*I know this because I was ordered to enter it.*

The candle guttered in a draft from the stairwell. Casper cupped it closer and kept reading, though some part of her had already begun to understand what the letter was going to say next, the way one understands the shape of a fall before the body finishes it.

*The order did not come from below. It came from the Provisional Council itself — from three names, signed and counter-signed, which I copied here because paper is more honest than my memory will remain:*

*Colonel Anselm Vay, Commander of the Second Ward.*

*Aurel Doss, Provost of Supplies.*

*Magistrate Corin Havel.*

*Havel drafted the order. Doss provided the warehouse and the men's pay. Vay gave the assurance that no inquiry would follow. I entered the false cause of death at Havel's instruction and was paid a month's salary for my discretion, which I took, God forgive me, because I have a sister and her children depend upon me, and dead clerks protect no one.*

*There are witnesses still living. I name them here so that whoever reads this may find them before others do:*

*Marta Renk, the printer's widow, lodging with her mother on Candle Street.*

*Oskar Pell, carter, who carried two of the six men from the warehouse and can describe them.*

*The boy at the chandler's on Quay Lane, who saw lanterns carried in after curfew and heard what he heard.*

*Tam Yarrow, printer, Bridge Street, who set Renk's final pamphlet and has reason to fear the same men.*

Casper read the name twice. Then a third time, as though the letters might rearrange themselves into someone else's father — a stranger, a man she had never shared a table with, never watched hunched over his stone with a mallet and a lifetime of caution.

*Tam Yarrow.*

*Yarrow printed the pamphlet under threat — they came to him first, you see, before they came to Renk. They wanted to know how far the pamphlet would travel and who would carry it. He answered truly, which is how Renk came to die. He has been paid since to keep silent. I do not blame him. Fear is a currency everyone spends eventually. But he knows things, and knowing things is dangerous, and if this letter finds him I ask him to consider what his silence has purchased and whether the price has been paid in full.*

Below that, the hand grew weaker, the ink thinner, the sentences shorter, as if the writer were rationing his strength line by line.

*If I am dead when this is read, let it stand as testimony. If I am alive, come to the hospice of Saint Ferren across the water, and I will give you the rest. There is more. There are depositions. There is enough, if anyone cares to use it, to hang three men.*

*Ivet Sarn, clerk of the Third Registry.*

*Forget my face, if you can. Remember the names instead.*

* * *

Casper sat in the loft for a long time after she finished reading, and the letter did not change, and neither did the candlelight, and neither did the sleeping child — but everything else had quietly come apart beneath her, the way ice comes apart on the river in thaw: not with a crack but with a slow soft giving-way, so that one moment the world held your weight and the next moment it simply didn't.

Corin Havel.

That was the name that broke through first, because that was the name with a face attached, and the face belonged at her father's table.

Once a month, sometimes twice, Magistrate Corin Havel dined with Tam Yarrow. He arrived in a hired chair with his collar up against the river damp, and he ate roast fowl and drank the good wine her father saved for such occasions, and he talked about type-foundries and tariffs and the lamentable state of the roads, and he laughed at her father's jokes, which were old jokes, jokes Casper had heard since she could read. He was a broad, comfortable man with a laugh like a door swinging on well-oiled hinges. He had ruffled Wren's hair once — years ago, when she was small enough to be ruffled — and told her she had her mother's serious eyes.

He had dined with them eleven days ago. Casper could date it precisely because it was the night the lamb burned and her father apologized too many times, and Havel had waved the apology away and said, *Tam, Tam, you worry like a compositor. Everything sets right in the end.* And her father had laughed, and the laughter had had a strange thin quality Casper had noticed and dismissed, the way one dismisses weather, or a neighbor's cough, or any small wrongness that costs nothing to ignore.

Everything sets right in the end.

She looked down at the sleeping child. Renk's granddaughter. The word arrived whole, unbidden, and landed in her chest like a dropped galley. Aldous Renk — she knew the name the way every printer in Vellmark knew it, the way apprentices knew it, half-legend, half-warning. *Renk of Tanner's Row*, who printed what needed printing and priced his sheets so students could buy them. Who had said, famously, at some dinner or other, that a press was the only honest witness because it couldn't be bribed, only broken. They had broken him, then. With hammers, in a salt warehouse, while a clerk wrote down a lie about fever and flight and took a month's salary for the privilege.

And her father — her cautious, careful, decent father — had been the hinge of it. Not the murderer. Never that. But the man who answered truly when killers asked questions, and who was then bought, month after month, year after year, with whatever sum made silence affordable. She thought of the private letters he burned in the hearth. She thought of the ledger drawer with its false bottom. She thought of the way he checked the street before unlocking the shop each morning, a habit she had always read as thrift.

Safe silences. That was the family trade, wasn't it? Set the safe words, burn the unsafe ones, keep the accounts balanced. She had learned it at his elbow the way other girls learned lace-making. And all along, underneath the routine of it, the silences had not been neutral. They had not been mere absences of speech. They had been load-bearing walls in someone else's crime.

Marta Renk, on Candle Street. Oskar Pell, the carter. A boy at a chandler's. Witnesses still living — though *still* was doing terrible work in that sentence, because the Council was winning, everyone knew the Council was winning, and victorious men tidied their loose ends the way her father tidied his stone, scraping the type clean for the next job. How long before Marta Renk's lodging caught fire? How long before Oskar Pell's cart lost its wheel on a bad road?

How long before a hospice across the river received a visitor it should not have received?

The candle had burned down nearly to her fingers. Wren shifted in her sleep and murmured something — a word or two, unintelligible, a child's dream-talk — and turned her face toward Casper without waking. In the low light her resemblance to nothing and no one was absolute; she was just a small tired girl in a borrowed shawl. But the letter made her otherwise. The letter made her the granddaughter of a murdered man, the last living proof of Aldous Renk beyond ink, and suddenly the weight of her was enormous — heavier than the case of great primer Casper could lift only with both arms, heavier than the press itself.

*Forget my face, if you can. Remember the names instead.*

Vay. Doss. Havel. She repeated them silently until they were fixed, the way she fixed a difficult line of type — reading it backward, letter by letter, so that the meaning couldn't seduce her eye into skipping what it didn't want to see. Colonel Anselm Vay, whose soldiers drilled in the market square each morning now, their boots striking cobbles in rhythm. Aurel Doss, whose supply wagons had been rolling through Bridge Street for a week, requisitioning grain at prices no one dared refuse. Corin Havel, who dined on roast fowl and laughed at old jokes and worried, apparently, about nothing at all, because everything always set right in the end.

She folded the letter along its original creases. Wrapped it in the oilcloth. And then — this was instinct, the compositor's instinct, the instinct of a woman whose whole life was the proper placement of things — she did not put it back in the ledger drawer, where her father's hands would find it and where her father's fear lived. Instead she lifted the loose board beneath the window sill, the one that covered the cavity where they stored the shop's oldest stock, and laid the letter flat among the yellowed sheets of a devotional work nobody had ordered in twenty years.

Then she sat back on her heels and faced the fact she had been circling all night:

She could not unknow this. That was the whole horror and the whole point of reading. Her father had built his life on the discipline of not-knowing — burning letters unread, or pretending they were unread, keeping his eyes on the forme and off the street. She had just dismantled that discipline in the space of a candle stub. Whatever came next, she would come to it knowing.

And knowing had a texture. It felt like vertigo, standing in a familiar room and finding the floor was a painting of a floor. It felt, also — she tested the feeling carefully, guiltily, the way one tests a sore tooth — like resolve. Like the first firm footing after days of thaw.

Because there was a hospice across the river, and a dying clerk who had said *come*, and a list of witnesses with a clock running over every name. Because there was a second document in the drawer, older, browned at the edges, that she had not yet dared to unfold — her father's, she was nearly certain, her father's in the way that shame is owned. Because there was a girl asleep on a sack of type whose grandfather's last pamphlet had passed through her own family's hands, and whose grandfather's blood was on the hands of a man who had eaten at her table and called her father *Tam, Tam, you worry like a compositor.*

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked. Casper went rigid.

Footsteps — her father's tread, unmistakable, the uneven rhythm of a man favoring his left knee on cold nights. He paused at the bottom of the ladder to the loft. She could see, in imagination, his upturned face, listening. The candle in her hand had almost nothing left of it; she pinched the wick with wet fingers and sat in the dark, heart slamming, smelling tallow smoke and cold iron and the faint river-stink drifting through the shutter cracks.

He didn't climb. After a long moment his footsteps moved away, toward the kitchen, toward the embers — and stopped there, and stayed, and she understood that he was sitting up in the dark the way she was sitting up in the dark, both of them orbiting the same secret from opposite sides.

In the morning, she decided. In the morning she would take the second paper from the drawer, the old browned one, and she would sit across from her father at the worktable among the type cases, and she would say the name *Aldous Renk* aloud in his hearing for the first time in however many years it had been unsayable, and she would watch his face do whatever it was going to do.

Through the shutters, far off toward the Fishmarket, a drum began — the Provisional Council's evening proclamation, one beat, two, three, patient as a metronome. Counting down something. Casper sat in the dark above her sleeping shop and listened to it, and for the first time in her life she understood exactly what her father's silence had been buying, and exactly who had been selling.
