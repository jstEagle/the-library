## 4. My Father's Bought Years

The letter lay between them on the composing stone like a thing that had fallen from a great height.

Casper had not meant to read it. That was the story she would have told, if anyone ever asked — and no one ever would, because the Yarrow family did not ask questions of one another; they only set them in type and locked them away. She had found it because her father hid things badly. He was a man who could place a single em-quad in a dark chase by touch alone, who could feel a hairline crack in a forme with his thumbnail, but he hid a letter behind the loose board above the quoin shelf as if he were a child concealing stolen plums, in the same spot, always, for thirty years.

She had read it twice by lamplight while he slept. Three names of officers. One dead printer. A list of witnesses still breathing, numbered like entries in a ledger. And at the bottom, in a clerk's cramped hand: *Tam Yarrow knows. Tam Yarrow was paid.*

Now she stood across the stone from him in the gray of morning, before the shop opened, before the apprentice boy came to sweep, and said nothing, because she had learned silence from him and could wield it better than he could.

Tam Yarrow looked at the letter. He looked at his daughter. His hands, which had never once trembled over a case of type, hung at his sides like tools whose owner had forgotten their use.

"You went through my things," he said.

"I went through your lies," Casper said. "They were easier to find."

---

*Twenty-two years earlier, in the winter of the bread riots, Tam Yarrow had been thirty-one years old and afraid in the ordinary way, the way all men in Vellmark were afraid — the low constant hum of it, like the river under the ice. He remembered that winter now the way an amputee remembers weather.*

*A man came to the shop after closing. He wore no uniform, which was itself a kind of uniform in those days; the Council's early enforcers dressed like merchants, like clerks, like anybody, so that fear had nowhere safe to settle. He carried a manuscript wrapped in oilcloth and set it on the composing stone without ceremony.*

*"Aldous Renk," he said. "You know the name."*

*Everyone knew the name. Aldous Renk printed truth the way other men printed almanacs — regularly, cheaply, and with useful tables at the back. His pamphlets passed hand to hand through the laundries and the ferry landings, and the Council had burned his press twice and each time he had surfaced again, like a drowned man walking out of the river, dripping ink.*

*"He's finished," the man said. "This is the last thing he wrote. It will be printed tonight, or you will be finished instead. The Council prefers printers who can take instruction."*

*Tam unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers gone clumsy. He read the first page standing up. He sat down halfway through the second. It was Renk's testament — names, dates, sums paid to silencers, the whole architecture of the coming regime laid out in a reformist's plain hand, ending with a paragraph addressed directly to whoever held the page:*

*If you are reading this, someone was brave enough to set it, or cowardly enough to be made to. Forgive him either way. Fear is a currency too, and most men are paid in it whether they consent to the wage or no.*

*"I have a wife," Tam said. "A daughter."*

*"Yes," the man said pleasantly. "We know."*

*He printed it that night. Two hundred copies on the old hand-press in the cellar, the one the assessors had misreported as broken years ago — the one lie of omission in Tam Yarrow's life until then, and how small it seemed afterward, how almost innocent. He printed Renk's last words in the dark while his wife slept and his daughter dreamed whatever children dream, and at dawn men came and took the bundles from his doorstep and left a purse on the stone where the oilcloth had been.*

*Three days later Aldous Renk was pulled from the Vell, his hands bound with printer's cord. Tam knew the cord's weight. He had bought it himself, a full spool, from the chandlery on Cooper's Lane.*

*The purse kept arriving. Quarterly, like rent. First from the man in merchant's clothes, later by courier, later still left in the shop's cash box as if by magic, though Tam understood there was no magic in Vellmark, only men who could open locks. He told himself he took the money because refusing it would look worse. He told himself he took it for Casper's shoes, for the roof, for the apprentice's wages. He told himself so many things that telling became its own trade, and he grew skilled at it, the way a man grows skilled at any craft practiced daily for two decades.*

*The money bought silence, and the silence bought safety, and the safety cost him everything he had pretended it protected.*

---

"Say it isn't true," Casper said. "Say the clerk wrote it wrong. Say you'll laugh, and I'll burn this, and we'll open the shutters and set the morning's job like always."

She wanted him to lie. That was the terrible discovery of her own heart in that moment — she wanted the lie, wanted it the way a drowning woman wants driftwood that cannot hold her, because the lie would let her go on being the daughter of a printer rather than the daughter of a payment.

Tam Yarrow looked at his daughter and found that after twenty-two years of practice he could not do it. Not to her face. Not with the letter lying there bearing his name in a stranger's hand.

"It's true," he said.

The words came out small. In sixty years of speaking, he had never heard his own voice sound like that.

"The pamphlet. The money. All of it." He braced both hands on the composing stone, as if the room were a deck in rough water. "Renk was already a dead man when they brought me his pages. Do you understand? They didn't need me to kill him. They needed me to carry him. A testimony means nothing unless it travels, and travel costs, and every man who touches it becomes a link in a chain they can pull."

"So you let yourself be a link."

"So I stayed alive," Tam said, and the old self-justification rose in him like bile, twenty years rehearsed, and died in his throat when he met her eyes. "No. That's the version I tell myself at night, girl, and you deserve better than my night version. I took the money. I took it every quarter for twenty-two years and I banked some of it and spent some of it and used some of it to pay off the assessor who misreported our cellar press, and every coin of it felt like a stone going down smooth and sitting in me permanent. You want to know what shame is? Shame is not the night you do the thing. Shame is every morning afterward, waking and finding you're still the man who did it."

Casper's hands were cold. She pressed them flat against her apron. "The witnesses in this letter. The ones still alive. Did you know about them?"

"No." And here something moved through his face that she had never seen there — not guilt, which he wore like an old coat, but horror, new-cut. "I never asked what else the money covered. Asking questions is how men end up in the river wearing printer's cord." He stopped. "That's what I told myself. That not-knowing was innocence. It isn't. Not-knowing is just innocence's shape, worn hollow."

"And Renk's family?" Casper's voice cracked on *family*, and she hated it, hated the tremor, because she needed to be iron in this room or she would come apart. "There's a granddaughter, Father. There's a seven-year-old girl asleep in my loft right now whose grandfather those men strung up like a ruined pamphlet. Did you ever wonder what happened to his people? In twenty-two years, did it cross your mind even once?"

"Yes," Tam said quietly. "Every quarter. When the purse arrived, I would think: somewhere his widow is counting coins too, or counting graves. And then I would put the purse in the strongbox and go back to setting type for the grain merchants, and the thinking would stop, because stopping the thinking is the service they were actually buying. Not my press. My not-thinking."

---

*He remembered the first time he spent the blood money on something for Casper. She was six. New boots, red leather, the finest in Cooper's Lane, and she had run up and down the shop floor in them laughing, and he had knelt to buckle them and seen, stitched into the tongue, the little stamped mark of the tannery that supplied the Council officers' households, and he had laughed with her anyway, because what else could a man do, and the laughing had torn something in him that never quite healed right. After that, every good thing in his life had a mark stamped into it somewhere. He learned to stop looking for the marks.*

*He remembered the night he heard Renk was dead. He had walked to the river without deciding to walk there. Stood on the landing at Cooper's Stair where they'd pulled the body out. The water had looked so ordinary. That was the thing nobody told you — the river doesn't change color when they put a man in it. It takes him the way a press takes a sheet: indifferently, evenly, and ready for the next impression. Tam had stood there a long time, and somewhere in the standing he had made the arrangement with himself that would govern the rest of his life: I will live, and living will be my punishment, and I will call the punishment prudence.*

*It was a coward's bargain and he had honored it faithfully for twenty-two years, the way cowards honor all their bargains, punctually and without courage.*

---

"There's more in the letter than names," Casper said. "There's a list of witnesses. Living ones. And the man who wrote it — a clerk named Sarn — he's dying, and he sent his granddaughter here because—" Her voice caught and steadied. "Because he thought a printer might still be worth something. Isn't that a joke, Father? Of all the doors in Vellmark, the dying man picked ours."

Tam flinched as if she'd struck him.

"He didn't pick it blind," he said. "Sarn. Ivet Sarn — small man, gray coat, ink-stained cuffs, came with the quarterly couriers the last few years. He counted the money into my hand. Never spoke beyond the business." Tam's gaze went past her, to the window, to the gray light coming up over the rooflines toward the river. "Last quarter, he lingered. Counted slow. And at the door he said a strange thing. He said, 'Yarrow. What do presses remember?' And I said presses don't remember anything, they only repeat what they're told. And he said, 'That's why we chose yours,' and left."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere below the floorboards the river worked at its pilings, patient as time.

"Why yours," Casper said slowly. It wasn't a question; she was assembling it aloud, the way she composed a line, piece by piece into the stick. "Why does a dying clerk choose the shop of a bought man to hide his granddaughter and his testimony?"

"Because a bought man is already watched," Tam said, "and therefore assumed clean. Because I've spent twenty-two years proving myself harmless, and harmlessness is the best camouflage in Vellmark." His mouth twisted. "Or because—" He couldn't finish it.

"Or because he thought underneath the purchase you were still the man who printed Renk's last pamphlet in the dark," Casper said, "and printing it, whatever they paid you after, made you the last printer alive who touched his testimony." She came around the stone and stood close to him. She had her mother's height and her father's hands, and just now she had something neither of them had given her, or perhaps something her father had given her without knowing — the understanding that words set down do not die quietly. "You carried it once, Father. Under duress. Now it's come back to your door and there's no duress except conscience, and I'm asking you: will you carry it freely?"

Tam Yarrow looked down at his hands. Ink in the creases, forty years deep, that no lye or pumice had ever fully lifted. He thought of the red boots. He thought of the river taking a man indifferently. He thought of his daughter at six, laughing, running, and of his daughter now, standing in front of him offering him the one transaction of his life that money had never touched.

"If I say yes," he said hoarsely, "they will come for us. Not today. But the historians are already going shop to shop, and Moll keeps lists, and lists are the Council's true scripture. Everything we print, everything we hide — if it's found, it's not evidence anymore, it's confession. And confessions in Vellmark get read aloud at executions."

"Then we don't get found," Casper said. "And if we do, we get found holding the truth instead of the purse."

He should have argued. Every prudent bone in his body — and he was a man built mostly of prudent bone — told him to argue, to burn the letter, to send the child away with the coin purse and lock the shutter and survive, survive, survive, the word he had worshipped for two decades like a small mean god.

Instead Tam Yarrow reached beneath the loose board above the quoin shelf — the same board, always, for thirty years — and drew out a second packet, flat and tied in waxed string, and laid it beside the clerk's letter on the stone.

"What is that?" Casper whispered.

"My account book," her father said. "Not the one Moll will see. Every payment. Dates, amounts, the marks of the couriers. Twenty-two years of quarters." His hands were shaking now, finally, and he let them. "If we're going to testify, daughter, we testify to all of it. Mine included. Set it in type if you must. I'll stand at the case myself."

And it was there, watching her father untie the waxed string with a coward's trembling and a penitent's resolve, that Casper understood what had been passed down to her along with the trade — not just the smell of ink and the grammar of justified lines, but the debt itself, the family's unpaid balance, waiting twenty-two years for a hand steady enough to pay it.

Outside, boots rang on cobbles in marching rhythm, closer than patrols usually came. A knock fell on the shop's front door — three measured strikes, official as a seal.

Through the glass, in the rising light, Casper could see the grey coat and ledger-bound arm of a man from the Provisional Council, and behind him, soldiers.

"They're early," Tam breathed. "Moll isn't due till Thursday."

The knock came again, harder, and beneath the floorboards, in the type loft, Wren stirred in her sleep.
