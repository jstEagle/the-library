## 5. The Historians Arrive

The drums began at dawn.

Casper heard them from the loft, where she had slept badly in the narrow cot beside the type cabinets, one arm curled around Wren's shoulder through most of the night. The child had cried in her sleep twice — once for a grandmother whose name Casper did not yet know, once for a door — and each time Casper had pressed her palm flat against the small back until the trembling eased. Now the drums rolled up Vellmark Street in long, ceremonial pulses, and beneath them the river bells rang out of sequence, three quarters ahead of themselves, as though even the churches were eager to be on the winning side.

Ten days, perhaps less. Everyone in the city could feel it the way one feels weather coming through a bad tooth.

"Up," Casper whispered to Wren. "Breakfast first, then the loft, then not a sound until I come for you."

Wren nodded with the grave competence of a child who had already learned that survival was a series of instructions followed exactly. She ate her bread and dripping standing up, eyes on the floor, and when Casper lifted the trapdoor behind the devotional stock shelves, she climbed down into the warm dark of the paper loft without being told twice. Casper lowered the door, dragged the pressstone over it, and stood a moment with her hand on the cold granite, listening to her own heartbeat slow.

Below, her father was already at work — or pretending to be. Tam Yarrow stood at the stone sink washing ink from his hands with a thoroughness that had nothing to do with cleanliness, and when Casper came down he did not look at her. They had not spoken of the letter since the night she had confronted him. They had not spoken of much at all. The shop had developed a new silence, layered over its old ones, like gesso over a painting someone wished to hide.

The knock came at nine.

Not the knock of a customer. Customers knocked twice, quickly, apologetically, the way people did who feared printers might be contagious. This knock was three measured strokes, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who had never in his life needed to hurry for anyone.

Tam went to the door himself. Through the front glass Casper saw a man on the step in a grey coat cut too well for a clerk and too plainly for an officer, carrying a leather folio under one arm and a walking stick he clearly did not need. He wore spectacles with round steel rims, and behind them his eyes performed the remarkable trick of seeming both mild and absolutely awake.

"Master Yarrow." His voice carried pleasantly through the glass. "Edvard Moll. Historian to the Provisional Council. You'll have received the circular."

"We received it," Tam said.

"Then you know I'm not here to buy anything or arrest anyone." Moll smiled. It was a good smile, practiced but not insincere; that was the unsettling thing about it. "I'm here because we are about to win this war, Master Yarrow, and winning is the easy part. What comes after is memory. May I come in?"

Tam stepped aside. Casper watched from behind the composing stone as the historian entered the shop the way other men entered churches — slowly, hat removed, head tilted to take in the cases, the press, the hanging sheets drying like pale laundry along the ceiling wires. He touched nothing. That frightened her more than touching would have.

"You still have a Stanhope," he said, nodding toward the iron press against the far wall. "Most shops have gone to cylinder machines. Yours must be thirty years old."

"Forty," said Tam. "It prints slow and it never breaks. Like me."

Moll laughed, genuinely, warmly, and made a note anyway. Casper saw the small book come out of the folio, saw the neat pencil line drawn across the page. *One press. Iron. Functional.* Her stomach tightened around nothing.

*The circular.* It had arrived four days ago, a printed broadsheet bearing the Council's seal: all printing houses to be visited by appointment, presses inventoried, output reviewed, cooperation invited. *Invited.* The Council had learned to use soft words the way siege engineers used sappers — quietly, under walls.

"And you are?" Moll turned to her, and his gaze was polite, brief, and total, taking in the ink on her fingers, the sleeplessness under her eyes, the way she stood between him and the staircase.

"Casper Yarrow. My father's compositor."

"A family trade." He inclined his head. "There's something fitting in that. Printing has always been a family trade — knowledge handed down hand to hand like a flame you cup your fingers around." He said it beautifully. She would remember later how beautiful it had sounded, and how cold she had felt hearing it. "I wonder, Miss Yarrow, do you love the work?"

"It pays the rent."

He laughed again, delighted, as if she had said something charming rather than true. "Honesty. Rarer every day in this city. Well." The folio opened on the counter with a soft crackle of oiled leather. "To business. The Council is commissioning an official history of the revolution. Its causes, its course, its triumphs. Every licensed print house will contribute — jobbing stock, forms, broadsheets under Council imprint. There will also be—" and here his voice took on the gentle cadence of a man delivering difficult news he had rehearsed until it sounded kind, "—a review of past output. Nothing punitive. But a history cannot be written honestly unless we know what has been printed, by whom, and for whom. Ledgers, Master Yarrow. I'll need to see your ledgers."

Something moved in the room that was not air. Casper felt her father before she saw him — felt the stillness come into his shoulders, the particular frozen quality he acquired, which she now understood she had been misreading her whole life as dignity and which was, in fact, fear worn so long it had polished smooth.

"The ledgers are business records," Tam said. "Customers' names."

"Yes." Moll's smile deepened by a degree. "That is generally what makes them useful."

* * *

They brought the ledger books out one by one, and Casper watched Edvard Moll read her father's life.

He read the way she imagined a surgeon reads a body — without prurience, without haste, with complete attention. The great ledger first, eleven years of orders: wedding invitations and funeral notices, tax forms for the magistracy, hymnals for Saint Ferren's, price lists for the tallow merchants, three thousand playbills for the Gilt Theatre the season the fire closed it. His pencil moved in small ticks. Then the debt book. Then the waste book, where rejected jobs and spoiled sheets were recorded — and here his pencil stopped.

Casper's heart stopped with it.

She knew what the waste book held. She had helped keep it since she was twelve. Spoiled impressions, botched formes — and, in Tam's cramped hand, entries that had always seemed innocuous and now burned off the page like brands: *pamphlet, 500, no imprint. Pamphlet, 800, no imprint, collected after hours.* Aldous Renk's final pamphlets, set and pulled in this very shop while the reformist printer himself lay dead in a ditch somewhere beyond the Tannery Gate, and the man who had ordered them murdered walking free with a Council commission pinned to his coat.

"Busy year, this one." Moll tapped the page with one finger, not looking up. "'Pamphlet, five hundred, no imprint.' No customer named?"

"Paid in cash," Tam said. His voice was level. Casper marveled at it, and hated it, and understood it. "Some clients prefer discretion. We don't ask."

"No," Moll agreed mildly. "Printers who ask questions tend to become news themselves." He let the sentence sit in the room like smoke, then turned the page. "You wouldn't recall who placed these?"

"I process hundreds of jobs. My daughter manages the accounts."

And there it was — passed to her, smoothly, the way a man passes a hot dish across a table, and Casper understood that this too was part of the purchase, the old bought silence extending its term. She met the historian's eyes over the ledger.

"Some men came at night," she said. "No names given. Cash on collection. My father doesn't permit questions; it's how he's kept this shop alive through three changes of government."

"Three," Moll repeated, with appreciation. "Yes. Survival is a discipline, Miss Yarrow. Most people think it's a virtue. It isn't. It's a discipline, and it requires forgetting things on schedule." He closed the waste book gently, as one closes a coffin lid, and made another note. "I'll want copies of these pages. Standard procedure. And I'll want the dates cross-checked against the Council's own records — there may be discrepancies, there always are, and honest historians love a discrepancy. It's where the truth hides, if truth is what we're after."

He said it lightly. He said everything lightly. But Casper watched his pencil and understood that the discrepancy he loved was already found: that somewhere across the city, in the Council's archives, the murder of Aldous Renk had been recorded as a robbery, or an accident, or a regrettable excess by persons unknown — and that the waste book open under his fingers was the thing that would hang those persons unknown, if it survived, or absolve them, if it did not.

*If it did not.*

The thought arrived whole, cold, complete, the way the worst thoughts do. Moll was not here to destroy anything. Destroying was crude and left gaps, and gaps bred rumors. He was here to *copy*. Within weeks the official history would exist in ten thousand copies — fine paper, good ink, sewn bindings, distributed to every school and garrison and parish chest from Vellmark to the border — and the Council's version of Renk's death would be the only version anyone under thirty had ever heard. The waste book would sit forgotten on its shelf, unread, contradicted, absurd beside the handsome official volume, until damp or mice or a decree about obsolete papers finished it. Records did not need burning anymore. They only needed outliving.

Unless they were moved. Unless they were copied and scattered and hidden inside other words, hymnals and almanacs and children's primers, seeded through the city like spores before the frost came down.

Her hands, resting on the composing stone, had gone cold.

"Miss Yarrow?" Moll was watching her. "You've gone somewhere."

"Counting spoiled sheets," she said. "It's grim reading, our waste book."

"It usually is." He smiled, and for a moment — one unguarded moment, gone as fast as it came — the mildness dropped away entirely and she saw the thing underneath, which was not cruelty exactly, nor ambition, but a vast patient appetite, the appetite of a man who intended to eat the past and found the prospect nourishing. Then the mildness returned, seamless. "Well. I've taken enough of your morning."

He rose, settled his coat, accepted Tam's stiff bow at the door. On the threshold he paused, struck — or appearing to be struck — by an afterthought.

"One more matter. Purely administrative." He consulted his little book. "Your neighbor, the widow Cassel, two doors down — she's registered a lodger. A girl, seven or eight years old, seen at her window Tuesday last. But the widow Cassel says the girl was never formally listed, and the census officers can't find her anywhere on this street." He looked up, and the round spectacles caught the grey light and gave nothing back. "Children wander, of course. Especially in wartime. Still, an unregistered child is a paperwork problem, and I do hate paperwork problems. If one should turn up — anywhere on this street — the Council would look kindly on whoever reported it. Kindly indeed."

The silence lasted two heartbeats. Casper counted them.

"We haven't seen any children," Tam said. "We're printers. Children don't come here."

"No," Moll agreed. "They rarely do." He put on his hat. "Good morning, Master Yarrow. Miss Yarrow. I'll send a boy for the ledger copies tomorrow. Take care of the Stanhope. In the new Vellmark, we'll want every working press we can count."

*Every press we can count.* The door closed behind him. Through the glass they watched him go down the street unhurried, pausing at the Halvard shop two doors along, where old Halvard's son was already holding the door open, already bowing, already smiling the smile of a man who believed that compliance was a country you could emigrate to.

* * *

Tam did not speak for a long time. He stood at the window until Moll's grey coat vanished around the corner by the chandler's, and then he walked to the sink and washed hands that were already clean, and Casper watched her father come apart by degrees, quietly, the way ice leaves a windowpane — no drama, just a steady going to water.

"He knows," Tam said finally. "About the girl. He knows and he gave us three days to decide what kind of fools we are."

"He doesn't know what she is. He knows a child was seen. That's not the same."

"It's the same enough." Tam turned. His face had the grey, scraped look of a man who had spent forty years learning exactly how much fear a body could hold and was now discovering the reserve capacity. "Casper. Listen to me. Tomorrow he sends for the ledgers. The day after, some clerk compares dates. The waste book, the pamphlets, Renk — if that thread is pulled, it pulls everything. It pulls me, it pulls you, it pulls whatever poor soul left that letter on our doorstep. We burn the waste book tonight. Both copies of the pages. We burn the letter. And the girl goes to the widow Cassel, registered, or to the river road, anywhere, gone, by tomorrow's dark."

"She's seven years old."

"She's seven years old and she is a loaded pistol lying in our house!" It burst out of him louder than he had spoken in years, and then immediately he lowered his voice, glancing at the ceiling, at the loft, and Casper saw the terrible arithmetic happening behind his eyes: *she hears everything, children hear everything.* "Do you think Moll inventories presses because he loves machinery? Every press counted is every press that can be silenced. Every ledger copied is every ledger that can be corrected. He told us the plan to our faces, Casper, because telling us changes nothing — that's what power does now, it announces itself politely and waits to see who flinches. I have flinched my whole life. It kept us alive. It kept *you* alive. Flinch once more."

Casper stood very still among the cases, among the little metal letters that held, in their compartments, every word the neighborhood had needed for eleven years — births, deaths, debts, prayers, prices. Somewhere above her head, behind the trapdoor, a child was sitting in the dark doing what Casper had told her to do: waiting, silently, trusting the instruction.

"No," she said.

Tam stared at her.

"They'll copy the ledgers tomorrow. So we make sure the copy they take is worth copying — accurate, dull, harmless, every entry exactly right except the ones that matter. The real record, the waste book, the letter, whatever else that letter leads to — it gets copied too, Father. By us. Tonight. And it gets hidden where Moll's tidy history will never think to look, in a hundred pieces, in a hundred places, so that no fire and no decree and no historian with a beautiful voice can ever gather it all back up again." She heard her own voice steadying as it went, surprise herself with it. "You bought our safety with your silence for eleven years. I understand why. I'm not going to spit on it. But the bill has come due, and Moll is the collector, and silence won't pay it anymore. Only paper will. Our paper."

For a moment she thought he would strike her, or weep, or both. Instead Tam Yarrow looked at the Stanhope press — forty years old, slow, faithful, misregistered on every civic list as half-broken — and something moved through his face that she had never seen there before, something older than fear and younger than shame.

"The Halvard boy is bringing their ledger copies to Moll's office Thursday," he said at last, quietly. "By cart. Unattended at the ferry steps for the length of one crossing." He rubbed his jaw. "God help me. What am I saying. What are you making me say."

"What you already know how to do," Casper said. "Survive. Just — this once — surviving for the record instead of from it."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came a small sound: a child in the loft, shifting her weight, humming almost inaudibly — three notes, over and over, a scrap of some song no one had taught her in this house. Casper climbed the stairs to bring her water and her supper, and halfway up she stopped, listening.

Because the tune was familiar. She had heard it years ago, printed on a ballad sheet, a reformer's marching song banned in her childhood, sold in the market squares for a copper until the coppers ran out and the sellers disappeared. A song of Aldous Renk's circle. A song no seven-year-old should know by heart.

Casper stood on the stairs with her hand pressed flat against the wall, and understood that the child above her was not cargo to be smuggled, not evidence to be hidden, not a problem to be solved before Thursday's cart.

She was a witness. And witnesses, Casper thought, climbing the rest of the way, were the one record no historian could inventory — if someone lived long enough to hear them out.
