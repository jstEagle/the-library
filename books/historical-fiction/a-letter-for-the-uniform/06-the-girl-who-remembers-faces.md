## 6. The Girl Who Remembers Faces

She had been in the type loft for three days before she began to speak.

Casper learned to read her the way she read a page of uncorrected proofs — patiently, by what was missing as much as what was there. The girl did not cry. That was the first strange thing, and the thing that frightened Casper most, because children who did not cry had been taught not to, and teaching a child that took either great cruelty or great necessity. She sat among the cases of twelve-point Caslon with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, a small gray shape in the lamplight, watching the door.

Her name, when it finally came, arrived sideways, the way most true things did in that house.

It happened on the third evening, during the hour when the shop went dark and Tam Yarrow climbed the outside stair to bring up bread and watered wine, because he would not let his daughter be seen carrying food to a stowaway twice a day. He set the plate down on an overturned galley tray and said, gruffly, the way he said everything now, "Eat, little wren."

The child looked up at him. And something crossed her face — not fear, not quite recognition, but the flicker of a word being tested against a memory — and she said, very quietly, "That's my name."

Tam froze with his hand still on the tray. Casper, standing at the foot of the loft ladder, felt the whole room tilt a degree.

"Wren," the girl said again, as though confirming it for herself. "Grandfather called me Wrennie. Because I was small and I hid in things." She looked around at the pigeonholes of type, the drawers within drawers, the whole architecture of small hidden compartments, and Casper saw her understand where she was, and find it funny, or something near funny. A ghost of a smile. "He would have liked this place."

"Your grandfather," Tam said carefully. His voice had gone flat, the voice he used with customs men. "What was his name?"

The girl's mouth worked. She looked down at her hands, which were folded in her lap with a primness no seven-year-old owned naturally — a taught primness, a *be-still-and-be-forgotten* primness — and when the name came out it came out like something she had practiced saying silently so many times that saying it aloud cost almost nothing:

"Aldous Renk."

* * *

Afterward, Casper could not remember whether she had sent her father down the ladder or whether he had gone of his own accord, pale as a sheet of unsized paper. She remembered climbing up into the loft and sitting down across from the child, cross-legged, the way you sit with someone whose trust is a bridge that will bear only one weight at a time.

"You knew his name was dangerous," Casper said.

Wren nodded. "Papa said never say it in the street. Grandfather too. Grandfather said names were like money — you didn't count them out loud where people could see your hands." She said it in a slightly deeper voice, a borrowed cadence, and Casper realized with a jolt that the child was imitating a dead man's speech, that she carried him inside her the way the loft carried galleys: stored, protected, waiting to be printed.

"Tell me about him," Casper said. "Only if you want to."

Wren considered this with terrible seriousness, the way children weigh requests they have learned are not really requests. Then she unfolded herself and crawled across the boards to sit beside Casper, close enough that their shoulders touched, and began.

"He smelled of ink," she said first, and Casper's chest tightened, because that was the first thing anyone said about a printer, always, and the child said it with the authority of someone reporting a scientific fact. "But different ink than yours. His was — " she hunted for it, frowning, " — sweeter? No. Older. Like a church that isn't used anymore." She glanced up. "Is that silly?"

"No," Casper said. Her own throat had gone tight. "Ink smells different depending on how long it's stood. What did he print?"

"Everything. That's what he said. He'd hold up his hands" — she held up her own small ones, fingers spread — "like this, and say, *these have set every kind of truth there is, Wrennie, prayers and prices and once a love letter so beautiful the man who wrote it cried.*" She lowered her hands. "And then last year he stopped printing the Council things and only printed the other kind. The kind that made Mama cry."

"What other kind?"

"The kind with names in them." Wren's voice dropped. She had learned that much, at least, about the world: that names were the dangerous part. "Names of men who did things. Grandfather said somebody had to write the names down or it would be like it never happened. He said forgetting was a machine too, and somebody else's machine, and we weren't going to help run it."

Casper stared at her. Somewhere below, the shop clock ticked through the quarter hour, and out in the street a patrol passed, boots striking cobbles in the rhythm Vellmark had learned to sleep through. The child recited her dead grandfather's philosophy in the matter-of-fact tone of a girl describing supper, and Casper thought: *she has been made into a vessel. Someone poured all of this into her knowing she was the last container left.*

"Who told you to come here?" Casper asked gently. "Who brought you to our door?"

Wren went still. It was a different stillness from shyness; it was the stillness of a lock. "I'm not supposed to remember his face."

"But you remember other things about him."

A pause. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the loft vent, righted itself.

"He coughed," she said at last. "All the time. And he gave me a coin every day and told me to hide it, and when I asked why he said, *because a person with a secret purse has somewhere to go.*" Her chin trembled once and she mastered it, that awful trained mastery, and Casper wanted to take her by the shoulders and tell her she was allowed to cry here, allowed to cry anywhere, that the whole economy of silence her family lived by was a lie dressed up as wisdom — but she didn't, because the child was not finished, and interrupting a witness mid-deposition was a sin Casper's trade had taught her to respect.

"There was a boat," Wren said. "We went under the bridges at night and he kept me down low and told me stories about a cat. And then a house with a green door, and then another house, and everyone was old and coughing." She frowned. "I think they were all sick. Not sick like fever. Sick like — " she searched again, and found it, and Casper felt the word land in her own stomach like a stone — "sick like they were already forgotten, so nobody bothered to make them better. He took me to a bed by a window. There were four beds. He said, *this is the last stop, Wrennie, and after this it's your friend at the print shop.*"

"His friend at the print shop," Casper repeated.

Wren nodded. "He wrote the letter at the window. It took him a long time because his hand shook. And then he held my face in both hands" — she raised her own hands and cupped the air, and Casper saw exactly, horribly, the gesture — "and he said, *forget my face. If they ask you, forget my face. Faces get people killed. But don't forget anything else. Don't forget one single thing else. You're the remembering part now.*"

The lamp guttered. Downstairs a floorboard settled. Casper became aware that her eyes were wet and that the child was watching her with grave attention, cataloguing this too, storing it, adding it to the archive she had been ordered to become.

"I remember lots of things," Wren said, almost apologetically. "I know he coughed blood on Thursday mornings. I know the letter-man's coat had a torn pocket and he kept the coins in the good one. I know Grandfather's press had a crack shaped like a river. I know three songs and two prayers and where the green door was if we ever need to run back. I know the men who came to the house wore grey sashes, and one of them had a ring with a black stone, and he" — her voice thinned to a wire — "he was the one who told us to stop printing. He was polite. Grandfather said polite men were the worst kind, because they already knew nobody would believe you about them."

*A black stone ring.* Casper turned the detail over and felt it click into place beside the letter upstairs, hidden beneath her mattress, with its three named officers of the Provisional Council. One of those officers dined at her father's table. One of them might wear a ring with a black stone.

"Do you remember his face?" Casper asked. "The polite one. The ring."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. And Casper watched something move behind the child's eyes — the war between two orders, *forget faces*, and whatever deeper law a seven-year-old keeps that says some faces are yours to carry until someone can use them.

"I wasn't supposed to," Wren whispered.

"I know."

"But you're the print shop." She said it as if it explained everything, and perhaps it did; perhaps Ivet Sarn, dying at his window over the river, had described the Yarrow establishment in terms a child could hold — *the print shop, the safe place, the people who keep words* — and the description had taken root as a kind of faith. "You're where the remembering goes. So it should go to you. Even the parts that aren't supposed to."

She scooted closer. She took Casper's hand and arranged it, palm up, with the gravity of a ritual. Then she leaned in and spoke against Casper's ear, so quietly that the words seemed less heard than pressed there, like letters into soft paper:

"Tall. Grey hair cut short like a soldier. A scar through one eyebrow, white, like a line of chalk. And the ring." She pulled back. "Black stone. Square. Gold band worn thin on the inside because he turns it and turns it when he talks. Grandfather said watch a man's hands, Wrennie, the mouth lies but the hands are honest."

Casper sat motionless. Through the loft vent she could hear the city breathing its curfew breath, the far-off clang of the night watch, the river moving under the bridges with its cargo of secrets. In her mind the letter lay open on her mattress, and one of its three names detached itself and put on a grey sash and a chalk-white scar and began turning a square black ring.

*Officer Dain Corvess. Commissioner of Provisions.* The man who had sat at Tam Yarrow's table in autumn, complimenting the soup, laughing at her father's careful jokes.

"Casper?" Wren was watching her. "Did I do it right?"

"You did it perfectly," Casper said, and meant it in a way the child couldn't yet fathom. She gathered the girl against her side, and Wren went willingly, bonelessly, the way exhausted children do, her head finding the hollow of Casper's shoulder as though it had been measured for it.

"I'm tired of remembering," Wren murmured, already half asleep. "It's heavy."

"Then rest," Casper whispered into her hair. "I'll carry it tonight."

But lying awake afterward in the dark above the type cases, with the child's breath slow against her collarbone, Casper understood that the weight did not divide; it multiplied. Every fragment the girl surrendered was another page demanding to be set, verified, preserved. A granddaughter. Renk's granddaughter, alive, hidden in a loft full of lead and paper, while the man who had ordered her grandfather's death ate well in the new city being built on top of the old one.

They had been keeping silence in this house for eleven years. Now silence had a face, and it was asleep on Casper's arm, and it trusted her.

At dawn she would go to her father. At dawn she would ask him, with the girl's remembered details sharp as freshly cast type in her mind, exactly how well he knew Officer Corvess — and whether the dinners and the gifts and the years of careful neutrality had bought more than quiet, whether they had bought his signature on the order that emptied a green-door house and left a child at their door.

But dawn was hours off, and the letter was upstairs, and Casper lay listening to Wren breathe and rehearsed, over and over, the sentence she would need: *Father, the child remembers faces.*

* * *

She woke to voices below — low, urgent, her father's and another's she half recognized — and eased herself from under Wren without waking her. On the landing, in the gray light, she found Tam at the trapdoor, hunched, listening, his knuckles white on the frame.

Below, in the shop, a man's voice, smooth and unhurried, asking questions about paper stock and registration schedules.

"Who is that?" Casper breathed.

Tam didn't look at her. When he answered, his voice was ash. "Corvess," he said. "Commissioner of Provisions. He's come about the census of presses." A pause, and then, quieter, worse: "Casper — he's asking about the Halvard fire. Eleven years ago. He's asking who else was working here then."

Downstairs, the smooth voice laughed pleasantly at something, and the sound rose through the floorboards like water rising in a cellar.

"And Casper," her father whispered, "he's asking whether we've seen any children lately."
