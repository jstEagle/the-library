## 7. Finding Ivet Sarn

The river was the color of old pewter that morning, and it smelled of thaw and coal smoke, the two smells of Vellmark in the last cold week of a dying order. Casper stood at the ferry landing with Wren's hand swallowed inside her own mittened one and tried to look like nothing at all: a printer's daughter crossing to the Salt Quarter on an errand for her father, a girl with chapped cheeks and no opinions.

She had rehearsed the errand until it sat in her mouth like a smooth stone. *My father needs lampblack from Ferren's chandler's. He says the Salt Quarter sells it cheaper.* Lampblack was plausible; her father went through it by the barrel, setting his careful gray advertisements for mourning cloth and patent tonics. Nobody arrested a girl for lampblack.

Nobody had yet arrested a girl at all, but the patrols were new since the Halvard seizure, and new things in Vellmark grew teeth quickly.

"Keep your eyes down if they stop us," she murmured to Wren. "Count your buttons. Count them all the way to fifty."

"I have nine," Wren said.

"Then count them eleven times."

Wren nodded with the terrible seriousness of seven-year-olds given orders that matter, and Casper felt the weight of what she was doing — carrying the man's granddaughter back across the river toward the hospice where he lay dying — settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. It would be safer to leave the child hidden in the type loft among the devotional stock, safer by half, but the clerk Ivet Sarn might not last the day, and a dying man who had smuggled a granddaughter out through a curfew storm deserved better than secondhand questions put to him by strangers. Besides, there was the matter of faces. The letter had said *forget my face*, and Casper intended to ask Sarn why a face needed forgetting, and whether forgetting it now would do anyone any good.

The ferryman was a broad, incurious man named Doss who had poled the crossing for thirty years and had survived three changes of government by the simple method of never appearing to notice any of them. He took their coppers without looking up.

"Business?" he asked, which was not curiosity but ritual.

"Chandler's errand," Casper said. "Lampblack."

"Lampblack." He grunted. "Mind the soldiers on the quay. They're counting boats today."

They were counting more than boats. On the far landing two Council troopers in the gray-and-black of the Provisional stood with a ledger open on a plank table, stopping each passenger, and behind them a third was nailing something to the customs post. Casper read it as the ferry ground against the pilings: a proclamation on the registration of printing presses, the words *by order* repeated twice in heavy black type that some other printer's son had set, some other father's shop, and she thought: *we are all of us typesetting our own chains now, and being paid by the letter.*

"Eleven times," she whispered, and squeezed Wren's hand, and stepped off into the crowd of market women and fish porters, becoming dull, becoming invisible, walking at the pace of someone thinking about soap.

The soldier at the table barely glanced at her. "Name?"

"Casper Yarrow. Yarrow's Printing, Tanner's Row." She said it before she could choose otherwise; a lie about her name would unravel faster than truth about her errand. "Crossing for lampblack."

"Yarrow." His eyes came up then, and she saw him reach for the name the way a man reaches for a coin he thinks is counterfeit. "Your father dines with Councillor Vasse sometimes, doesn't he? There was something in the gazette."

"He sets Vasse's election bills," Casper said. "That's all. Printers dine where they're paid to."

It was true, and it worked the way truth often did on small men — it bored them. He waved her through, already calling the next name, and Casper walked up the quay with her heart going like a press under steam, hating that her family's bought respectability had just spent itself on her behalf like a coin her father hadn't known he'd given her.

* * *

The hospice of Saint Aldhelm had been a granary once, then a barracks, then whatever the city needed it to be, which was the biography of most buildings in Vellmark. It stood at the end of Rope Walk with its long low roof sagging like a tired back, its windows fogged with the breath of too many sick. A wooden saint over the door had lost an arm to weather or vandalism; the remaining arm was raised in blessing or surrender, and Casper could not decide which, and suspected the carver hadn't either.

Inside was the smell she would remember for the rest of her life, though she didn't know it yet: tallow, carbolic, wet wool, and underneath it all the sweetish smell of bodies being patiently defeated. A lay brother with ink-stained fingers — she noticed the ink first, always the ink — directed them down a corridor of pallets separated by hung sheets, and when she gave Sarn's name his face did something complicated.

"You'll be the fourth this month asking after him," he said quietly. "The others wore better coats than you."

"What were they?"

"Men with a historian's manner. Taking notes. Asking when he'd last been lucid, how long the physicians expected him to last, whether he received visitors." He looked at Wren, and his voice dropped further. "I told them he was past sense. Which was nearly true, and is truer every hour. If you have business with him, have it fast. And child—" this to Wren, gently, "—there are apples in the refectory if you ask Sister Maret. Tell her Brother Anselm sent you."

He understood. That was the thing Casper kept discovering in these weeks: how many people understood, silently, and moved aside, and said nothing, the whole city holding its breath around its own knowledge. Her father's silence was only one note in a very large chord.

Sarn's alcove was at the corridor's end, where the light came grayest through a window crusted with salt haze. He was a narrow man folded into a narrow bed, yellowed as old paper, and at first Casper thought Brother Anselm had understated it — he seemed entirely gone, eyes closed, breath whistling faintly through lips gone thin as a leaf. Then, without opening his eyes:

"If you've come to take my deposition, young woman, you're late. I took it myself. It's in a letter."

Casper's mouth went dry. "You know why I'm here."

"I know the sound of Yarrow type when I hear about it." His eyes opened then, and they were astonishingly present, two dark points in the ruin of his face. "Ivet Sarn spent forty years as a records clerk. You learn to know typefaces the way priests learn Latin. Your father set Aldous Renk's last pamphlet. I have handled it. The e's lean left. Everything of Tam Yarrow's leans left, like a man perpetually stepping out of someone's way."

Casper pulled the stool close. "The letter came to my father's shop with a child."

"With Wren." Something moved across the ruined face — grief, or relief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Is she well? Is she fed? Don't answer loudly. Yes, I can see she's well, she has your mitten and your wary walk." He coughed, a long tearing sound, and lay still after it with his eyes shut, and for a moment Casper thought she'd lost him already. Then: "I don't have much time, and less voice. So listen, because I will say this once and I am a clerk, and clerks do not repeat themselves."

She leaned in. Beside her, obediently, Wren had slipped away toward the refectory, drawn by apples and by the instinct of children to leave rooms where adults begin to speak in lowered voices.

"Aldous Renk printed the truth for twenty years," Sarn said. "Small truths. Tax rolls that didn't match. Grain shipments that vanished. Then larger ones — names, dates, the little economy of favors between the old magistrates and the officers who now call themselves a Provisional Council. When the revolution came, everyone assumed Renk would flourish. Truth-tellers prosper in revolutions, don't they? That's the story." A thin smile. "But Renk's truths didn't care whose side they embarrassed. Three weeks ago he began assembling an account of the prison-yard killings at Marlow Gate — men shot without trial by officers acting on Council orders, including an order signed by Vasse himself. He came to me because I keep the registry copies. I gave him what I could prove."

"And they killed him for it."

"They killed him in his own shop with his grandchildren sleeping upstairs. Three officers. I won't say the names aloud here — walls in a hospice are thinner than paper — but the letter your father holds names them, and names me as witness to the registry evidence, and names two others still living. A ferryman. A night watchman at the Marlow gatehouse." His breath caught. "I wrote that letter knowing I wouldn't live to sign anything better. I sent it to Tam Yarrow because Tam owed Renk a debt he's been paying in cowardice for years, and because a frightened man keeps secrets better than a brave one. Bravery talks. Fear sits on things."

"That's a hard accounting of my father."

"It's a precise one. I deal in precision." He turned his head, laboriously, and looked at her straight on. "But here is what fear cannot sit on forever, Casper Yarrow. Documents breed. While I lived I kept copies. Under the third floorboard of my old rooms above the chandlery on Rope Walk — the landlady stores my trunk — there is a packet. Depositions. The ferryman Oskar Hale, taken down word for word, sworn before a notary before the notary fled north. The watchman Pell. My own written account, dated, witnessed by Brother Anselm here, who is braver than his habit of obedience suggests. Names, dates, the registry numbers of the orders themselves. Enough to hang Vasse twice over, if hanging were ever again a thing done to the right people."

Casper's hands had gone cold in her lap. "Why give it to me? You gave the first letter to my father."

"Because your father will burn it. Won't he?" He said it without cruelty, the way one states a figure in a ledger. "He's had ten years of practice burning. But the girl at his door — the one who hid the child instead of turning her out, the one who read a sealed letter addressed to another man — that one I'm gambling on." He coughed again, softer. "I watched you just now. You haven't denied reading it."

"No," Casper said. "I read it."

"And what did you feel?"

"Vertigo," she said, before she could dress it up. "Like standing at the top of a stair in the dark and finding there's one more step than there should be."

Sarn laughed — a dry whisper of a laugh, costing him visibly. "Good. Vertigo is honest. Certainty is what historians sell." His hand crept out from under the blanket, light as a bird's bone, and gripped her wrist. "Listen. They came asking when I'd die. Do you understand? A historian's assistant, with a notebook, asking the physicians to estimate. Moll's office. Edvard Moll is already writing the official history, and in his history Renk died of natural causes, and the Marlow Gate dead were traitors shot lawfully, and men like me are footnotes pending deletion. Every record I touched in forty years at the registry, he can unmake in a season. Paper is only paper while one man holds the press." His grip tightened with surprising strength. "While *everyone* holds it, it becomes memory. Memory outlives whoever wins. That is the whole of politics, girl. The rest is ink."

"The depositions," Casper said. "Third floorboard. Trunk. I'll get them today."

"Not today. Today you'll walk out slowly and buy your lampblack and go home by the bridge, not the ferry — they count the ferries. Tomorrow, early, before the patrols change shift at dawn, when Rope Walk is nothing but cats and fishwives." He was fading now; she could see it happening, his words coming slower, spaced wider, a press running out of power. "One more thing. The letter said forget my face. Did you wonder why?"

"Yes."

"Because in a week or a month, when Moll's men trace the child back along her road, everyone who helped her will be asked what they saw. And the safest answer is *nothing*. But—" his eyes found hers, and held them, "—forgetting is a skill, and like all skills it's wasted on the wrong student. Some faces should be remembered so exactly they can be testified to. Remember mine, Casper Yarrow. I am Ivet Sarn, records clerk of Vellmark, and I swear that what is in that packet is true, and I would sign it if my hand would obey me. Let my face stand witness instead."

She bent over the bed, close enough to see everything — the ruined teeth, the pulse beating in his temple like something trapped, the astonishing steadiness of his gaze — and she made herself look, deliberately, the way she studied type before setting it, letter by letter.

"I remember you," she said. "Ivet Sarn. Clerk of records."

"Good girl." His eyes closed. "Now go. And tell Anselm on your way out that his apples have improved."

At the corridor's turn Casper looked back once. Sarn lay with his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting for a document to be brought to him, patient as only clerks and the dying are patient, and the gray light through the salted window fell across the bed like a sheet of blank paper.

Brother Anselm met her at the door with Wren's sticky hand in his. "The trunk," he said, very low, without preamble. "He told you about the trunk. I witnessed the depositions — my mark is on each page. If anything happens to him before tomorrow, come to me. I can open the landlady's door with a prayer and a hairpin." He pressed something into Casper's palm: a key, small and green with age, on a loop of string. "Or with this, if prayer runs slow. Third floorboard. Mind the patrol on Rope Walk after the bells — they stop at the chandlery to warm themselves, and Ferren is a talker."

"Everyone knows everything here," Casper said, half to herself.

Anselm smiled with real sadness. "In Vellmark, knowing has never been the danger. Only saying." He squeezed Wren's shoulder. "Take the child home by the bridge, and count your blessings, printer's daughter. You've just been made a witness yourself."

* * *

The bridge at midday was a gauntlet of gray-and-black. Soldiers checked bundles at both ends — actual bundles, a woman's laundry shaken out in the cold wind, a fishmonger's baskets probed with bayonets — and Casper walked across with Wren's hand in hers and nothing in her arms but the memory of a dying man's face and the weight of a small green key against her palm, where she'd sewn it into her mitten's lining with six stitches of black thread while Wren counted her nine buttons eleven times on a refectory bench.

Below them the river carried the light in long broken pieces. On the far bank, smoke rose from the direction of the Halvard print works — not fire, just the chimneys of the Council's new central press bureau running day and night, printing the official history while it was still happening. Moll's machine. Somewhere in those stacks of fresh paper, Aldous Renk was already becoming a footnote.

"Was he your friend?" Wren asked suddenly, looking back at the hospice roofline. "The man in the bed."

"He was a friend of your grandfather's."

"Grandpapa printed true things," Wren said, with the flat certainty of a child quoting scripture learned by rote. "Mama said that's why we have to be quiet."

Casper stopped, there in the middle of the bridge, in the wind, with soldiers at either end and the whole city watching itself pretend. She crouched to the child's height.

"Wren. Tomorrow morning I'm going to bring you something your grandfather helped make. Words on paper. True ones. And I need you to do one thing for me — the same thing the kind man at the door asked your mother to do. Can you carry something and tell no one?"

Wren considered this with grave brown eyes, Aldous Renk's eyes, Casper realized suddenly, seeing the dead printer for the first time in the set of the girl's jaw.

"I carried the letter," Wren said. "I didn't tell anyone but you."

"Then you can do it again."

They crossed off the bridge into the Tanner's Row crowds, and Casper walked fast now, urgency climbing her spine vertebra by vertebra: dawn tomorrow, the third floorboard, the trunk, the depositions — and then what? Paper in hand was not yet paper in hands. She thought of Jorun Beck, her father's old apprentice, who'd gone underground printing ballads in a cellar somewhere in the Salt Quarter, and of the proclamation nailed to the customs post, presses registered, presses seized, and she understood that she was about to need a printer who had already decided to lose everything.

Behind her, across the river, the hospice bell began to toll — one stroke, unhurried, for a soul departing — and Casper did not let herself believe it was Sarn, not yet, not with the key still warm against her wrist.

But she walked faster anyway, pulling the child through the crowd, racing the bell.
