## 8. An Old Apprentice's Debt

Jorun Beck heard the knock at the third hour past midnight, and he knew before his hand touched the bar that it was trouble, because nobody knocked like that unless they meant to be forgotten by morning.

Three knocks. A pause. Two more. Then silence — the particular silence of someone standing very still on the other side of a door, listening for soldiers.

He had learned to read knocks the way other men read faces. For four years he had lived below a chandler's shop on Rope Lane, in a cellar that smelled of tallow and wet stone, with a press so old its screws had been replaced twice with iron scavenged from a broken gate. Four years of printing ballads that no honest man would admit to singing. *The Miller's Lament.* *Ten Bells for the Hanged.* The one about Aldous Renk that everyone knew and no one could prove had come from this room. He printed two hundred copies at a time, folded them into bales of rag destined for laundries up and down the river, and slept badly, which was the price of the trade and he paid it willingly enough.

But a knock at midnight, coded, patient, frightened — that was not a customer wanting ballads. That was someone bringing him something heavier than paper.

He opened the door a hand's width, chain still fastened, and saw Casper Yarrow standing in the dark with her hood pulled low and a satchel clutched against her chest like an infant.

"Saints' teeth," he said, and unchained the door. "Get in before the street remembers you."

* * *

The cellar was lit by a single oil lamp turned so low the flame was more blue than yellow. Jorun bolted the door behind her, then stood a moment with his ear against the wood, counting his own heartbeats, listening for footsteps overhead. The chandler slept like the dead and snored like the dying; nothing stirred above but mice in the tallow bins.

Casper pulled back her hood. In the lamplight she looked older than her years, and thinner than she'd been at Midwinter — though Jorun supposed they all did now, in the tenth day of the siege, with the Council's drums audible across the water even here, even underground, a slow pulse beneath the floorboards like the city itself had a failing heart.

"You shouldn't have come," he said. "Not to me. Not now."

"I had nowhere else."

"That's not true. You had everywhere else, and you chose here." He gestured her toward the stool beside the composing stone, because standing arguments were loud arguments. "Which means you're either desperate or you think I owe you something. Sit down. Tell me which."

She sat. She set the satchel on her knees and kept both hands flat upon it, the way a woman keeps hands on a thing she has decided not to surrender.

"You were my father's apprentice for six years," she said. "You ate at our table every Saintsday. You set your first full page in our shop. When you left — when you *fled*, Jorun, let's use the true word — my father told the inspectors he'd dismissed you for theft rather than admit what you really printed. He perjured himself to keep them off you. I know because I was there, and I was twelve, and I understood exactly what I was watching."

Jorun said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, a shutter creaked in the wind off the river.

"So yes," Casper went on, "I think you owe him. And I think you owe yourself. And I think — " her voice caught, steadied, " — I think you owe a dead man whose name you've been printing in ballads for four years without knowing the half of it."

"Aldous Renk." Jorun said the name quietly, as one does in cellars. "Everyone prints Renk. He's become a song. Songs are safe. Dead men make excellent lyrics precisely because they can't be interrogated."

"He wasn't murdered for a song."

"No?"

"He was murdered by three officers of the Provisional Council. By name. By date. With witnesses still living who saw the order signed." She lifted the flap of the satchel, and Jorun saw the corner of a sealed letter, and beneath it a sheaf of loose pages in a clerk's cramped, failing hand. "I have testimony, Jorun. Real testimony. Depositions taken before the killing, naming the men who gave the order. And I want it printed. Properly printed. Set and proofed and struck in enough copies that no historian alive can gather them all back in."

For a long moment Jorun did not move. Then he crossed the cellar, lifted the lamp, and carried it to the far wall where a shelf of type cases hung in the dark like sleeping birds. He stood looking at them, his back to her.

"Do you know what happens to a press these days?" he said, still facing the wall. "Two streets from here, Halvard's shop was entered last week by men with an inventory book. They measured his bed, counted his founts of type, weighed his paper stock, wrote it all down in a ledger with a Council seal. They didn't seize anything yet. They just — *counted*. Like a man counting sheep he intends to slaughter later. Every press in Vellmark is being registered, Casper. Numbered. Mapped. When the city falls, and it will fall within days, they will come back down their list with soldiers instead of clerks. First they take the presses that print pamphlets. Then they take the presses that *could* print pamphlets. Then they burn the type, because lead melts and a melted alphabet cannot argue."

"I know about the registrations."

"Do you? Do you know that my own press is on no list because I bribed the chandler to swear this cellar holds nothing but candle molds?" He turned. His face in the lamplight was hard and tired and younger than his thirty years. "I have survived four years by being small. Small press, small print runs, small lies, small risks. One ballad at a time. Two hundred copies that scatter like sparrows and leave no trail home. And you come to me asking me to become a lighthouse."

"Yes," Casper said. "That is what I'm asking."

* * *

He made tea because his hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling. There was no sugar; the siege had eaten sugar weeks ago. He poured two clay cups and sat across the composing stone from her, and for a while neither drank, and the drums came faintly through the earth, patient as rain.

"The letter," he said finally. "Who wrote it?"

"A clerk named Ivet Sarn. He served in the Council's registry. He copied the order before it was sealed — the order that sent the men to Renk's shop. He's dying now, in a hospice across the river. He sent the letter and his granddaughter together to my father's door, because my father—" She stopped. Started again. "Because twenty years ago my father printed Renk's last pamphlet under duress, and after Renk died, certain men paid Tam Yarrow to forget everything he knew. And he took the money. He took it and he stayed silent and he raised me inside that silence like a wall around a garden."

Jorun set his cup down. Something cold moved through him, not quite surprise. He had wondered, over the years, why Tam Yarrow — the finest compositor on the north bank, a man who could set a page blindfolded — had never once printed a word against the old regime while lesser men bled for less. He had assumed caution. Caution was common. Bought silence was rarer, and heavier, and it explained the look he sometimes caught in Tam's eyes when ballads about Renk were sung too near the shop.

"And now the wall has a hole in it," Jorun said.

"Now there's a child," Casper said. "Seven years old. Renk's granddaughter. Hidden in my father's type loft because there's nowhere else in Vellmark safe enough for her and nowhere else I trust. She watched men come for her grandfather. She remembers their faces better than Sarn remembers their names. So it isn't only ink we're protecting, Jorun. It's the last living witness to all of it, wrapped around a written record that names three officers who will sit in the victors' government when the drums stop. If those pages surface with their names on them, they'll burn every press on their list to find the source. And if the child surfaces—"

"She doesn't surface."

"She never surfaces again. Not until it's done."

Jorun rose and walked the length of the cellar and back. It was eleven steps each way; he had counted them a thousand times during bad nights. At the shelf of type cases he stopped and ran his thumb along the edge of the case holding fourteen-point long primer, the face his master Tam Yarrow had taught him to love first, the sturdy old letters that had set his apprenticeship pages, hymnals and handbills and one forbidden pamphlet that had ended his life above ground forever.

"If I say yes," he said, "I'm not saying yes to printing. Printing is easy; I've been printing sedition in my sleep for years. I'm saying yes to becoming the kind of man they hunt with lists instead of patrols. Registered presses get seized. Unregistered presses get *found*. And found presses get their operators hanged from the bridge with their galleys burned beneath them, as a lesson in typography for the whole city."

"I know."

"You know, but do you understand? Because there's a difference. Knowing is a thing you do with your head in daylight. Understanding happens at three in the morning when boots come down the stairs and you realize the only way out is the coal chute and you're leaving behind everything you built." He tapped the type case with one knuckle, gently, the way one taps a coffin lid. "I chose this life, Casper. Nobody forced me into the cellar. But I chose it small, and small is how I've kept breathing. What you're asking is that I stop being small."

"I'm asking you to be exact," Casper said. "One document. Set clean, proofed twice, printed well. Not a ballad, not a broadside scream. A record. Dated, named, witnessed. Something that reads like history instead of rage, because rage gets thrown away and history gets hidden in floorboards and hymnals and passed downriver in flour sacks. My father taught us both that. Words survive according to how they're dressed."

Despite himself — despite the fear sitting on his chest like a cat — Jorun almost smiled. "She sounds like him. Does she know she sounds like him?"

"She knows."

* * *

He pulled the deposition pages toward him and read them by the blue-flamed lamp, and the cellar grew colder around him as he read, or seemed to. The clerk's hand was cramped and worsening page by page, as if the man had raced his own death to finish. Names. Dates. A signature copied out in facsimile. An order for the arrest of Aldous Renk amended in another hand — the amendment reading, in effect, *do not bring him to trial.* Three names appended as executing officers. Witnesses listed beneath: a ferryman, a choir mistress, a night watchman, a bindery girl — five souls, each with an address, each with a fate that Jorun could guess at if any of the three named officers ever saw these pages.

"They'll kill the witnesses," he said. "Before anything else. Before they come for presses, they'll come for these five addresses."

"Sarn thinks some are already gone. He wrote the list anyway. He said a record should tell the whole truth even when the truth is that we were too late." Casper's jaw was tight. "He said — I wrote it down because I don't want to get it wrong — he said, *let the imperfect page stand, so long as it stands honestly.*"

Jorun looked at her across the composing stone. Ten days ago, he thought, she had been setting wedding invitations and tax notices in her father's shop, living inside the bought silence, keeping the books. Now she sat in a traitor's cellar at midnight with a dead man's testimony in her lap and murder in her eyes — no, not murder. Something older and stranger. The look of a person who had decided what she was willing to lose and found, to her own surprise, that the list was shorter than she'd feared.

"When?" he asked.

"As soon as possible. Days. Before the parade, before the historians finish their inventory, before Moll—"

"Moll." Jorun's head came up sharply. "Edvard Moll? The Council's man with the little gray notebook?"

"You know him?"

"He visited the chandler upstairs. Asked about cellar space. Asked whether I rented any." Jorun laughed, without humor. "The chandler swore I was a model tenant who kept bees. Bees, Casper. In a windowless cellar. Moll wrote it down without blinking, which frightened me more than a search would have. That man collects lies the way I collect type — sorted, sized, ready for use." He rubbed his eyes. "If Moll is sniffing at your father's shop already—"

"He's been there. He lingered over our ledgers."

"Then your clock isn't running in days. It's running in hours. Whatever you're going to do, you do it now, tonight, and you accept that half-done may have to be good enough." He pushed back from the stone and stood, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — flatter, harder, the voice he used for giving instructions in the dark. "Here's how it will be. We compose here, in this cellar, at night, a case at a time. Your father sets — and yes, he sets, because if Tam Yarrow's hands aren't on this page then it's half a lie, and I won't put out half a lie. I proof. We lock the form, we pull no more than fifty copies at a time, and the sheets leave this cellar within the hour, scattered through the channels I've spent four years building — laundries, taverns, a ferryman, choir lofts. No stockpiles. Nothing for them to find in quantity. Fifty sheets at a time, like seed, not harvest."

"And your press? If they find it—"

"They won't find it. They'll find a coal chute, an empty room, and a chandler who swears to God he rented to a beekeeper." He held out his hand across the composing stone, and Casper took it, and her grip was cold and steady. "Wary allies, then. You bring me paper — good paper, devotional stock from your father's shelves, the kind nobody questions. You tell no one, not Wren, not anyone, that this cellar exists. And Casper — " he tightened his grip once, briefly, " — if they take me, you finish it without me. Promise me that. The work matters more than the worker. That's the whole catechism of this trade."

"I promise."

"Good." He released her hand and turned to the shelf and drew down the case of long primer, feeling the familiar weight settle into his arms, forty pounds of borrowed voices. "Now go home the long way, past the tannery, and don't look back at the door. Come tomorrow night after the patrol turns at the bell. Bring the depositions. Bring your father." 

At the top of the stairs, with her hand on the door, Casper paused. "Jorun. Why did you say yes? Truly. After four years of staying small."

He considered lying, decided against it. "Because I set my first page in your father's shop," he said. "And because I've sung about Aldous Renk for four years, and a man can only sing so long before the song starts asking what he's done besides sing."

She nodded once and slipped out into the dark, and Jorun barred the door behind her and stood a long time in the blue lamplight, the case of type heavy in his arms, the drums pulsing up through the floor like a second heartbeat.

Then he set the case down, trimmed his lamp higher, and began sorting sort — and it was barely an hour later, deep in the small hours, when the knocking came again. Three knocks. A pause. Two more.

But Casper had left barely an hour ago, and she would not have doubled back on the same night, and nobody else alive knew this door.

Jorun stood very still, lamp lowered, and listened to the knocking come again — patient, coded, and wrong.
