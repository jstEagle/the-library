## 9. The First Seizure

The sound came through the morning fog the way bad news always came to Vellmark: first as a rumor, then as boots.

Casper was at the stone sink scrubbing ink from her knuckles when she heard them — not marching, exactly, but that particular cadence of soldiers who did not need to march, because no one would stop them. Six pairs of heels on wet cobbles, moving up Tanner's Row toward Halvard's. She knew the street by its sounds. She had grown up listening to it the way a sailor listens to weather: the groan of the tannery carts at dawn, the bell of Saint Ferren's tolling the half hour off-key, old Mistress Quell scolding her cat in three languages. This morning all of it had gone quiet except for the boots.

She dried her hands and moved to the front window, standing where the frame would shadow her face. Down the row, the Halvard shop stood with its shutters still closed, its painted sign — *HALVARD & SONS, FINE JOB WORK* — flaking gold above the door. A modest establishment. An honest one. Old Bertrand Halvard had printed christening invitations for half the families on the Row, and every year he set the Vellmark almanac without complaint about what the weather-sayers got wrong.

The soldiers stopped at his door. There were six of them, then four more rounding the corner from the river lane, and behind them a clerk in a grey coat carrying a ledger under his arm like a hymnal. Casper recognized the coat. It was the uniform of the Council's Commission of Records — the men who had spent the last fortnight walking from shop to shop with their inventories and their polite questions, weighing type by the pound as though words were flour.

"They're at Halvard's," she said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. Inside her chest something was moving very fast.

Behind her, in the dark of the composing room, Tam Yarrow looked up from the case he was cleaning. He had been cleaning cases all week — not because they needed it, but because his hands wanted occupation while his mind went elsewhere, and everyone in the family had learned not to ask where. Now his eyes went to the window, then to the ceiling, to the loft where Wren slept curled among the empty paper bales like a bird in a nest of rags.

"Wake her," he said quietly. "Get her down here where I can see her."

Casper took the stairs two at a time.

* * *

Wren woke slowly, the way she always did, blinking at Casper with an expression that took a moment to arrange itself into trust. Seven years old, and she had already learned that waking was the most dangerous part of the day — the moment before you remembered where you were and who you could safely be.

"What is it?" the girl whispered.

"Soldiers," Casper said. "At the neighbor's. Nothing to do with us."

It was the second lie Casper had told her that week, and both times Wren had watched her with those grave, unchildlike eyes and accepted the lie the way children accept weather: as something larger than herself, to be endured rather than argued with. But this time, as Casper lifted her down from the loft, the girl's small hands tightened around her neck.

"I heard the boots," Wren said. "Ivet said when you hear boots, you go still and count your breaths until they pass."

"Who taught you that? Your grandfather?"

"No." Wren shook her head against Casper's shoulder. "Ivet. He said Grandfather never learned to hide. He said that's why—" She stopped herself, the way she did whenever she approached the edge of the thing she had seen, whatever it was, in the house by the river where Aldous Renk had died. "He said printers think paper will protect them. He said paper only remembers. People have to do the protecting."

Downstairs, the front door of the Halvard shop opened.

They heard it even through the walls — the particular creak of Bertrand Halvard's door, which needed oiling, which had needed oiling for twenty years, which would never need oiling again after this morning. Then voices. Halvard's voice first, pitched high with a craftsman's indignation: *by what authority, sirs, I hold a license, my work is christenings and almanacs, look at the books yourselves—*

Then a soldier's voice, low and bored. Then the clerk's, smooth as poured tallow.

Tam moved to the window. Casper came to stand beside him, Wren pressed between them, and together the three of them watched through the gap in the shutter as the seizure unfolded two streets away with the terrible efficiency of something long rehearsed.

They brought out the press first.

It was a newer machine than theirs, a double-franklin bought at great expense eight years ago, and it took six soldiers working in shifts to walk it down the steps and onto a dray cart waiting in the fog. The iron feet gouged the cobblestones. Halvard stood in his doorway in his nightshirt and waistcoat, one shoe on and one off, watching his livelihood carried into the street like a body.

"The ledger," the clerk was saying, tapping his own book. "You reported twelve hundredweight of type, Master Halvard. My count says fourteen. Where is the surplus?"

"In the floor," Halvard said. "In the walls. Every printer keeps spare type in odd corners; your own commission knows it. It's pi, man, broken sorts, worn letters—"

"It is unregistered movable type," the clerk said, "which is now a matter for the tribunal." He made a note. The scratch of his pencil carried in the still air. "And the apprentices?"

"Gone home to their mothers a month past. You know that. Your office released them."

"Then we'll verify with the mothers." The clerk closed his ledger with a soft, final snap. "You'll come with us regarding the surplus, Master Halvard. It should be settled by evening."

"Settled—" Halvard laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "For surplus type? For pi?"

One of the soldiers took his arm, not roughly, which somehow made it worse. Roughness would have been an event, a thing to shout about. This was procedure.

And then the children came.

There were three of them — Halvard's grandchildren, the youngest set of sons-and-daughters his dead wife had left him, and they spilled out of the doorway in their nightclothes the way children spill anywhere, drawn by the noise, too young to read the grammar of soldiers and carts. The eldest couldn't have been more than ten. She grabbed her grandfather's free hand.

"Grandpapa, where are they taking the press?"

"Nowhere, little bird. A loan. A loan to the Council, that's all it is." He was smiling at her. Casper watched him build the smile plank by plank, a craftsman's smile, load-bearing, holding up his whole face while his eyes went over her head to the soldiers and said everything the mouth denied. "Mind the shop. Mind the shutters. Your mother will be back from her sister's by Thursday, and we'll sort this out like sensible people."

"You're crying, Grandpapa."

"Printer's dust," he said. "Bad for the eyes."

The soldier cleared his throat. Halvard bent, kissed the top of the girl's head, and let himself be walked away down Tanner's Row in one shoe and one stocking foot, his hand raised in a wave he did not turn around to complete. The girl stood in the doorway holding her brothers' hands until the fog swallowed her grandfather, and then she did the thing that Casper knew would follow her for the rest of her life: the girl reached up and straightened the crooked shop sign, *HALVARD & SONS*, squaring it carefully to the doorframe, as if orderliness itself might summon him back.

Then she took her brothers inside and closed the door that needed oiling.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The dray cart groaned away toward the river with the press chained to it, and the fog closed behind it, and Tanner's Row resumed its sounds one by one — a cart, a bell, a dog — as though the street itself were practicing innocence.

Wren had gone very still against Casper's side. Casper knelt to her level.

"You counted your breaths," she said. "Good girl."

"He didn't come back," Wren said. "You said it was nothing to do with us, and they took him anyway. In his nightshirt." Her chin trembled once, and she mastered it with visible effort, the way she mastered everything, and Casper thought: *she is seven, and she has already learned to master her own face. What did they do to her, in that house by the river, that taught her this?*

"People are taken for many reasons," Tam said from behind them. His voice was careful, level, the voice he used for quoting prices to difficult customers. "Halvard kept poor accounts. That's the truth of it. Poor accounts and a big mouth."

"That's not the truth of it, Father."

"It is close enough to the truth of it that you'd be wise to learn from it." Tam crossed the room and began, deliberately, closing the shutters — front ones, back ones, the little pane over the washroom, until the shop went dim as a cellar at noon. When he turned around, his face was grey. "Now. We are going to speak plainly, the three of us, because plain speaking has become a luxury we can no longer afford to waste. Casper. The letter. The papers you brought back from across the river. The galleys Jorun Beck has been setting in his hole like a rabbit. All of it. Today. Burned."

Casper felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when she stood up too fast. "Burned."

"Every page. The letter, the depositions, the proofs, any copy you've made, anything in Jorun's keeping that carries our smell on it. And the girl goes upriver tonight."

"To where?"

"Away. To a convent, a farm, a widow with more kindness than sense — I don't care, so long as she's on water and gone before the parade drums start. I'll pay it. I'll sell the second case if I must." He said it fast, the words tumbling over each other, and Casper realized with a strange, cold clarity that her father had rehearsed this speech. Perhaps all night. Perhaps since the boots started. "You saw what happened to Bertrand Halvard, and Halvard printed *almanacs*. Almanacs! What do you imagine they'll do to us, when they find what we've been keeping? They won't take the press, Casper. They'll take the shop, the house, you, me — and they'll take her, and they'll take her somewhere there's no coming back from, because she isn't surplus type. She's a witness. Witnesses get drowned. Ask anyone who worked for the last king."

"She's seven."

"She's evidence." The word came out of him harsh, and he flinched at his own voice, and for a moment Casper saw the war inside him laid bare: the father who had fed and sheltered and hidden this child fighting the printer who had survived thirty years in Vellmark by knowing exactly how much trouble a man could afford. "That's not how I mean it," he said, quieter. "God forgive me, that is not how I mean it. But it is how *they* will mean it, and we must think as they think if we're to keep her breathing. Casper. Look at me. Everything I have done these thirty years — every job I refused, every name I didn't print, every silence I sold — I did it so this family would be too dull to notice. Too small to bother with. And in one month you have made us the most interesting household on Tanner's Row."

"We haven't been taken," Casper said.

"Because Moll hasn't decided what we are yet." 

The name landed in the room like dropped lead. Edvard Moll, the historian, with his soft voice and his patient eyes and his ledger full of other people's lives. He had sat in this very room eleven days ago drinking tea he praised too warmly, running his finger down the Yarrow ledger, lingering — Casper could still feel it, a fingertip drawn along her spine — lingering over the entry from nine years ago, the year of Renk's death, the year of her father's bought silence. He had smiled and said the Yarrow records were *admirably complete*. Admirably complete. As if completeness itself were the crime, and he its archivist.

"Moll counts everything," Tam said. "Presses, apprentices, children. And when the Council is crowned and the parade is done, he will sit down with his inventory and reconcile it, the way clerks reconcile anything — and what doesn't reconcile, he will come for. Do you understand? Our only hope is to become a rounding error. A shop so clean and so quiet that when he reads our name, his pencil keeps moving."

"And Halvard?" Casper asked. "Was Halvard a rounding error?"

"Halvard was a lesson." Tam's hands were shaking now; he hid them in his apron. "Sent two streets away so we could watch it from the window. They chose his shop because we can hear it. Everyone on this row can hear it. That is not accident, Casper. That is arithmetic."

From the corner, small and steady, came Wren's voice.

"I can leave."

Both of them turned. The girl stood by the cold stove with her arms wrapped around herself, wearing the oversized smock Casper had cut down for her, looking older than her seven years in the way she only did when she was frightened and refusing to show it.

"If I'm the danger, I can leave. Ivet taught me the ferryman's name. He said if ever—" She swallowed. "He gave me instructions. Before he died. He said if the people hiding me became afraid, I was to say a word to the ferryman and go to the sisters at Marlow Bend, and never tell them my real name, and never—" Her voice finally broke, just once, a hairline crack in good glaze. "Never tell anyone what I remember. Even if they're kind. *Especially* if they're kind."

The silence after that was the worst yet. Casper looked at the child offering to erase herself — to bury her grandfather twice, to carry Renk's memory locked and silent in her head into some convent yard where no one would ever ask — and understood, with sickening precision, exactly what her father was proposing. Not cruelty. The opposite. Terror dressed in love's clothing, love's vocabulary, love's desperate arithmetic: *one child saved against a whole family lost.* It was the same sum the Council ran. It was the same sum Moll offered, in his soft voice, over tea.

And it was almost enough. That was the horror of it. Standing in the shuttered dimness with the ghost of Halvard's laugh still hanging in the air, Casper felt the pull of it in her own chest — *burn it, send her, be dull, survive* — and she understood that compromise never arrived looking like betrayal. It arrived looking like mercy. It arrived with one shoe on and one shoe off, smiling at its granddaughter, saying *printer's dust, little bird.*

"No," Casper said.

"Casper—"

"No. Listen to me, both of you." She heard her own voice come out level, and marveled distantly that it could, that somewhere in the last month she had grown this spine. "If we send her away tonight, she arrives at Marlow Bend tomorrow, and Moll's clerks arrive next week, because the ferryman talks, the sisters write letters, and Moll counts everything. We wouldn't be saving her. We'd just be choosing the place she's caught." She knelt, took Wren's hands. They were ice. "And if we burn the testimony, then everything Renk died for, everything Ivet crawled across that hospice floor to pass to us, everything Halvard paid for this morning with his press and maybe his life — all of it becomes nothing. Ash. And Moll writes his official history, and in it, your grandfather died a traitor, and no one — not you, not your children, no one ever — will be able to prove otherwise. Is that the safety you want to buy, Father? Because that's the price. That's what the coin purse buys. Silence forever, for everyone, including her."

"She is a child."

"She is a *record*," Casper said. "The last living one. And records aren't kept by hiding them in the safest place. They're kept by copying them. By putting them in a thousand hands. Ivet knew that. Renk died knowing that. Paper only remembers, Father — people have to do the protecting. Well then. Let's protect it properly."

Tam stood very still. Outside, muffled by the shutters, the ordinary sounds of Tanner's Row went on — and beneath them, faintly, from the direction of the river, Casper could swear she heard the first rehearsal of parade drums, or imagined she did, the rhythm of the future coming for them all.

When Tam finally spoke, his voice was hoarse. "Jorun's cellar. Is it ready? Are the pages set?"

"Three-quarters. He needs help. He needs type he trusts, and hands that know the work." Casper rose. "He needs you, Father. Both of us. Tonight."

Her father looked at her for a long moment — looked at her the way, she realized, he must have looked at Aldous Renk's final pamphlet nine years ago, seeing in it both his ruin and his one chance at a different kind of survival. Then he untied his apron with hands that had steadied, and said:

"Then we work fast. Because as of this morning, girl, we're no longer a rounding error. And Moll counts everything."

He was reaching for his coat when the knock came — three slow raps at the front door, unhurried, courteous, and through the shutter slats Casper caught the grey flash of a coat and the glint of a familiar pair of patient eyes.

Edvard Moll had come back.
