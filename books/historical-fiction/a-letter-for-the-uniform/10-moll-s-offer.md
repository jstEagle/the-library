## 10. Moll's Offer

He came at the hour when the shop was at its most honest — that grey interval after the lamps were lit but before supper, when the ink smell thickened and the day's work lay exposed on the stone like something flayed. Casper heard the knock at the front, three soft raps, courteous as a creditor's, and her hands went still over the composing stick before she understood why.

Tam opened the door himself, and she watched his shoulders drop half an inch — not relief, exactly, but the particular slump of a man greeting someone he had already surrendered to.

"Master Yarrow." Edvard Moll stepped in out of the wet, shaking rain from a coat too fine for Vellmark's weather, wool dyed the deep Council blue that had only begun appearing in the city these last weeks, the way a rash appears before the fever is named. He was younger than Casper had remembered from the ledger visit — or perhaps he had seemed older then, surrounded by soldiers and inventory lists. Alone, he was almost mild: a narrow man with a scholar's stoop and eyes the colour of pond water, patient, untroubled, seeing everything at the bottom.

"Historian." Her father's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used with customers who haggled badly.

"I was in the quarter," Moll said, as though the quarter were anywhere within four streets of his offices across the river. "I thought I would pay my respects without the escort. Soldiers make conversation difficult. They make *thinking* difficult, which I suspect is half their purpose."

Casper set down the stick. There was no reason to leave the room and every reason to, and she found herself unable to do either, so she stood among the standing type with her hands folded, wearing the expression she had perfected over ten days of hiding Wren in the loft: pleasant, empty, a face like unsized paper waiting to be printed on.

Moll glanced at her. "Your daughter keeps late hours."

"She sets type," Tam said. "Type doesn't care what hour it is."

"No," Moll agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't. It cares about accuracy. That is its whole virtue, isn't it? A press cannot lie. It can only repeat what it is given." He removed his coat but did not sit until Tam gestured him into the customer's chair by the stove, and then he sat precisely, knees together, hands on his knees, a man visiting a church of a faith he did not share but respected as an institution. "I have been thinking about presses a great deal. About what they repeat, and to whom, and how long."

"The official history," Tam said. "You said the Commission—"

"Yes. The Commission." Moll smiled faintly, as if at a child reciting a catechism. "The *Official History of the Transition*, commissioned by the Provisional Council, to be printed on registered presses under Commission supervision. Twelve hundred pages. Maps. Portraits. An index." He turned his pond-water eyes toward the hearth, where a low fire worked at a knot in the log. "I have been collecting accounts, Master Yarrow. From everyone. That is what a historian does — he collects, and then—" the smile deepened by a degree, "—he decides."

*Decides.* The word sat in the room like a coin dropped on stone.

Casper moved to the kettle, mostly to have something to do with her body. Pouring tea gave her a position near the door, near the stairs to the loft where Wren was, at this moment, meant to be reading by candlelight behind the stacked galleys of psalters. She had told the girl: if anyone comes, you are a cousin. You are asleep. You make no sound until morning. Wren had nodded with the terrible competence of children who have already survived more than their faces should hold.

Moll accepted the tea with a small bow of the head. He drank nothing. He held the cup as one holds a prop.

"Vellmark will fall within the week," he said, conversational as a man discussing weather. "Everyone knows it. The drums you hear at night are rehearsal drums. After the fall there will be a period of — let us call it adjustment. Accounts will be settled. Some of them loudly. Most quietly." He looked at Tam over the rim of the cup. "Quietly is better, I think. Don't you?"

"I think," Tam said slowly, "that a man should be careful what he agrees with in this city."

"Oh, entirely. That is why I am here privately, without escort, off any record whatsoever." Moll set the cup down. "Which brings me to a small matter. A matter of housekeeping."

Casper kept her face still. She had learned stillness from the type case itself, from the discipline of picking letter after letter without error while her heart did other things entirely. But her mouth had gone dry, and she was glad the kettle gave her reason to stand with her back half-turned.

"A child has been seen," Moll said. "In this quarter. At night. Small, seven or eight years old, dark-haired. Seen twice at your back door, Master Yarrow — once by the lamplighter, once by a woman who keeps the corner house and watches everything because her life contains nothing else worth watching." He paused, letting the silence fill with the fire's small crackling. "Children are being moved through the city just now. Relatives of persons of interest. Persons of interest is the phrase the Council uses; I confess I did not choose it. Many such children will be found and taken into care." His voice did not change at all. "Care is a broad word. Some of them will be questioned. Some of them will simply disappear into households upcountry, which is, I think, the kinder outcome, though no one asks the children which kindness they prefer."

"You've come about a child you haven't seen," Tam said, "on the word of a lamplighter."

"I've come," Moll said, "to offer you protection."

And there it was, laid on the counter between them like goods awaiting appraisal. Casper turned. She made herself turn, slowly, holding the kettle as if considering whether to refill his cup, and she looked at Edvard Moll directly for the first time since he entered.

He met her gaze with perfect calm. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in all the nights that followed — not menace, never menace, but calm. Menace would have been easier to refuse. This was the look of a man offering to carry something heavy for you, who genuinely believed you were foolish to stagger under it yourself.

"Protection," she repeated.

"Protection." He spread his hands. "Master Yarrow, I know what you were. Everyone of any consequence knows what you were — the best compositor on this bank of the river, the man who set Renk's pamphlets when Renk could barely afford paper. You were a craftsman of the old freedom, before the old freedom became inconvenient to the people who now promise a new one. You chose silence. I have read enough ledgers to recognize a purchased silence; it has a particular texture. It is very quiet, and it is very expensive, and it is paid for in perpetuity." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "I am offering to retire that debt. A household registered as loyal collaborators receives a protection certificate. No searches. No seizures. Your press remains yours. Your daughter—" the pond-water eyes touched Casper again, gently, "—remains unexamined. And the child, if there is a child, is placed somewhere safe, far from questioning, far from the settling of accounts. A farm upriver. I know such places. I have arranged such placements before. They are real. The children are alive. You may verify that in five years if you are able."

"And in exchange," Tam said.

"In exchange, the letter."

The word fell into the room and rang there. Casper felt the floor tilt beneath her, the vertigo of a name spoken aloud that had lived only in whispers and wax seals and her own sleepless memory — *the letter* — as if Moll had reached into the loft, into her own chest, and lifted it out into the lamplight.

"I don't know what you mean," Tam said.

"Of course not." Moll picked up his tea and drank, finally, delicately. "Then let me say what I mean, so we needn't play further. Somewhere in this city is a document naming officers of the incoming government in the matter of Aldous Renk's death. I do not know its contents precisely. I know it exists. I know it is dangerous — not to me, Master Yarrow; I am a historian, I am safe the way furniture is safe — but dangerous to certain men whose names I suspect appear in it, and those men are currently deciding how much of Vellmark burns during the transition, and how much is merely looted. If the document reaches them first, they will burn generously." He set the cup down with a click. "If it reaches *me* first, it goes into the official archive, sealed, catalogued, preserved. Not destroyed — I want to be honest with you, I am a historian, I do not destroy sources. But preserved in a box that no one opens for fifty years, by which time everyone named in it will be dead of ordinary causes, and the document will be a curiosity instead of a death warrant. And the family that surrendered it will be written into the account as *loyal printers of the transition*. Do you understand what that means? It means your name in twelve hundred pages. It means your shop named as a site of civic importance. It means that when the next purge comes, and there is always a next purge, some future clerk looks up Yarrow in the index and finds the word *collaborator* beside it, and moves on."

He let that sit. Outside, the rehearsal drums had started early — a muffled rolling from the parade ground, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"That is mercy," Moll said. "I want you to hear me say it plainly: this is mercy, dressed as it must be dressed, because mercy always arrives in uniform. I am not threatening you. I have not reported the lamplighter. I walked here alone in the rain, which no Council man does unless he intends to deal honestly. I am telling you that the world you live in is about to be reorganized around you whether you consent or not, and I am the only person in the new world who is offering you terms instead of presenting them."

Casper stood very still. And here was the thing she hated herself for, the thing that would keep her awake for years afterward: for one long breath, she *wanted* it.

She wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air — wholly, without philosophy. She saw it entire: the certificate on the wall, Wren fed and hidden on some upriver farm with clean sheets and enough bread, her father sleeping without that flinch he carried now like a hunched shoulder, the shop warm, the presses hers, the future legible. All she had to do was hand over a piece of paper. A piece of paper that had already ruined one family, that had arrived wrapped in fear, that she had never asked for. What was a document against a child? What was Aldous Renk, dead eight years, dead beyond any help, against a living girl asleep above her head?

The temptation did not come as weakness. That was the horror of it. It came as *sense*.

And then, beneath the sense, she heard Wren's voice from three nights ago, thin and precise in the dark of the loft: *"He told me not to forget his face. The man who carried me. He said forgetting was how they win."*

They win. Not by force — force was loud and temporary. They win by arrangement. By certificates and indexes and kind men with pond-water eyes who preserve documents in boxes no one opens. By mercy, dressed as it must be dressed.

"Master Moll," Casper said.

"Casper," Tam said softly, warningly.

She ignored him. "If the letter came into your hands — hypothetically — how would it arrive there? Would you take it from us, or would we give it?"

Moll regarded her with what looked like genuine approval. "Given," he said. "Always given. A seized document is contraband and gets destroyed in the confusion. A surrendered document enters the archive properly, with a receipt. I would write you a receipt myself." He smiled. "You see how carefully I have thought about this. I have thought about your safety more, I suspect, than you have."

"Because you're paid to think," Casper said. "That's what a historian is in a time like this. Someone paid to decide."

Something flickered behind the pond-water eyes — not anger; she suspected Moll did not permit himself anger; perhaps recognition. "Yes," he said simply. "Someone paid to decide. And I am deciding, right now, in this room, to be generous. That decision expires. Decisions always expire, Miss Yarrow. The lamplighter talks to the corner-house woman, the corner-house woman talks to a sergeant, and sergeants do not walk anywhere in the rain alone." He rose, taking up his blue coat. "I will not ask for an answer tonight. I ask only that you consider what the letter is *for*. Documents do not save the dead. Renk is dead. Whatever names that paper carries, hanging them will not re-ink one line of his pamphlets. Ask yourselves what the paper buys, and for whom, and whether the price is being paid by the same people who collect the good."

At the door he paused, coat over his arm, and looked back at the two of them — father and daughter among the standing type, lit yellow by the lamp.

"For what it's worth," he said, "and it is worth little: I believe the document is genuine. I believe the names in it are guilty. I even believe it ought, in some better world, to be read aloud in the square." He settled the coat over his shoulders. "But we do not live in that world. We live in this one, where I am the kindest thing likely to knock on your door. Good evening, Master Yarrow. Miss Yarrow."

The latch clicked. His footsteps faded into the wet street, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be and everywhere already won.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Upstairs, faint through the floorboards, came the small scuff of Wren turning a page — she was supposed to be asleep; she was reading anyway; Casper loved her for it and was terrified by it in the same instant.

"He knows," Tam said at last. His voice had gone old. "Not everything. But he knows enough that the rest is only a matter of time. Lamplighters. Corner houses. God, the whole city is a ledger now, every window an entry."

"Father." Casper set the kettle down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. "What did the letter buy us? All those years ago. When you took the money for silence. What did it buy?"

Tam looked at her across the shop — at the cases, the stone, the ancient hand-press sulking in the corner under its sheet, misreported broken these twenty years. He looked at her the way a man looks at a well he threw something into long ago and has spent every year since hoping no one would draw it up.

"It bought this," he said finally. "This exact hour. It bought us sitting here while a man offers to sell us our lives back at a fair price." He passed a hand over his face. "And it bought that child her hiding place, and you your chance — I don't know. Maybe silence buys nothing. Maybe it only *lends*, and tonight the note came due with interest." He straightened, and something moved through his face that Casper had never seen there before, or had seen only once, years ago, over a galley he would never explain: shame and craft tangled together, grief with ink on its fingers.

"Moll is right about one thing," he said quietly. "The dead can't be saved. But I set Aldous Renk's last pamphlet myself, line by line, and I have regretted every silent year since. I am done being a receipt in another man's archive." He looked toward the stairs, toward the loft, toward the girl reading forbidden by candlelight behind the psalter galleys.

"Get Jorun Beck," Tam Yarrow said. "Tonight. Tell him we'll need his cellar — and tell him his old master wants to set type again. Real type. Before Moll's generosity expires."

Above them, faintly, a page turned.
