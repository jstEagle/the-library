## 11. Setting the Truth by Night

The loft above the Yarrow print shop had been built for storing paper, not for secrets, and it betrayed them both.

By day it looked as it always had: bales of rag paper stacked in their oilcloth, the old galley racks, the dust of ten years sifting down through the gaps in the floorboards to settle on the press below like grey snow. But by night — from the hour when the curfew bells rang and the patrols' boots began their slow circuit of Inkwell Row — the loft became a country of its own, lit by a single shuttered lamp, ruled by the small iron laws of the composing stick.

Casper learned those laws again each evening as though for the first time. *Hold the stick firm. Read backward. Trust your hands more than your eyes.* Type was honest in a way people were not; a letter set upside down stayed upside down until someone corrected it, whereas a man could spend thirty years setting true words by day and burning false ones by night, and no one would ever know but himself.

Her father knew. That was the thing she was still learning to look at straight on.

They worked in shifts of three hours, because longer than that and the eyes began to lie about spacing, and because Jorun Beck insisted that exhaustion made printers sloppy and sloppiness got people hanged. He had come up through the trapdoor the first night with ink already under his fingernails and a cellar-man's stoop he hadn't had when he'd left Tam's apprenticeship nine years ago, and he had looked around the loft at the paper, the type, the lamp, and said only: "You'll want a second stick. One man can't hold measure and justify at once."

He'd been right, the way he was usually right, in the flat unglamorous way of men who had survived by noticing things.

* * *

The depositions ran to eleven pages. Casper had copied Ivet Sarn's hand into clean fair script first — she would not let Jorun or her father work from the dying clerk's original, with its water-blotted margins and the places where his pen had trailed off into nothing — and then she had broken the text into takes, the way one broke any long job.

Page one: the date, the place, the name of the witness. *I, Ivet Sarn, clerk of the Third Registry, being of sound mind and failing body...*

Page two: the order itself, dictated to Sarn in a room above the Saltgate barracks, the words of Captain Drev Halden — *see that the printer Renk is silenced, and see it is done so it cannot be traced to us* — transcribed that same night in Sarn's private cipher, because even then, three years ago, a clerk in Vellmark understood which words needed hiding.

Pages three through seven: names. Dates. The two soldiers who had actually held Aldous Renk down in his own shop while another set fire to the stock, and what became of them afterward — one promoted, one dead of drink, both of them alive long enough to talk, and one of them, Sarn wrote, *had talked, to me, weeping, twice.*

Pages eight and nine were the worst. They were the witnesses still living, listed with addresses, habits, weaknesses. A laundress on Tanner's Walk who had seen officers leaving Renk's street at an hour no officer should have been there. A ferryman. A choirboy grown now into a choirman. And at the bottom of page nine, in Sarn's smaller, more careful hand, as if the pen itself had hesitated:

*Tam Yarrow, printer, knows the contents of the final pamphlet and the circumstances of its commissioning. Has been paid for silence. Is afraid. May be reached through his shop.*

Jorun had read that line aloud the first night, flatly, without looking at either of them, and the silence afterward had lasted so long that Casper had counted the patrol boots passing below — eleven steps, twelve, thirteen — before her father said, "Set it as written."

"Tam." Jorun had turned then, and his face in the lamplight was older than his years. "It names you as bought."

"It names me truthfully," her father said. "Set it as written. If we're going to hang for this, we'll hang for the whole of it, not the pretty part."

* * *

So they set it as written, night after night, and Casper came to understand something about her father that she had never guessed in all the years of working beneath him at the case.

She had thought his silence was a wall. It was not. It was a wound, and he had been carrying it the way men carry wounds — privately, badly, with a stiffness that everyone mistook for dignity.

On the fourth night she came up the ladder early and found him already at the case, working by feel in the near-dark, and she stood on the top rung watching him without his knowing. His hands moved among the type with a tenderness she had only ever seen him give the tools themselves. He picked each letter as if it were the last of its kind. And when he reached the passage — she knew the passage by heart now, could recite it in the dark herself — where Sarn's testimony described the payment, the sum, the second meeting, the handshake in the back room of the Saltgate tavern, Tam Yarrow stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. Set down the stick. Took off his spectacles and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and then settled into the deep silent rhythm of a man weeping the way he had taught himself to weep: without sound, without permission, without witness.

Except he had a witness.

Casper climbed the last rung and did not say anything. She took the stick from the shelf where he'd laid it, chose her measure, and began setting the next take beside him, their elbows almost touching in the lamplight, the little iron voices of the type clicking under their fingers like rain on a tin roof.

After a long while her father blew his nose, put his spectacles back on, and read what she had set.

"Your spacing's loose," he said, and his voice was wrecked and steady at once, which she would remember for the rest of her life.

"Your kerning's criminal," she said.

And they went on setting the truth together, letter by letter, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and cold tea, while below them the city slept in the arms of men who believed it would sleep forever.

* * *

The problem of hiding galleys was Jorun's, and he solved it the way he solved everything: by thinking like a coward, which he said was simply thinking like a survivor.

"They're inventorying presses," he said, on the fifth night, chalking figures on the floorboards. "Moll's clerks count every case, every font, every ream. What don't they count?"

"Broken type," Casper said. "Worn type. They noted our long-s is half depleted."

"Right. And what does a pious city buy by the hundredweight this season?" He tapped the chalk against the stack of devotional stock in the corner — the sermons, the catechisms, the Lives of the Saints that formed the dull bread-and-butter of the Yarrow trade, waiting for binding.

Casper saw it before he finished the sentence, and felt the particular cold pleasure of a good solution. "Galleys inside the formes."

"Galleys locked inside the formes," Jorun agreed. "A deposition page locked up tight in a chase, faced with a sermon page on either side, packed in the middle of a signature of saintly lives. If Moll's men open the shop tomorrow, they find forty pages of Saint Brigit and the widow of Bethany. Let them read every word."

They built the hides that night. Each deposition galley went into a wooden chase, locked, then sandwiched between formes of genuine devotional matter and wrapped in waste sheets, and the whole bundle went into the center of the paper bales, wrapped in oilcloth that already smelled honestly of the storeroom. By dawn the loft looked exactly as it had looked for ten years, except that it weighed slightly more, and Casper lay in her narrow bed below listening to the first patrol pass and feeling — for the first time since the child had come to the door — something that was almost peace.

Almost. Because peace, she was learning, was just fear that had found a place to stand.

* * *

On the seventh night Wren came up.

They had kept the girl below, sleeping in the trundle behind the kitchen stove, told that the men upstairs were "mending the roof," and she had accepted the fiction with the grave courtesy children extend to adult lies they have decided not to challenge. But children wake, and children climb, and Casper came back from a bucket trip to find the girl sitting cross-legged on the floorboards beside the type case, watching her father's hands with the absolute attention of the young.

"What do the letters say," Wren asked, not looking up.

"Tonight they say that my grandfather didn't die of nothing," Tam said quietly, without stopping his work. "That he died of men, and the men have names."

"Will the names make them sorry?"

Tam's hands stilled over the case. Casper saw him weigh the question the way he now weighed everything — against the ledger of everything he had failed to weigh for thirty years.

"No," he said at last. "Sorry isn't what printing does. Printing does remembering. Being sorry is for after, if they've got the courage."

Wren considered this. Then she reached out and touched one piece of type — a lowercase e, Casper saw, worn nearly smooth — and asked if she could hold it. Tam put it in her palm and closed her fingers over it.

"That's the most important tool in the building," he told her. "One letter. All the rest is just more of that."

* * *

They finished the composition on the ninth night, near three in the morning, and stood over the last galley together — Jorun, Casper, Tam — reading it in the shuttered lamplight, proofing it against Sarn's fair copy line by line, the way you proofed anything that mattered.

It was clean. Eleven pages, every name spelled true, every date checked twice against the cipher key Sarn had provided, every comma argued over and settled. At the foot of the final page, where a document like this would normally carry a signature, there was instead the line they had agreed upon, the line Casper had fought for and won:

*Testimony recorded in progress. The witness's death prevented his final mark. This record stands unfinished, and therefore unaltered.*

"Unfinished and unaltered," Jorun murmured. "That's either very brave or very foolish."

"It's the only version I could sign myself," Casper said.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, came a sound that none of them had heard at that hour before: the knock of a fist on the shop's front door, deliberate, unhurried, official.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

Jorun snuffed the lamp with his palm. In the sudden dark, the three of them stood among the hidden galleys and the cooling type, listening, as the knock came again — patient as a historian, persistent as ink.
