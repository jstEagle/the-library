## 12. The Informer

The knock came at dawn, soft as a moth against the door.

Casper was already awake — she had been awake most of the night, her fingers still ink-stained from the loft, where three galleys of deposition lay hidden between sheets of *The Consolations of the Blessed* like knives wrapped in linen. She stood in the dark of the shop and watched through the shutter slats as Frau Ostmeyer from the chandler's shop two doors down spoke to a Council runner in the grey light. She could not hear the words, but she could read the shape of them: the woman's hands moving, pointing — not at the Yarrow door, not quite, but at the chimney above it, where smoke had risen at hours when no honest household burned its fires.

*Odd hours,* Casper thought. *Everything we do now happens at odd hours.*

The runner wrote something in a small book. Frau Ostmeyer pressed her shawl closed against the morning damp and went inside, and the runner walked on down Kessel Lane, pausing once to look up at the print shop's windows with the flat professional interest of a man counting things that might later need to be counted again.

Casper let the shutter fall and stood in the dark with her heart going hard against her ribs.

Her father came down the stairs a quarter hour later, tying his apron, and she told him in four sentences what she had seen. Tam Yarrow did not curse. He had passed beyond cursing weeks ago, into some quieter country of dread. He only nodded and said, "We burn nothing else after dark. And the girl—"

"The girl stays," Casper said. "But not here."

* * *

Wren had grown into the loft the way a plant grows into a crack — quietly, patiently, making herself part of the structure. She knew which floorboards creaked and stepped around them without being told twice. She had learned to set single lines of type under Casper's eye, her small fingers surprisingly sure among the sorts, and she could name the letters by their faces the way other children named saints. At night she slept in the nest of felt blankets behind the paper stock, and in the mornings she helped sweep the shop floor while customers came and went, playing the role of a printer's orphan niece so well that Casper sometimes forgot it was a role.

She was seven years old and she had already learned that survival was a craft, like typesetting. Casper both marveled at this and grieved it.

That morning, after the runner had gone, Casper climbed to the loft and sat with Wren among the cases, and told her plainly: someone had talked about the smoke, and soon there would be men who came with the right to open every drawer and lift every floorboard, and Wren could not be here when they did.

"Where will I go?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. That steadiness frightened Casper more than tears would have.

"To a friend of your grandfather's," Casper said. "A man who prints true things. You'll be safe there until we finish."

"Until the letter is done." It wasn't a question. Wren had heard enough whispered talk over the past weeks to understand what was being made above her sleeping head — she had even asked once whether she would be allowed to set a line of it herself, and Casper had promised her the date line, the last line on the last page. *Set in Vellmark.* Wren had repeated the words to herself for two days afterward like a prayer.

"Until the letter is done," Casper said. "And then everyone will know what happened to Aldous Renk. Everyone. His name in a thousand hands."

Wren looked at her hands, small and smudged at the fingertips with ink that never fully washed out anymore. Then she nodded, once, with the gravity of a magistrate, and began folding her blankets.

* * *

They chose the following night, a Tuesday, because Tuesday was market-eve and the checkpoints along the river road were traditionally lax while carts queued up for the dawn crossings. Tam argued for waiting; Jorun, sent word through the laundry girl who carried messages folded into wet linens, argued for going. *Presses are being pulled off the streets daily,* his note said. *Every night you wait is a night the net tightens. Send her with someone who looks boring.*

*Someone who looks boring.* Casper turned the phrase over and realized he meant her. She was twenty-six, plain-dressed, unremarkable, a woman carrying a bundle of laundry or a basket of mended goods — invisible, if she did the work of invisibility properly.

"I'll take her myself," she told her father. "No courier. No middleman. If I'm searched, I'm a seamstress delivering finished work to a cellar workshop on Tanner's Row. I can say it asleep."

Tam looked at her for a long time across the candlelit composing stone. There was something new in his face — not pride exactly, and not fear either, but some compound of the two that had been settling into him ever since the night he had set his own confession into type and wept over the case. He reached beneath the counter and brought out the coin purse, the one that had come with the child and the letter, heavy with Council silver.

"No," Casper said.

"It buys passage if you're stopped. It saves her if you're taken and she isn't."

"That money is evidence."

"It's *money*, Casper. It has been many things. Tonight let it be money." He pushed it across the stone toward her. "Your grandfather kept this press running through the plague year by bribing a quarantine officer with less than this. Survival is not always clean. I know something about that." A bitter half-smile. "I know more than I ever wanted to."

She took the purse. Its weight in her pocket was like a small cold animal.

* * *

They left at midnight, through the back, down the alley that smelled of tallow and river rot. Casper had dressed Wren in boy's clothes, her hair cropped short with the shop scissors three days earlier — a necessity, they'd told her, and the girl had sat solemnly through it, watching her own curls fall like shed feathers onto the floorboards. Now she carried a bundle of actual mending, so that if anyone looked inside the sack they'd find socks and shirt collars and nothing else.

Vellmark at midnight was a city holding its breath. The revolution had been coming for months the way weather comes — first rumor, then wind, then the smell of it — and now the Provisional Council's drums could be heard some nights across the water, practicing their victory before there was any victory to practice. The streetlamps were lit at half measure, oil being requisitioned for the war effort, and whole lanes drowned in darkness between pools of guttering light. Their footsteps sounded enormous. Casper kept Wren's hand in hers and matched their pace to a slow, unhurried walk — *boring*, she reminded herself, *be boring* — though every nerve in her body screamed to run.

The first checkpoint stood at the corner of Kessel Lane and the Bridge Road: two soldiers and a lantern, stopping a drunk cooper and turning him gently homeward. Casper saw them from a distance and turned casually down an alley she'd known since childhood, a cut-through behind the dye-works that smelled of ammonia and wet wool. It added ten minutes and cost them the lantern-light entirely; she navigated by memory, by the slope of the cobbles, by the sound of the river growing louder on their left.

"Are you afraid?" Wren whispered. Not *are we in danger* — children understood danger as weather, as something that happened around them. But *are you afraid* was a question about Casper herself, and it deserved honesty.

"Yes," Casper said. "Very much. But fear is just a feeling. It doesn't get a vote."

Wren considered this. "My grandmother used to say fear was a cat. You feed it too much and it takes over the house."

Casper laughed despite everything — a small startled sound, instantly swallowed. "Your grandmother sounds like she'd have run a fine print shop."

"She ran a bakery. She said printers were just bakers who worked with words instead of dough." A pause. "Grandfather said bakers were printers who worked with dough instead of truth."

They crossed the Bridge Road two blocks south of the checkpoint, flattening themselves against a wall while a Council patrol passed with boots that rang like hammers, and slipped into the maze of Tanner's Row.

* * *

The second checkpoint was the one Jorun hadn't known about.

It stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row itself — not the usual pair of bored soldiers but six of them, plus a sergeant with a list and a lantern, checking papers at the narrow entrance. Behind them Casper could see the dim shapes of carts being unloaded, and she understood: they weren't hunting people tonight. They were inventorying. Moll's great census of presses and paper and movable type, conducted street by street. Tanner's Row housed three cellars that printed things, and the Council wanted to know about all of them.

Casper drew Wren back into the shadow of a buttress and thought.

There was another way in — the coal chute behind Jorun's cellar opened onto a yard shared with the old rope-walk, reachable through a gate on Cordwainer Street that hung broken on its lower hinge. She'd gone out that way once before, in the dark, with galleys wrapped in oilcloth. But the chute meant leaving Wren to climb down alone into the dark while Casper stayed above to close the hatch — and worse, it meant approaching the Row from the far side, doubling back through streets where patrols moved freely, adding another hour to a night that already felt like a held breath slowly crushing her.

Or she could try the front. Walk up to the lantern and the sergeant with her forged delivery story and the coin purse burning in her pocket like a coal.

She looked down at Wren. The child was looking back at her, waiting, trusting, and in that look Casper found something she didn't expect: not weight but ballast. The girl's trust did not crush her. It steadied her, the way well-set type holds firm under the press.

"We go around," she murmured. "Hold my hand. Don't run unless I run."

* * *

The long way took them through the fish-market square, empty except for gulls picking at the day's leavings, and down Cordwainer Street where a patrol passed them so closely that Casper could smell the wet wool of a soldier's coat. She became, in those minutes, the most boring woman in Vellmark: a tired seamstress dragging a sleepy nephew home after a late errand, shoulders rounded, eyes down, mind utterly blank of anything but the next step. Fear was a cat, she told herself, and she was feeding it nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing.

At the rope-walk gate she lifted the broken hinge and eased them through into the yard, black as pitch, smelling of tar and hemp. The coal chute was where she remembered — a wooden hatch at ground level, half hidden by weeds. She pried it open and the smell of coal dust rose up, warm and stale.

"I have to go first," she whispered, "and then you follow, and I'll be right below you. Can you do that? Climb down backwards, feel for the rungs?"

"My grandfather's cellar had a ladder," Wren said. "To the loft. I climbed it when I was five."

"You're practically an expert, then."

Casper lowered herself into the dark, found the iron rungs set into the brick, and descended eight feet into the cellar's coal bin, then called softly upward. Small feet appeared in the rectangle of lesser darkness above. For one suspended moment Wren stood silhouetted against the night sky — a small, brave, ridiculous figure in boy's clothes clutching a bundle of socks — and then she climbed down, sure-footed, breathing fast but silent, and dropped the last foot into Casper's arms.

Casper held her there a beat longer than necessary. Then she pulled the hatch closed overhead, sealing them in the absolute dark together.

"Well done," she whispered.

"I wasn't scared," Wren whispered back. Then, honestly: "I was a little scared."

"A little scared is the right amount. Too little means you're not paying attention."

A scrape, a knock, a voice low through the wall: "That you, Yarrow?" And lamplight bloomed as Jorun Beck slid back the bolt of the coal-room door, squinting, unshaven, ink to his elbows as always. He took in the two of them standing in the coal dust — the woman and the child, hand in hand, gray with grit — and something moved across his face that was almost tenderness and quickly disguised itself as gruffness.

"Through the chute? In the dark?"

"There were six soldiers and a sergeant with a list at the Row's front door," Casper said. "Your street is being inventoried, Jorun. Presses counted, paper weighed. Maybe not tomorrow, maybe not this week — but soon."

Jorun exhaled slowly and rubbed a dirty hand over his face, leaving a fresh smear. "Moll's men were at the ferry landing today asking the boatmen who moves freight at night. Someone gave them my laundry girl's description." He looked at Wren, then back at Casper. "You understand what this means. We're not hiding anymore. We're racing."

"I know."

"How long until the pages are ready?"

"Four nights. Five at the outside. My father is setting the confession himself — he won't let anyone else touch that case."

Jorun nodded slowly. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had a month ago, carved by worry into something harder and more certain at once. "Then we print at your shop, on the old press, and we move the sheets the same hour they dry. Laundries, taverns, choir lofts, the ferryman. I've been mapping it." He managed something like a grin. "Hope, Yarrow. Organized hope."

He led them out of the coal room into the cellar proper, where the smuggled press stood under canvas and the smell of ink was thick enough to drink. Wren looked around with wide, grave eyes — a working cellar, a real press, the place where her grandfather's friend printed true things — and Casper watched her memorize it, the way she seemed to memorize everything now, hoarding details against whatever future was coming.

Casper stayed only long enough to warm her hands and eat the heel of bread Jorun pressed on her. Then she had to go back the way she'd come — alone now, through the dark streets, past the lanterns and the lists and the men who served them — and the thought of it should have hollowed her out.

Instead, climbing back up through the coal chute and standing a moment in the yard under the indifferent stars, she discovered something strange in herself: the fear was still there, the cat pacing every room of her, but it had stopped being in charge. Somewhere between the checkpoint and the chute, between the lie she'd worn like a coat and the child she'd carried like a torch, courage had stopped being a thing she admired in others and become a thing her own legs simply did, one cobblestone at a time.

She pulled the hatch shut and started for home through the sleeping city.

She was three streets from Kessel Lane when she saw the glow — a dull orange flicker above the rooftops ahead, too steady for firelight, too red for a lamp. She stopped in the mouth of an alley and stared, and her stomach turned slowly over as she recognized the shape of what it meant: torches. Torches and lanterns gathered in quantity, on her street, near her father's shop.

She began to run.
