## 13. Ivet Dies, the Record Lives

The hospice across the river had once been a merchant's house — Casper could see it in the bones of the place, in the wide staircases built for ladies in silk and the tall windows meant to frame a view of the water. Now the windows were grayed with grime, the silk was gone, and the grand rooms held rows of iron cots where the dying of Vellmark waited out their last weeks under wool blankets that smelled of camphor and wet stone.

She came at the hour when patrols changed shift, when the ferryman's lantern swung twice instead of once to signal a clear crossing. Jorun had taught her the signal. She carried a covered basket as though she were bringing bread to a sick aunt, which was not entirely a lie; there was bread in it, and beneath the bread, wrapped in oilcloth, the pages she needed Ivet Sarn to read.

The nun at the desk — or the woman dressed as one; in these days even charity wore disguises — looked up from her ledger with eyes that had stopped asking questions years ago.

"Third floor," she said before Casper spoke. "The clerk. You're the second visitor this week."

Casper's heart stuttered. "Who was the first?"

"A man with soft boots." The woman returned to her ledger. "He didn't go up. He sat in the parlor an hour, then left. We get such men. The dying attract them like flies to a wound."

*Soft boots.* Moll's men wore soft boots; she'd noticed it the day he'd come to the shop, the way they moved through her father's premises without a sound, reading spines of books they never opened. She climbed the stairs slowly, letting her breath steady, letting her face settle into the mild blankness of a worried niece. Fear, she had learned, was not the absence of composure but the management of it — a lesson her father had taught her without ever meaning to.

Ivet Sarn occupied a cot by the window on the third floor, in a corner where the last light of the afternoon fell across his blanket like something rationed. He was thinner than he had been ten days ago, if that were possible; the illness had gone about him like a tide going out, leaving the shape of the man visible under the skin. His hands lay on the blanket, and Casper thought: *those are clerk's hands*. Long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticles in a way no hospital could wash away, the nails trimmed close for handling paper.

His eyes found her before she reached the cot. They were clear. That frightened her more than the rest of him — the body collapsing while the mind held its post like a garrison that would not surrender until the order came.

"You came back," he said. His voice was a dry rustle. "I told you not to."

"You told me a great many things." She set the basket down and sat on the stool beside him, arranging herself as family, as a niece weary from travel. "You also told me where to find you, which suggests you hoped I wouldn't listen."

A ghost of a smile moved across his face. "Wren?"

"Safe. Eating more than her share. Drawing faces on the backs of spoiled proofs — she has a gift for it. She remembers everyone." Casper hesitated. "She asked me to tell you she kept the coin purse under her pillow. Every night."

Sarn turned his head toward the window. For a long moment he said nothing, and Casper watched his throat work, watched the discipline it cost him to keep his face still. When he spoke again his voice had dropped lower, as though the words themselves needed shelter.

"I served Aldous Renk eleven years. Did he tell you that? The letter — did it say?"

"It said you were his clerk. It said you took the depositions yourself."

"I took them down in shorthand, in my own hand, so that no compositor would ever know what passed through the shop." His fingers moved slightly on the blanket, tracing shapes — she realized he was setting type in the air, some old habit of a man who had lived among cases and galleys. "Renk trusted me with everything. Names, dates, the money accounts. And then one evening in autumn he said to me, *Ivet, if anything happens to me, the record must survive me. Not the pamphlet — anyone can write a pamphlet. The record.*" Sarn closed his eyes. "He knew. He knew what was coming, and he stayed anyway. He said a printer who runs is just a man who runs. A printer who stays is a press."

The light was failing. Casper rose and lit the small lamp on the shelf above the cot, cupping the flame against drafts, and saw the sister glance up from the corridor and look away again, granting them their shadow.

"The depositions," Casper said quietly. "There are witnesses alive. Your letter names them. But a list of names isn't testimony. If we print only the letter, Moll's historians will say it's forgery — a dead man's accusation scribbled by partisans. I need the depositions. I need them in your words, dated, witnessed."

"They're not written." Sarn's eyes opened. "They're here." He touched his temple with two fingers. "Eleven years, Mistress Yarrow. I am the file room. Bring me paper and I will empty it before this body finishes emptying itself."

---

She brought paper. She brought the good rag stock from the shop's back shelves, the heavy laid sheets Tam reserved for marriage certificates and bills of lading, because Sarn insisted — *if they find it, let it look like what it is, a document made to last* — and for three evenings she sat at his bedside with the lamp turned low and took dictation while the hospice breathed around them.

It was slow. His voice failed by degrees each night, and there were hours when she wrote nothing at all, only held his wrist between phrases and counted the pulse like a compositor counting spaces, waiting for the line to resume. But what came off his tongue, she set down exactly: dates, ranks, the names of streets, the number of steps from Renk's door to the corner where the officers' coach had waited with its lamps unlit. He remembered the color of the coach. He remembered that one of the three men had laughed — a short laugh, quickly suppressed, "the kind of laugh a man makes when he is embarrassed to be enjoying himself." He remembered that Renk had been carrying proofs of the final pamphlet, and that the papers had scattered in the street, and that afterward a soldier had gathered them up carefully, almost respectfully, and put them in the coach, because even murderers in that regime respected type.

"He understood what he was doing," Sarn said one night, in the voice of a man reporting weather. "Captain Dreyse. The youngest of the three. He stood over the body and he said, *This is how history is made, old man.* Those were his words. Write them down." Casper wrote them down. Her hand did not shake. She was surprised at that, later — that the hand could be steady while everything behind it burned.

On the fourth evening she brought the question she had been carrying since the hospice stairs.

"There was a man here. Soft boots. He sat below an hour and left. Was he looking for you, or for the pages?"

Sarn was quiet a long time. The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick.

"Both," he said at last. "Moll knows a deposition exists. He has always known — Renk told three people he was keeping a private record, and one of the three talked years ago, before any of this mattered, when it seemed like gossip. What Moll doesn't know is whether the record survived Renk. That uncertainty is the only wall between us and the soldiers, Mistress Yarrow. And walls like that don't stand forever. They stand until someone pays a neighbor enough."

"Then we finish quickly."

"We finish *truly*," Sarn corrected, with a flicker of the clerk's exactness that illness could not erode. "Quickly and truly. There is no other way worth the paper."

---

It was on the fifth night that she understood what he meant, and it cost her more than any patrol ever would.

She arrived past curfew, having crossed the river in a coal barge whose skipper owed Jorun a favor older than either of them, and found Sarn propped higher than usual, his breath whistling faintly, his eyes too bright. The sister met her at the stairhead.

"Tonight," the woman said simply. "If there are things to say, say them."

Casper sat. She spread the pages — nine sheets now, dense with her careful hand — and began, as she had begun every night, by reading back the last passages for his correction. Sarn listened with his eyes closed, nodding, occasionally lifting one finger to halt her: *"Not the fourteenth. The twelfth of Frostmonth. Check the almanac if you doubt me."* She checked; he was right. He was always right. Eleven years of keeping another man's record had made his memory into an instrument that nothing, apparently, could knock out of true.

Then, near midnight, he said: "The last page. There must be a last page. My attestation. *I, Ivet Sarn, clerk to Aldous Renk, do swear that the foregoing was taken from my own knowledge and my own hand—*" He stopped. Coughed — a deep, wet sound that made the sister stir at the far end of the ward. When he could speak again, the brightness in his eyes had dimmed to something steadier and sadder.

"Fetch the sister. A signature needs a witness."

The sister came. She brought ink — real ink, from somewhere; Casper did not ask — and held the lamp while Casper guided the pen into Sarn's fingers. He signed the ninth sheet. *Ivet Sarn*, in a hand that trembled like a plucked string but was unmistakably a clerk's hand, trained across thousands of ledgers. The sister signed beneath it. Casper signed beneath her.

"One page remains," Sarn whispered. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll have strength. Set the case tonight — you printers, you understand — set the case, and I'll fill it tomorrow."

She promised. She walked down through the sleeping ward and out past the ledger-keeper, who marked the hour without looking up, and crossed the black river with the depositions against her chest like a second heartbeat, and she did not sleep.

Because she had understood, sitting at that cot, what the morning would bring. She had watched men die before — Vellmark was full of dying, these days — and she knew the arithmetic of breath. Sarn would not see tomorrow's dusk. Whatever he meant to give her on the tenth page, he might give it at dawn, or he might not give it at all. And the tenth page was the keystone: his sworn statement binding the nine sheets together, the sentence that transformed a folder of notes into testimony.

*Set the case tonight,* he had said. And the thought arrived then, quiet and reasonable, wearing the clothes of prudence: *set the whole case tonight. Compose the attestation in his voice, in his words — he dictated the formula himself, you have it verbatim — and tomorrow, if his hand fails, guide the pen through it. He intends to sign it. You would only be printing what he already said.*

No one would ever know. The sister would hold the lamp. The ink would dry. And the record would be whole, unassailable, finished — safe from every historian's quill, every charge of forgery, every sneer of *unsigned draft, hearsay, partisan invention*. One forged stroke, made in mercy, made in a dying man's own intended words, and the truth would wear armor for a hundred years.

She sat up in the loft until the candles burned to pools, Wren asleep in the nest of blankets behind her, and turned the idea over the way she had once turned counterfeit coins in the shop's drawer, feeling for the wrongness in the weight.

And the wrongness was everywhere, once she let herself feel for it.

Because the record was not armor. That was the whole of it — the thing Renk had died understanding and Sarn was dying to teach her. The record was not a weapon to be sharpened, not a wall to be thickened, not a document engineered to survive cross-examination. It was a *witness*. And a witness who tells one lie — even a small one, even a loving one, even a lie the witness himself approved in advance — is a witness who can be broken on the stand forever. Moll would not attack the nine sheets; he would hunt the tenth. Men like Moll did not waste themselves on fortresses; they looked for the single stone laid by the wrong hand. And if he found it — if some clerk in some future archive matched the ink, the pressure, the angle of a pen guided by a living hand over a dying one — then every true word in the nine sheets would fall with it. She would have traded the entire record for the appearance of completeness.

*We finish truly.* He had said it knowing he might not live to finish at all.

She snuffed the candle. In the dark, Wren stirred and murmured something — a name, perhaps, or a face being drawn somewhere behind closed eyes — and Casper lay down beside the child and made her decision the way she set type: one character at a time, chosen deliberately, locked into the line.

---

Sarn lasted two days.

They were strange days, suspended ones — curfew extended after some trouble at the granaries, ferries halted, the city holding its breath — and Casper spent them crossing and recrossing a river that had become a border, carrying bread baskets full of type-high truths. On the first day Sarn was lucid in the morning and gone by noon, sunk in a fever-haze where he called out figures from ledgers thirty years closed, correcting entries no one had asked about. On the second day he surfaced near dawn, found her already sitting at the cot — she had not left; the sister had stopped pretending not to notice — and looked at the paper in her lap with an expression she would carry the rest of her life.

"You didn't write it," he said.

"No."

"I told you the formula. You could have set it. No one—" He coughed, long and terrible, and she held the cup to his lips and waited. When he could speak, his voice was smaller, but his eyes had cleared all the way to the bottom. "No one would have known."

"You would have known," Casper said. "That's the same thing."

For a moment something moved through his ruined face that was not pain. He lifted one hand — those ink-stained, long-fingered, faithful hands — and rested it on the ninth sheet, on his own trembling signature, as a man rests his hand on the shoulder of a son.

"Renk used to say," he whispered, "that a printer's honesty lives in the smallest things. Not in the big brave pamphlets. In whether she sets the comma the author actually wrote, when no reader alive would notice." His breath caught. "You would have noticed. That's why I gave it to you and not to the committees, not to the exiles, not to any of the loud brave people. The loud brave people would have improved it."

He dictated the tenth page that morning, in fragments, with rests between sentences during which Casper held his wrist and counted and waited, and the sister held the lamp, and outside the window the river went on moving under the patrol boats, indifferent, patient, keeping its own long record of everything. He got as far as the middle — *and I attest further that the witnesses named herein were examined by me personally, in the year—* — and there he stopped, and looked at the window, and said, in a tone of mild professional regret, as though noting a delivery that would arrive late:

"I shan't finish this today."

"No," Casper said. "But it's set. In type. Waiting for your words. Nothing else goes in that space — not mine, not anyone's. It waits for you."

He smiled then, fully, and turned his face toward the light.

He died that evening, between vespers and dark, with the sister's hand in his and the river turning silver and black beyond the glass. Casper was on the stairs when it happened, sent down to warm broth he would never drink, and she learned it from the sudden quality of silence that rose through the old house — a silence with a specific gravity, the silence of a line that has just been locked and will never be unlocked again.

---

She printed the tenth page three nights later, on the old Albion press in the back room, alone, by one shaded lamp.

She printed it exactly as it stood: nine complete sheets of sworn testimony, and the tenth unfinished, ending mid-sentence in the year of the examinations, followed by a line she composed herself and set in smaller type, centered, like an epitaph:

*Herein the attestation of Ivet Sarn, clerk, ends as he left it — unwritten, not unsaid. The remainder died with him. This record claims nothing more than it can prove, and proves everything it claims.*

Below that: *Testimony in progress. Taken down from the dictation of Ivet Sarn on five successive evenings, witnessed. Unsigned pages follow his signed pages. Let the reader judge which is the stronger document — the one made perfect, or the one made honest.*

Jorun, when she showed him the proof, read it twice and then looked at her for a long time across the cellar where his press stood among the sacks and barrels.

"Moll will use it against us," he said at last. "An unfinished page. He'll stand in front of the Council and wave it like a torn flag. *See? Even their martyrs couldn't finish.*"

"Yes."

"And you won't fill it in."

"No."

Jorun nodded slowly, and something in his face shifted — the wariness thinning into respect, the respect settling into something like relief, as though he had been waiting days for evidence that the person he was risking his neck beside was worth the risk. "My grandmother used to say," he offered, "that a lie in a good cause is still a lie — it's just a lie with better manners. She was hanged for less wise opinions than that." He squared the proof on the composing stone. "All right. Nine and a fraction. We print it as it stands. God help the historian who tries to correct it."

They worked until dawn setting the first full forme. And it was as she locked the chase — the cold iron biting her palms, the type standing solid and true, every comma where a dying man had actually placed it — that she heard footsteps on the stairs above, rapid ones, and then Jorun's apprentice bursting down into the lamplight, white-faced, coat half-buttoned, gasping the words that ended the strange suspended peace of those weeks:

"Soldiers at Yarrow's shop. They've taken your father."
