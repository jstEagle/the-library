## 14. Tam Is Taken

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was the first wrong thing. Council officers did not knock. They arrived like weather, all at once and without apology, and they came at night, when a man's household was scattered and slow. This knock was polite. Three measured raps, the knuckle not the fist, and Tam Yarrow stood in the middle of his own workshop with a composing stick still warm in his hand and understood, with the terrible clarity that sometimes visits cowards a moment before everyone else, exactly what politeness meant.

"Caspar," he said, too loudly, to the stairwell. "Go up. Take Wren with you."

She appeared anyway — she always appeared; obedience was a language he had never taught her well — with her sleeves rolled and ink to the second knuckle, and her eyes went from him to the door and back again, reading him the way she read a page of type, quickly and entirely.

"Tam Yarrow?" The man on the step was young, uniformed, and wore the grey-and-green of the Provisional Council's civil bureau rather than the black of the military patrols. Behind him stood two others and, at the kerb, a covered wagon with its bench cleared of everything but shackles.

"I am he."

"By order of the Bureau of Restitution, you are remanded for examination of debts outstanding to the Crown of the old regime." The officer read it from a paper, and Tam heard the words as one hears a tune played slightly wrong — right notes, wrong key. *Restitution.* He had printed enough official notices in thirty years to know that the ugliest work always dressed itself in the cleanest Latin.

"Debts," Casper said from the stairs. "What debts?"

"That will be established at examination." The young officer had the grace not to look at her. "He may bring personal effects. Paper and ink will be provided."

Paper and ink would be provided. So they meant to keep him long enough for writing to matter — a deposition, perhaps, or a confession, or a list. Tam set down the composing stick with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit them, and went upstairs past his daughter without meeting her eyes because if he met them he would say something true, and truth, in this house, had always been the one commodity they could not afford.

Wren was in the loft, folding devotional sheets into quarters with the solemn efficiency of a child who had learned too early that work was shelter. She looked up at him and he saw Aldous Renk in the set of her mouth — the man he had helped kill with silence — and he knelt, joints cracking, and kissed the top of her head where the hair smelled of dust and lamp oil.

"Mind Casper," he said. "Do exactly what she says. Even the things she doesn't explain."

"Are you going away?"

"For a little while." He smiled, and knew it was the wrong smile, the one that told children everything. "I'll leave you something to set. The psalm of the prisoner. You know the one — *out of the depths.*"

"Out of the depths I cry to thee," she recited, obedient, and he rose and went down before his voice could betray him, because a coward learns early that his voice is the first thing to break.

* * *

They took him through streets still wet from the night's rain, past shuttered shops and the boarded windows of the Halvard house, where someone had chalked a crude crown crossed out on the boards. The city was holding its breath; everyone could feel the parade drums coming the way animals feel weather. A woman selling chestnuts looked at the wagon and then carefully at her brazier, deciding with visible effort that she had seen nothing. That was Vellmark now: a whole city practicing blindness, and Tam its most accomplished practitioner.

The Bureau of Restitution occupied what had been the customs house, and its halls smelled of wet plaster and cold tea. They put him in a room with a table, two chairs, a clerk with a ledger, and a window high enough to show only sky, which Tam recognized as deliberate — no rooftops, no river, nothing by which to mark an hour. Timelessness was an instrument. He had seen it used on others, once, years ago, through the glass wall of a different office, and had been grateful even then that it was not being used on him.

"Tamuel Yarrow," the clerk began, "proprietor, print works, Candle Lane. Registered under the old regime, license renewed—" and so forth, the biography recited back to him like charges, each neutral fact laid out as evidence that he existed and could therefore be taken apart.

Then the questions began, and they were not about debts at all.

They were about correspondence. About letters received in the last season. About visitors after curfew hours. About whether a child had been observed at the premises on Candle Lane.

"No child," Tam said.

The examiner — a soft-voiced man named Corvel, who never raised his eyes above the ledger — turned a page. "Your daughter is twenty-four. We are given to understand she is not the child in question."

"Then there is no question."

"There is a report," Corvel said mildly, "of lamplight at irregular hours. Of a face at a rear window. Small. Young."

"Apprentices are small. My last apprentice was five feet nothing and forty years old, but the lamplight made him look young. Ink preserves a man oddly."

Corvel wrote this down, which frightened Tam more than any threat, because men who write down your jokes intend to use them later. The questions circled, patient as flies. Had he known Aldous Renk? Yes — everyone in the trade had known Renk. When had he last seen him? Nine years ago, give or take. What had Renk printed last? Tam felt the old scar tissue flex around the lie he had maintained for nine years, the lie he had purchased at a price he had never let himself count in full.

"A pamphlet," he said. "On grain tithes. Nothing seditious in it. I checked every line myself."

"You checked every line yourself," Corvel repeated, and wrote it, and Tam understood he had just signed something. Every line. Himself. As though a man who checked every line could later claim ignorance of what the lines contained.

They kept him until the light through the high window went from white to gold to nothing, and then a soldier brought him bread and salt fish and left him alone with the sound of his own pulse, and Tam sat in the dark and did the arithmetic he had avoided for nine years.

* * *

Nine years ago there had been rain too, and a man standing in his workshop dripping on the floorboards, wild-eyed, smelling of fear and cheap tobacco, holding a manuscript wrapped in oilcloth.

"They'll burn me, Tam. Not the pamphlet — me. I've seen the order drafted. Moll has it in his cabinet already, waiting only for a pretext." Aldous Renk had laughed, and the laugh had been worse than weeping. "A pretext. Do you know how cheap pretexts are now? Cheaper than type."

"Give it to someone else."

"To whom? Name me a printer in this city who isn't already watched, or bought, or frightened. You're the only press left that Moll hasn't bought — yet. And that's why I'm here, Tam. Because you're the last honest press in Vellmark." He had said it with admiration, and Tam had felt the admiration like a knife between the ribs, because honesty was precisely the thing he had been spending for twenty years, a coin at a time, printing what was safe and declining what was true until the word *no* had worn smooth in his mouth from use.

"I have a family," Tam had said.

"I know. I'm asking you to spend them. There's a difference between risking a thing and spending it, and I know the difference, God help me, better than anyone alive." Renk had put the oilcloth bundle on the composing stone between them like a wager. "Print it small. Print it plain. No name on it but mine. If it costs you, I'll say publicly, before witnesses, that I forced you — that you refused and I threatened you. I'll make myself your villain. It's the last useful thing my name can do."

And Tam — Tam had done neither yes nor no. That was the truth he had never spoken aloud, the truth that sat in him like a swallowed stone. He had not refused. He had not agreed. He had said *let me think*, and Renk had nodded, and gone out into the rain, and three days later the soldiers had come for Renk instead, and the pamphlet had burned in Renk's own press room with the man, some said, not far behind.

Except that was not quite the truth either. The full truth was this: two days after Renk left his shop, Tam had gone to the offices of the Provisional Council's precursor — to the man who was then merely a rising administrator, Edvard Moll, thin and courteous and already smelling of the future — and asked, in the careful language of a tradesman, whether there might be work for a discreet printer. Whether discretion, properly placed, might be worth something.

Moll had received him with tea and no surprise at all, which Tam should have understood immediately: Moll had expected him. Perhaps had counted on him. Perhaps the whole visit by Renk had been known before Renk made it.

"You've read the manuscript," Moll had said.

"I've read it."

"And?"

"It's treason."

"It's arithmetic," Moll had said pleasantly. "But yes. Treason. And you will tell me, won't you, everything about how it came to you — the hour, the manner, the state of the man — because testimony from a respected printer is worth ten informers." And then, seeing Tam's face, he had added, gently, the hook going in: "There will be compensation. Discretion is a trade good like any other. And your daughter is what — fourteen? Fourteen is an expensive age. Schools, clothes, a future. The old regime is generous to its friends, Master Yarrow, and memorable to its enemies. Which would you rather be remembered as?"

So Tam had testified. Not to the murder — the murder had not happened yet, and he told himself, for nine years, that this mattered, that there was a moral distance between informing on a pamphlet and informing on a life — but to everything else: the hour, the manner, the desperation. His testimony had been the pretext. He had handed them the pretext, gift-wrapped in respectability, and four days later Aldous Renk was dead in a cellar off Tanner's Row and the pamphlet was ash, and Tam had come home with a purse heavy enough to pay for Casper's schooling, her clothes, her future, and had put it on the kitchen table without a word, and Casper's mother had looked at the purse and then at his face and had asked no questions either, and so the silence had become a family heirloom, passed hand to hand like a christening cup.

He had told himself for nine years that he had not killed Renk. Sitting in the dark of the Bureau's holding room, listening to the building settle and a guard cough somewhere down the corridor, Tam finally admitted what he had known since the night Casper stood in his workshop with the sealed letter open between them: that he had killed Renk the way a man kills by opening a gate. The blade had belonged to another hand. The opening had been his.

* * *

They moved him after three days to a cell proper — narrow, clean, a plank bed, the high window now barred — and the questions continued, and changed.

Corvel was replaced by a man who did not introduce himself, who wore civilian grey over a soldier's posture, and who began, on the fourth morning, not with debts or letters but with a single sentence:

"Master Yarrow. Your daughter is hiding a witness."

Tam had rehearsed this. He had spent three nights rehearsing it, lying on the plank bed, composing and recomposing his answers the way he once composed lines of type — checking each word for weakness, kerning his silences. He had decided long before this morning that whatever else he failed at, whatever else his cowardice cost, it would not cost the girl. Renk's granddaughter. The child with the psalm on her tongue. Whatever the price of silence was now, it had gone up since nine years ago, and he found — with a kind of astonished grief — that he was willing to pay it.

"I don't discuss my daughter's affairs."

"She was seen. At your shop. After curfew. A small face at a rear window." The man folded his hands. "We also know a letter reached your premises in autumn, under seal. We do not have the letter. We believe you do."

"You believe wrongly."

"Do you know what happens to printers who obstruct the Bureau?" The man's voice remained conversational, almost bored. "Nothing dramatic. Your license lapses. Your stock is inventoried as restitution. Your daughter loses her trade and acquires a file. Files follow people in Vellmark now, Master Yarrow. Files are forever. Whereas cooperation—" he spread his hands— "cooperation is forgotten by everyone, including us. It is the only mercy this regime offers: anonymity. Most men find, once they taste it, that they prefer it."

Most men. Yes. Tam thought of himself at forty, crossing the square to Moll's office with his hat in his hands, preferring anonymity, purchasing it, wearing it like a good coat for nine years.

"I am an old man," Tam said. "My memory is failing. Ask me about typefaces. Ask me about the flood of '41. But ask me nothing about papers, because I will tell you nothing true, and I imagine you'd rather have my silence than my lies."

The man studied him for a long moment, and then did the thing that Tam feared far more than shouting: he smiled, and gathered his papers, and stood.

"We'll speak again," he said. "Take the time to consider. Consider especially what your silence purchases for *her*. You are protecting a letter. She is protecting a child. Only one of those protections can survive an examination." He paused at the door. "Oh — and Master Yarrow. There is a historian who has asked after you. Moll. He remembers you fondly, he says. He may come himself."

* * *

That night Tam lay on the plank bed and did not sleep, and found, strangely, that he was not afraid.

Or not afraid for himself — that was the discovery, the one that kept surfacing in him like a body in flooded ground. All his life his fear had been for himself first, and his family second, and the world not at all; that ordering had seemed natural, even virtuous, *a man provides*. But lying in the dark with the bars' shadow printed across the ceiling — he could not help reading them as type, cast enormous, a line of rules and dividers — he tested himself the way he tested a forme, pressing here and there for give, and found that everywhere he pressed the answer came back the same: not the letter, not the names, not the girl.

Let them keep him. Let them file him. A file was a kind of record too, and he had earned a place in one.

He thought of Casper setting type by lamplight — he knew she was doing it, knew it as certainly as he knew the weight of his own hands; she had her mother's stubbornness and his craft and neither of their cowardice, or rather she had his cowardice and had chosen, deliberately, daily, to outvote it — and he thought of the pages she must be composing somewhere above the city, in a loft or a cellar, the depositions, the names, the dates, and he thought: *let mine be among them.* Not the bought years. The other thing. This thing, small and late and insufficient: an old printer in a cell, saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing, until the silence itself became the last line of type he ever set.

In the morning they brought him bread and water and, with the bread, a slip of paper he was not supposed to see — a clerk's error, or perhaps not an error. He read it twice, committed it to memory, and ate it.

It was in Casper's hand, disguised in a borrowed script, and it said only: *Father. Hold fast. We hold fast. — C.*

Hold fast. Printer's term. *Hold fast* — what you say to the apprentice gripping the tympan, what you say before the pull of the bar, when the whole forme is locked and ready and nothing can be corrected anymore.

Tam Yarrow sat down on the plank bed in the light of the high barred window and wept, quietly, for the first time in nine years — not in despair, he realized, but in something adjacent to joy — because his daughter had sent him a compositor's blessing, and because for the first time in his life he deserved it.

He did not hear the boots in the corridor, nor the key in the lock, nor the voice that said, courteously, "Master Yarrow — an old friend is here to see you," and then, with the particular pleasure of a man keeping a promise kept too long, "You remember Historian Moll."
