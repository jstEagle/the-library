## 15. Bargaining with a Corrupt Man

The summons came the way winter came to Vellmark — not all at once, but in a slow graying of the light until one morning you woke and could no longer remember what warmth had felt like.

It was a printed card, which Casper found almost funny. The Provisional Council, in its hunger for legitimacy, had discovered typography. *Tammas Yarrow is held at the Citadel on matters of debt and inquiry. He is comfortable. He will remain so at the Council's discretion.* No signature. No seal beyond the cheap stamped eagle that had begun appearing on every official document since the fall of the old magistracy — an eagle drawn by some committee, its wings too short for any sky.

*At the Council's discretion.* She read it four times at the kitchen table while the shop lay dark behind her and Wren slept upstairs in the type loft, curled among the empty cases like a kitten in a drawer of wool. Four times, and each time the words rearranged themselves into their true sentence: *We have him. We know why we have him.*

She had not cried when they took him. Two officers had come at dusk, polite as tax assessors, and Tam had gone with them without argument, pausing only at the door to look back at her — and she would carry that look for the rest of her life, whatever length of life remained. It had not been fear. It had been instruction. *Say nothing. Finish it.* A coward's whole biography reversed in a single glance, spent like a coin he'd been saving all his years.

Now she sat alone with the card, and outside the window Vellmark went about its frightened morning: the fish carts rolling early before the patrols thickened, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys because everyone had learned to burn quietly, a drum corps practicing on the far quay for the victory parade that grew louder in rumor every day. The city was holding its breath between regimes, and in that held breath, men like Edvard Moll did their best work.

She should feel nothing but hatred for him. That was what she told herself as she dressed, as she took down her good coat, as she counted out the coins from Ivet Sarn's purse one final time and felt their cold weight in her palm — thirty-one pieces of silver, more or less, the exact sum a dying clerk had trusted to a child's small hands. But what she felt instead, pressing the purse flat against her ribs beneath her coat, was something worse than hatred. It was recognition. Because she was about to do what her father had done. She was about to buy silence.

The only question — the question she turned over through every street between the shop and the Citadel — was whose silence, and how much it would cost, and who would be left holding the shame when the transaction closed.

* * *

They kept Tam in a room that was not a cell, and this was Moll's first message. Casper understood it the moment the guard walked her up two flights of stairs instead of down into the stone. Cells were for people the Council meant to punish. Rooms were for people the Council meant to use.

Edvard Moll rose from behind a plain table when she entered, and she had to give him credit for his craft: he looked genuinely glad to see her. He was a soft-bodied man with a scholar's stoop and a face built for sympathy, the kind of face that made confessions feel like conversations. He wore ink stains on his right middle finger — real ones, earned — and he gestured her toward a chair with the courtesy of a man receiving a colleague.

"Mistress Yarrow. Thank you for coming. Though of course," and here the smile tilted, "you had little choice. Sit, please. Tea?"

"No."

"Tea," he said anyway, and poured two cups, and set one near her hand as if it were an offering at a shrine. "Your father sends his regards. He's well. He sleeps well, actually — better than he ever did at home, I suspect. Guilt is a poor mattress."

"You've questioned him three days."

"I've *talked* with him three days." Moll settled back into his chair, folding his hands over his stomach. "There's a difference, and your father knows it, which is why he's still comfortable. He hasn't given me a single thing worth having. Not a name, not a date, not so much as the contents of his own ledger." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Which tells me everything, of course. An innocent man has nothing to protect. Your father protects *something*, Mistress Yarrow, with the discipline of a monk. And monks, as history teaches us, are always protecting either God or gold."

Casper kept her eyes on the tea. Steam rose off it in a thin gray thread. Somewhere below them, boots rang on flagstones; the Citadel had never been quiet, even under the old magistrates, even when her father had delivered pamphlets to its door as a young man, back when printing truth was merely dangerous instead of fatal.

"What do you want?" she said.

"Straight to business. Good. I admire that in printers — you're the only tradesmen who understand that every word costs exactly what it costs." He leaned forward. "I want what I wanted when I visited your shop. Cooperation. Order. A clean account. The Council is about to be not a rebellion but a *government*, and governments require histories, and histories require sources. Your father is a source. You are a source. The child—"

"There is no child."

"—the child who was seen being carried through your courtyard by lamplight on the night of the twelfth," Moll continued, without heat, without hurry, as though correcting a misprint, "is also a source. Or evidence. Depending on how things go."

The word landed in her chest like a fist. *Seen.* Someone had seen. The neighbor with the smoke complaint, or someone else entirely, someone she had never thought to count. She let none of it reach her face. Her father had taught her that much across ten years of burning letters: the face is a press plate, and whatever you set upon it stays there.

"My father owes debts," she said. "Old debts. Everyone in Vellmark owes old debts. Take the shop's earnings against them. Take the stock. He'll sign anything you put before him saying so. Then let him go."

"Oh, I intend to take things," Moll said pleasantly. "I intend to take the letter you're hiding. The testimony. Whatever Aldous Renk's people assembled before we closed their mouths. I intend to take it, catalog it, and bury it inside an official history so thorough and so dull that within a generation no one will believe Renk was ever murdered at all. He'll have died of a fever. There will be a plaque." He sipped his tea. "That's what historians are *for*, mistress. Not the past. The future. We decide what it remembers."

"And me? What do I get for helping you decide?"

He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was the most frightening thing yet, because it meant he believed she was already his.

"You get your father. You get the child delivered somewhere safe — truly safe, I keep my arrangements, ask anyone who has dealt with me. You get your name in the acknowledgments of the official account, which will outlast every ballad and every pamphlet ever run off a seditious cellar press. And you get—" he spread his hands, encompassing the room, the Citadel, the gray city beyond the window, "—to survive. Which is not nothing. In ten days it will be everything. The parade comes, the seizures begin in earnest, and every unregistered press in Vellmark goes to the foundry. Including, I'm sorry to say, that lovely old hand-press of yours that the inventory clerks somehow marked *broken*." His eyes flickered, just slightly, and Casper felt the floor tilt under her: he knew. He knew the press was not broken. The misreport had been his, a trap laid months ago, waiting to see who crept down at night to use a dead machine. "Bring me the letter, and none of that machinery ever needs to wake."

* * *

She asked for time. He gave her until evening, because generous men make better traps. Then a guard walked her down through the Citadel's corridors, past rooms where other people were being talked with, and out into a day that seemed obscenely bright.

She did not go home. She went to the river.

The coin purse sat in her hands on the ferry bench as the waterman poled them across the brown current, and she stared at it the way her father must once have stared at the payment he took after Renk died — money that was not really money but a verdict, a price list, a receipt for a soul. Thirty-one coins. Ivet Sarn had saved them from his dying wages to fund a child's escape, and now Casper Yarrow was going to spend them purchasing a lie.

Because that was the shape of it, and she had worked the shape out on the ferry with a cold clarity that scared her. She could not save everything. Jorun's cellar was compromised or soon would be; the master galleys were hidden but fragile; Wren could not be moved twice in one week without someone noticing. And Tam — Tam was the hinge. If they broke him, or worse, if they simply *kept* him, they owned her. So she would trade. Not the letter — never the letter — but a letter. A decoy, written in her father's hand, in her father's careful style, naming names that were almost right and dates that were almost wrong, a document plausible enough to satisfy Moll and poisoned enough to ruin him if he acted on it. Her father, she realized with a strange hot pride, would compose it beautifully. He had spent twenty years learning what false words looked like. He was the finest forger of honesty in Vellmark.

And the purse would go with it. Let Moll weigh those coins in his palm and believe he had bought the last of Sarn's evidence along with the Yarrow silence. Corrupt men trusted transactions; it was the one faith they had. Give him a transaction, and he would stop looking for a conspiracy.

The waterman glanced back at her. "Rough crossing, miss?"

"The smoothest I'll ever make," she said, and paid him with one of Sarn's coins, and watched the river take it — no, she caught herself, she hadn't thrown it, though for one wild moment she had wanted to, wanted to hurl the whole purse into the current and let the truth sink where no historian could catalogue it. But truth didn't work that way. Truth was type: worthless locked in the case, world-moving once it was set and pulled and distributed. The coins were just lead. She could melt them down whenever she chose.

* * *

Moll received her at dusk, in the same room, and she brought the parcel wrapped in oilcloth the way important things are always wrapped, and she watched his hands as he untied it, and his hands trembled — just barely, just at the fingertips — because corrupt men tremble at the moment of acquisition the way honest men tremble at confession.

Inside: the purse, and the letter.

She had written it herself, in a borrowed hand, over two days of terror and craft. It named three officers — two dead already, untraceable, and one, Captain Vess, who was real enough to check but positioned in the document as a witness rather than a murderer, so that pursuing him would only unravel Moll's own certainty. It named dates scattered across two years. It named Aldous Renk with reverence and got his death subtly wrong — a knife instead of the rope, a Tuesday instead of the Thursday that lived in Wren's memory like a scar. Everything in it was almost true, and everything in it was a hook.

Moll read it twice. She saw the moment the poison entered him: his brow smoothed, his lips moved faintly around *Vess*, and she understood that somewhere in the back of his mind a rival was already being promoted into an enemy. He would verify. Verification would consume him. Historians, she thought, could always be defeated with a better footnoted lie.

"This is everything?" he said. "The original testimony, the depositions — this is the whole of it?"

"It's the whole of what my father kept," Casper said. "He wasn't brave. He was paid to forget, and he forgot very well. What survived, survived by accident, in an envelope he couldn't bring himself to open." She let her voice crack on the last words, and it was not entirely acting; grief was cheap and abundant that week, and she spent it freely. "You have it now. All of it. My father signs your debt papers tonight, and we walk out of here together."

Moll looked at her for a long moment, and she met his gaze with the flat, tired honesty of a woman who has surrendered, and she thought: *this is what my father learned to do, this exact thing, this wearing of the bought face,* and she hated him for teaching it to her and loved him for making it survivable and could not separate the two feelings, not then, perhaps not ever.

"You understand," Moll said softly, "that if this is incomplete — if so much as a single true page surfaces later — the arrangement dissolves. And arrangements dissolving are very hard on families."

"Then pray I'm a good liar," Casper said, "because I've certainly had practice watching one."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, or its cousin — and then he rang the bell, and the paperwork came, and Tam signed it with a hand that shook only once, at the signature, the way a man's hand shakes when he has just been resurrected and isn't sure he deserves the terms.

They walked out of the Citadel side by side, father and daughter, into streets already darkening, past patrol fires and shuttered windows, and neither spoke until the bridge, until the river was under them and the Citadel was a lit hulk at their backs.

"He believed it," Tam said at last. His voice was raw from three days of disuse.

"Yes."

"And the galleys—"

"Safe. Jorun has them. And the old press wakes tomorrow night." She stopped at the center of the span, where the black water slid below, and finally let herself shake, the delayed trembling of the whole long day. "Papa. I sold a lie today and it worked, and I can't wash the feeling off my hands. Is it always like this?"

Tam looked at her, and in the lantern light his face was older than the city, and wet-eyed, and unbowed for the first time in twenty years.

"Worse," he said. "When the lie is *true*. Come home. We have pages to set."

Behind them, across the river, a bell began tolling the curfew — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the drums of the victory parade had begun their rehearsal, counting down the nights.
