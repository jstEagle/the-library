## 16. The Distribution Plan

Jorun Beck had learned the city the way other men learned scripture: by heart, and in the dark.

He sat now at the trestle table in his cellar with a sheet of cheap grey paper before him and a stub of charcoal behind his ear, and he drew Vellmark as he knew it — not as the mapmakers drew it, not as the Council's surveyors had drawn it for their new registers, but as it moved. The river first, because everything in Vellmark moved along the river or against it: a thick stroke bending through the middle of the page like a slow animal turning over in its sleep. Then the bridges — three of them still standing since the fire took the Old Chain Bridge in the spring — and then the streets, not as lines but as currents, each one marked with what flowed along it and when.

Laundry water, flowing downhill every morning from the wash-houses on Tanner's Row into the public cisterns, carried by women whose arms knew no borders.

Bread carts, rolling before dawn from the ovens of Millgate to every quarter that could pay.

Choirboys, walking to evening practice at Saint Ferren's and the Chapel of the Drowned Saint, carrying songbooks under their arms.

Ferryman's skiff, crossing at the willow stairs four times an hour, carrying anything that fit beneath a tarpaulin of eel traps.

He marked them all with small signs of his own devising — a wave for the river traffic, a wheel for the carts, a bird for the choir lofts — until the grey sheet began to look less like a map than like a body's circulatory system, veins and arteries all leading away from a single heart.

The heart was a hand-press in a print shop on Candlemakers' Lane, misreported years ago as broken, and it would soon be beating out the truest document Jorun Beck had ever touched.

* * *

Casper Yarrow arrived after nightfall with her hood up and the smell of lamp oil in her hair, and behind her came the child, Wren, moving the way she always moved now — close to Casper's shadow, quick through doorways, eyes doing their silent terrible work of remembering every face she passed.

"Your assistant?" Casper asked, nodding toward the stair that led up to the chandler's shop above.

"Gone home to his mother's in Saltmarsh for the week." Jorun kept his voice level. He did not say what he had begun to suspect about Pell's new boots, or the way the boy counted the sheets left drying, or how he'd started asking questions about what exactly they were printing that needed so much secrecy when everyone knew the Council was going to win anyway. That suspicion lived in Jorun like a splinter worked deep into the thumb. You didn't dig at it in front of others. But you didn't forget it either.

"Wren," Casper said softly, "you remember Jorun."

The girl looked up at him with those grave dark eyes and nodded once, formal as a magistrate. "You set type crooked," she said. "On your ballads. The third line always drifts."

Jorun barked a laugh before he could stop himself. "Does she now."

"She reads," Casper said, and there was something fierce and wounded in the way she said it, the way you'd say *she survives*. "Aldous Renk taught her letters on his composing stick before she could reach the case without a stool."

Then let her learn this too, Jorun thought, and pulled the stool out beside him at the table. "Come here, little compositor. I need eyes that don't blink."

* * *

He laid the map flat between them and lit the second lamp, and for two hours he did what he had been born to do, which was think in routes.

"The mistake," he said, tapping the charcoal stub against the paper, "is to imagine we're distributing a pamphlet. We're not. A pamphlet is a thing; patrols know how to hunt things. They seize a bundle off a cart, they arrest a carrier, they count themselves victorious, and the story dies in a evidence locker." He shook his head. "What we have is testimony. Testimony doesn't travel like goods. It travels like disease. One reader becomes ten readers, and every one of them is a press we don't have to feed."

Casper leaned over the map, and he watched her read it the way she read a galley — fast, missing nothing, correcting silently. She'd grown up over a press too. Different press, same religion.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"Plainly, then." He drew the first circle, around the laundries on Tanner's Row. "The wash-house women. My aunt Doretta runs the big one, forty tubs, and half the wives and widows of the Council garrison send their uniforms there. Sheets go in folded inside bundles of collars. Collars go back to officers' quarters. Their own households will read our testimony aloud to them over supper, and there is nothing any provost can do about it, because you cannot search every shirt in Vellmark."

Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled young.

"Taverns next." Second circle, east bank, six marks. "Not the big ones — the Council has ears in the big ones. The narrow places. The Gilded Eel, where the ferry crews drink. Mother Hask's, down by the tanneries, where the men who load the barges argue politics till the lamps die. And the Taproom of the Nine Saints, which sounds loyal enough to pass any inspection and is owned by a woman who hated Renk's murderers before it became fashionable." He tapped each mark twice. "In each one, one trusted man or woman gets ten copies. Not fifty. Ten. Enough to seed a room, not enough to hang a district if they're caught."

"And they'll be caught," Casper said quietly. "Some of them."

"Yes." Jorun didn't soften it. He had watched men die for ballads thinner than this testimony, and he would not insult the living by pretending the arithmetic was kind. "So we choose people who already carry risk like a coat they've worn so long they sleep in it. Debtors. Men with brothers in the old militia. Women who've buried sons. People for whom the truth is not an idea but a debt. Those are the only carriers who don't drop the parcel when the road gets ugly."

"The choir lofts," Casper said, reading his third sign. Her finger rested on the mark for Saint Ferren's. "You want children."

"I want songbooks." He met her eyes. "Every choirboy carries a bound book of hymns to practice. Loose sheets tucked into the bindings — who searches a child's hymnal? Who dares? And choirs travel, Casper. Saint Ferren's sings at the garrison chapel on feast days. The Chapel of the Drowned Saint serves the harbor officers. Our sheets would sit in the laps of the very men they name, singing above them every Sunday, and none of us would have to do a thing but fold paper small."

"That's—" Casper stopped. He saw her wrestle with it, the printer's instinct against the protector's fear, the woman who had already lost one father to this fight and would not easily spend children to buy the victory. "It's brilliant," she said finally. "And I hate it."

"Both can be true. My master used to say ink is honest but printing never is. Every act of putting words into the world is a gamble with other people's safety." He looked at Wren, who had gone very still, listening the way she listened to everything. "We keep her out of it entirely. She's carried enough."

"I heard my grandfather's presses," Wren said, unexpectedly. "At night. Through the floor. He said the sound was a heartbeat and the city couldn't live without it." Her chin came up. "I'm not afraid of songbooks."

"You should be," Jorun said gently. "That's why you'll stay exactly where Casper puts you."

* * *

They argued the river for an hour, and the argument was good for them both, because it forced the plan to survive contact with doubt.

The ferryman was Jorun's oldest card: Oskar Vell, who ran the willow-stairs crossing and owed Jorun his life from the cholera summer, when Jorun had printed the broadsheet naming the tainted well while the Council still swore the water was sweet. Oskar would take copies upriver to the mill villages without being asked twice.

"But the bridges are the choke point," Casper said, tracing the three crossings. "Moll's men have been counting papers since the seizures started. If they find one bundle on one bridge—"

"They won't find a bundle, because there won't be bundles." Jorun turned the charcoal and began shading the river itself. "Think smaller. Think wetter." He grinned, and it felt strange on his face, hope pulling muscles it hadn't used in weeks. "Oskar's eel traps come up every dawn. Eels go to market wrapped in wet cloth. Wet cloth goes everywhere — fishmongers' stalls, the garrison kitchens, the officers' mess on Council Row. Paper wrapped in oilskin rides inside the eel-cloth, comes out at the market, passes hand to hand at the fishmongers' row. Cold, slimy, stinking of river. No soldier in Vellmark will put his hands in it twice."

Casper stared at him. Then she laughed — really laughed, short and startled, the first time he'd heard it since the Halvard press came down two streets from her father's shop with its owner dragged away in front of his children. The laugh cracked something open in the cellar's air, and even Wren smiled, quick and secret.

"You've been planning this," Casper said.

"I've been planning since the day they shot Aldous Renk and called it a robbery." The laugh went out of him. "Five years. I set seditious ballads and sold them for pennies and told myself it was enough, it was something, it kept the flame alive. It wasn't. Ballads make people feel. This—" he flattened his palm on the blank space in the center of the map, where the testimony would live, the names, the dates, the depositions, Renk's last words in his own hand — "this makes people *know*. Feeling fades by morning. Knowing doesn't. Moll can burn every press in the city and it won't matter, because knowing spreads faster than fire. That's the whole plan, Casper. That's the entire architecture of it. We are not trying to save paper. We're trying to infect a city."

* * *

Near midnight Casper took Wren up the outside stair to the cot Jorun kept for long print-nights, and the girl went without protest, though at the top step she paused and looked back at the map glowing under the lamp.

"Will it work?" she asked. Not childish. The question of someone who had already learned that adults lie beautifully and that the truth is usually uglier and more useful.

"It will work," Jorun said, "if every piece holds."

She considered this with terrible seven-year-old gravity, and then she said, "Pell counts the sheets when you're asleep," and went up.

The cellar went very quiet. Casper came slowly back down the stair, and they looked at each other across the map, and neither spoke for a long moment.

"He's been restless," Jorun said at last. "New boots. Questions. Asking why we bother when the Council's won already." His jaw tightened. "I told myself it was a boy being a boy."

"You told yourself what I tell myself about my father's old ledgers," Casper said. "What we want to be true, set in the biggest type we own." She sat down heavily. "Do we move the printing? Change the schedule?"

"No." Jorun heard his own voice come out flat and certain, and recognized it as his father's voice, and his master's voice, and every stubborn dead printer's voice going back to the invention of movable type. "If we scramble now, we lose days, and days are the whole war. Moll closes the net the week of the victory parade — you said it yourself, the inventory lists are nearly done, every registered press accounted for. We print on schedule. But Pell works the day shift from tomorrow, alone, and sees nothing but devotional stock. And after the run—" he looked at her, and finished it plainly, because she deserved plainness — "after the run, we settle accounts with Pell before the soldiers do."

Casper nodded slowly. Then she reached across the table and turned his charcoal map a quarter-turn, studying the circulatory system of the city they meant to infect, the laundries and taverns and choir lofts and eel-smelling boats, all of it waiting on a few thousand sheets that did not yet exist.

"Four nights," she said. "My father's old press can do it, if the frisket holds and we don't sleep."

"We weren't going to sleep anyway." Jorun picked up the charcoal and added one last mark to the map — a small square in Candlemakers' Lane, unregistered, uncounted, officially broken for eleven years. The last press standing. The heart.

Outside, far off toward the Council quarter, drums were practicing for the victory parade, steady and patient as a pulse counting down. Jorun listened to them for a moment, then blew out the second lamp.

"Four nights," he agreed. "And pray the walls of this cellar are better listeners than my apprentice."
