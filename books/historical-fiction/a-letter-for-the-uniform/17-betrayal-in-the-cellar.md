## 17. Betrayal in the Cellar

The knock came at half past the fourth bell, and it was wrong.

Casper had learned the grammar of doors in the weeks since she'd begun carrying galleys through Vellmark's checkpoint streets — the double rap of Jorun's fist, the shuffle of the ferryman's boots, Wren's small knuckles drumming a rhythm she'd invented and refused to explain. This was none of them. This was iron on wood, unhurried, certain, the knock of men who did not need to ask permission because they had already been given it.

She looked up from the composing stone where she'd been breaking down the morning's forme, her fingers black to the second knuckle, and saw Jorun go still in the way that animals go still — not calm, but listening with his whole body.

"Out," he said. No whisper. His voice came out flat and hard as a locking plate. "Coal chute. Now."

"Jorun—"

"They're here, Cas." He was already moving, kicking aside the rag pallet where Wren slept curled around the coin purse like a dragon around its hoard. He shook her awake without gentleness, and Casper loved him for it, because gentleness would have cost them the seconds they were about to spend. "Up, little bird. Up and quiet or I'll put you in a sack myself."

Wren woke the way she always woke — instantly, silently, her eyes going from face to face, reading the room the way Casper read type. Whatever she read made her scramble up without a sound.

Below them, at the street door of the cellar print shop, someone tried the handle. Once. Twice.

"The back," Casper said. "There's the chute behind the paper racks — it goes up to the coal yard on Tanner's Lane."

"I know where my own chute goes." Jorun had crossed to the press — his press, the old Albion he'd bought broken and rebuilt with his own hands, that smelled of tallow and lamp-black and seven years of his life. But he didn't go for the galleys. He went for the shelf beneath the bed, where the moulds lay wrapped in oilcloth.

And Casper understood, with a cold that started in her stomach and climbed her spine one vertebra at a time, what he meant to do.

* * *

They had come to the cellar three nights ago with everything that mattered: the master galleys of Sarn's deposition, locked in a wooden case disguised as a shipment of psalters; Wren, drugged with poppy syrup so her nightmares wouldn't carry; and Tam's confession, still smelling of the loft, still bearing the small tremor in the letters that Casper could not correct without destroying the truth of it. Her father had set those lines weeping, and the tears had gotten into the case, and she had left them there. Let the historians of whatever came next puzzle over the watermarks. Let them know a man's hands shook when he wrote down what he'd done.

Jorun's assistant, Pell, had seemed glad enough to see them. A broad, amiable young man who swept the cellar and ran deliveries and laughed too easily at Jorun's jokes — Casper had liked him. She had even, once, let him carry the psalter crate up from the ferry landing, because her arms ached and he offered and it seemed safe. It seemed safe because he smiled.

She thought of that now, watching Jorun tear the oilcloth from the type moulds, and felt something in her chest turn over like a forme dropping.

"Pell," she said. "It was Pell."

Jorun didn't answer. The knocking had stopped, which was worse than the knocking. She could hear boots now, many boots, on the street above, spreading along the front of the building the way water spreads under a door.

"How much do you think they paid him?" she heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away, someone else's voice, a voice in a pamphlet. "Or did they just threaten him? Did they show him what they do to printers' assistants?"

"Cas. *Chute.*" Jorun had the moulds stacked against his chest, and the little hammer he kept for breaking type, and his face in the lamplight was grey as lead. "Take the case. Take the girl. Go."

"Not without you."

"You don't get to argue. Not tonight." He shoved the wooden case into her arms — it weighed nothing and everything, four hundred years of the dead pressing their testimony into her ribs — and turned to the moulds. "I'm slow, Cas. You've seen me climb the ladder. If I come up that chute after you, we all hang. This way, maybe only I hang."

"That is not *comforting*, Jorun Beck."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true." He glanced at Wren, standing pressed against the coal bins with the coin purse clutched to her chest, her eyes enormous. Something in his face cracked open, just for a moment — the face of the boy who'd apprenticed under Tam Yarrow, who'd taught Casper to tie a printer's knot when they were both children ink-stained to the elbow. "Little bird. When this is over, if you ever set type, remember: cut clean. Don't be afraid of the knife. The knife's the kindest tool in the case."

Then the street door burst inward, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

What Casper remembered afterward, in the long years of piecing the night back together, was never the whole of it — only fragments, sharp as sorts dropped on stone:

Lamplight flooding down the stairwell, six lanterns at least, and the smell of wet wool and leather straps that she would never again smell without her heart slamming.

Jorun turning toward the press with the moulds in his arms, not away from it.

His voice, raised for the first time in all the years she'd known him, pitched to fill the cellar: *"Here! It's here! Nothing leaves this room!"*

A sergeant's shout — *the historian wants the plates whole, Beck, Moll wants them whole!* — and then the crack of wood, and Jorun swinging the moulds against the corner of the press bed with all his weight behind them, once, twice, the sound of lead and brass giving way like bones.

Then hands, many hands, and Jorun going down under them, and Casper already moving, because some part of her had obeyed him before her mind consented — she had the case, she had Wren's wrist, and they were at the paper racks, hauling them aside, and the coal chute stood open before them, a black throat breathing the cold of Tanner's Lane.

Behind her, the sounds had become terrible. She would not look. She made herself not look, and hated herself for the mercy of not looking, and knew Jorun would have told her — *go, Cas, don't watch, watch nothing but your feet* — and so she did not watch. She lifted Wren onto the sloped boards of the chute and pushed her up into the dark, and the girl went like smoke, silent, climbing, and Casper came after with the case hugged to her chest, the wood scraping her forearms raw, coal dust rising around them in a choking cloud.

Below, a man screamed — not Jorun; Jorun's voice was lower, rougher, cursing steadily, wonderfully, a litany of profanity that Casper would treasure later in ways she couldn't yet imagine — and boots hammered across the floor toward the racks.

Toward the chute.

She reached the top. Cold air, coal-smell, the grey beginning of dawn over the rooftops of Tanner's Lane, and she hauled Wren out by both arms and rolled with her behind the coal heap just as a lantern appeared below them in the shaft, bobbing upward, a soldier's face tilted back, shouting something about the chute, the chute—

But the chute was steep, and the case was heavy, and Casper had done the arithmetic Jorun had done: whoever climbed it would be slow, and alone, and visible against the greying sky.

"*Run,*" she breathed to Wren, and Wren ran.

* * *

They went east through the coal yard, over the fence into the tannery yards — the stench there was a blessing, a wall of rot that no patrolman would linger in — then south along the drainage ditch behind the currier's sheds, Wren's hand locked in hers, the case banging against Casper's hip until she slung it across her back like a corpse. Twice they heard whistles behind them, cutting the morning into pieces. Twice they flattened themselves in the ditch among the tannery filth and waited, Casper's heart beating so hard she was sure it would give them away, sure the soldiers could hear it, smell it, see it through the ground.

The third time, it was Wren who pulled her flat — the child hearing footsteps before she did, the child whose whole short life had been an education in the sound of men coming to take things. They lay in the muck while two soldiers passed on the lane above, talking about bread rations, about a woman on Cooper Street, about nothing, about everything ordinary, while underneath them a printer was being beaten and a cellar full of light was being emptied into sacks.

When the boots faded, Wren looked at her. Coal dust streaked her face except where tears had cut channels through it, and her eyes were dry now, finished with crying, older than seven years by several centuries.

"He broke them," she said. "The moulds. Like you said he would, that time."

"I didn't say he would."

"You said people break things so the words can't be unmade backwards." Wren wiped her nose on her sleeve, leaving a black smear. "Is Jorun going to die?"

Casper had spent ten days learning which questions to answer honestly. This was one of them. That was the whole discipline, really — her father's silence had been a lie built of answered questions, and she would not build her own house on that foundation, even to comfort a child, even to comfort herself.

"I don't know," she said. "He knew what might happen when he stayed. That's not the same as dying, but it's not the same as being safe, either. He chose it so that you and I and the pages got out. So whatever happens to him — it bought something. We have to make it worth the price."

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of children, who have not yet learned to flinch from arithmetic.

"Then we have to run faster," she said, and got up, and pulled Casper by the hand.

* * *

They reached the Yarrow shop as the bells rang six, coming in through the alley door, through the reek of the tanneries, coal-dusted and mud-hemmed and carrying a dead man's testimony on Casper's back.

Her father took one look at them and began pulling off his apron.

"No," Casper said.

"They'll trace the chute to Tanner's Lane, Tanner's Lane to the tanneries, the tanneries to anywhere. You need to be gone from this city by noon—"

"Papa." She set the case on the composing stone and opened it. The galleys lay inside, untouched, the type still locked, Sarn's depositions in their careful rows, Tam's trembling confession, Renk's last words as the clerk had recorded them. Above them, folded in oilcloth, lay the one thing Jorun had told her to take and she had almost left behind: the layout map of the city, every laundry and tavern and choir loft marked in Jorun's blunt carpenter's hand, the veins through which a thousand copies could flow. "They have Jorun. They have Pell, or Pell is drinking his payment somewhere and doesn't yet know what he's bought. They will have my description from Pell by tonight, and yours, and this shop is registered in every ledger Moll keeps." She looked up at her father, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "There is one press in Vellmark that isn't in any ledger. Because you reported it broken eight years ago, and nobody ever came to check."

Tam Yarrow went very still.

"The old hand-press," Casper said. "In the loft. Under the canvas. Papa — it's the last one standing."

For a long moment her father said nothing. He looked at the galleys. He looked at Wren, who stared back at him with Renk's eyes, though he didn't know that yet — though perhaps, Casper thought, watching his face, he was beginning to. Then he rolled up his sleeves, slowly, the way a man rolls them for work he has been avoiding his whole life.

"Its platen spring is shot," he said. "And the screw needs greasing, and half the furniture rotted in the damp. It will take a day to make it pull a clean impression. Maybe two." He picked up a candle. "We have until they finish with Jorun Beck. So we'd best not waste the daylight arguing."

He was halfway to the loft stairs when Casper caught his arm.

"Papa. Jorun said—" Her voice failed, gathered itself. "He said to tell you he paid his debt. All of it. He said you'd know what he meant."

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and steady, and she saw in them the apprentice he had been and the coward he had chosen and the man he was becoming now, at the end, in the last hours his city would give him.

"Yes," he said. "I know what he meant. Get the canvas off the press, Casper. And send the little one up with the grease pot."

Above them, through the loft window, the drums of the Provisional Council had begun to rehearse their victory. Below the floorboards, in a cellar across the river, Jorun Beck was paying for every word they were about to print. Casper took the canvas in both hands and pulled, and the old press emerged into the candlelight like something exhumed — and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that loss was not the end of purpose.

Loss was the whetstone of it.
