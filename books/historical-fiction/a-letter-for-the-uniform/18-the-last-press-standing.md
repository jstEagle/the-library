## 18. The Last Press Standing

Tam Yarrow had spent thirty years lying to machines, and the old press knew it.

It stood in the back room of the shop like a relic from a drowned country — a hand-press his grandfather had bought secondhand from a monastery that was dissolving, its iron frame painted over so many times that the paint itself had become geology, strata of brown and black and a green no one mixed anymore. The registration ledger, when Edvard Moll's clerks had come through in the autumn with their clipboards and their little brass stamps, listed it as *broken, frame cracked, not serviceable.* Tam had said those words himself, standing before the clerk with his hat in his hands, and he had been believed, because Tam Yarrow had spent thirty years being believed when it mattered and ignored when it didn't, and both had kept him alive.

The frame was cracked. That much was true. It had been cracked for eleven years, since the night he'd let it sit idle rather than repair it, because a broken press was a reason to say no, and saying no was the one skill his silence had taught him well.

Now Casper stood beside it with a candle in each hand, and the light moved across the iron like water finding the shape of everything it touched.

"Say it again," she said.

"It's broken," Tam said. "That's what I told them."

"And is it?"

He put his palm flat against the platen. The iron was cold in a way that iron never quite stopped being, even in summer, even under a printer's hands. Cold as a coin passed under a table. Cold as the purse he'd taken for his silence, all those years ago, that had sat in a drawer until he could no longer bear to look at it and had buried it in the garden beneath the currant bush, where it presumably still lay, feeding nothing.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

* * *

They worked by candlelight because candlelight was deniable. A lamplit window at two in the morning invited questions; candles guttered everywhere in Vellmark now, since the Council had begun rationing oil for its own wagons, and half the city sat dark by nine out of poverty and the other half out of prudence. The shutters were drawn. The sign on the door still read YARROW & SON in letters his grandfather had gilded, though there had never been a son, only Casper, and Tam had never corrected the sign, and she had never asked him to, and he understood only now — standing in the smell of old ink and tallow with his sleeves rolled to the elbow — how many things they had never said to each other because the saying would have opened a door into a house where neither of them wished to live.

"The crack runs here." He traced it with one finger, along the cheek of the frame where the iron had given way sometime before her birth or just after. "Grandfather clamped it with these straps and poured lead into the seam. It held. It'll hold again if we can find lead."

"There's lead in the type cases," Casper said. "Broken sorts. The bin under the stairs."

"That's type metal. It has antimony in it — too hard, too brittle—"

"Papa." She set the candles down on the stone shelf where he used to set them when she was small and allowed to watch but not to touch. "We have type metal and we have tonight. Tomorrow Moll finishes his inventory of the east quarter, and then there will be no presses anywhere in this city that aren't his, except this one, which doesn't exist. We pour what we have."

He looked at her — at his daughter, who at fourteen had been setting type faster than any journeyman he'd ever employed, who at nineteen had caught him burning a letter in the grate and had known, had *known*, and had said nothing for years because she had learned the family trade, which was not printing but silence — and he felt something break in his chest that was not grief, or was grief of an entirely new denomination.

"Yes," he said. "We pour what we have."

* * *

The work was slow and holy and it hurt his back.

They began by stripping the press down to its bones: the platen lifted off and laid on wool blankets on the floor, the screw cleaned of eleven years of dust and hardened grease with lye and rags and finally Casper's own thumbnails, the garters — the leather pads that softened the impression — found where he remembered hiding them, in the false bottom of the paper drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, stiff as shrouds but whole. Somewhere in the middle hours Tam realized he was talking. Not about the press. About his grandfather, who had bought this iron off a monk who wept while counting the coins; about his father, who printed ballads during the bread riots and hid the formes inside covers of psalters; about the year of the fever, when Tam himself, a boy of nine, had pulled the bar while his father worked the ink, and the sound the press made — *thock, sigh; thock, sigh* — had seemed to him the sound of the city breathing.

Casper listened while she scraped. She did not interrupt. Once, when he described his father's hands — how they'd smelled of oak gall ink even at church, even at funerals — she looked up, and the candlelight was in her eyes, and she said, "Mine smell like that too. I used to think everyone's did."

"They don't," Tam said. "I'm sorry. For a great many things, but partly for that. You should have been able to think everyone's did."

She went back to scraping. But something between them had shifted, some beam long jammed swinging free, and the room felt larger than it had.

* * *

Near midnight they melted the lead.

Tam built a fire in the little casting stove, the one meant for making new sorts, and they fed it broken type — letters that had worn past use, ligatures gone soft at the edges, a whole case of long-s characters nobody had wanted since before the war. The metal went down reluctantly, then all at once, silver-bright, throwing a light up into their faces that made them look, for a moment, like figures in someone else's story.

"You understand," Tam said quietly, watching the crucible, "what we're doing. We're melting words to make a press that can print words. There's a joke in it somewhere and I'm too tired to find it."

"We're melting dead letters," Casper said, "to make a living sentence."

He laughed — actually laughed, a short rough sound that startled him, that he hadn't heard come out of himself in years — and the laugh turned into something else halfway up his throat and he had to turn away and pretend to check the draft.

Because he knew what the sentence was going to say. He had set part of it himself, three nights ago, alone in the loft while Casper slept: his own confession, in twelve-point, the same size as scripture. *I, Tam Yarrow, printer of Vellmark, do testify that in the month of the frost I accepted payment from officers of the Provisional Council in exchange for my silence concerning the death of Aldous Renk.* He had set it letter by letter, and his hands had shaken, and when he reached his own name — his own name, in type, in the accusative, permanent, reproducible, a name that could be pulled a thousand times and be guilty a thousand times — he had stood at the case and wept like a child, silently, the way he did everything that mattered.

And it had been the first clean thing he had done in thirty years, and it had felt like dying and like being born, and he had never told Casper, though he suspected she knew. She knew everything. She always had. That was what his silence had cost him: not his daughter's love, which had proved more stubborn than he deserved, but her *knowledge of him*, which he had sold cheap and could never buy back — except perhaps now, perhaps tonight, with lead and lye and candlelight.

"Papa," she said. "Hold the mold steady. I'll pour."

He held it. She poured. The metal ran bright into the channel, filled the seam of the old crack, hissed once, and was still.

* * *

They let it cool while they ate. Bread and hard cheese and the last of the apple preserve, sitting on crates in the back room like apprentices, which was what they were, both of them, apprentices to the same stubborn craft. Through the shutters came the sound of the city: distant boots, a patrol's lantern swinging its pale arc across the gap under the door, drums rehearsing somewhere across the river for a parade whose route had already been painted on the official maps. The Council's victory was four days old in every newspaper but hadn't yet reached the streets, the way a wave is announced by the sea pulling back.

"They're close," Casper said. "Moll. He's close. Jorun sent word the Halvard lists are finished — every press in the east quarter stamped and sealed. When they get to us—"

"They won't find a press," Tam said. "They'll find a broken one. I've told you—"

"And if they look? If they open the drawer, if they see the forme?"

"Then they'll see devotional stock." He met her eyes. "I've done this before, Casper. Hiding truth inside psalms. It was cowardice then. Let me make it craft now."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "When you set your confession — your page. I saw it in the loft. I didn't read it. I didn't have to."

Tam set down his bread. His hands, which had betrayed him so many times — reaching for the purse, signing the receipt, turning his face from Aldous Renk's empty chair — were steady now, and he watched them as though they belonged to a better man.

"I set it at night," he said. "After you slept. Because I couldn't do it in front of you, and I couldn't do it without doing it at all. Do you understand the difference? Thirty years I've lived in that difference. The thing I can't do in front of you, and the thing I can't not do."

"I understand it," she said softly. "I've lived in that difference my whole life. You built it. I inherited it."

"And I would give—" His voice failed. He started again. "I would give anything to have built you a different inheritance. A shop full of wedding invitations and almanacs. Weather predictions, mostly wrong. A life so dull the historians would never once write your name."

"Don't," Casper said. "Don't you dare apologize for giving me the press instead." She took his hand — his ink-stained, trembling-then-steady hand — and pressed it flat against the cold iron cheek of the old machine. "Feel that. Grandfather's straps. Your father's ballads. My first forme, the one I dropped, the one you re-set without scolding me. Everything true anyone in this family ever made went through this iron. Moll can have the ledger. He can have the stamp and the seal and his official history with its gold tooling. He cannot have this. It was never on his list because it was never for sale."

Tam Yarrow bowed his head over his daughter's hand and the iron beneath it, and for the first time since the frost, since the purse, since the letter burning green and curling in the grate — he prayed. Not to any god he could have named. To the press. To the craft. To whatever keeps faith with ink.

* * *

They raised the platen together near dawn.

The lead in the seam had cooled to dull gray, and when Tam tested the cheek with a knuckle it rang — rang, after eleven years of silence, a clear bell-note that went through the floorboards and up into the loft where Wren slept curled among the empty type cases, wrapped in Casper's coat. Above them, small feet pattered; a small face appeared at the hatch, hair sleep-tangled, eyes enormous in the candlelight.

"Is it fixed?" the girl whispered.

"It's fixed," Tam said.

"Will it print?"

"It will print," he said, and heard in his own voice a vow he had no right to make and made anyway. "It will print anything we ask it to."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, nodded once, and withdrew. A child smuggled out of a murdered man's house with a sealed letter and orders to forget a face — and she trusted the press, because children trust the tools of whoever feeds them, and because she was Aldous Renk's granddaughter and the blood in her had opinions about print.

Casper was already climbing toward the loft. "Jorun should come before first light. If we're going to compose the final pages, we compose them today, lock the forme, and be ready to run the moment the drums start. Papa — the paper. How much good stock is left in the cellar?"

"Enough," Tam said. "Barely enough."

"Barely is a number." She paused at the hatch, looking down at him across the candlelit room, across the resurrected press, and smiled — a tired, fierce, unguarded smile he had not seen on her face since she was young enough to wear it without calculating who might see. "Thank you. For saying it wasn't broken. Eleven years ago and tonight. Both times."

Then she was up into the loft, and Tam stood alone in the back room with the old press, his hand resting on the bar his grandfather had gripped, his father had gripped, and which waited now for his own.

Outside, far off across the river, the rehearsal drums stopped. In the sudden silence he heard, very distinctly, boots on cobbles — many boots, unhurried, organized, coming up Copperlane from the direction of the Halvard yard. And beneath the boots, fainter, a voice he knew: smooth, reasonable, tired with the fatigue of a man doing necessary work — Edvard Moll, saying something to the soldiers that Tam could not catch, except for the last few words, carried clear across the sleeping street:

"—and the Yarrow shop. Wake them."

Tam blew out the candles.
