## 19. Night of the Thousand Copies

The hand-press smelled of linseed and forty years of dust, and when Casper leaned her weight against the bar and felt the screw turn, she thought: *this is what my hands were made for.*

It was past midnight. The drums had started an hour ago — not near yet, but coming, a slow arterial pulse from the east of the city where the Council's army would enter at dawn with banners and historians and lists of names to cross out. Vellmark was falling in the way a house falls: not all at once, but beam by beam, each sound distinct, each one final. And here, in the back room above the flooded paper store, by three candles and a shielded lamp, Casper Yarrow was printing the thing that would outlive all of it.

"Pull," Tam said.

She pulled. The platen came down; the screw bit; she felt the type meet the dampened sheet through the wood and iron like a heartbeat transmitted through bone. Then the bar up, the frisket open, and there it was — page four of the testimony, ink still shining wet, Ivet Sarn's careful sentences standing in double columns like a fence built against forgetting.

"Good impression," Tam said.

"You've said that eleven times."

"I've meant it eleven times."

They worked without speaking for a while after that. There was no need. In thirty years of setting other people's words, they had learned the language of the press — the knock of a loose quoine, the sigh of a sheet laid true, the small dry rasp of the brayer running low on ink. Casper's father moved through that language like a man come home to a house he'd been exiled from. She watched him, between pulls, straightening the furniture of the forme, and saw that his hands had stopped trembling somewhere around two o'clock, and understood that whatever this night cost him, he did not want to be anywhere else in the world.

* * *

The galleys had come up from Jorun's cellar in a fish cart before curfew, wrapped in oilcloth under a false bottom, along with Jorun himself, who arrived smelling of coal smoke and dried blood and refused, flatly, to stay behind.

"They broke my press," he said, when Casper tried. "They broke my *hands*, or close enough. You think I'll sit in the dark listening to you do this without me?" He held up his fingers, swollen at two knuckles, and flexed them deliberately, wincing, as if to prove they still belonged to him. "I can't set type tonight. But I can feed sheets, and I can carry them out, and if soldiers come through that door I'm big enough to make it expensive."

So Jorun fed sheets. Tam pulled the lever on the old press — his shoulder knew its weight even if his pride had forgotten — and Casper stood at the stone, composing, correcting, locking up each new forme while the last one printed, so that the press never stopped, never cooled, never gave the night a chance to settle around them.

The stack grew on the drying rack in ranks, page by page, like a wall being raised.

Page one: the deposition of Ivet Sarn, clerk to the Provisional Council, dictated in the hospice across the river with his voice failing and his eyes terribly clear. *I kept the minutes. I wrote down what they decided, because someone had to write down what they decided, and then I understood that writing it down was the same as deciding to keep it.* The date of Aldous Renk's death. The address of the house. The names of the men who gave the order.

Casper had set those names herself, letter by letter, choosing each piece of type with the deliberation of a woman laying stones over a grave. **Colonel Aurel Vance. Captain Denz Moray. Provost-Adjutant Hesk Otteline.** When Moll's official history was written — and it would be written, within weeks, in editions fine enough for the Council library and cheap enough for every schoolroom — those men would appear as restorers of order, as patriots, perhaps as mourners. Here, in ink, on rag paper, they appeared as what Sarn's minutes said they were.

Page three held Renk's last words. Not the famous ones from the trial — the dignified sentences the broadsheets had already worn smooth — but the ones he'd said in the yard afterward, recorded in Sarn's minutes because Sarn had been there with his notebook out of habit, God forgive him. *Tell them I printed what was true, and that truth is not sedition, it is only memory wearing work clothes.* Casper had read that line forty times while composing it, and it still caught in her chest like a splinter too deep to draw.

And page six — the last forme locked and ready on the stone, waiting its turn — was Tam's.

She had found him at the case at half past one, alone by the candle, setting it himself though his fingers shook and he mispicked letters twice for every ten. He hadn't asked her permission. He hadn't asked anything. He had simply begun, and when she stood beside him he spoke without looking up, in the flat voice of a man reading a bill of lading:

"It goes in. If it doesn't go in, the whole record is a weapon with a bent edge, and they'll use the bend to blunt everything else." He picked a capital T, hesitated, put it back, picked another. "*I, Tam Yarrow, printer of Vellmark, confess that in the winter of—*" His voice failed. He cleared his throat. "In the winter following Renk's death I received payment from persons unnamed, in exchange for which I destroyed my proofs, surrendered my correspondence, and agreed to deny ever having set his final pamphlet. I have denied it for nine years. I am now un-denying it."

He had wept then — quietly, horribly, the way men of his generation wept, as though ashamed even of grief — and Casper had put her hand over his on the case, and neither of them had said anything, because there was nothing to say that the type itself wouldn't say better.

Now that confession sat in its forme on the stone, and Casper looked at it the way you look at a wound that has finally been cleaned.

* * *

The drums came closer around three o'clock.

They had been a rumor of rhythm for hours; now they acquired direction, weight, the particular authority of sound that intends to arrive. Down in the street, shutters banged. Somewhere a dog began to howl and was joined by others, a ragged chorus rising along the river. The city turning over in its sleep, unable to wake properly, dreaming of boots.

Jorun paused with a fresh sheet in his hands. "Parade drums. They're rehearsing the entry. Dawn, maybe sooner — the bridge companies will want to march in first light with the mist still on the water. It photographs well." He almost smiled. "Moll will have told them. Moll understands ceremony."

"Then we have until dawn," Casper said.

"We have until they decide dawn has arrived early." He laid the sheet on the tympan. "Keep pulling."

Tam pulled. The press groaned — a sound Casper had decided, somewhere in the second hundred impressions, was the oldest prayer in Vellmark. *Lord, let the ink hold. Let the paper hold. Let us hold.*

Her arms burned. Her shoulders had passed through burning into something else some hours ago, a deep structural ache, as if her skeleton were being reset. Sacred exhaustion, she thought — no, not thought; the word arrived whole, unbidden, the way words did when you had spent your life among them. It was what the old monks must have felt copying scripture by rushlight, knowing the raiders might come before morning, copying anyway. Every line a wager against darkness. You didn't print because you would win. You printed because the alternative was to let the dark write its own account and call it history.

She wiped her palms on her apron and went back to the stone.

* * *

Around four, Wren came down the ladder.

She was supposed to be asleep in the loft above, curled in the nest of blankets they'd made her among the cases of nonpareil and brevier, and for a moment Casper was going to send her back up. But the child crossed the floor barefoot and quiet and stood watching the press, watching the sheets rise and fall, and her face in the candlelight had a gravity no seven-year-old face should hold.

"That's Grandfather," Wren said.

It wasn't a question. Casper followed her eyes to the drying rack — to the sheet hanging there with the excerpt they'd chosen to lead the pamphlet, Renk's name set large beneath it, and beneath the name the line about truth wearing work clothes.

"Yes," Casper said. "That's your grandfather."

"He looks like the letters look." Wren touched the hem of the sheet, very lightly, the way you touch something sleeping. "Papa said he was made of letters. He used to let me stand at his case and hold the stick." She looked up. "Is he in all of them? All the pages?"

"In every copy," Casper said. "There will be a thousand copies. Maybe more, if the paper holds."

"A thousand." Wren considered this with terrible seriousness. "Then everyone will know him."

"Everyone," Casper said, and heard her own voice crack on the small word, and knelt, and let the child put her arms around her neck. Over Wren's head she saw Tam stop pulling — saw him watch them, his daughter and the granddaughter of the man whose silence he had sold — and saw him turn back to the press and pull harder than before, as if the lever could carry what his voice could not.

After a while Casper carried Wren back up the ladder, and the child slept again, and the press went on.

* * *

Dawn did not come gently.

The drums stopped around five — stopped the way a held breath stops — and in the silence that followed, the city made its small terrified sounds: a shutter, a footstep, the ferry bell tolling wrong, twice, the signal Jorun had taught them meant troops on the far bank. Grey light crept into the high windows, thin as skimmed milk, and by it Casper could see the full extent of what they had done: seven hundred sheets and more, ranked on lines strung across the rafters, page after page of testimony drying in the cold air like laundry hung out by ghosts.

"Sixty more pulls on this forme and we're done with the text," Tam said hoarsely. "Then the colophon."

"The colophon?" Jorun looked up.

"Every honest book signs itself." Tam's voice was rough but steady. "Printed by T. Yarrow & Daughter, Vellmark. Let them come for the signature. Let them know exactly whose hands made it."

Casper stared at him. For nine years her father had been a man who signed nothing, who kept the ledger clean and the opinions invisible, who had taught her — she had believed — that survival was anonymity. Now he stood at the press proposing to carve their name into the thing that would get them killed, and his face in the grey light was the calmest she had seen it since childhood.

"Do it," she said.

She composed the colophon herself, small and perfect, centered on the last leaf: *Set and printed in the night of the Council's entry, that the record might outrun the drums.* Then the shop name. Then, after a moment's hesitation, one line more, which she did not discuss with anyone: *For Aldous Renk, and for the child who remembers his face.*

She locked the forme. Tam pulled the first proof. The press sang its iron note, and outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Casper had been dreading all night: fifes. High, bright, disciplined, playing a victory march down the boulevard by the river — the vanguard arriving ahead of schedule, the parade beginning while half the city still slept, so that it would wake to find the future already marching past its windows.

"They're early," Jorun said. He was already moving, pulling the finished driest sheets off the rack, squaring them, binding the first complete sets with the twine they'd cut for the purpose. "The laundries open at six. Marta Kessin takes her cart to the fish market at half past. If the ferryman's boy runs the first bundle across before the bridge companies seal the crossing—" He worked as he talked, quick and precise, hope converted into logistics. "We need the distribution moving in the hour. Less."

"Then let's finish the run," Casper said, and turned back to the stone for the next forme, and her arms took up the ache again like an instrument taking up a theme.

They printed as the light strengthened. They printed as the fifes drew nearer and the drums resumed, closer now, close enough to feel in the floorboards, in the ink stone, in the fillings of her teeth. They printed as the first cheers went up in the square beyond the canal — thin, coerced-sounding cheers, the applause of people who had learned which sounds kept them safe — and as those cheers multiplied and hardened into something the organizers could report as enthusiasm.

Seven hundred and eighty. Eight hundred. Nine.

Casper lost count of her own body. There was only the rhythm — compose, lock, dampen, lay, pull, lift, hang — the liturgy of it, the sacred exhaustion of it, her father's shoulder against hers at the bar, Jorun's great hands feeding paper like a man feeding a fire he intended to outlast the winter. Once she looked up and saw tears on Tam's face and realized they were not grief, or not only grief. It was the look of a man hearing music he had forbidden himself for nine years.

At nine hundred and sixty, the last forme was locked. At nine hundred and eighty, the last sheet was pulled. At nine hundred and ninety-one, the press fell silent, and the three of them stood in the ringing quiet, ink to their elbows, sweat and lamp-black on their faces, surrounded by a thousand copies of the truth.

Tam laid both hands flat on the press bed, the way you'd rest them on the shoulders of an old friend.

"There," he said. "Signed."

* * *

It was Jorun who heard it first — not the parade, which was a roar now, a weather system of sound rolling toward the cathedral square, but underneath it, nearer, wrong: boots in a pattern no parade uses. Boots stopping at corners. Boots at the end of their own street.

He blew out the candles. In the sudden grey gloom he moved like a much younger man, sweeping sheets into sacking, handing bundles through the trap to the boy from the fish market who had appeared at the window exactly as arranged, silent, quick-handed. Bundle after bundle went out over the rooftops and down the drainpipe into the laundry cart below. The drying lines emptied. The racks emptied.

Casper stood at the window with Wren pressed against her side and looked down the length of Copper Lane.

At the far end, where the lane met the canal road, a file of soldiers had stopped. A civilian figure walked ahead of them, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, and even at that distance, even in the uncertain light, Casper knew the silhouette — the slight forward stoop, the scholar's walk, the man who lingered over ledgers.

Edvard Moll had come to see the presses of Vellmark. All of them. One last time.

"Casper," Tam said quietly behind her. "How many are still inside these walls?"

She counted fast. "Two hundred. Maybe two-fifty. The rest are gone — the market, the laundries, the ferry. The first sets crossed the river before the bridge closed." She turned from the window. Her heart was hammering, but her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady — steady as they had been all night, steady as type in a locked forme. "Whatever happens next, Father — it's printed. It's out. A thousand copies."

Below, at the head of the lane, Edvard Moll looked up at the Yarrow sign, and smiled, and gestured the soldiers forward.
