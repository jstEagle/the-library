## 20. Moll at the Door

The last sheets were still wet when the drums stopped.

Casper heard it first as an absence — the parade's rhythm that had thudded through the floorboards all night suddenly gone, and in its place a sound far worse: boots. Many of them, unhurried, in step. Not the scattered running of patrols chasing curfew-breakers through the Salt Quarter, but the measured approach of men who had been told exactly where to go and exactly what they would find when they got there.

She stood at the press with ink to her elbows, the two-hundred-and-ninth sheet in her hands, and watched Tam lift his head from the composing stone like a man hearing rain after drought — knowing it was too late for the crops, knowing it was too late for anything but standing in it.

"Wren," he said quietly.

"Loft," Casper said. "Go."

Her father moved faster than she had seen him move in years, up the ladder with the candle snuffed between finger and thumb, and by the time the knocking came — three heavy blows, then three more, the knock of authority rather than inquiry — Wren had vanished into the dark above the type cases, and Tam had descended again, smoothing his apron, arranging himself into the shape of an old printer surprised at his own door.

Casper laid the wet sheet face-down on the pile beneath the devotional folios. Two hundred and nine copies. She counted them the way a drowning woman counts breaths.

The door shook under a fourth blow.

"Open in the Council's name."

Tam opened it.

Edvard Moll stood in the grey end-of-night light with six soldiers behind him and the street empty beyond them, emptied, Casper realized, deliberately — the patrol must have turned the corner's residents indoors or into their windows, so that whatever happened here would happen without witnesses. Or rather, she thought, without witnesses who would be permitted to remain witnesses. Moll himself was immaculate: grey coat brushed, gloves clean, the small ledger he always carried tucked under one arm like a hymnal. Only his eyes betrayed the hour — red-rimmed, sleepless, bright with something that was not quite triumph and not quite fear.

"Master Yarrow," Moll said. "You are awake late."

"Printers keep printers' hours," Tam said. "Ink dries when it dries."

"So it does." Moll stepped inside uninvited, and the soldiers came behind him in a clatter that filled the shop from wall to wall, their presence shrinking it, their torchlight making the hanging sheets glow amber like relics. One of them moved to the back door without being ordered; another took up position at the foot of the loft ladder, and Casper felt the geometry of the room close around her daughter-hidden-in-the-rafters like a hand closing around a moth.

Moll walked slowly among the cases, trailing one gloved finger along the type racks, pausing at the ancient hand-press as though greeting an acquaintance. He had not changed since his last visit — same careful voice, same scholar's stoop, same habit of touching things as if ownership were a matter of proximity — except that now there were soldiers, and now there was no pretense of inventory.

"You know," he said conversationally, "I have spent my life among records. Birth rolls, tax ledgers, muster books, the minutes of councils great and petty. And I will tell you what thirty years of reading has taught me." He turned. "Every record is a confession. Every archive is a list of debts. The only question is who lives long enough to collect."

"We're simple tradespeople," Tam said. "Our debts are paper and lamp oil."

"Yes," Moll said softly. "Paper."

He crossed to the stack of finished work by the press — the devotional folios, *The Lamentations*, honest stock that had genuinely been printed this week and would be sold honestly come morning — and lifted the top copy. Beneath it, half-exposed where Casper's haste had misaligned the cover, lay a testimony sheet. The ink had dried matte. The header was legible even upside down:

*Testimony concerning the death of Aldous Renk, compositor of Vellmark, taken from living witnesses.*

Moll read it the way he did everything: completely, silently, and without any change of expression. Then he set it down with exaggerated gentleness, aligning its corners with the folio beneath, and looked at Casper.

"How many?"

Casper said nothing.

"Casper." He used her given name deliberately, the way he had since her childhood, when he had been a visitor at her father's table discussing fonts and tariffs over wine — before she had understood that the man who praised her father's discretion was cataloguing it. "I have read your hand in the shop ledger since you were nine years old. I know how you count stock. How many sheets left this building tonight, and into whose hands?"

"None have left," Tam began.

"Don't." Moll did not raise his voice; he lowered it, which was worse. "Master Yarrow, I have admired your caution for fifteen years. I have *depended* upon it. Do not spend my admiration on a lie I can disprove by breakfast." He drew off one glove, finger by finger. "Two hundred sheets, I would estimate, from the quantity of stock missing against your purchases. Perhaps fewer. It hardly matters. What matters is whether they are stacked in a cellar waiting for my men, or already moving through this city in baskets and hymnals and laundry bundles, in which case—" he paused, and for one moment the historian's mask slipped and Casper saw underneath it a tired man doing arithmetic he hated, "—in which case we are having a different conversation entirely."

*It is already moving.* Casper kept her face still and let him wonder. Jorun had left four hours ago with the first hundred sheets divided among six couriers — the ferryman's wife, the choir mistress at Saint Aldhelm's, the laundries on Tanner's Row. By dawn the words would be in forty places Moll could not search simultaneously. But Moll did not need to know how far the water had spread to know the dam had broken.

"The girl," Moll said.

The word landed in Casper's chest like a dropped type case.

"There was a child seen at your back door during the curfew storm. A neighbor — you know Vellmark's neighbors, Master Yarrow, better than I do, you've paid enough of them — reported lamplight at strange hours, voices, a small figure at a window." He replaced his glove. "A child of seven, perhaps eight. Dark-haired. The report says she was carried out of the Renk house the night it burned, though the fire rolls list no survivors."

"She isn't here," Casper said.

"No," Moll agreed pleasantly. "But she has been seen here. And I have soldiers, and soldiers are thorough, and there is a ladder in this room leading to a loft full of hiding places that I could search in ten minutes." He let the sentence hang. Then, gently, terribly: "Or I could walk out of this shop and never mention her name in any report."

* * *

He made the offer sitting down, which was somehow the most obscene part of it. He pulled the stool from beside the press — the stool where Casper had sat since childhood learning to justify a line of type — and folded himself onto it with his hands on his knees, a reasonable man proposing reasonable terms in a reasonable tone while armed men filled the room around him.

"Hear me fully before you answer," Moll said. "I am not offering you what you think I'm offering. I am not asking you to betray anyone. That hour has passed; I know it; you know it. The sheets are printed or distributed, the names are set, and no bargain struck tonight unsays them. I am a historian, Mistress Yarrow. I understand finality better than most men."

"Then what are you asking?"

"I am asking you to help me write the ending." His voice softened into something almost confiding. "The Council wins tomorrow. That is certain — the parade is already forming. And every victory needs its account. There will be an official history, commissioned, authorized, printed on presses I control. In it, Aldous Renk died in an accident of the civil disorder — regrettable, investigated, closed. The officers named in your little broadsheet will be decorated men with families and reputations, and your sheet will be called forgery, sedition, the work of foreign agents. That account will be written whether you assist me or not. My offer changes nothing about it."

"Then why make it?"

"Because of the *other* history." Moll leaned forward. "The one that survives in cellars and hymnals and grandmothers' memories. The unofficial one. You cannot stop it — fine, I accept that, I am not a fool — but you can *shape* it. Surrender the master galleys and the plates. Give me the names of your couriers. Testify, quietly, that you were coerced into printing falsehoods by agitators, and I will see that the underground account remembers you as a reluctant instrument rather than a willing one. Your father walks free. Your shop keeps its license. And the child—" he paused, letting the word sit between them, "—the child goes free as well. Out of the city, upriver, with papers, to whoever claims her. Alive, Mistress Yarrow. Fed. Educated, even, if the right people are grateful. Renk's blood ends its line in safety instead of in a cell or a ditch."

He rose, and his voice took on the cadence of a man closing a sale he believed in.

"You cannot save the truth by dying for it. You can only decide what the truth costs, and who pays. I am offering you a price that is almost nothing — a few names, some metal plates, a statement you'll sign once and never think of again. Against that: a child's life. Weigh it. You are a printer; you understand weighing. Ink against blood. Paper against flesh. Choose the flesh."

* * *

The silence afterward was very long, and in it Casper found herself strangely calm.

She thought about her father's letter, burned eleven days ago in the stove behind the composing stone — the first lie she had ever caught him telling, the seam of the whole garment. She thought of Tam setting his own confession into type three nights ago, weeping openly at the case, each letter a small act of penance. She thought of Jorun's broken grin saying *a press that prints nothing true is just furniture*. She thought of Ivet Sarn dying across the river with an unsigned page under his hand, refusing to the last to claim certainty he didn't possess, and of her own refusal to forge his signature even to strengthen the case — because a record built on one lie was a house built on one rotten joist.

And she thought of Wren in the loft above her, breathing as quietly as a seven-year-old could breathe, listening to every word.

"Mistress Yarrow," Moll prompted. "The terms are generous. I will not improve them."

"You're right about one thing," Casper said.

His eyebrows rose slightly — invitation.

"You're right that every record is a confession. You taught me that yourself, years ago, at my father's table, when I thought you were only talking about bookkeeping." She wiped her hands slowly on her apron, leaving two dark crescents. "So let me tell you what your offer confesses. It confesses that you know the sheet is true. If it were forgery — if it were sedition, if it were the work of foreign agents — you would not need my galleys. You'd burn the lot and print your rebuttal. You want the plates because destroyed originals leave questions, and you are too good a historian to leave questions. You're not buying silence, Edvard. You're buying corroboration. And the fact that you'd trade a child's life for it—"

"A child's life is precisely what I'm *preserving*—"

"—tells me everything about what your official history is worth." She picked up the top testimony sheet from the stack, still smelling faintly of oil and lamp-black, and held it where the torchlight caught the header. "Read it again. *Taken from living witnesses.* Living ones, Edvard. Plural. You can take my plates, my hands, my father, this whole street — and somewhere in Vellmark tomorrow someone who saw what happened to Aldous Renk will read this and recognize their own memory in it, and they will say so, to someone, sometime, in a kitchen or a tavern or a confessional, because that is what people do with true things. You cannot arrest remembering. Believe me. I've watched my family try for twenty years."

Moll's composure held, but something behind it shifted — the arithmetic again, running and rerunning. "You are choosing ruin."

"I'm choosing the version of events that outlasts you."

"The Council will outlast me. The Council will outlast everyone in this room."

"Regimes don't," Casper said. "Records do. Ask any historian." She let the smallest pause fall. "Oh — forgive me. You *are* one."

One of the soldiers laughed — a short, startled bark — and smothered it instantly, and Casper saw Moll register it, file it, add it to some private ledger of costs.

He stood very straight then, and the weary man vanished entirely, and what remained was the instrument of the Provisional Council in its final, most dangerous form: not cruel, not stupid, simply certain that order justified anything, including itself.

"Search the loft," he said.

* * *

They went up in twos, boots hammering the ladder, torches sweeping the rafters. Casper stood beneath the loft with her heart going like a press at speed and her face arranged into nothing at all, while Tam gripped the edge of the composing stone so hard the wood creaked. Above her head she tracked the sounds: cases dragged, sacking thrown aside, the scrape of the loose panel behind the third rack—

—and then a soldier's voice, flat with disappointment: "Empty, sir. Dust and old stock. Some rat droppings. No child."

Casper did not let herself blink. *The coal chute,* she thought. *Jorun's route. She went out the way I taught her, the night of the search — or Jorun came back for her.* She would not know until later which mercy it had been; she refused, in that moment, to guess, because guessing was a luxury and refusal required all of her.

Moll listened to the report with his head tilted, and for a long moment Casper believed — hoped — that some portion of him was relieved. Historians, she thought wildly, must be the only men who hate being right.

"Very well." He straightened his coat. "Master Yarrow, you and your daughter will accompany these officers for questioning regarding seditious publication. The premises will be sealed pending confiscation." He turned at the door, and his eyes found hers one last time, and there was no gloating in them — only a kind of bleak professional curiosity, a man watching a rare document burn and trying to memorize it. "For what it is worth, Mistress Yarrow — and it is worth little — I believe you. I believe every word of your broadsheet. I have believed it since the day I inventoried this shop and found a page of Renk's last pamphlet hidden in your father's earliest ledger, and said nothing, and hated myself efficiently for fifteen years." He put on his hat. "That is what serving power does to a historian. Remember it kindly, if your underground ever writes me down."

"Is that a request?" Casper asked.

"It's a deposition," said Edvard Moll, and gave the order to march.

The soldiers took Casper's arms. They took her father's. As they pushed her through the doorway into the grey morning — the drums beginning again, distant, for the parade, for the victory, for the new regime's first sunrise — Casper looked back once at her burning-candle of a shop, at the stack of two hundred and nine sheets sitting in plain sight among the psalms because Moll had never thought to look twice at what he assumed was safe.

Behind them, up the hill, the bells of Saint Aldhelm's began to ring the hour.

And in laundries and taverns, in choir lofts and ferry houses, in a hundred pockets across the waking city, the truth was already moving — faster than boots, faster than drums, faster than any historian could run.
