## 21. Ashes and Paper

The fire began in the type loft, where the galleys had been hidden for so long that Wren thought of the smell of ink and wood shavings as the smell of safety.

She was in the cellar of the Yarrow shop when the first crack came — a sound like a knuckle popping, enormous, and then another, and then a groaning as if the whole house were a man waking from a long sleep and finding himself in pain. She had been sitting on the coal step with her knees drawn up, counting the coins in Ivet Sarn's purse for perhaps the hundredth time. Eleven coins. One of them was bent at the edge, bitten by someone long ago, and Wren liked to press her thumbnail into the dent and imagine whose teeth it had been. It was a game she played to keep herself from listening to the sounds above her: the creak of the great hand-press working through the night, the shuffle of Casper's feet on the stairs, the low murmur of Tam Yarrow's voice reading aloud to check his own setting, word by word, like a man praying in a language he had only just learned.

She had learned, in these weeks, to measure the world by its sounds. The drumbeat from the parade square, steady as a heart. The boots in the street, always more of them now, always heavier. The bell of the Ferryman's chapel tolling six, which meant Jorun Beck's runners were already moving with their bundles wrapped in laundry and hymnal covers, out through the laundries and up into the choir lofts, along routes drawn on paper so thin it could be swallowed if the soldiers stopped you. And beneath all of it, the rhythm of the press — *thump, slide, thump* — the heartbeat of the shop itself, the sound that meant the truth was still being made, sheet after sheet after sheet.

Wren did not understand all of what they printed. She understood some of it. Her grandfather's name was in it — *Aldous Renk*, set in letters taller than her thumb — and the words *murdered* and *ordered* and *the night of the twelfth*. There was a page about her grandfather's last pamphlet, and a paragraph about a printer named Tam Yarrow who had been paid for his silence, and Wren knew, without anyone explaining it to her, that this paragraph was Tam's confession, and that he had wept while setting it, and that Casper had stood beside him at the case with her hand on his shoulder and said nothing, because there are moments when silence is not cowardice but company.

And there was a page about her. Or nearly about her — about a child carried out of a burning print shop seven years ago, carried by a dying clerk named Ivet Sarn, who had walked through curfew storms and checkpoint streets with her bundled against his chest, who had left her at a stranger's door with eleven coins and four words: *forget my face, remember.*

Wren remembered his face. That was the one thing she refused to do, though she had promised. She kept it folded up inside her the way she kept the bent coin in the purse: private, worn smooth by handling, hers.

Now the ceiling above her head groaned again, and ash began to sift down through the floorboards like gray snow, and Wren understood that the sounds had changed. The press had stopped. The footsteps overhead were running.

* * *

Later — much later, years later, when she would tell this story and retell it until it wore smooth as the coin — Wren would try to order the next hour into something like sense, and she would fail. Fire does not make sense. Fire is a mob; it has no argument, only appetite.

She remembered climbing the cellar stairs two at a time into heat. She remembered the loft door standing open and light pouring out of it that was not lamplight but orange and alive and roaring, and the smoke hanging in it like curtains in a window of hell. She remembered Casper's face appearing out of that brightness, black with soot except where tears had cut two clean tracks down her cheeks, and Casper shouting words that the fire ate before they reached her — something about the street, about going, about *now* —

She remembered Tam Yarrow.

He came down the burning stair with a bundle of damp sheets pressed to his mouth and nose, an old man moving with the desperate speed of the young, and he caught her up under the arms as if she weighed nothing, as if she were a bundle of stock to be carried clear of harm. He smelled of ink and sweat and smoke, and underneath all of it, faintly, of the lavender water he used on his beard every morning, the small vanity of a careful man.

"Listen to me," he said, and his voice was calm in a way that frightened her worse than the flames. "You know the coal chute. Behind the bin. You've watched us use it."

She nodded. She couldn't speak.

"Down it, out into the yard, over the wall to the cooper's lane. Don't stop. Don't look back. Whatever you hear behind you—" His hands tightened on her arms, and for one moment his composure broke open like a dropped plate, and she saw everything inside him: the shame, the bought years, the long penance of ink, and love, fierce and late-arriving and entirely unearned and given anyway. "Whatever you hear, you keep running, do you hear me? Because you're carrying them now. You're carrying all of us. The sheets go out tonight or we burned for nothing, child, and I will not have burned for nothing."

"Ivet said forget his face," Wren whispered. "Should I forget yours?"

Tam Yarrow looked at her then, and something passed over his face that she had no name for yet, though one day she would call it peace.

"No," he said. "Remember mine. Remember all of ours. That's the work now. Go."

She went. Behind the coal bin the chute opened into darkness, and she slid down it into cold and filth, and came out in the yard on her hands and knees among cinders, and scrambled for the wall.

From the top of the wall — she was halfway over it, splinters biting her palms, the cooper's lane gaping below her — she heard the roof beam go. The whole sky filled with orange. Sparks rose in a column like prayers, like a thousand copies of something ascending all at once, and the heat pushed against her back like a great hand, and somewhere inside the roar she heard Casper scream her father's name, once, twice, a third time that broke apart in the middle.

Wren did not look back. He had told her not to look back. But looking back and hearing are different things, and no instruction on earth could have closed her ears.

What she heard was this: the beam falling, and beneath it, impossibly, a man's voice still giving orders — *the girl, get the girl clear, don't mind me* — and then nothing, and then the soldiers arriving too late, boots and shouting and the hiss of water that would save nothing worth saving.

She dropped into the cooper's lane and ran.

* * *

The city took her in the way cities take in small dirty children at night: without comment.

She ran through lanes that stank of smoke and river, past shuttered windows behind which she could feel eyes, past a barricade of overturned carts where men with staves watched her pass and let her pass, because even in Vellmark, even on the worst night of its history, a soot-black child running alone is a thing that opens doors rather than closing them.

She found Jorun Beck by following the ballads. That was how Casper had told her to find him, if ever she was alone, if ever the worst happened: *go where the songs are sung loudest and ask for the man with the burned hands.* So she ran toward music — a tavern called the Drowned Anchor, near the ferry stairs, where through a fogged window she could see lamplight and hear voices raised in a song she almost knew, a song about a printer who told the truth and died for it, a song that had not existed three days ago and now lived in forty throats at once.

The sheets had gone out. Even as the Yarrow shop burned, they were moving through the city like blood through a body. A laundry girl had stuffed them into baskets of wet linen. A ferryman poled them across the river sewn into his coat lining. In the choir lofts of three chapels, singers tucked them between the pages of their psalters, so that when the congregation opened to the appointed hymn, they found instead the names of three officers of the Provisional Council and the date of Aldous Renk's murder and the words of the witnesses, and read, and read again, and copied by hand what they could not carry away.

Inside the Drowned Anchor, the singing stopped when she pushed open the door. Forty faces turned. Forty faces saw: a child of seven, gray with ash, bleeding at the palms, wearing grief the way other children wore Sunday clothes.

Jorun Beck came around the end of the bar slowly, the way you approach a bird with a broken wing. His hands were scarred exactly as promised, old burns gone silver. He crouched in front of her.

"You're Renk's granddaughter," he said. Not a question. "You're Wren."

"The shop," Wren said. Her voice came out very small. "The shop is burning. Mister Tam went back for—" and here the sentence failed her, because finishing it made it true, and she wanted, just for a moment longer, to live in the unfinished version where maybe he had gotten out, where maybe Casper had pulled him free, where maybe tomorrow she would sit in the type loft again and count coins and listen to the press beat like a heart.

Jorun gathered her up against his chest. Around the room, grown men and women were crying openly, and no one was ashamed of it, and the tavern keeper began setting out bread and hot wine without anyone asking, and Wren understood dimly, through the fog of her own loss, that she had run out of a burning building and into something that had never existed in Vellmark before: a public, a people bound together by pages, a body of strangers who had all read the same truth in the same night.

"Where is Casper?" Jorun asked softly, above her head.

"They took her." This from the doorway — the laundry girl, breathless, her basket still on her hip. "I saw it from the wash-yard. Moll himself walked her out. She wasn't fighting. She was watching the fire." The girl's voice shook. "She was watching the fire like she was making sure it got all of it."

*All of it.* The decoys, the false ledgers, the empty galleys stacked in the loft as offerings. Casper had fed the fire the way her father had once fed the Council — with paper — but where his silences had been bought, hers were spent deliberately, every scrap of burnable falsehood surrendered so that the one thing that mattered was already leagues away, folded into linen, sewn into coats, sleeping in psalters.

Jorun held Wren tighter.

"Then it's done," he said. "God forgive us, and keep them — it's done. They can arrest the printers. They can't arrest the reading."

But outside, down the street, the drums were still beating for the victory parade, and the fires of the seized shops glowed along the river like a string of fallen stars, and the Council's historians were already awake in their offices, dipping their pens, beginning the work of the other story — the story with no murdered reformist, no bought silence, no child at a door. Two histories now ran through Vellmark, side by side, like two rivers under one bridge, and only one of them had witnesses.

Wren, exhausted past weeping, slept in the crook of Jorun Beck's arm, and dreamed of her grandfather's face, and Ivet Sarn's face, and Tam Yarrow's face, and Casper's — a gallery of the dead and the taken, hung in the dark behind her eyes.

When she woke at dawn, gray light in the smoke-hazed windows, the tavern was full of people copying the testimony out by hand.

They asked her to read it aloud. She was seven years old, and her voice shook, and she mispronounced *deposition,* and no one corrected her, and every soul in the room listened as though the words were coming down from a mountain.

She began: "Let it be known that the following is testimony-in-progress, unfinished, unsigned, and true."

Outside, the drums went on beating. It did not matter anymore. The paper was loose in the world, and paper, unlike printers, unlike fathers, unlike shops, does not burn twice.
