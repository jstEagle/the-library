## 22. A Letter for the Uniform

The gate of Vellmark Prison opened on a morning that smelled of rain and river mud, the same smell Casper Yarrow had carried with her through nine years of stone corridors, and for a long moment she simply stood on the far side of it, letting the wind touch her face, uncertain whether her legs remembered how to walk without a wall to follow.

She had imagined this day so many times that its arrival felt like a forgery — a page set in imitation type, close enough to fool the eye at arm's length but wrong somehow under close inspection. In her imaginings there had always been music, crowds, Wren running toward her across the wet cobbles. What there actually was, was a guard who would not meet her eye, a bundle of her few possessions tied in cloth, and a city she no longer recognized.

Vellmark had changed hands again. She had followed it from inside as best she could — scraps of news passed cell to cell, whispered during the exercise hour, smuggled in the bindings of permitted devotional books, because even now, even after everything, words still traveled by paper or not at all. The Provisional Council had eaten itself. The officers whose names she had set into type all those years ago had turned on one another, and the historians who had rewritten her testimony had been rewritten in their turn, and then the river people had risen, and the choir lofts had sung the wrong hymns on purpose, and one gray dawn the Council's banner had come down from the customs house like a rotten tooth pulled.

Now there was a new government, or something calling itself one, and among its first acts had been to open the prisons. Not out of mercy, she suspected. Out of the same instinct that had driven Edvard Moll to commission his official history: the need to be seen doing the correct thing, early, before anyone could say otherwise.

She walked down through the terraces toward the river. Her body was thinner than the body she remembered; her hands were the same hands, though, ink-stained in the creases in a way that no amount of prison lye had ever fully scoured, and she found herself looking at them as an old friend looks at another across a long separation.

The print shop was gone. Of course it was gone. She had known it was gone; she had heard the fire described to her during her trial, had listened with a strange detachment as a prosecutor with a careful voice explained what the accelerants had done to the loft stairs, the type cases, the ancient hand-press her grandfather had bought before she was born. But knowing a thing and standing in front of its absence were different trades entirely. The lot where the shop had stood was fenced now, and weeds grew up through the scorched foundation stones, and someone had left flowers against the fence — dried, replaced, replaced again, a small economy of remembrance maintained by hands she could not see.

She stood there a long while. A woman passing with a market basket slowed, looked at her, looked at the fence, and said nothing, but touched two fingers to her brow in the old printer's salute — the gesture apprentices used to make when a master passed — and walked on.

So they knew her face. Or they knew a face, and had decided it was hers.

* * *

They had canonized her testimony. That was the word the new government's own broadsheets used, *canonized*, printed in their fat black letters beside engravings of Aldous Renk looking nobler than any man who had ever smelled of ink could have wished to look. The depositions Ivet Sarn had dictated in his dying weeks were reproduced in schoolbooks. The names of the three officers were carved into a memorial wall near the ferry landing, and children on their way to lessons touched the carved letters for luck, not understanding — how could they understand? — that those letters had been set first by a frightened girl in a loft full of hidden galleys, working by candle stub while drums beat out a victory parade in the square.

It should have satisfied her. Some nights in her cell, imagining release, she had told herself that satisfaction was all she wanted: the record alive, the names legible, the lie starved of air.

But the record they had made of it was not quite her record. They had smoothed it. Sarn's unsigned final page — the one she had refused to forge, the one printed honestly marked *testimony-in-progress* — was now presented in the official editions as complete and signed, a small forgery committed in the name of honoring a man who had refused all forgery. Renk's last words had been polished until they gleamed; she remembered them rougher, more human, more afraid. And Tam — her father, who had died pulling a child clear of their burning shop, who had wept setting his own confession into type — Tam appeared in the canonical version either not at all or as a nameless loyal printer, his complicity sanded away, his redemption erased along with his guilt, because guilt made the story less clean and the new government wanted clean stories the way Moll had wanted them.

Memory outlives whoever holds power, she had believed in the loft with the presses running and the door about to break. She still believed it. But she had learned, in nine years of stone, that memory does not survive power untouched. It survives the way a sheet survives the press — marked, pressed, sometimes torn, never quite the document it was in the compositor's hands.

That was the work now. That was the work that remained.

* * *

Wren came to her on the third evening, at the rooms the new government had grudgingly provided — a printer's flat above a chandler's shop, chosen, Casper suspected, because someone in the ministry had a sense of irony. Wren was sixteen now, tall, dark-eyed, with Renk's stubborn jaw, and she did not run across the room the way Casper had once imagined. She stopped in the doorway, and the two of them looked at each other across nine years and one fire, and then Wren crossed the floor in four strides and held her so hard that Casper's ribs ached, and neither of them said anything for a very long time, because there are sentences that only silence can set properly.

"They teach you in school," Wren said finally, her face pressed against Casper's shoulder. "They teach us your testimony like scripture. We recite the dates. There's an examination."

"Do you pass?"

"I pass." A pause. "I hate it."

Casper drew back and looked at her. "Hate it?"

"It's dead when they do that." Wren's voice had the particular ferocity of the young defending something they love from being loved badly. "Grandfather — Aldous Renk — he's a statue now. You're a chapter. Jorun Beck is a footnote, and he lost an eye in that cellar and taught me half of everything I know. It's all so *tidy*. And none of it smells of ink."

Casper laughed — the first real laugh in nine years, surprising her own mouth — and put her hands on either side of the girl's face. "Then you'll have to make it untidy," she said. "That's the inheritance. Not the clean version. The smudged one."

* * *

The young officer came on a Thursday, in the rain.

She heard him on the stairs — boots, unhurried, the tread of someone official but nervous — and by the time he knocked she had already decided how the interview would go, because she had conducted a hundred versions of it in her head over nine years, against Moll, against prosecutors, against the endless imaginary tribunals of the sleepless.

He was very young. Twenty, perhaps twenty-two, in a uniform so new the creases had not yet fallen out of it, carrying his cap in his hands like a schoolboy and a document case under his arm like a burden. Rain dripped off his shoulders onto the chandler's landing.

"Madam Yarrow." He swallowed. "I'm Lieutenant Ansel Corve. I — thank you for receiving me. I know you receive no one."

"You received me," she said. "Come in before the stair rots under you."

Her rooms were spare: a table, two chairs, a window over the street, and — the government had allowed her this, the single concession she had requested — a small composing stick and a case of worn type, with which she set sentences for no purpose but the setting, keeping her hand true. The officer's eyes went to it immediately, the way a musician's eyes go to an instrument, and something in his face loosened.

"You were printing," he said. "Just now. Before I knocked."

"I am always printing. It's the only prayer I know."

He sat when she indicated the chair, perched on its edge, and opened the document case, and then did not take anything out of it. His hands were not quite steady.

"Madam Yarrow, I need to tell you why I've come, and I need you to let me finish, because if I stop I won't start again." He drew a breath. "I was born the year after the fire. Everything I learned about the fall of the Council, I learned from the schoolbooks. From the memorial wall. From the official edition of *A True Account of the Murder of Aldous Renk and the Testimony of Casper Yarrow*." He said the title carefully, bitterly, the way one says a word one has recently discovered is false. "I believed it. All of it. I became an officer of the new government partly because of it — because it seemed there had been a great wrong, and men had died to right it, and I wanted to stand on the right side of whatever came next."

"And?"

"And three months ago I was assigned to the archives. Records seizure duty." He reached into the case at last and drew out a pamphlet — octavo, cheap paper, water-stained, its edges soft with handling. "We were cataloguing material from the Moll years. Confiscated documents. And I found this."

He laid it on the table between them, gently, the way one lays down something that might still be alive.

Casper looked at it and her breath went out of her slowly, because she knew it. She knew it the way a mother knows a child's cry in a crowd of cries. Cheap paper. Two columns. The compositor's hand unmistakably hers — she could see her own habits in it, the tight leading, the way she had set the small caps for the officers' names, the slightly crooked rule between the deposition text and Sarn's marginal notes, crooked because Jorun had been holding the galley edge steady while soldiers' boots shook the cellar floor above.

One of the thousand copies. One of the sheets that had gone out into the rain-soaked night while the door broke behind her. This one had been confiscated — seized from some reader, some laundry, some hymnal, some choir loft — and had lain in Moll's archive for nine years, waiting.

"Why," she said quietly, "are you showing me this instead of burning it?"

"Because of what's written on it." He turned the pamphlet over. On the reverse, in pencil, in a hand she did not recognize, someone had written — densely, furiously, in the margins and across the columns — corrections. Dates checked against other dates. Names expanded. And at the foot of the last page, in larger strokes, almost gouged into the paper:

*THE OFFICIAL EDITION LIES. THE FINAL PAGE WAS NEVER SIGNED. THE PRINTER'S FATHER SET HIS OWN CONFESSION AND IT WAS BURNED OUT OF THE RECORD. FIND THE SMUDGED COPIES. COMPARE THEM. THE TRUTH IS IN THE INK.*

"That note has been in the archive fifteen years?" Casper asked. "Who wrote it?"

"We don't know. It was catalogued as anonymous annotation. But Madam Yarrow—" the lieutenant leaned forward, and now his voice dropped, urgent, frightened, alive— "there are others. I've found eleven copies in the confiscation vaults. Eleven different readers, eleven different sets of corrections, over the years. People compared them. People *kept* comparing them, quietly, for a generation, while the schools recited the tidy version. There's a whole shadow edition of your testimony, built by strangers, in pencil, in the margins. And the government is about to publish a new commemorative edition for the anniversary, and it is going to repeat every lie the last one told, and I—" he stopped, steadied himself— "I don't want to stand on the wrong side of whatever comes next. Again. Everyone says that. I'm saying it and meaning it."

Casper picked up the pamphlet. Her copy. Someone's copy. Under her fingers the paper was soft as cloth, and she thought of the night it was printed — the sacred exhaustion, the drums, the door — and of Tam's tears falling into the case as he set his confession, and of Jorun's ruined eye, and of Wren at seven years old clutching a sealed letter, whispering *forget my face*.

Nobody had forgotten. That was the thing nobody in power ever understood. Nobody had forgotten.

"The final page," she said. "Sarn's unsigned draft. I have it in my head. Every line. Nine years of reciting it to myself in the dark to keep it from dying."

The young officer's eyes were bright. "Will you help me? Will you set it down — the true version, the smudged version, with the corrections, with your father named — before the commemorative edition locks the lie in place for another generation?"

Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and somewhere down the street a hand-bell rang the hour, and Casper Yarrow looked at the composing stick on her table, and at the water-stained pamphlet that had traveled further and lasted longer than any regime that tried to own it, and felt the old vertigo, the old resolve, rising in her like water finding its level.

"Lieutenant Corve," she said, "do you know how to distribute ten thousand sheets without the ministry noticing?"

His face went white, then fierce. "No, madam."

"Good," said Casper Yarrow, reaching for her type. "Neither did we, the first time. It's printed, boy. Now we learn to print it truer."
