## 3. What the Ledger Says

The rain had stopped by morning, which was the house's way of pretending nothing had happened, the way a man whistles after he's said something unforgivable.

I came down before the household stirred and found the girl asleep in the stillroom cot where Mrs. Petch had dumped her, wrapped in a horse blanket that smelled of liniment and other people's winters. She slept like someone who had learned already — at four years old — that sleep was a thing you did thoroughly because you couldn't be sure of it again. Her thumb was hooked in the collar of her coat as though she meant to keep hold of even her clothes.

*Forget this face*, the note had said.

I had looked at it for a long time instead. That's not defiance, exactly. Defiance is throwing the note in the fire. What I did was worse: I committed a crime with no witness but myself. I memorized her. The chilblain-red patch on her left ear like a dropped berry. The gap between her front teeth you could see when she breathed through her mouth. A small crescent scar at her hairline, old and healed, the kind a child gets from falling against a fender. I filed her away in the one place no order could reach, and then I went about my duties like a man who had done nothing at all, because that is what servants do. We are trained from boyhood to carry things without letting them show on our faces. Coal, trunks, corpses of birds off the drive. Secrets are just heavier freight.

But by the second day the freight was shifting. Edith Marchmont had given orders about the girl — feed her, keep her out of sight, say nothing to nobody — and then vanished into her study with the door shut, and the whole household moved around that closed door like water around a stone. Nobody asked who the child was. That was the strange part. In a house full of women who chained themselves to railings and shouted in public squares, nobody asked a single question. Which told me they all knew something, or suspected enough not to want it confirmed, or were so practiced at not-asking that it had become their native language.

Me, I wanted confirmation. I wanted it the way a tooth wants a tongue.

So on the third morning, while the house was at breakfast and I was officially cleaning the Daimler, I let myself in through the study's garden window, because a driver has grease on his hands and therefore license to go where boots shouldn't.

* * *

You can tell a great deal about a family from how they file their sins. The Marchmonts filed theirs beautifully. Edith's correspondence alone ran to four cabinets — letters from prison governors, from barristers, from other women whose names I'd read in newspapers while men beside me on trains tutted. There was a drawer of newspaper cuttings, each one trimmed square as a stamp. There were photographs: Edith young and furious; Edith older and furious with better tailoring; a daughter I'd never heard mentioned, laughing on a lawn somewhere, holding a dog. The daughter had Edith's jaw and none of her armor. She looked like a woman who hadn't yet learned the world would charge her for smiling.

No photographs of children younger than that. No christening records loose on the desk. Nothing about a girl with a berry-red ear.

It was under the floor of the fourth cabinet — literally; a board lifted, and beneath it a tin box — that I found the ledger. Not a household account book. Those sit open on hall tables everywhere, honest as weather. This was an estate book, bound in green calf gone soft at the corners, with *Marchmont Properties & Holdings, 1871–* tooled on the spine. Property. Holdings. Words that sound like fields and houses until you learn what else a family can hold.

I told myself I'd only look. Reading letters before delivering them is my private sin, small and victimless, like taking sugar cubes. Opening a locked-away ledger is a different animal entirely, and I knew it, and I opened it anyway. Curiosity in a servant is treated as a disease. Very well. I was diseased. I turned pages.

The early entries were what you'd expect: acreage, tenancies, rents collected in guineas, a dispute over drainage with a farmer named Hobbs that ran three years and was won on a technicality. Then, 1881, a page headed *Inventory — Ashcombe Estate, taken upon the death of Charles Marchmont Esq.*

Inventory. As though the dead man had been a ship.

I read the list the way you read any inventory, half-asleep. Oak sideboard, one. Portraits, seven. Carriage horses, four. And then:

*Ravensworth, T., gardener, aged 44, and issue — L., aged 3. Valued with the estate per entail.*

And below it, in a clerk's tidy hand, a figure. A sum of money. My father and I, priced together, like the oak sideboard and the carriage horses. Like the portraits. Seven portraits, two Ravensworths.

I sat down on the floor of Edith Marchmont's study, among her dust and her secrets, and I did not move for a long time.

Here is the thing I need you to understand, because it took me years to understand it myself: it wasn't the money that hollowed me. It was the arithmetic underneath it. I had grown up in this house believing I was employed by it. Employed. A word with edges to it. An employee can leave. An employee's father could have packed his tools and walked down the drive and been free, and instead my father had measured orchard trees for forty years and taught me to count everything by what could be carried, and I had thought — God help me, I had thought — that he stayed out of loyalty.

Loyalty. Or the entail. One word each, and only one of them appears in a legal document.

Every kindness reorganized itself around that entry like iron filings around a magnet. The Christmas shillings from old Mr. Marchmont — wages, or maintenance of stock? Mrs. Petch teaching me my letters in the kitchen because "a boy ought to read" — was that charity, or was it a man improving his property, the way you'd mend a roof? Edith letting me drive her, listen to her, stand in rooms where cabinet ministers' wives whispered — had she ever once, in all those years, thought of me as anything other than an asset with good posture? When she called me *Lior* instead of *Ravensworth*, warm as a hand on the shoulder, was that intimacy? Or was it what you call a thing you own, the way farmers name cows?

You'll say: but the entail was decades old, surely lapsed, surely nobody alive thought in those terms. You're probably right. That's the cruelty of it. They didn't need to think in those terms anymore. The thinking had been done for them, done so long ago it had hardened into furniture, into habit, into the simple unexamined fact of the world: some people are the house, and some people are *in* the house. I had spent twenty-three years being furniture that believed itself family. And the worst of it — the part that made me laugh, actually, sitting there on the floor, one short ugly laugh that startled me — was that I had found the girl's origins nowhere in that book either. Whatever she was to these people, she wasn't in the inventory. For one mad moment I envied her that. Then I remembered the note. *Forget this face.* Being outside the ledger isn't freedom. Sometimes it's just a smaller kind of owned.

I copied the entry. Don't ask me why; a man copies things when he doesn't yet know what they're for. Pencil, onto the back of a garage invoice, in my careful chauffeur's hand: the date, the heading, the line with our names, the sum. Then I put the ledger back under its board, closed the tin, pressed the floorboard home, and climbed out the garden window with my heart going like a governor set too high.

* * *

That afternoon I polished the Daimler until it insulted the sunlight, and I thought about my father's hands. Big hands, cracked at every knuckle, hands that had grafted apple stock so patiently you'd swear the trees were being reasoned with rather than cut. He used to walk me down the orchard rows and make me count aloud. Not trees — he didn't care about trees. He made me count what we could carry. One bag of apples, one blanket, one photograph if it fit. "Everything you love," he'd say, "ought to weigh less than your strength," and I'd thought it a gardener's proverb, a bit of orchard wisdom, charming and poor. It wasn't a proverb. It was a contingency plan. He knew what the ledger said. He'd known since before I could talk, and he'd raised me light on purpose, portable, ready to be carried out of a life at a moment's notice — except he'd died before the moment came, and I'd stayed, heavy with belongings and gratitude and the idiot conviction that I belonged.

Belonged. There's a word that deserves to be taken out and shot. Belonging, I now understood, is a coin with two faces, and the reverse reads *belongs to*. I had spent my life hearing only the obverse.

The girl appeared at the garage door near dusk, barefoot on the cold flags, having escaped whatever attic arrangement Mrs. Petch had devised. She stood there watching me work the polishing cloth along the wing, and I watched her back, and neither of us said anything, because what is there to say between two entries that aren't in the book?

Then she said, "Are you the one that's going to take me away?"

"Not me," I said. "A lady will. A ship."

"Ships sink," she observed, with the serene authority of the very young.

"This one won't."

"How do you know?"

"I don't," I said, and she considered this, found it acceptable — honesty apparently being novel enough in her short life to count as comfort — and padded off across the yard.

I looked at my hands. Grease to the wrist. Gardener's son, chauffeur's trade, valued per entail at a figure I could recite now from memory alongside the price of seven oil paintings. Somewhere upstairs Edith Marchmont's door was still shut, and behind it, I'd have bet the Daimler, plans were moving — papers, lists, arrangements — plans that had a word attached, and the word was *family*, and I knew with a certainty I couldn't explain to anyone, including myself, that I was not going to be on that list. Assets don't get evacuated. Assets get left. That's practically their definition.

Well then. If the ledger wouldn't make me kin, I'd see what a pen could do.

There were travel documents to be thought about. There was a girl with a berry-red ear who needed a name, a birthplace, a history — needed them forged clean enough to pass a dockside inspection by a man with a rubber stamp and no imagination. And there was, somewhere in this county, a shipping office with berths going cheap to anyone with paper and confidence.

I wiped my hands on the rag, slow, thinking it through the way Father taught me to count: what can be carried. A child can be carried. A lie can be carried. A grudge — a grudge weighs nothing at all, and I intended to pack three.

From the house, faint through the evening air, came the telephone bell ringing in the hall, once, twice — urgent, insistent, the sound of the world arriving whether a household is ready for it or not. I heard Mrs. Petch answer, heard her voice climb, heard the study door open at last above me with a creak I felt in my teeth.

Edith Marchmont came down the steps into the yard still holding the telephone cord's worth of bad news in her face, and the first words she said to me in three days were not about the girl at all.

"Get the car round, Lior," she said. "They've declared it. It's war."
