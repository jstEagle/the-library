## 4. Edith's War Room

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Wars, I have since learned, do not arrive on Sundays like respectable grief; they come midweek, when the accounts are open and the bread is proving and no one has time to be dramatic.

I was polishing the Daimler when the boy on the bicycle rattled up the drive, and I knew before he spoke. You learn to read the shape of bad news the way you read weather — the boy sat his saddle too straight, too careful, the way a man carries a tray of glasses he cannot afford to drop.

"Telegram for Mrs. Marchmont."

"I'll take it."

"Sign here."

I signed. My hand did not shake. I noted that it didn't, and filed the observation away with all the other things I had begun collecting about myself — small exhibits in a case I was building against my own innocence.

Edith Marchmont took the telegram from me standing in the hall, still wearing her gardening apron, dirt under her nails the color of old rust. She read it twice. Her face did what her face always did, which was nothing — a face trained out of its own expressions sometime around the turn of the century, I'd have guessed, back when she learned that a woman who shows what she thinks hands her enemies the knife handle first.

"Lior," she said. "Cancel everything for Thursday. And send word to the housekeeper at Ashcombe that we'll want beds made up. Six of them."

"Six, ma'am?"

"Did I stutter?"

She hadn't. Edith Marchmont had never once stuttered in her life; I'd have wagered the Daimler on it. But six beds meant six people, and the Marchmonts were three, counting the child nobody named.

* * *

You will want to know what I saw, those first days of August, and I will tell you honestly: I saw a woman build a fortress out of a drawing room.

The maps arrived first — rolled, oilcloth-backed, smelling of ink and government — and then the men, two of them from some ministry whose name Edith pronounced as though it owed her money. The morning room became a planning office by Wednesday noon. The chintz chairs were pushed to the walls like witnesses. A long table went down the center, and on it spread the coast of France in pieces, pinned at the corners, curling slightly at Calais where the humidity got under the paper.

I carried tea into that room four times a day, and I listened. Not eavesdropping — or not only. A driver hears things. It is one of the occupational hazards, along with lumbago and petrol burns, and I had long ago stopped pretending I couldn't hear what was said behind me at forty miles an hour down a country lane. Men talk over a chauffeur as though he were upholstery.

What they said now was this: *berths*. Ships, passages, priority lists. One of the ministry men kept saying *civilian evacuation contingencies* in the tone other men reserve for golf scores, and the other kept correcting him with figures. And Edith sat at the head of the table with her hands folded and asked questions that sounded administrative and were not.

"And the family provisions," she said. "Confirmed?"

"The Marchmont allocation stands at three berths, madam. Subject to revision." He said *subject to revision* the way doctors say *we shall see*, which is to say: already preparing you for disappointment.

"Three," Edith repeated. "And dependents travel with the principal?"

"Dependents are covered under the principal's allocation, yes."

Something moved behind her face then — something quick, gone before it could be named, a fish turning under ice. I poured the tea and left, and stood in the corridor doing arithmetic I did not yet know I was doing.

Three berths. Edith. Her son Gerald, if he could be persuaded to leave England, which was doubtful, since leaving England would mean leaving his clubs and his creditors in the same week. And the child.

But the child was nobody. That was the arrangement. The child was a foundling delivered by rain and mischance, taken in out of charity, unnamed in any document, unmentioned in any letter. Charity does not get a berth. Charity gets the workhouse, if it gets anything, when the ships go out.

Unless the child was not charity.

Unless the child was family.

* * *

From Edith's own account, which she set down years later and which I have read more times than I will admit:

*I knew he listened. Of course I knew. A man who reads sealed letters before delivering them does not lose the habit at the pantry door.*

Yes — she knew about that too. She had known for years, I would discover, and had said nothing, and I have spent whole evenings wondering whether that mercy was kindness or husbandry, whether she kept me the way one keeps a tool that knows its work. I have decided, finally, that both answers are true and that most love between people who are not equals consists of exactly that ambiguity. But I am ahead of myself. In August of 1914 I did not know she knew. In August of 1914 I thought I was a ghost in my own house.

*I watched him pour tea and count with his eyes. He counted the maps. He counted the chairs. He counted us. I have employed many servants and understood perhaps three of them, and Lior Ravensworth was the only one I ever caught measuring the exits.*

*Gerald wanted the berth for himself. I knew that before he telephoned, the way one knows rain is coming from the ache in an old break. My son had spent thirty-one years practicing for a war that would make him important, and now that importance required passage rather than a uniform, he intended to claim it. Three berths. Himself, me, and — he would say the child was not family, because saying it aloud was the only way he could make it true.*

*And there was the matter of Margaret.*

My daughter's name I will write here because no one else ever wrote it. Margaret, who married badly and fled worse, who sent letters I burned unread for two years out of a discipline I mistook for principle, and who died in a rented room in Boulogne with a daughter she had never once mentioned in any of those letters. The solicitor's man told me in June. I paid for the burial and asked one question: *what happened to the girl?*

He did not know. I hired someone who did. And in July, on a night of rain, my granddaughter was carried up my drive by a woman paid to forget her face — because whoever arranged it knew that Edith Marchmont's household would be watched, and that an acknowledged grandchild was a hostage, and that a nameless foundling was merely furniture.

*Forget this face,* the note said. As though a face could be forgotten on instruction. As though I had not spent sixty years forgetting faces on instruction — my mother's disapproval, my husband's disappointments, my own reflection during certain years I will not itemize — and knew precisely how impossible the task was.

So I gave the child a bed in the blue room and no name, and I waited for the war to give me cover. War is useful that way. Whole families can be smuggled through a national emergency without anyone having the leisure to ask questions.

*Except Ravensworth asked. Not aloud. But I saw him asking, every day, with his eyes, and I began to understand that I had made an error of taxonomy. I had classified him as staff. The ledger said so — the ledger my grandfather's grandfather wrote, the ledger I had never once opened until that summer, God forgive me. And a classified thing can be moved like furniture. But Lior Ravensworth was not furniture. He was a question walking around my house in a chauffeur's coat, and I had given him three weeks and a child to grow curious about.*

*Two keepers of secrets, circling. He thought he was circling me. I let him think it. There are advantages, at my age, to being underestimated, and I had been cashing that particular dividend since 1889.*

* * *

The evening it came clear, I was mending the child's coat by the kitchen fire — her coat, the foundling's coat, the garment with no owner and therefore no mender but me — and she sat beside me watching the needle with the fierce concentration of the very young, as though stitching were a magic trick she intended to steal.

"You're Lior," she said.

"I am."

"That's a funny name."

"It's a Welsh name. It means a lot of things went wrong somewhere back down the line."

She considered this. Four-year-olds take etymology seriously. "I don't have a name," she announced, with neither self-pity nor drama, the way you might report that it was raining. "Nobody says it."

The needle went through the wool. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us the ministry men were rolling their maps, and Edith's tread crossed the landing overhead, unhurried, metronomic, a woman pacing her own fortress.

"Everyone has a name," I said. "Some of them are just being kept somewhere safe for a while."

"Where?"

"Somewhere dry," I said. "Names keep well. Better than coats."

She put her hand flat on my knee, briefly, the way birds land, and took it away again, satisfied. And I looked at her face — the face I had memorized on the first night against all orders — and I did the arithmetic properly for the first time. The jaw was Edith's jaw. I had seen that jaw across a steering wheel for nine years, set and stubborn, above every scarf and collar Edith owned. The child had it in miniature, waiting to grow into its inheritance. The grey eyes were Edith's eyes. Even the way she watched the needle, patient and acquisitive, was Edith watching a ministry man explain something she already understood.

Margaret. The disgraced daughter whose name the house never spoke, whose portrait had come down off the stairs sometime before my employment began, whose existence I knew only as a closed door on the second floor that even the housekeeper dusted around rather than opening.

The child was not charity. The child was the third berth, walking around in a mended coat, learning my name.

I finished the seam. I banked the fire. And upstairs, Edith's footsteps stopped — directly overhead, directly above me, deliberate as a signature — and stayed stopped long enough for both of us to know that we each knew.

Neither of us knocked. Yet.

That night I checked the hall table, and the telegram tray, and the rack of letters awaiting the morning post, and there it was: a letter in Gerald Marchmont's hand, addressed to his mother, thick enough to hold a threat. I held it under the lamp and read the direction twice — *Mrs. E. Marchmont, private and confidential* — and I did not open it.

I set it down. I walked away.

I got as far as the bottom of the stairs.
