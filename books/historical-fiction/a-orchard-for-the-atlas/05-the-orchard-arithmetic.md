## 5. The Orchard Arithmetic

My father taught me to count before he taught me to read, and the two lessons were not as different as you might suppose. Both began in the orchard. Both ended with the same instruction: *count what can be carried.*

He'd stand at the head of the long rows with his cap off — my father never counted with his hat on, said it was like lying to God with your face covered — and he'd point his chin down the lines of apple trees and say, "Go on then, Lior. How many?"

And I'd run the rows, small hands slapping bark, calling numbers until I lost my place, and he'd laugh, not unkindly, and say I'd counted wrong because I'd counted everything instead of counting what mattered. A tree that bore heavy but bruised easy was worth less than a tree that bore little and kept. A tree too far from the gate was worth half its number, because what couldn't be carried wasn't truly yours.

"Everything's arithmetic," he told me, the last autumn of his life, pressing a windfall into my hand. "You just have to know what you're counting for."

I thought of him now, forty minutes past dawn, walking the orchard rows alone for the first time since the war orders came. The grass was wet through my boots. Somewhere behind me the house was already turning itself inside out — maids carrying ledgers to the converted drawing room, Edith Marchmont's voice cutting through corridors like a wire through cheese — and here I was among the trees, counting.

Old habit. Or perhaps not habit at all. Perhaps it was the way a man takes inventory of himself before he does something that cannot be undone.

Forty-one trees in the east row. Nine of them bearing this year. I knew each one the way other men know their cousins, which is to say better than they deserved and without much affection. This tree had dropped its fruit on my head when I was six. That one had sheltered the first cigarette I ever smoked and the second. At the foot of the third tree from the gate, wrapped in oilcloth inside a biscuit tin, my father had buried his savings — eleven pounds, four shillings — against a day of running he never got to take.

The day had come for someone. Just not him.

I stopped at the row's end and did what I'd been doing all week: I measured the girl.

She was four years old. She could carry nothing herself — not a bag, not a coat, barely her own name, which we hadn't given her yet. Everything she needed would have to be carried by paper. That was the arithmetic of it, and once I saw it plainly, I could not stop seeing it. Papers weigh nothing. Papers cross borders in a pocket where a child cannot. Papers say *this girl is loved by someone with standing* in ink, and ink has more authority than any aunt or grandmother or driver ever will.

One berth. One child. One set of papers that would have to be true enough to survive being looked at by men whose whole profession was looking.

*Count what can be carried.* What could be carried was paper.

*

The trouble with forgery, I discovered, is not the penmanship. Penmanship is vanity, and vanities can be copied. The trouble is that gentlemen do not merely write differently — they *hesitate* differently. Their letters are formed without fear of correction, because no one has ever corrected them. Every stroke says: *no one would dare question me.*

My own hand said the opposite. My hand had been beaten into neatness at the parish school and then into smallness by twenty years of writing things down for other men. My letters apologized for existing.

So I practiced.

I took the best specimens available — Edith's correspondence, naturally; her son Gerald's army letters, which I carried and read, as I read everything; an old railway permit signed by a magistrate who had lunched at the house twice and left his signature on wine lists like a calling card. I sat in the coach house after the day's driving was done, with the lamp turned low so the glow wouldn't show under the door, and I copied.

Gerald's hand was the useful one. Loose, confident, slightly drunken even on the page — a hand that had never once been struck. I filled three sheets trying to make my *R*s sit back in their chairs the way his did, sprawled and entitled. I ruined a fourth. On the fifth, something happened: my wrist remembered that it had no master, and the letter came out wrong in exactly the right way.

I sat looking at it for a long time. It felt less like writing than like theft, and less like theft than like escape.

Here is what nobody tells you about learning a gentleman's handwriting: you must learn to think in his grammar first. Gentlemen do not write *the bearer, Lior Ravensworth, aged 31, is entrusted with the child.* They write *the bearer of this document and the child accompanying her are known to me personally and are traveling on urgent family business.* Known to me *personally*. Urgent *family* business. Words that open doors by implying the doors were always open.

Family business. I wrote the phrase four times and each time it landed in my chest like a stone thrown down a well, taking longer to hit bottom.

Because there it was, the flaw in my arithmetic. Papers could carry the girl across water. Papers could not make her family. Only blood did that, according to every ledger and law and magistrate in England, and her blood — whatever it was, wherever it came from — had already been used to abandon her once. Written on a card and pinned to her coat: *forget this face.*

Well. I had not forgotten it. I had done the opposite of forgetting it. I had memorized it the way you memorize a combination, a route, the location of buried money — as something you intend to come back for.

*

On the fourth night it rained, and I went out to the orchard anyway, because the coach house lamp had begun to feel like a confession waiting to be discovered, and because there was a question I needed to ask my father's row of trees whether they answered or not.

I stood at the third tree from the gate, over the spot where the biscuit tin lay, and I asked it: *Whose orchard is this?*

Not who owned it — that was written somewhere in some office in London, in a hand far grander than Gerald's, and the answer was the Marchmonts. But ownership and belonging are different trees that bear different fruit, and I had spent thirty-one years confusing them. I belonged to this estate the way the east row belonged to it: planted, pruned, harvested, entered in a book. I had learned last week, in the study, by lamplight, exactly how thoroughly I was entered in that book. *L. Ravensworth, inherited with the Orchard Cottage tenancy, valued at—* Valued. There had been a figure. I have decided not to repeat it, even to myself, though I confess I remember it to the shilling.

Eleven pounds, four shillings, my father had buried beneath this tree, counting on a day of running. And the estate had counted him back — counted him as part of the ground itself, as fixed as rootstock, as transferable.

I put my palm flat against the wet bark. The rain ran down my wrist.

"Right," I said to the tree, to my father, to whoever audits these conversations. "Then here's the sum."

The girl needed papers. Papers needed a gentleman's hand. A gentleman's hand needed confidence, and confidence, it turns out, is simply grief with its sleeves rolled up. I had plenty of grief. I had thirty-one years of it, inventoried and shelved, every kindness reframed now as maintenance, every *good lad, Lior* revealed as praise one gives a fence post that holds up well.

I went back to the coach house soaked through and wrote until dawn. By the time the kitchen bell rang I had produced a travel pass in a hand indistinguishable, I believed, from Gerald Marchmont's own — authorizing passage for one adult and one child dependent, ports southward, urgency stated in that magnificent bored tone the upper classes use for matters of life and death. I signed it with a flourish I stole from the magistrate's wine list.

Then I burned the practice sheets in the stove, all nine of them, and watched my old handwriting curl and go grey, and felt — I'll be honest — lighter than any man has a right to feel while committing a crime before breakfast.

There remained only the problem that no amount of penmanship could solve: the papers named a destination, but destinations require decisions, and the decision — whose berth, whose sacrifice, whose name goes on the manifest — belonged to a household that was circling itself like dogs over a bone. Edith knew more than she said. Her son wanted more than he'd admit. And somewhere between them, a berth was shrinking toward one, the way a tide goes out and shows you exactly what's stranded on the sand.

I hung up my wet coat and went in to drive Mrs. Marchmont to her war room, wearing another man's confidence in my breast pocket like a stolen watch.

It was, I reflected, ticking away nicely.
