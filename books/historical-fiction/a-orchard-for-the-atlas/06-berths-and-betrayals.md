## 6. Berths and Betrayals

The list hung on the pantry door like a sentence awaiting appeal. Three names, written in Edith Marchmont's upright hand: *M. Marchmont — berth confirmed. E. Marchmont — berth confirmed. C. Ashworth — berth pending.*

Three berths for a household of four, if you counted the child, which no one did aloud. If you counted me, which the ledger said I wasn't worth counting at all.

I had learned to read that list the way I'd learned to read letters not addressed to me — sideways, quickly, with my hands busy on something honest. I was polishing the silver that week because polishing gave me an excuse to stand in the kitchen while Mrs. Penhale read the list out loud each morning, her voice tightening by one notch per name lost.

By Thursday there were two.

*E. Marchmont's* berth had evaporated somewhere between Whitehall and Southampton, dissolved into the great bureaucratic fog of 1914, where a woman's passage could be cancelled by a clerk she'd never meet and re-issued to a man who'd never earned it. Edith took the news standing up, which was her way. She thanked the boy from the shipping office as though he'd delivered flowers.

"They'll find another," she told me in the corridor afterward, not looking at me, speaking perhaps to herself. "There are always more ships. There are never enough reasons to be on them."

I understood then what I'd been refusing to understand since the night of the rain: that the child was the reason. That *C. Ashworth* — Clara, though only I called her that, quietly, when we shelled peas together in the scullery — was sailing under a name invented for her the way a coat is cut down for a smaller body. Edith's granddaughter. Her disgraced daughter's daughter. The family shame wearing a borrowed surname and eating her bread and butter crusts-first, left to right, like a small barbarian.

I said nothing. That was becoming my whole profession: saying nothing, knowing everything.

* * *

He arrived on a Friday, in a motorcar too fine for the lane, all horn and gloss — Gerald Marchmont, the son, home from London on a tide of cologne and grievance. I took his hat and his case and his contempt in that order, and all three fit neatly on the hall stand.

He was not what I expected. I think I expected a villain, some broad-shouldered brute. Instead he was a thin man with his mother's exact eyes and none of her spine, the sort of man who'd been losing arguments to her for forty years and had come, finally, to win one by changing the rules.

"Mother." He kissed her cheek like a man paying a debt under protest. "We need to talk about this... arrangement."

"Clara is sleeping."

"I'm not talking about the girl." He glanced at me, dismissed me as furniture — a mistake people made twice with me, sometimes three times if they were slow learners. "In the study."

They talked for two hours. I know because I stood outside the door with a tray of tea going cold, which is a servant's legitimate occupation and also, conveniently, the best listening post in any house in England. Doors muffle words but not tones, and the tones went like this: hers, level as a spirit gauge; his, rising through indignation into something that cracked near the end and came out pleading.

"...my passage, Mother. *My* wife's passage. You'd send a child of no name and no—"

"She has my blood."

"She has *nothing*!" And then, quieter, the voice men use when they've found a weapon they're ashamed of but will use anyway: "There are people who would pay well to know. Papers could be drawn. Questions asked about how she came to be here, whose she is, what it makes *you*. One letter, Mother. One letter and your precious cargo goes nowhere, and I sail instead."

The silence after that lasted long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Eleven.

Then Edith's voice, so calm it frightened me: "You will leave this house tonight, Gerald. And you will remember that whatever you threaten, I have survived worse men than you with better handwriting."

* * *

The letter came two days later, and I took it off the post tray before anyone else touched it.

This is the part where I should tell you I hesitated. I didn't. Or rather — I hesitated exactly long enough to be ashamed of how little I hesitated. My hands had been intercepting things since I was eleven years old; they knew their trade. The envelope was thick, solicitor's stock, addressed in a hand I recognized from the study door: Gerald's, or his lawyer's writing through Gerald's mouth.

I carried it to the tack room, which had become my study, my confessional, my forge. I sat on a hay bale among the harness leather smell and the ghost of my father's pipe smoke, and I opened it.

It was worse and better than the threat. Worse, because it was real — sworn statements, dates, the name of Edith's daughter written out in full, the circumstances of a birth in a cottage hospital seven years ago, all of it assembled with the patient malice of a man building a cage out of other people's history. Better, because it was addressed to the Admiralty's passenger office, unsigned, unfinished. A weapon still being ground.

I read it twice. The first time as Lior Ravensworth, driver, thief of mail, reading what he should not. The second time as something else — as the man who'd memorized a four-year-old's face against explicit orders, who'd taught himself the loops and flourishes of gentlemen's hands so he could forge a child's passage, who now understood that loyalty and truth had stopped being the same road somewhere back and were pulling apart like a team gone wrong on a hill.

Because here was the trap of it: every word in that letter was true. The child was illegitimate. She *was* born of a scandal. Gerald wasn't lying; he was simply aiming truth like a gun at a little girl asleep upstairs with jam behind her ears.

And I was the keeper of another document, the ledger, that was equally true and equally aimed. *Lior Ravensworth, inherited asset of the estate.* Truth, I was learning, is just a fact that someone has decided to use against you. Loyalty is deciding whose facts you'll carry.

I held the pages over the lamp. The solicitor's fine paper caught slowly, curling like a leaf in autumn, and I watched Gerald Marchmont's careful accusations turn to ash and drift onto the straw. Somewhere in the blackening I thought: this is what I am now. A man who burns true things to protect truer ones.

Mrs. Penhale found me an hour later sweeping ash out the tack room door, and I let her believe I'd been burning rubbish. In a sense I had.

* * *

That evening the list on the pantry door went down to one name.

Not by Edith's hand. By telegram — the shipping line, apologizing with bureaucratic grace, confirming that due to requisition only a single civilian berth remained on the *Corinthian*, sailing Tuesday, non-transferable except upon presentation of full documentation at the dock.

One berth. Two Marchmonts and one Ashworth, and one Ravensworth who officially existed nowhere at all.

Edith stood before the list a long time, her back to me, and I watched her shoulders do something I'd never seen them do: settle, like a house settling, accepting its own weight. When she turned, her face was composed into that calm that I knew by now was not calm at all but arithmetic — the same orchard arithmetic my father taught me, everything measured by what can be carried, everything else let go.

"Lior," she said. "Tuesday. Be ready at six."

"Ready, ma'am?"

"To drive us. All the way to the dock." She looked at me a moment longer than the sentence required, and I felt the question underneath it, the one neither of us had asked yet: *whose blood do you serve?*

"Yes, ma'am," I said, and went out to the garage, where my forged papers lay hidden inside the spare tyre, and began practicing Gerald Marchmont's handwriting.

Because I'd burned his letter, yes. But I'd kept one thing from the post tray that morning, folded in my breast pocket next to my heart like a coal: a second envelope, smaller, addressed in Gerald's hand to a man at the passenger office — and this one finished, signed, sealed.

Whatever it contained, it would reach the dock before we did.

Unless I reached it first.
