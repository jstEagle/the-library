## 7. Forget This Face

The girl had a name by then, though not her own. I'd taken to calling her Pip, on account of the seed she'd arrived as — something small and unlikely, dropped where no one had planned for it to grow. She answered to it without complaint, the way children answer to anything that's said warmly enough, and every time she did I felt the small criminal pleasure of having gotten away with something.

She slept in the blue room now, the one with the water stain shaped like Portugal on the ceiling. Edith had stopped pretending the child was passing through. There were new blankets. There was a hairbrush with real bristles. There was, most tellingly, a second chair pulled up to the nursery table, which is the sort of domestic arithmetic that announces permanence more loudly than any deed or christening.

It was in that room, on a Tuesday of thin grey rain, that she asked me.

I was mending the latch on the window — the Marchmonts' house had the habit of coming loose at its joints, like an old man shrugging out of his coat — when I heard her stop arranging her dolls. The silence of a child who has stopped playing is louder than their noise. You learn to hear it the way you learn to hear a letter being opened behind you.

"Mister Lior." She pronounced it properly now, two syllables, no longer *Misser*. "The paper that came with me."

My hands kept working the screwdriver. That is the trick of it — you keep your hands stupid so your face has nothing to report.

"What about it?"

"It said forget this face." She said it evenly, the way she recited her prayers, words worn smooth from handling. "Will they forget it? Will you?"

I turned around. She was sitting very straight among her scattered dolls, four years old and asking the question the way adults ask about weather — already braced for a bad forecast.

Here is what I should have said: *Don't fret. Grown-ups say things in notes that they don't mean. Here, hold this spanner.* Here is what I wanted to say, which was nothing, because a promise is a door and once you open it you are responsible for everything that walks through.

What I actually said was: "No."

She waited. Children know a one-word promise is a cheap coin; they wait for the counting.

"I read that note," I said, crouching down to her level, my knees cracking like green wood. "I'm the only one who read it twice. And I'll tell you what I decided that night, in the rain, holding you under my coat." I tapped her once, lightly, on the chest. "That face isn't going anywhere. It's written down somewhere better than paper."

"Where?"

"In here," I said, tapping my own head this time. "Where nobody can burn it."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "Can I see it?"

"The face?"

"The writing. In your head."

And because I could think of no honest way to refuse, I told her. I described her own face to her — the freckle low on the left cheek like a period ending a sentence, the gap where the tooth had gone missing in June, the left eyebrow that broke in the middle as if someone had started drawing it and changed his mind. She listened to herself being described the way you'd listen to a stranger's story, fascinated and slightly suspicious, and when I finished she nodded once, satisfied, as if I'd confirmed a suspicion she'd been carrying since the gate.

"That's right," she said. "Don't forget."

"No fear of it."

I went back to the latch. But something had shifted in the room — some load-bearing thing. I understood, screwing that small brass fitting tight against the rain, that I had just signed something. Not papers. Worse than papers. A promise made to a four-year-old is the only kind that has no loopholes, because she will never understand why it wasn't kept, and I would never be able to live in the same country as her disappointment.

Edith Marchmont paid me wages. Edith Marchmont had, in her oblique way, been decent to me across twenty years — decent the way one is decent to a good coat, keeping it brushed and mended. But the ledger in her study had my name entered in iron-gall ink under *Assets*, between a bay mare and the south meadow, dated 1889, the year of my birth. I was not employed by this family. I was *held* by them. Every kindness had interest accruing on it.

The girl saw me as nothing written anywhere at all. Which meant I was free to be anything.

You cannot unknow that.

* * *

I was forging documents in the coach house at eleven o'clock that night when the lamp went dark.

Not blown out — covered. A hand over the glass, deliberate, patient. And a voice I knew from twenty years of instruction and quiet courtesy, saying my name the way she said it when the household accounts were short.

"Lior."

Edith Marchmont stood in the doorway in her dressing gown, silver hair unbound, holding the lamp she had smothered. Behind her the orchard was a black suggestion of trees, and beyond it the sea made its old argument with the shingle.

I did not stand up. Standing would have looked like flight, and there was nowhere to fly to, and besides, my hands were full of evidence: a blank passport form, a bottle of ink, three practice sheets in a hand I'd spent two weeks teaching myself — the looping, confident script of men who sign things without reading them because the world does what they say.

She came closer. Looked down at the table. At the sheets.

I want it recorded that she looked at them for a long time. Long enough for me to count the trees through the window out of pure nerves — eleven apple, three pear, one plum gone hollow-hearted — the old arithmetic my father taught me, everything measured by what could be carried. These papers I could carry. This girl I could carry. Everything else was already lost.

"You've been practising," she said at last.

"Yes, ma'am."

"The descenders are too proud. You loop below the line like a man showing off." She picked up a sheet, tilted it to the dead lamp as if light might still be coaxed from it. "A clerk writes humbly. He's paid to disappear into the hand."

"Then he's learned something tonight," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting.

She set the sheet down. When she spoke again, all the wryness had drained out of her voice, and what remained was the voice I imagined she used in the war room, over maps, over lists of berths and names.

"Do you know what happens to a man caught forging travel documents in wartime?"

"I've had time to consider it, ma'am."

"And still you sat here, night after night, with a lamp lit like a lighthouse."

"A lamp lit," I said, "is a man with nothing left to hide behind."

She was quiet. Out in the orchard the wind moved through the apples, and I thought — absurdly — of my father, who had pruned those trees, whose name was also in that ledger, entered even lower than mine, under tools perhaps, or livestock, and who had taught me the carrying-arithmetic because carrying was all we'd ever been permitted.

Then Edith asked her question. One question, and I felt it go in like a splinter driven clean and deep.

"Whose blood do you serve?"

Not *whose orders*. Not *whose house*. Blood. She knew — of course she knew, she'd known since the ledger, maybe since before me — that this family had owned my blood for two generations and called it employment. She was asking whether I served the line that held me in its book, or something older and less legible.

I thought of the note pinned to the girl's blanket. *Forget this face.* I thought of her eyebrow broken in the middle. Of the freckle like a full stop. Of my own promise, signed in the only ink that can't be forged.

"Not yours," I said. "And not mine either. Hers."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Somewhere down the lane a dog gave up barking at nothing, and the sea kept on with its work.

Then Edith Marchmont reached across the table, gathered up my three practice sheets and the blank form, folded them with the crisp economy of a woman who has folded many worse things, and put them inside her dressing gown.

"You loop too proudly," she said, turning for the door, "but the dates are correct, and the ship leaves Saturday if the convoy holds. Come to the study tomorrow at nine. Bring the girl's true name — the whole of it — and don't write anything down until I've seen the berth list."

She paused in the doorway, a black shape against the grey orchard, and added, quietly, almost to herself:

"My daughter used to loop like that."

* * *

She was gone a long time before I moved. I stood in the cold coach house doing sums I hadn't done since childhood — not what could be carried now, but what had just been given. Papers returned instead of burned. A berth acknowledged. A confession offered sideways, the only direction confessions travelled in that house: *my daughter*, alive enough to have a handwriting, disgraced enough to have a granddaughter left at gates in the rain.

At nine o'clock the next morning I knocked on the study door with the girl's name held whole in my head, unwritten, the way I'd promised her it lived — safe from burning. Inside, Edith had laid out the berth list face-down beside the ledger, and the ledger lay open to a page I hadn't seen before, and her hand rested flat upon it as if holding down something that wanted to fly.

"Sit," she said. "Before we do this, there's a line in this book you ought to read. And then, Lior — there's a decision only one of us can make, and I've begun to suspect it isn't me."
