## 8. The Last Berth

The dock at Southampton smelled of coal smoke and wet rope and, underneath it all, the particular sourness of a country learning to say goodbye in bulk. I had driven Edith's Daimler through the dark with a four-year-old asleep against my shoulder and a forged passport warming in my breast pocket like a second heart, and I had told myself the whole way down that I was only the driver. Only the driver. A man can tell himself anything if he says it often enough and keeps his eyes on the road.

The ship was the *Aldermoor*, fourteen thousand tons of grey indifference, and her gangway was already swallowing people at a rate that suggested she had somewhere better to be. Which, I suppose, she did. There was a war on. Everyone was leaving or everyone was staying, and the ones leaving were mostly women, children, and men too valuable or too frightened to stay, and the officials at the foot of the gangway had the faces of men who had learned to count souls like sacks.

I carried the girl. Her name — the name that was on no ledger anywhere, because Edith had burned every page that might have held it, or so she believed — was a small warm weight against my collarbone. She slept the way children sleep on trains and in cars: completely, without apology, trusting the motion to do the work of safety.

Edith walked ahead of me through the crowd, and I will say this for the old woman: grief had made her straighter, not bent. She wore black as though it were armour rather than mourning, and when sailors stepped into her path she parted them like weather. She had secured one berth. One. Three weeks ago there had been three — one for her granddaughter, one for her daughter's memory, one for herself, though she'd never said so aloud. Then two, when her son Gerald arrived from London smelling of brandy and entitlement and demanded his place "as a matter of blood." Then one, when the shipping office discovered that wartime does not negotiate.

One berth, and three claimants, if you counted me. And I did count myself. That was the whole trouble. For thirty years I had been counted by others — entered in a ledger in my father's time as *L. Ravensworth, asset, estate-born* — and I had made my peace with being an entry. But a girl had looked at me across a kitchen table six days ago and asked, in the grave tones of the very young, whether I was the man who fixed things, and something in me had answered *yes*, and yes is a dangerous word. It commits you.

At the foot of the gangway, Edith stopped.

She did not turn around at first. She stood looking up at the ship the way one looks at a verdict, and then she turned, and her eyes went past me to the sleeping child, and something moved behind them that I have spent years trying not to name, because naming it would mean admitting I saw it, and seeing it would mean we had both been human all along, which would have made everything harder than it already was.

"The papers," she said.

I gave her the passport. My forgery — my hours in the orchard with a stolen pen, teaching my hand the fat, confident loops of gentlemen, my father's arithmetic running under every stroke: *what can be carried, what must be left*. She read it the way she read suffragist correspondence, which is to say thoroughly and without mercy.

"Ravensworth," she said. "You've given her your mother's name."

"Mirabel," I said. "It seemed a shame to waste it."

"It is a good name." She handed the papers back, and for a moment neither of us spoke, and around us the crowd flowed like water around two stones. "There is a berth below. Second class. It is booked under Marchmont."

"I know," I said. "I booked it."

"You booked it in my name."

"I booked it in the name you gave me authority to use." I shifted the girl higher on my shoulder. She murmured and gripped my lapel with one small hand, and I felt that grip go all the way down through my chest and out the other side. "There is a question outstanding between us, Mrs. Marchmont, and I think we both know what it is. So let me save us both the unpleasantness. The berth is hers. It was always going to be hers. You knew that when you brought me those papers last night instead of turning me over to the police, and I knew it when I wrote them."

Her jaw tightened. Behind us, an official called something about final boarding, and the word *final* dropped into the morning like a stone into a well.

"You understand what you are refusing," she said quietly. "Canada is not England. There are no orchards there waiting for a Ravensworth. You would have started again. A man with your hands could have started again."

"A man with my hands has been starting again since before I was born," I said. "According to your ledger, I never started in the first place. I was simply *continued*."

"That page is torn out."

"So I'm told." I looked at her then, fully, and I found I wasn't angry anymore. Anger had been the fuel for the forgery, but the forgery was done, and what remained underneath was something quieter and much more expensive. "Mrs. Marchmont. Six nights ago you put a child in my arms and told me to take her to the station and say nothing. You asked me to forget her face. I want you to know that I considered it. I considered it the entire drive, and I want you to consider what it means that I am standing here anyway."

Something crossed her face — not softness exactly; Edith Marchmont did not do softness the way other women did it, all warmth and yielding. Hers was the softness of a woman deciding, finally, to stop fighting a battle she had already won without noticing.

"They'll question whoever remains," she said. "If they trace the child to this house—"

"They won't trace her to you. They'll trace her to me. And I will give them a story so dull they'll thank me for telling it." I adjusted the girl's coat, brushed a curl from her forehead with a thumb I did not entirely trust to stay steady. "You taught half the women in this county that obedience is a choice. Don't look surprised that your chauffeur was listening."

For a moment — one moment — Edith Marchmont laughed. It was a small sound, quickly folded away, but it was real, and I put it away in whatever room in me I kept the things worth keeping, which was growing crowded lately and would grow more so.

* * *

Boarding was a machinery, and machinery does not care about your epiphanies.

An official with a clipboard and a moustache like a hedgerow checked our papers at the gangway. He looked at the passport. He looked at the girl, who had woken by then and was regarding him with the solemn, unblinking appraisal that four-year-olds deploy like artillery. He looked at me.

"Travelling alone?" he asked her.

"She's travelling with me as far as the gangway," I said. "Her aunt is aboard. Second class. Marchmont."

He checked a list. Somewhere above us a gull screamed its opinion of the whole arrangement. The girl reached up and took my finger — my whole hand, really, her fingers barely closing around two of mine — and the official watched her do it, and something in his hedgerow softened by perhaps a centimetre.

"Frightened of ships, sir?"

"Petrified," I said. "Best she doesn't see it."

He stamped the passport. He stamped it without ceremony, without suspicion, without any idea that the confident loops of handwriting on it had been produced by a man who measured orchards for a living, and I felt the stamp go through my own chest as well. That was the moment. Not later, at the rail. Here. A rubber stamp on cheap paper, and a stranger's boredom, and freedom passing from my hands into a four-year-old's grip.

We climbed the gangway. At the top, a steward took the papers and pointed the way, and I walked the girl down the corridor to the second-class berth with her hand in mine and her questions arriving in their usual formation — why is the floor humming, where is the sea now, is the man with the trolley the same man as before — and I answered them all, because answering questions was the one inheritance I had that couldn't be entered in a ledger.

At the cabin door, she stopped.

She looked up at me with Edith's grey eyes set in someone else's younger face, and she said, "You're not coming."

It was not a question. Children always know. They stand at the border of adult decisions like animals before weather, and they know.

"No," I said. "Not this ship."

"Because of my face? The note said to forget my face."

I crouched down to her level. My knees cracked; I was older than I pretended. Around us the corridor hummed with departure, doors closing, trunks sliding, the great animal respiration of a ship preparing to leave.

"Listen to me, Mirabel," I said, and giving her my mother's name out loud did something to my voice that I chose not to examine. "That note was wrong, and the person who wrote it was frightened, and frightened people write stupid things. Your face is the one face I intend to keep. I will remember it when you're tall. I will remember it when your hands are bigger than mine. And one day — when the noise is over, and it will be over — you'll get a letter from a man who grows apples near the sea, and it will say: *the third tree from the wall came back this year, and so did I.* Can you remember that? Third tree from the wall?"

"Third tree from the wall," she repeated, gravely, as though accepting a commission.

"And when you write back, you sign it Mirabel. Not Marchmont. Mirabel. It's yours. I'm giving it to you. Nobody ledgers a name once it's given."

She considered this with the full seriousness of four years of accumulated wisdom, and then she threw her arms around my neck and held on with the grip that had been undoing me for six days, and I held her back and did not let my face do anything it would regret, because she would carry my face too now, across an ocean, and it ought to be a good one.

Then footsteps in the corridor — official ones, unhurried, the kind that come toward you when your name has come up somewhere — and a voice saying *Marchmont party, Marchmont party,* and I kissed the top of her head, stood, and walked away without looking back, because looking back is how men lose arguments with themselves.

* * *

I made the gangway with ninety seconds to spare, which was generous of the tide.

Below, on the dock, two men in civilian coats were speaking with the boarding official, and even from the height of the deck I could see the quality of their attention. It was the attention of men reading a list. One of them held a paper, and the paper had been typed, and typed papers about departing ships are never good news for anyone standing still.

The mooring lines came off. The *Aldermoor* shuddered, gathered herself, and began — slowly, with the reluctance of all large departures — to move. I went to the rail on the landward side, among mothers and sweethearts and streamers of paper, and I watched the dock slide away, and the two coats walk along beneath me, tracking the ship, checking their list, and one of them looked up — directly up, at the rail, at me — with the flat professional gaze of a man who will find out eventually, and finds out sooner when people run.

So I didn't run. I took off my cap, because a man seeing off family takes off his cap, and I held it at my chest, and I let the wind take my hair, and I watched the girl's ship become a smaller thing and then a smaller thing, carrying my mother's name out past the harbour mouth into the grey.

Behind me on the dock, the coats had stopped walking. One raised his arm — not waving. Pointing.

The ship blew her whistle, long and low, a sound that goes through the ribs and stays there, and I understood, standing at the rail with my cap in my hands and my name climbing the gangplank of somebody's memory, that I had finally defined myself. Not the ledger line. Not the livery. Not the driver. A man who had measured everything by what could be carried had just given away the last thing he owned, and found, remarkably, that his hands were not empty. They were planted.

The whistle sounded again. The coats were moving toward the dock gates now, toward the road, toward wherever the list said I had come from.

Third tree from the wall, I thought. Come back this year.

I turned and walked inland to meet them.
