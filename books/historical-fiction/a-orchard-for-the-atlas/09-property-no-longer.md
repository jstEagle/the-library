## 9. Property No Longer

They took me at the dock, which I suppose was efficient of them. Why waste a carriage ride on a man who had already delivered himself to the one place in England where a missing child would be missed?

The constables were polite about it. That was the strangest part. One of them held my elbow as though I were an elderly aunt crossing a cobbled street, and the other carried my satchel, which contained nothing but a change of shirt, a half-eaten apple, and a forged travel document I had been too slow to feed to the sea. Evidence, they called it. I called it the finest piece of handwriting I ever produced, and I was sorry to see it go into a manila envelope rather than a frame.

The interrogation room at the harbour office was green. Everything in it was green — the walls, the desk, the blotter, even the tea, though that may have been the tannin. A magistrate sat behind the desk with the particular stillness of a man who believes patience is a weapon, and a clerk sat to one side with a pen poised like a stiletto.

"Name," said the magistrate.

"Lior Ravensworth."

"Occupation."

Here was the question, then. The whole of it, folded small. I thought of the ledger in Edith Marchmont's study, the entry made in 1861 in a hand like iron railings: *One male child, Ravensworth, aged two years, received in settlement of debt. Asset of the estate. To be maintained.*

"Driver," I said. "And reader of letters. Though only the ones I carry."

The clerk's pen scratched. The magistrate did not smile. "You were seen placing a child aboard the *Corinthian* this morning. A child for whom no berth was issued. A child travelling under papers bearing your own description."

"Not my description," I said. "My handwriting. There's a difference, though I grant it's a subtle one."

"Where is the child's family?"

I had rehearsed this on the walk from the dock, rehearsed it the way my father had taught me to count trees — everything measured by what could be carried. What could I carry into this room? Not the girl's name. Not Edith's shame. Not the son's letter, which existed now only as ash and as a hole in my conscience the size of a first-class cabin.

"I wouldn't know," I said. "I am not family."

"Then why—"

"Because," I said, and here I abandoned rehearsal entirely, "your ledger says otherwise, sir, and I have come to distrust ledgers."

* * *

From Edith's diary, the third of September, 1914:

They have taken the driver. I learned of it from the maid, who learned of it from the fishmonger's boy, who learned of it, presumably, from God, as news travels faster in this town than grace does.

I have spent the morning deciding whether to go to him. This is not the decision it appears. The decision was made four days ago, when I stood in the orchard and watched him teach my granddaughter — my granddaughter, and there is the word at last, worn smooth from turning it over in the dark — to whistle. A man does not teach a child to whistle unless he intends to remember her. I have known enough men in my life to know that whistling is a promise.

My son believes I should let the law take its course. My son believes many things, most of them convenient. He believes the child is a scandal to be buried, that the berth was wasted, that Lior Ravensworth is a servant who stole a passage. He said *servant* the way his grandfather used to say it, the way the ledger says it, and I understood then that the ledger has outlived the grandfather who wrote it and will outlive us all unless someone tears it.

I am going into town this afternoon. I am taking the ledger.

I am an old woman and I have been polite for seventy years, and I find I have run out of it entirely.

* * *

The magistrate asked me the same question eleven times. I counted, because counting is what I do when I am afraid — trees, berths, questions. Eleven times some variation of *who is the child*, and eleven times I gave him the only answer that was both true and safe.

"Read me the entry," I said, on the twelfth pass, "and I'll tell you who I am."

He frowned. "What entry?"

"The estate ledger. Marchmont Orchard, Kent. Entry dated the fourteenth of March, 1861. I can recite it if the clerk's hand tires. *One male child, Ravensworth, aged two years, received in settlement of debt. Asset of the estate. To be maintained.*" I let the words sit in the green air. "That's who I am, sir. By your own paper. An asset. And assets, I'm told, don't have nieces or granddaughters or family of any kind. They have owners. So you'll have to ask the owner who the child was, and I'd advise you to hurry, because the owner is seventy-three years old and walks with a stick."

The magistrate looked at me the way men look at a horse that has begun to quote poetry. "You are claiming," he said slowly, "that you are property."

"I'm not claiming it. I'm *citing* it. There's a difference, though I grant—"

"—it's a subtle one," he finished, and for one moment I thought I saw the ghost of a smile, which would have been the first human expression in that room since the tea. "The ledger will be produced. Until then you will be held."

"May I ask for what?"

"Abduction. Forgery. Possibly more, once the ship's manifest is examined."

"Then I'll ask for a window," I said. "I've been held in worse rooms with less cause, and the ones with windows go faster."

* * *

From Edith's diary, continued:

The magistrate's chambers smell of pipe smoke and self-regard, and the man himself received me with the condescension men reserve for widows in good coats. He had the ledger open on his desk before I was halfway to the chair. He had sent for it, he said, that very morning. He wished to discuss the entry of 1861.

"Then we are agreed on the document of record," I said, and sat without being asked, which unsettles them every time.

He wished to discuss the boy. He called Lior *the boy*, though the man is thirty years my junior and has carried my luggage through two continents. He said the boy had obstructed the evacuation of a minor, had forged papers, had — and here the magistrate lowered his voice as though the walls were children — *claimed in open session to be property*.

"He did no such thing," I said. "He read your ledger aloud. There is a difference, though I grant it's a subtle one."

I confess I stole that from him. He will never know. It is the small thefts that keep a woman young.

I asked to see the entry. He turned the book toward me, and there it was, my grandfather's hand, iron railings and all: *One male child, Ravensworth, aged two years, received in settlement of debt. Asset of the estate. To be maintained.*

My grandfather bought a two-year-old to settle a debt for horse feed. I have known this since I was nineteen and found the page by candlelight, and I have done nothing about it for fifty years, and there is no forgiveness for that, only amendment.

The magistrate was saying something about the boy's sentence, about transportation being suspended in wartime but detention being a different matter, about how a man with no family and no standing could be made to disappear very quietly, and I heard myself say, "May I?" with my hand on the ledger.

He assented, the fool. He thought I wished to read it.

* * *

They brought me up from the cells in the late afternoon, and I heard her before I saw her — that particular tread of Edith Marchmont's, half walk and half verdict.

The magistrate's chambers had been rearranged by war. The ledger lay open on the desk, and Edith stood over it with her gloves still on, and the magistrate was saying, "Mrs. Marchmont, if you will simply—"

"I will not simply," she said. "I will specifically."

She put both hands on the ledger the way my father used to put both hands on a sapling he meant to pull. And then, before the clerk could rise, before the magistrate's mouth finished forming the word *Madam* — she tore the page out.

Not delicately. Not along the crease. She ripped it free with a sound like a gull taking a fish from the water, and she held it up, 1861 and all its iron railings, and she said:

"This page is a lie, sir, and lies do not become true by being bound in leather. My grandfather settled a debt of eleven pounds with a living child and wrote him down as an asset, and this family has been maintaining that fiction for fifty-three years at the cost of every kindness it ever pretended to give." She turned to me, and her voice did something I had never heard it do in nineteen years of service — it cracked. "Lior Ravensworth is not an asset of my estate. He never was. He is kin. Kin of my choosing, which is the only kind of kin worth having, and I will stand in any court in this country and say so until they carry me out feet first."

The page was still in her fist. The magistrate said, "Mrs. Marchmont, that is evidence—"

"It is," she agreed, "and I am destroying it," and she did — she walked to the grate, where a small fire kept the tea warm, and fed the page in piece by piece, iron railings and all, and stood there until the last corner curled.

I have seen ships leave. I have seen a child carried up a gangway on the strength of my handwriting. I have seen my own name in a ledger written as though I were a mare. None of it undid me the way that page burning did.

"Kin of my choosing," she said again, to the magistrate this time, dusting ash from her gloves. "Now. About the child on the *Corinthian*. I should like to make a statement regarding my granddaughter, and you will find, sir, that I am a great deal harder to tear than a page."

* * *

They did not release me that night, but they stopped asking who the girl was, because Edith had begun telling them, in detail, at length, with dates — and the truth, it turns out, is the one document no one can confiscate.

Near midnight a constable brought me tea, actual tea, and said the magistrate wished me to know that the matter of the ledger was "resolved." I said I was glad to hear the estate had lost an asset. He said, not unkindly, that it seemed the estate had gained a family, and that in his experience the second was far more trouble.

He was right about that. It would be trouble for years — inquiries, affidavits, a son in London writing letters that smelled of ash. But sitting in that green room with cold tea and a torn book somewhere down the hall, I found I could measure the trouble and it was nothing. It could all be carried.

What could not be carried was already at sea, wearing my mother's name, and somewhere ahead of her was an orchard I hadn't planted yet.
