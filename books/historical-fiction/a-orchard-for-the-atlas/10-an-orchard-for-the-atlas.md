## 10. An Orchard for the Atlas

The first tree I planted here was a Bramley, and I planted it crooked, and I have never once regretted either fact.

That was eleven springs ago, if you count the spring we're standing in now, which I do, because counting is a habit I've kept the way other men keep dogs — not because it's useful anymore but because it knows my voice. Eleven springs of salt wind off the Channel, of gulls who consider my apples a public amenity, of soil so thin in places you could read a newspaper through it. The landlady at the time thought I was mad. A man with no family, no references, and a face that made constables look twice, wanting to buy four acres of cliff-top nothing above a fishing village that smelled permanently of herring and ambition.

"You'll never get them to grow," she told me, nodding at the exposed slope like it had personally wronged her.

"They grew once," I said, "in worse dirt than this."

She didn't ask where. That was the mercy of those years. Everyone had a before they weren't asking about.

* * *

I still drive. Not for an employer — never again for an employer — but the village keeps a motorcar now, shared between six households, and when someone needs to get to the station or the market or the doctor in town, they send for me. It's honest work. It's work I chose, which turns out to be the entire difference between labor and servitude, though it took me thirty-odd years and one very good lawyer named Mrs. Marchmont to understand it.

Yes, Edith Marchmont lived another nine years. She came down here twice, both times unannounced, both times inspecting my trees with the critical eye she'd once turned on cabinet ministers. The second visit she stayed a week and left me her writing desk, the green room's desk, the one where she had drafted a hundred suffragist letters that I carried sealed and read in secret and delivered with clean hands.

"I imagine you've earned the right to know what people write," she said, handing over the key. "You always did anyway."

I said nothing. There are moments when silence is the only honest reply, and she knew it, and that was our whole understanding, finally spoken aloud.

She died in '23. I drove to the funeral. Sat in the back of the church behind the family — what remained of the family, which was mostly lawyers and one son who would not meet my eye, which suited us both. On the order of service, under her name, someone had printed *Beloved Mother*. It was true as far as it went. It went about as far as most ledgers go.

* * *

The letters come on Thursdays.

They come unsealed. That is the whole point of them, the small daily miracle of them, and I will confess that after twenty-five years of carrying other people's sealed words — reading them anyway, guiltily, hungrily, like a man drinking from a well he wasn't allowed to touch — there are still Thursday mornings when I hold an open envelope and feel something in my chest loosen that has been clenched since 1914.

She writes longhand, slanting rightward, ink-blue, decisive. She signs herself Ruth. My mother's name. She chose to keep it when she came of age, against the advice of every sensible person in Canada who told her a fresh start wanted a fresh name.

"It isn't a fresh start," she wrote, aged nineteen, in a letter I still keep in the desk drawer. "It's a continuation. You gave me your mother so I'd have somewhere to stand. I'm not giving up the ground."

Ruth. Who is twenty-nine now. Who teaches school in a town outside Toronto, in a brick building with a bell, and tells her students about orchards the way missionaries tell about distant lands. Who married a quiet man named Aldous who repairs clocks, which she informs me is the opposite of planting trees — he spends his life making things forget time, and I spend mine making things obey it.

Her letters arrive full of questions that are really answers:

*Do the cod still run past the point? Is the third row of Russets fruiting yet or did the frost take the blossom? Have you forgiven the gulls? Have you forgiven anyone else?*

I answer all of them. Even the last one. Especially the last one.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of an orchard, the real kind, the kind my father taught me by the wrong light and which I have spent my whole life correcting.

He counted trees by what could be carried. How many apples fit in a sack. How many sacks fit a cart. What a tree was worth stripped down, portable, liquid — worth to whom, I never asked, because I already knew, because my own name sat in a ledger between the silver and the sheep, valued, carried, owned.

But a tree itself cannot be carried. That is its whole genius. You put it in the ground and it refuses, absolutely refuses, to be moved again. Its value is precisely the part you can't take with you. Every apple is a debt paid forward to a season you may not live to see. My father taught me to count and I have spent forty years learning to plant instead, and if there is a difference between those two things, then that difference is the distance between the ledger and this letter.

Because this letter is unsealed. Anyone might read it. The postman, the widow Prynne who sorts the village mail and reads everything out of professional pride, God love her. And that openness is the point. A sealed letter says *this belongs to me and not to you*. An open one says *I trust the world with these words*, and trust, I have come to believe, is the only inheritance worth the paper.

* * *

Last autumn, Ruth wrote and asked if she could come home.

Home. She used the word without quotation marks around it, without irony, and I sat with the letter on my knee for a long while looking at that single syllable doing all that work.

She'd been offered a headship. Her own school, her name on the door, the summit of everything a foundling girl with forged papers and borrowed blood was supposed to want. And she wrote: *Aldous says take it. I keep thinking about your trees. You once told me you measure nothing by what can be carried anymore, only by what was planted. I think I've been measuring myself by what can be carried. The title fits in a suitcase. So does the salary. But I don't know how to carry a classroom of children, and I don't want to learn.*

*I want dirt, Uncle Lior. Send me some.*

So I sent dirt. An actual envelope of it, which the postal authorities tolerated because the widow Prynne declared it "seeds" and glared them into agreement. Four ounces of cliff soil, thin and salt-bitten and stubborn. Under it I wrote instructions: find ground that nobody wants. Ground with weather. Ground that argues back. Anything easy was already taken by men who measure.

And now it is April, and the Blossom Festival is Saturday — our invention, three years old, consisting of the village, a fiddle, cider pressed from my own crooked Bramley, and a great deal of self-congratulation about beauty nobody present created. And this morning, walking the rows with my counting habit humming uselessly along — twelve, thirteen, fourteen, the old litany — I saw the postman's bicycle coming up the lane ahead of schedule.

Thursday letters come on Thursdays. This was Tuesday.

He handed me two envelopes. One was thick, Ruth's handwriting, Toronto postmark, several weeks old — delayed, misrouted, no matter.

The other was thin. Local. Fresh.

And the handwriting on it was hers too, but changed — looser, faster, written recently, written *here* — and the address was not my cottage at all but the Falcon Inn, down in the village, dated yesterday.

I stood in row fourteen with an apple tree's new blossom coming in white around me, holding a letter from a woman who was supposed to be across the ocean and was instead half a mile away, and understood that whatever she had decided about titles and suitcases, she had not waited for my permission any more than I had ever waited for anyone's.

The letter was unsealed, of course. I opened it standing among the trees I'd planted, in the wind off the water, and read it twice — the way I once read other people's secrets twice, except this secret was mine, given freely, and the reading felt nothing like theft and everything like harvest.

*Uncle Lior —*

*We landed at Southampton Tuesday morning. Aldous is with me; he wants to see whether English clocks keep worse time than Canadian ones. Don't meet us. Come to the inn at seven, and bring a sack of apples, and don't count them first.*

*I want to show you something I brought that couldn't be carried. It's in the ground already. You'll know where to look.*

*— Ruth*

I read it a third time, though there was no need. Some letters you read for information. Some you read the way you touch a tree in winter, checking for life under the bark.

Then I went inside and took down the sack — not the good sack, the ordinary one, the kind my father would have weighed and calculated and entered into a column — and I filled it without counting a single apple.

Twelve rows later, walking down through the orchard toward the village with the blossoms opening overhead and the sea going silver beyond them, I caught myself counting anyway. Old habits. Old litany.

But this time I wasn't counting what could be carried.

I was counting what had been planted, and for the first time in my life, the number came out larger than one.
