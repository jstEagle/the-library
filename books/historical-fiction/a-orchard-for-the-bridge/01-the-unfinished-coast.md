## 1. The Unfinished Coast

The ink froze first. Everything else in Aldous Vane's studio had frozen weeks ago — the fire gone to ash in the grate, the tea gone to skin in its cup, the man himself gone wherever the dead go when they leave no instructions — but the ink freezing was a small, personal insult, because it was the one thing in the room that was still supposed to be working.

I held the pen over the flame of the lamp until the nib ran liquid again and went back to the coast.

North of the Saltmere estuary there is a stretch of shoreline no cartographer loves: cliffs that lie about themselves on every survey, shoals that migrate with the seasons like gossips, and a village called Harrow's End whose harbor drowns twice a day and pretends otherwise. Vane had measured it eleven years ago, before I was born, or near enough, and his field notes for it were written in that cramped leftward hand of his, all abbreviations and contempt. *Cliff line unreliable. Locals superstitious re: tide. Soundings taken thrice, disagreeing thrice.*

Thrice disagreeing. That was Vane. Most men would have taken the average and gone to lunch. He had climbed down the cliff three separate mornings to argue with the sea, and the sea had won each time, and he had recorded his defeats in pencil so fine you'd miss them unless you knew to look.

I knew to look. I had been looking for six years, since I was eleven and my mother traded our name to him for bread and a bed above the shop, which is not how she'd put it, but it is what happened. Apprenticeship, she called it. Indenture of Kestrel Pemberton to Aldous Vane, mapmaker of Vessing, in consideration of room, board, and instruction in the noble art. What it meant in practice was that four of us lived in two rooms over a cold shop while I learned to draw coastlines the way monks learned to pray — constantly, humbly, and with the growing suspicion that nobody up there was listening.

The atlas lay open before me under the lamp. Forty-one sheets, forty-one finished. This was the last: *The Northern Coast from Saltmere to the Gray Stones*, and it was beautiful, because Vane's work was always beautiful even when it was wrong, and mine was accurate even when it was ugly, and between us we had produced something that would outlive both our reputations. His name went on the title page. Mine went nowhere. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement.

"Nearly done," I said aloud, to nobody, which had become a habit since October. Talking to a dead man in an empty studio sounds mad until you've tried it; then it sounds merely practical. The studio was the only place in Vessing where I could say what I actually thought, and the only listener who couldn't repeat it.

*Harrow's End,* I inked, and the cliff came down onto the page in a long confident stroke, and then I stopped, because the stroke was wrong.

Not wrong the way a bad drawing is wrong. Wrong the way a eulogy is wrong — technically true, spiritually hollow. I had drawn the cliffs as Vane had measured them, and Vane had measured them correctly, and yet the line had none of him in it. Six years of watching him draw, six years of hearing him say *a coastline is a confession, boy, the sea makes everyone tell the truth eventually*, and now here was his final confession and I was forging the signature.

I set the pen down and pressed my palms flat against the table until the wood's cold came up through them.

Here is the thing they don't tell you about grief, or rather, here is the thing nobody told me, possibly because everyone assumes you'll work it out yourself and most people do, just slowly and at cost: grief has measurements. It has scale. You can plot it. For me it arrived not as a wave or a wound or any of the words the poets use, but as unfinished lines. Every place in the atlas where Vane's hand should have been and wasn't. Every abbreviation in his notes I'd never get to ask him to expand. Every *thrice disagreeing* with no fourth sounding coming, ever, because the man who argued with tides was now on the other side of all of them.

Forty-two sheets. One signature missing.

And underneath the grief, quieter, colder, the other thing. The thing I didn't say aloud even to the empty room.

Finishing this atlas ended everything.

I don't mean sentimentally, though that too. I mean practically, arithmetically, the way I mean everything these days. While the atlas remained unfinished, Aldous Vane's name still sat over this shop like a roof. He had been dead five weeks — found face-down in the shallows below the North Quay, and the coroner's verdict was *misadventure*, which is the word Vessing uses when it doesn't want to look too closely at anything — and in those five weeks the Admiralty had left us alone precisely because the great Vane's masterwork was known to be in progress, and no sane office seizes a half-made atlas. Half-made atlases are worth less than their paper. Finished ones are worth more than houses.

The moment I signed the last sheet, the roof came off.

I had done this sum perhaps forty times since the funeral. It always came out the same. Finish the atlas, deliver it, collect whatever fee the Admiralty deigned to pay — and lose the only shield my family had. Mother knew it. She hadn't said it in words either, but she'd started mending clothes that didn't need mending, which is how Pembertons do arithmetic.

Below me, through the floorboards, I could hear the household being itself: Wren arguing with Tom about whose turn it was to fetch water, Mother's voice cutting across both of them with the flat authority of a woman who has won that argument every morning for nineteen years. Four of us in two rooms, and the fifth room — Vane's old counting room, cleared of him the week after they buried him — standing empty because renting it out would mean strangers in the building, and strangers ask questions, and questions were the one commodity the Pemberton family currently could not afford.

We were, in short, living above a dead man's reputation and calling it shelter.

I picked the pen back up.

The trick, I had decided, somewhere around the third week of pretending everything was normal, was to finish the atlas so well that whoever inherited it would need me. Not want me — need me. A finished atlas can be taken away. A finished atlas plus the only living person who understands its corrections cannot be dismissed so easily. I had spent five weeks annotating, cross-referencing, initialing every adjustment in a cipher of my own devising that I had not shown to anyone. Petty? Certainly. Vane would have called it *insurance against ingratitude* and then charged someone for the phrase.

So: the cliff line again, slower. And this time I let my own judgment into it, because Vane's soundings disagreed thrice and I had spent two days of the previous week down at Harrow's End myself, freezing, taking a fourth and fifth sounding, and the truth — my truth, the new truth — was that the channel had shifted forty yards east in the eleven years since his measurements. The sea had kept arguing. Someone had to record the outcome.

I drew the shoal where it now lay. I drew the drowned harbor telling its usual lies. And in the margin, in letters two points high, invisible to anyone who didn't know to hunt for them, I wrote *K.P., corrected, March.*

Let the Admiralty make of that what they liked.

* * *

Lamplight does something strange to a mapmaker's studio at night. It shrinks the world. All those coastlines and archipelagos, all that patient geography spread across the tables and pinned to the walls, and the lamp carves out a circle three feet wide and says: this is what exists. Inside the circle, the northern coast. Outside it, the dark, and in the dark, everything else — the debt, the winter, the question of what happens to a family whose protector is floating somewhere under the North Quay mud.

My stomach spoke into the silence, undignified. There had been bread at midday and there would be nothing else until tomorrow, because Mother's arithmetic had recently moved from *precarious* to *grim*, and grim meant the flour went further if it skipped a meal. I had offered to sell something. She had given me the look. The look said: *we have sold everything sellable, child, and what remains is either furniture or memory, and memory doesn't fetch much.*

Though Vane's instruments were worth a fortune, of course. His brass theodolite alone. But those weren't ours to sell — they were estate, and the estate was in probate, and probate in Vessing moves at the speed of sediment. So the instruments sat locked in their case like a fortune in a fairy tale, guarded by lawyers instead of dragons, equally unreachable.

Somewhere past midnight — I judged by the bells of Saint Ferren's, which rang the hours with a lisp, having cracked decades ago and never been recast — I heard the front door of the shop try to open.

Not open. Try. The latch lifted, paused, settled back. Then footsteps on the cobbles outside, unhurried, moving off toward the harbor end of Cooper's Lane.

I stood very still with the pen in my hand and counted to sixty.

Cooper's Lane at midnight gets two kinds of visitors: drunk ones, who rattle the latch without pausing, and careful ones, who pause. This one had paused. Drunks don't measure things. Careful men do.

When I finally crossed to the window and looked down through the gap in the shutter, the lane was empty, if you didn't count the fog, which was coming in off the harbor the way it did most nights in March, thickening between the lampposts like something being poured. Somewhere in it, a lantern bobbed once and went out.

Probably a watchman. Probably a drunk who'd thought better of it. Probably.

I have since learned that "probably" is the most expensive word in the language, but that night I simply stood at the window a while, fog-breath fogging the glass, doing sums again. Five weeks dead. Five weeks of quiet. And now a latch tried at midnight by careful feet.

Coincidence, said the reasonable voice, my mother's voice, installed permanently in my head with all her other furniture.

Coincidences cluster, said Vane's voice, from the chair where he wasn't sitting. *Two errors on one chart, boy, and you don't blame the weather. You suspect the surveyor.*

I went back to the table. What else was there to do? Fear is a poor employer; it pays nothing and demands constant attention. Work at least paid in inches of finished coastline.

But I will confess what I did before I sat down, because it matters later. I took Vane's map case down from the high shelf — the long oiled-leather tube he'd carried on every survey for thirty years, scarred at both ends, smelling of cedar and salt — and I looked at it. Just looked. It had been sitting up there since before he died; I hadn't opened it since, because opening it felt like going through his pockets, and I'd had enough of people going through things.

It was heavier than I remembered. Or I was lighter. Hard to say, at midnight, in a shrinking world.

I hung it back on its peg, unopened, and told myself I'd deal with it after the atlas was done. One catastrophe at a time. It's a good rule. It has never once worked.

* * *

By the third bell of the morning I had reached the Gray Stones themselves, the last feature, the terminus — black reefs lying off a headland like the vertebrae of something enormous that had lain down there to die and been patient about it. Vane's notes for the Stones contained a single sentence, uncharacteristically plain: *Do not trust the chart. Trust the stones.* Which was either profound seamanship or the man showing off, and with Vane it was usually both.

I drew them. I gave them their true positions, the ones I'd verified, the ones that would keep some future ship's master alive on a bad night. And when the last reef was inked and dried, I sat back, and the atlas was finished, and the studio was very quiet.

Finished. Forty-two sheets. Eleven years of a dead man's patience and six years of a living one's obedience, bound together in a portfolio that was now, as of roughly four minutes ago, worth more than everything else my family owned combined.

I should have felt triumphant. Masterpiece completed, apprentice vindicated, etcetera. What I actually felt was the specific chill of a man standing on a riverbank who has just realized the bridge behind him is gone. The protection of the unfinished was over. From this morning forward, the Pembertons were simply a widow, a son, a daughter, and a small brother, living above a valuable object, in a city where a valuable man had recently died face-down in suspiciously shallow water.

*Misadventure,* said the coroner.

*Twice disagreeing,* said the facts, quietly, in Vane's cramped leftward hand.

I cleaned the pen. I blotted the sheet. I closed the portfolio and tied it with the good cord, and as I did, my eye went up to the map case on its peg, hanging there in the lamplight, heavy and silent and patient as a coffin nail.

One catastrophe at a time, I told myself again.

Then the light in the yard below moved — a lantern, swinging slow through the gate of the little orchard behind the house, stopping directly beneath my window — and stayed there.

And did not go out.
