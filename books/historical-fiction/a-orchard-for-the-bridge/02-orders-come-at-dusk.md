## 2. Orders Come at Dusk

The light was failing in the way that autumn light fails in Vessing — not all at once, but grudgingly, like a man being talked out of his own opinion. I had been working by the window since midday, inking the final coastline of the northern sheets, and I had reached the part of the work where the hand does the thinking and the mind is free to wander, which is a dangerous arrangement for someone with my particular troubles.

Vane's atlas lay open before me. *A Complete Coastal Survey of the Vessing Reach and Adjacent Waters*, forty-one sheets, thirty-nine finished. His name on every one. My name on none, though my ink was in every line — he had insisted on that much honesty between us: "You draw it, Kestrel. I'll sign it. That way when the Admiralty complains about the depth soundings off Gull Neck, they can hang you instead." He'd said it laughing. Aldous Vane laughed at hanging the way other men laughed at weather.

I was three hours from done. Three hours from finishing the last thing he would ever finish, because dead men don't complete their work; they only leave it behind for other people to carry, like a debt or a coffin.

That was when the knock came.

Not a knock, really. A pounding — the kind of sound made by fists that are paid by the fistful. It came down through the floorboards of the studio, through the shop below, and up again into my chest, where it settled as a cold weight.

"Kestrel!" Mother's voice, from the stairwell. Carefully flat. Mother had many registers, and her flattest one meant *come quickly and bring your face*.

I capped the ink. I looked at the atlas — open to sheet forty, the coast north of the harbor, unfinished past the point where Vane had stopped drawing and started dying — and I did something I had never done before in that room. I closed it.

* * *

There were two of them, standing in the shop among the rolled charts and the smell of size and salt, and they wore their authority the way some men wear borrowed coats: slightly too large, but with real buttons. The elder was gray and dry as an old reed, with the patient eyes of a man who has watched many people lie and found it mildly entertaining. The younger had a jaw like a mooring bollard and stood with the particular stillness of someone who has been told to stand still and has mistaken this for a career.

The elder introduced himself as Under-Secretary Pell. No introduction was offered for the bollard-jawed one, which told me everything about him I needed to know.

"We have a writ," Pell said, and produced it — vellum, seals, the works, held out at arm's length as though I might be expected to read it upside down. "In the matter of the inquiry into the late Master Aldous Vane. All papers, charts, correspondence, instruments, and effects of the deceased are to be surrendered to the Crown."

"Surrendered," I said.

"Confiscated," said Pell, who apparently enjoyed synonyms.

"Seized," offered Bollard-Jaw, who enjoyed bluntness.

"What inquiry?" asked Mother. She had come down the stairs with a lamp she didn't need yet, which is a trick of hers — carrying light into rooms so that everyone else feels unlit. Her voice was mild as milk. "Master Vane died of a fever. There was no inquiry then."

"There was no inquiry then," Pell agreed pleasantly. "Deaths prompt inquiries, Mistress Pemberton. Treason prompts thorough ones."

The word went into the room and sat down among us like a guest nobody had invited. I watched Mother not react. It was a masterclass. Her face did nothing so completely that I understood, for the first time, how much practice she must have had.

"Treason," I repeated, because someone had to say something stupid while I thought.

"The late Master Vane kept company we find interesting," Pell said. "His papers will help us understand him. You were his apprentice, I believe?"

"I am," I said. "Was. Am." I was aware that I had begun conjugating badly and could not seem to stop. "His apprentice."

"And where are these papers? These effects?"

Here is what I knew, standing in our shop with the dusk coming blue through the windows: the map case was behind me, up the stairs, in the studio, under the drafting table where I had left it that morning after oiling its hinges. Inside it were the finished sheets, the working notes, six years of Vane's correspondence, and the letter — though I didn't know about the letter yet, God help me; I didn't know what slept in that case like a knife in a bread loaf. All I knew was that the Admiralty wanted it, that wanting things was what the Admiralty did instead of breathing, and that if Pell walked up those stairs, he would take everything Vane had ever touched.

And something in me — some animal thing below the thinking part, older than honesty, older than grief — said *no*.

Not *think about it*. Not *weigh it*. Just *no*, the way a hand pulls back from a hot iron before the mind has even registered heat.

"They're mostly gone," I heard myself say.

Pell's eyebrows performed a small, courteous ascent. "Gone."

"He was ill a long time. Fever takes the mind before it takes the body, sometimes." I heard my own voice do something I had never heard it do: it lied. Smoothly. With texture. "Near the end he grew — restless about the work. Said the Admiralty would only misuse it. He burned what he could and gave the rest to—" I paused, and the pause was the best lie of all, because Pell leaned forward to fill it, "—to be dispersed. Safe hands, he said. He wouldn't tell me whose. He said the fewer names I carried, the fewer names they could take from me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Bollard-Jaw shifted, wood creaking.

"That's a remarkable story," Pell said at last.

"It's a fever story," I said. "Fever stories generally are."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me: it came easily. That's the part that frightened me — not that I lied, but how well the lie fit my mouth, as if some craftsman had measured me for it years ago and it had hung in my closet all this time waiting for an occasion. I had spent six years learning to draw coastlines, which is to say learning that the truth of a shore is only visible from offshore, at a distance, through instruments. Apparently lying is the same trade practiced in reverse.

"You'll forgive us," Pell said, "if we look anyway."

"Of course," said Mother, before I could answer, and there was something in her voice — a hairline crack of warning — that told me she had already decided what the upstairs should look like when strangers saw it. "We have nothing to hide. But you'll want a receipt for anything you take. We're lodgers above this shop, Under-Secretary. We own the lease, not the building, and the landlord's solicitor is a tedious man who writes letters."

It was beautifully done. It turned the visit from a hunt into an errand, wrapped the whole business in tedium and paperwork, and made us — the family of a possibly-treasonous dead man — seem like people more concerned with landlords than conspiracies. Pell actually smiled.

"A woman of sense," he said.

"I'm a widow of sense," Mother corrected. "It's a smaller country but better governed."

* * *

They searched for two hours.

I will spare you most of it, partly because it was tedious and partly because watching strangers handle Vane's things was a species of pain I don't recommend and won't describe twice. They went through the shop first — Bollard-Jaw with a lantern and no grace, pulling rolls of charts from their pigeonholes and dropping them like dead fish. Then upstairs. They opened drawers. They read letters — Mother had perhaps eight of Vane's remaining, innocuous things about ink orders and tide tables, and I watched Pell skim them with those patient eyes and set each one aside with the small disappointment of a man panning for gold in a river that has already been worked out.

They found the studio. Of course they found the studio; it was directly above the shop and smelled of linseed and ambition.

They found the atlas.

This was the moment. I want to be precise about this, because later — much later, on other nights, in worse rooms — I would return to this moment the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Pell lifted the atlas from the drafting table, and it fell open to sheet forty, the unfinished coast, and he stood there holding six years of a dead man's devotion in his dry hands, and he said:

"This is it. This is what he was working on." And then, to Bollard-Jaw: "Take it down. Catalogue it."

"No," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Even the lamp flame seemed to lean in.

I hadn't planned to say it. It simply arrived, fully armed, out of the same animal place that had produced my earlier lie — except this time it wasn't a lie, which made it both braver and stupider. "That atlas isn't finished. Sheet forty is incomplete. Sheet forty-one doesn't exist. If you catalogue an unfinished survey as evidence, any cartographer worth his salt will testify it's worthless — a survey stops being a survey the moment it stops being accurate, and that coast—" I pointed at the unfinished shoreline with a steadiness I did not feel, "—hasn't been sounded since the storm year. Every chart of it in circulation is wrong. Vane knew it. That's why he was still working. You'd be seizing a book of errors."

"Why would that concern you?" Pell asked softly.

"Because my name is apprenticed to his," I said. "When the atlas is judged, I'm judged with it. If you want to hang someone over bad soundings, Under-Secretary, I'd rather you waited until the work is true."

Another silence. Longer than the first. Somewhere downstairs a shutter banged in the wind, and Bollard-Jaw's hand moved toward his belt and stopped, and Pell looked at me for a long, long moment with those patient eyes that had watched a thousand lies and found them mildly entertaining — and I understood, with cold clarity, that he was now doing arithmetic about me, and that I had just become a number in it.

"Finish it, then," Pell said.

"...Sir?"

"Finish it. The writ says papers and effects. An unfinished chart is a working document, and working documents belong to whoever completes them — that's guild law, and I've no appetite for arguing with the guild over soundings." He set the atlas back on the table with terrible gentleness. "You'll complete the survey within the month and deliver it to the Admiralty. In exchange, the Pembertons remain—"

"Lodgers," said Mother. "With receipts."

"—lodgers, undisturbed, pending the inquiry." He drew on his gloves. "You see how reasonable we are, apprentice. Reasonable men ask small things. Finish the atlas. Bring it to us. And think, in the meanwhile, about whether fever stories are the sort of tale a clever young person wants to keep telling."

They took the loose papers. They took the letters. They took Vane's logbooks and two boxes of working notes and, at the door, Bollard-Jaw paused beside me and looked at my hands — looked at them specifically, the way a man looks at a horse's teeth — and then left without a word.

I didn't understand that yet either. I would.

* * *

Midnight. The house breathed around me — Mother's slow clock-tick breathing from her room, Wren's deeper child-breath from hers — and I sat in the dark studio with the atlas closed under my hands and thought about what Pell had actually said, underneath the words, the way you read a chart's soundings underneath the pretty coastline.

*Reasonable men ask small things.* He had let me keep the atlas because he wanted the atlas completed — delivered — traceable. A finished survey in Admiralty hands closes an investigation neatly. Or perhaps he simply wanted to see whether I'd run. Either way, I had traded a lie for a leash, and the leash was called *finish it within the month*.

But here is what neither of them had taken: the map case.

They had catalogued papers, letters, logbooks, loose sheets. Nobody had inventoried the case itself — the old leather-and-brass field case Vane had carried along every coast of the Reach, the one thing in the studio that looked like a tool rather than a treasure. Tools get overlooked. That is the whole art of hiding, I realized somewhere in that dark hour: not concealment, but classification. Make them file a thing in the wrong drawer in their minds and they will never open it again.

Inside the case: the fair copies of sheets one through thirty-nine. Vane's field notebooks. His private correspondence — what remained of it after Mother's quiet culling, which I had never asked about and now, sitting in the dark, began very much to wonder about.

And I could not leave it in the house. Pell had been too reasonable, and reasonableness that thorough always comes back around. So I dressed, took the case, went down the back stair past Wren's door (her breath hitched as I passed, and I froze, and it resumed, and I chose to believe she slept), and let myself out into the garden.

The orchard behind the house was planted before the war, before the shop, before any of us. Six apple trees, gnarled and half-wild, and in their midst the old well, dry these twenty years, capped with a stone slab that two strong men would struggle to shift and one frightened boy could, by leverage and patience, persuade.

I won't pretend the work was easy. Stone weighs what stone weighs, and fear adds its own tonnage. I got the slab up on rollers — two lengths of firewood — and dragged it aside, and looked down into the black throat of the well, and smelled the cold mineral breath of it, the smell of deep places and old water and everything that has ever been dropped away and given up on.

I lowered the map case on the well rope. Thirty feet down it swung once against the stone and went silent, settling into the dark like a seed into soil.

Then I pulled the rope up, coiled it, replaced the slab, swept the drag-marks with a broom branch, scattered leaf-fall over the scuffed earth, and stood back in the moonless dark and surveyed my work.

A mapmaker surveys. It's reflex. And what I saw was this: a patch of orchard ground, indistinguishable from every other patch, above a dry well that no one had opened in a generation. Invisible — unless you knew. Unless you had seen me in the garden at midnight. Unless someone talked, or guessed, or noticed that the firewood pile was two logs short.

Nothing is hidden forever. That's the first law of hiding and the last law of maps, which are the same discipline: every secret place is marked on somebody's chart, even if the chart exists only in a single memory. Mine, now.

I stood in the orchard with my heart going like a drum in a parade and admitted the truth to myself, because it seemed only fair that someone should hear it: I had just committed my first crime. Not the lie — the lie was survivable, lies dissolve like ink in water. The crime was the *hiding*. Possession, concealment, obstruction — I didn't know the words yet, but the shape of them was already pressing against the inside of my chest. The Admiralty had come for Vane's papers and left with most of them, and the ones they hadn't taken were now thirty feet underground because a seventeen-year-old apprentice had decided, in the space of a heartbeat, that some things belonged to the dead rather than to the Crown.

And the worst of it — the part I keep circling like a gull over a fish-heap — is that it had felt *good*. Not the fear. The clarity. For six years I had been a boy who told the truth badly and felt guilty thoroughly, and tonight I had discovered a second self, smooth and quick, who could stand in front of an Under-Secretary and build a fever story brick by brick without his hands shaking. I didn't know that boy. I wasn't sure I wanted to. But he existed now, and he had the map case's location memorized, and there was no unbefriending him.

I went back inside. On the stairs, Mother's door stood open an inch — not sleeping, never sleeping on nights like this — and her voice came through the gap, low:

"Well?"

"Gone," I whispered. "Deep."

A pause. Then, in the flattest register of all, the one beneath the flat one: "Kestrel. Whatever's in that case — when it comes up again, and it will come up, buried things always do — you tell me before you act. Promise me that much."

I promised. It was the third lie of the night, and by then I was good at it.

I climbed to the studio, uncapped the ink, and worked until dawn on sheet forty, drawing the true coastline of a coast I had never seen, from soundings in Vane's hand, while below me the city slept on top of its secrets like soil on seed.

Three hours from finished. One month to deliver it. And thirty feet under the apple trees, the truth of Aldous Vane sat in the dark, waiting for the water table of events to rise.

It rose faster than I thought.
