## 6. The Neighbor's Lantern

Old Hosk smelled of tar and boiled mutton and had, for as long as I could remember, appeared at our door whenever the weather turned foul, on the theory — never once stated aloud — that a family in trouble is like a chimney: it draws attention only when it smokes.

He arrived the evening after the Admiralty men came with their writ, which meant he either had not heard about the seizure or had heard about it and come anyway, and with Hosk the second was always likelier. He stood in the doorway of the shop holding a lantern that had outlived three wars and possibly its own maker, and he looked past me into the dim interior where Mother sat very still by the cold stove.

"Evening," he said. "Saw the King's men walking down Cooper's Lane today. Three of them. Walking slow, the way they do when they want to be seen."

"Come in before you let the fog in," Mother said, and it was a measure of how bad things were that she said it kindly.

Hosk came in. He set the lantern on the counter between Vane's empty ink pots, and the light made a small yellow island of us all, and outside the fog pressed against the windows like something reading over our shoulders.

"I'll say this plain," he said, "because I'm too old for riddles and too poor for lawyers. Whatever they took from here, they'll be back for more. And whatever you're keeping that they didn't take—" he raised one knuckled hand before I could open my mouth, "—don't tell me. I don't want to know it. Knowing things is what got your master killed."

"We don't know anything ourselves," Mother said.

Hosk looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at me. Then he looked back at her, and something passed between them that I was not invited to, some old arithmetic of favors done and debts carried, and he nodded as if a sum had come out right.

"No," he agreed. "Nobody knows anything. That's the spirit." He tapped the counter with a finger gone brown as ship's biscuit. "But if anybody *were* to need to move something, or carry something, or stand somewhere looking harmless while somebody else did a thing — well. I've been harmless in this port for fifty years. Nobody suspects a man of my face. There's nothing left in it worth suspecting."

I want to record, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because later — after everything — I would give a great deal to feel it again. What I felt was relief. Warm, shameful, immediate relief, the kind a drowning man feels toward any plank, including planks that are technically someone else's property. Here was a way through the checkpoints that did not require my own face, my own hands, my own neck. Here was an old man offering to walk into the dark ahead of me carrying a light.

I said yes. God forgive the speed of it. I said yes before Mother could say no, and I saw her see that, and I saw her decide — visibly, the way one decides to leave a pot on the fire — to allow it.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, required Hosk to do almost nothing, which suited him. On the night I needed to move the map case from the orchard well to a new hiding place — because the well, I had realized with the clarity that comes at three in the morning, was the first place anyone would look once they thought to look anywhere — Hosk would walk his usual route home from the Anchor, past our shop, lantern swinging, whistling his tuneless whistle. He had walked that route every night for twenty years. He was furniture. He was weather. If anyone asked, he had seen nothing, because he never saw anything; his eyes, he liked to say, went out with the tide.

And while every eye on Cooper's Lane followed the furniture, I would go over the orchard wall at the far end, where the hawthorn grew thick, and do the actual work unseen.

It should have worked. It was, in fact, a good plan — good enough that I permitted myself, briefly, the luxury of thinking we might get away with everything, which is the luxury that ruins people.

* * *

Here is what I did not account for, and what no map of mine would ever have shown me, because it belongs to that class of hazard cartographers are helpless against: other people's habits.

Hosk's route home took him past the shop at half past nine. But on the night in question, the Anchor closed early — a funeral, a spilled cask, I never learned which — and so Hosk passed our door at half past eight instead, and then, finding himself abroad earlier than expected and unwilling to go home to his own silence, he did what he always did when the evening ran long: he stopped. He leaned in the alcove beside our shuttered window, set his lantern on the sill stone, and smoked his pipe, watching the fog come off the harbor the way he'd watched it come off for two decades.

He was there forty minutes. Anyone could have told him he was there. Half the lane did tell him, afterward, that they'd seen him there.

And among the people who saw him — this I learned two days later, from Wren, who learned it from the baker's boy, who learned it from everyone — was a man who did not belong to Cooper's Lane, standing in the mouth of the alley opposite, wearing a coat too good for the neighborhood and doing a very poor job of pretending to read a broadsheet in a fog thick enough to drink.

* * *

"They're asking questions," Wren said. She came into the studio without knocking, which she never did, and shut the door behind her, which she also never did, and these two small violations together told me more than her words did. "Down at the market. A woman I know — Marta, sells ribbon — says a man's been asking about us. About who comes and goes. She said he asked particularly about old men."

"Old men generally?"

"Old men who visit us." Wren sat down on Vane's stool without asking, another first, and looked at me with an expression I recognized because I had been wearing a version of it myself for days. It was the expression of someone doing sums they don't want the total of. "Kess. They think it's Hosk. They think he's the one helping us, and that means they think there's something to help *with*, and now they're going to pull him in and ask him until he tells them something, because Hosk couldn't keep a secret from a turnip."

"He kept ours."

"He doesn't know ours. That's why he can keep it." She picked up a pen from the bench and put it down again, restless. "You have to warn him off. You have to tell him to stop coming by, stop stopping, stop being seen—"

"And if I do that, I confirm it. A guilty family suddenly freezes out their oldest friend? That's not a warning, Wren, that's a confession with a bow on it."

"Then what—"

"I don't know." And I didn't, and saying so felt like handing her a stone to hold.

She held it anyway. That's the thing about Wren that frightens me even now: she will pick up whatever you hand her and look at it steadily and ask what it weighs. She was fourteen. She should have been asking about ribbons.

"If they take him," she said quietly, "he won't understand why. He'll think he did something wrong. He'll spend whatever they do to him trying to work out what it was." She stood, put the stool back exactly where it had been — she'd noticed I noticed things like that — and paused at the door. "You always said maps show you where the water is deep. Somebody should make one for this."

Then she was gone, and I sat alone in the master's studio with his unfinished atlas and his empty ink pots, and I did the thing I was best at, which was to draw lines and measure distances, except this time the territory was Hosk.

* * *

Let me lay it out the way I laid it out on paper that night, because honesty requires it and because I have promised myself I will not prettify this part, however much the rest of me wants to.

Option one: do nothing. The Admiralty men would question Hosk. Hosk knew nothing — genuinely nothing, bless him — and so they would get nothing, and eventually they would release him, annoyed, and the suspicion would remain but the evidence would not. Cost to Hosk: fear, perhaps a night in a cell, perhaps rougher handling than that. Benefit to us: time. Time being the only currency we had.

Option two: warn him off. End the visits, end the stops, end the lantern by our window. Cost: confirmation of guilt, likely acceleration of whatever they planned for us. Benefit to Hosk: none, really, since the suspicion would follow him regardless. Once a man has been seen, he cannot be unseen.

Option three: confess to him. Tell him enough — not everything, never everything — so that he understood what he was caught in and could protect himself deliberately rather than blindly. Cost: spreading the secret to one more pair of ears, one more mouth that could be opened by force or by age or by drink. Benefit: Hosk would at least know what he was suffering for, and knowing men are harder to break than confused ones.

I sat with those three lines until the candle guttered, and I will tell you what I chose, and I will tell you that I chose it quickly, and I will not pretend otherwise.

I chose option one. I did nothing.

I told myself it was for his protection — that any move I made would make it worse for him, that ignorance was armor, that the kindest thing was stillness. All of which was true, in the way that most convenient things are true. But underneath the truth of it lay the simpler fact, which was that doing nothing cost me nothing, and cost him everything, and I paid the difference out of his account without asking his leave.

That is the trade I made. I record it here so that at least the ledger exists somewhere, even if the sea takes the rest.

* * *

They took him on Thursday.

Not dramatically — nothing about the Admiralty in Vessing was dramatic; they had learned long ago that terror works better at room temperature. Two men simply called at Hosk's cottage on the shingle road, and walked back down the lane with him between them, unhurried, and Hosk was waving to people as he went, because he thought — God help me, he *thought* — that they wanted directions.

Wren saw it from the window. She came and found me in the studio and stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, and she didn't say *I told you*, which was worse than if she had.

"How long?" she asked.

"A day. Maybe two. He knows nothing, so they'll run out of questions."

"And then?"

"And then they'll let him go, and he'll come straight here, and he'll want to know what that was about, and I'll have to lie to his face."

"You're getting good at that," Wren said. It wasn't cruel. That was the terrible part — it wasn't cruel at all. It was just an observation, noted and charted, the way I'd note a shoal. My little sister was learning to survey me.

* * *

They released him Saturday morning, and he came to the shop Sunday, because of course he did, because he was Hosk.

He looked smaller. Not beaten — Hosk was too dense with living to be beaten by anything so brief — but diminished, the way a house looks after a storm has been through it: everything still standing, nothing quite where it was. He settled onto the bench by the stove without being asked, which he had earned the right to do decades ago, and he held his hands out to the cold iron, and for a while nobody spoke.

"Funny thing," he said finally. "They asked me the same question eleven different ways. Eleven. I counted, after. Kept me occupied." He turned his palms as if inspecting them. "The question was: what does the Pemberton family keep in that shop that the King's men didn't find?"

Mother's needle stopped. Started again. Stopped.

"And what did you tell them?" I asked.

"Told them the truth. Said I didn't know. Said I'd known Aldous Vane thirty years and never once been invited past the front room, and that mapmakers are secretive as priests and twice as proud of it." He smiled, a little. "They believed me, I think. Or they got bored. Hard to tell the difference with that sort."

"Hosk," Mother began, "we never meant—"

"Oh, I know what you meant." He said it gently, which somehow made it land harder. "You meant nothing. That's the trick of it, isn't it? Nobody means anything. Things just happen to people who happen to be nearby." He looked into the stove's dead throat. "But I'll tell you what I worked out, sitting in that room with the eleven questions. I worked out that there *is* something. Because innocent families don't get that kind of attention, and guilty ones don't get friends questioned — they get friends warned off first. And nobody warned me off. Which means either you didn't think of it—" he glanced at me, and I felt the look go through me like a sounding line touching bottom, "—or you thought of it and decided I was safer not knowing. And I've decided to believe the second one. Being believed-in is about all I've got left, so I'm keeping it."

The silence in that room had weight. I could feel it pressing on the floorboards, on the atlas under the loose flagstone, on all of us.

"What do you want, Hosk?" Mother asked at last. "Name it. If we have it, it's yours."

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was considerable.

"Want?" He stood, slowly, joints reporting in one by one. "I want my evenings back. I want to lean on your window and watch the fog and have it mean nothing to anybody, including me." He collected his lantern from the counter — he'd left it, that first night, saying he'd fetch it when he fetched it, and now I understood that he had left it the way a man leaves a hostage. "And I want you to remember, all three of you, that when this is over — whatever *this* is — you owe me one evening of honest company. No secrets. Just fog and pipe smoke. That's the price. Don't pay it now. Pay it when you can afford it."

He let himself out. The bell over the door gave its small tired ring, and then the shop was quiet, and Mother looked at me across the counter with an expression I had never seen on her before — not anger, not fear, but a kind of grim bookkeeping, as though a figure had come in that changed every column.

"That man," she said, "has loved this family longer than you've been alive. And you spent him in a week."

"I know."

"Do you? Do you actually know it, Kestrel, or do you know it the way you know the depth of water on a chart — a number written where your feet stay dry?" She rose. "Because there's more coming. Whatever you're carrying, whoever you're planning to carry it to — the price isn't going to be paid by you. You're clever. Clever people arrange for the bill to land elsewhere. Your father was clever. So was Aldous. Look how that ended for both of them."

She went upstairs. I stayed below a long while, in the dark, listening to the fog horn out in the roads, counting the interval between blasts the way I'd count intervals between soundings.

Two minutes. Safe water. Everyone believes safe water until the day the sandbar moves.

* * *

I found Hosk two nights later, out on the shingle road where he walked when he couldn't sleep, and I walked beside him without speaking for the length of the seawall, because some apologies have to be made in silence first.

"I should have warned you," I said at last.

"Aye."

"I told myself it was safer for you not to know."

"Aye. I expect you did." He puffed his pipe. The ember lit his face red and brief, like a signal from a far country. "Was it true?"

"Some of it."

"That's usually how it goes." We walked. The shingle crunched. Somewhere out in the black water a buoy clanked its idiot bell. "Boy. Listen. I'm not angry. Angry's for people who expected better, and I've known your family too long to expect better — I expect *this*. Stubbornness and sacrifice and everybody deciding what's best for everybody else without asking." He stopped and faced me, and the fog stood around us like walls. "But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you're too proud to buy it. Those men who took me — one of them was careless. Talkative. While I was waiting, he complained to the other about being stuck in Vessing. Said the real work was at the quay gate. Said come Monday there'd be no more cat-and-mouse, because the captain there had orders to search *everything* that moved toward the morning boats."

My blood went very quiet and very fast at once.

"The morning boats," I repeated.

"All of them. Every crate, every parcel, every soul." Hosk knocked out his pipe against his palm. "Now. I don't know what you're carrying, and per our arrangement I don't want to. But if I were carrying something, and I had until Monday—" he looked at me, and in the dark his eyes were younger than the rest of him, "—I'd stop drawing plans and start choosing which ones to burn. Goodnight, boy."

He walked on toward his cottage, lantern swinging, and I stood on the shingle with the fog closing behind him, doing the only arithmetic that mattered anymore:

One checkpoint I hadn't planned for. One captain with orders to search everything. And the morning tide, coming whether I was ready or not.
