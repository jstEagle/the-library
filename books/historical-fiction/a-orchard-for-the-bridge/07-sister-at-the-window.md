## 7. Sister at the Window

I want it recorded — by someone, somewhere, in some ledger that outlives all of us — that I noticed before anyone told me anything.

That matters to me. It shouldn't have mattered as much as it did, but I was fourteen that autumn, and fourteen is the age at which being underestimated becomes the central wound of your existence. Everyone in our house treated me like the decorative one. Kestrel had the maps and the ink-stained fingers and the tragic dead master; Mother had her silences, which she wore like jewelry; and I had the window seat in the front room, where I sat with my sewing and my mending and my apparently vacant face, watching the street.

Here is what they didn't understand about the window seat. It is the best vantage point in Vessing. From it you can see the shop door below, the crooked stair, the mouths of Cooper's Lane and Bridge Street both, and — on a clear day, if you lean — a sliver of the harbor water flashing between rooftops like a coin dropped in a drain. I had spent two years learning my city from that seat the way Kestrel learned his coastlines: patiently, obsessively, and without anyone thinking to ask me what I knew.

So when my brother began going out at night, I knew within three nights. And when he started lying about it, I knew within four.

* * *

The first night, I forgave him. Everyone walks at night sometimes. Vessing is a port city; half its business is conducted after dark by men who'd rather their wives didn't know the terms.

The second night, I noticed *how* he walked. That's the thing nobody tells you about having a mapmaker for a brother: you learn what purposeful looks like. Kestrel didn't stroll. He moved the way he drew — each step placed, corners taken at consistent angles, pauses only at points of decision. He went out at the hour when the lamplighters had finished and the watch hadn't begun their second round, and he went out through the kitchen door rather than the shop door, which meant he didn't want Hosk next door seeing him, which meant it wasn't innocent, because Kestrel was incapable of doing anything interesting that wasn't also secret.

The third night, I followed him as far as the corner of Cooper's Lane, then lost my nerve and my slippers' dignity in equal measure, and came home to sit by the window until he returned, counting the minutes like a miser counts coins.

He came back smelling of fog and lamp oil, with wet hems and a look on his face I'd never seen before. Not guilt. Guilt I knew from Mother's face every time anyone mentioned the war years. This was something else — a kind of terrible clarity, like a man who has just read his own sentence and is already composing the appeal.

"Where were you?" I asked, sweetly, from the stairs.

"Out."

"Obviously." I came down two steps. "Kestrel. I'm not Mother. You can tell me or not tell me, but don't insult us both with 'out.'"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him weigh something, and I watched him decide against me — against telling me, I mean. He decided I was too young, or too much at risk, or too much his little sister to be handed whatever he was carrying around inside himself along with whatever was hidden in the orchard.

Because yes, I'd found the map case. Or rather, I'd found the absence of it. The case had lived under his bed since the officers came with their writ, and I had gone looking for it — because I am my brother's sister, and snooping is simply the feminine form of cartography — and it was gone, and there was a smear of well-water on the floorboards of the kitchen, and I can add two facts together faster than most people in this city can count coins.

"I'm sorry, Wren," he said. "It's better if you don't know."

And here is the part I'm not proud of, though I've stopped being ashamed of it either: I let him believe I believed him.

"That's fine," I said. "I'll just ask Mother, then."

His face did something wonderful and awful — a whole storm passing over a very small sea — and he said, "You wouldn't," and I said, "Probably not," and we stood there in the dark kitchen, two liars recognizing each other across the table, and neither of us said another true word all evening.

* * *

You should understand something about our family before this story goes any further. We were, by reputation and by long habit, truth-keepers. That's how Mother framed it, anyway, usually while doing something with her hands so she didn't have to look at you. *The Pembertons keep true accounts.* Father had been an honest customs clerk until the fever took him, honest to the point of tedium; Kestrel had been raised on Aldous Vane's catechism of exactitude — *a chart that flatters is a chart that kills* — and I had been raised on the stories of both, steeped in the family virtue like linen in lye.

Which is why what happened on Thursday night should have shaken me more than it did.

* * *

Thursday night, the watch came.

Not for us, at first — or officially not for us. Two of them, a corporal with a voice like gravel settling and a younger man whose helmet was a size too big, working their way down our street with a list, knocking at doors. I heard them at the Finches'. I heard them at the widow Marsh's. And I heard Kestrel's bedroom floor creak, and I heard the kitchen door ease open, and I understood with a certainty that felt older than myself: my brother was climbing out into the night *with the watch fifty yards away*, and if they saw him go, everything he was carrying — whatever it was, wherever it was hidden — would come apart right there in the lane.

Mother was at the sink. She had her back to the room, which was her way of being present at things.

"They're coming here," I said quietly.

"They're checking doors," she said. "Locks and lanterns. It's nothing."

"It's not nothing." I was already moving. "Kestrel's just gone out."

The silence behind me had a texture to it. When I glanced back, Mother had turned, and her hands were still, and she looked at me the way you look at a clock that has begun keeping time in a language you don't speak.

"Wren," she said. "What are you going to do?"

And I want to be honest, since honesty is supposedly our inheritance: I don't fully know what I was going to do. I had perhaps four seconds and no plan, and what I had instead was something better than a plan, which is a performance. Fourteen years of being overlooked teaches you exactly how people expect a girl like me to behave, and I stepped out onto the stoop wearing that expectation like a borrowed coat.

They were three doors down. The young one saw me first and elbowed the corporal, and they came up our walk with the particular unhurried menace of men who enjoy their authority more than their work.

"Evening," said the corporal. "Pemberton house?"

"That's us," I said, and made my eyes wide, and wrapped my shawl tighter as though the cold were the biggest problem in my world. "Is it about the lantern? Because if Old Hosk's been leaving his lantern burning again, I've told him a dozen times—"

"Lantern?"

"He hangs it by the fence and it keeps everyone awake and half the street thinks it's a signal fire." I rolled my eyes, expertly. I want you to appreciate that eye-roll. I had rehearsed it against the pantry door for years, aiming it at Kestrel, and it deployed now with the smoothness of a well-oiled hinge. "Is that what this is?"

The young one almost smiled. The corporal consulted his list, unamused. "This is a household check. Who resides here?"

"Me, my mother, my brother." I counted them off on my fingers like a child reciting. "Kestrel's abed. He's got a commission deadline — charts for the harbor office — and when he's working he sleeps like the dead and wakes up unbearable, so if you've come to wake him, I'd stand well back."

Both of them looked toward the house. Both of them looked at the dark windows. And I stood there in the cold holding my fabricated brother in place like a paperweight, feeling — I cannot describe this accurately, but I will try — feeling *wonderful*. Not frightened. Wonderful. There was a moment, maybe two seconds long, when the corporal squinted past me at the shuttered front room and the whole structure of my lie hung in the air between us like a soap bubble, and I felt the way I imagine Kestrel feels at the exact moment a coastline comes out right: that click, that *yes*, that private communion with correctness.

"Your brother," the corporal said slowly. "He works for the mapmaker. Vane."

"Worked," I corrected, and let my voice drop into the careful flatness people use for the dead. "Master Vane died in the summer. Kestrel's finishing the atlas. It's all he does now." I hesitated — a hesitation I had *planned*, mind you, planned on the stairs thirty seconds earlier, because a hesitation makes a lie breathe — and added, "Why? Has something happened?"

"No. Household checks. Curfew's ten o'clock." He tucked the list away. "Keep your door locked and your brother abed."

"Oh, I will," I said, with the solemnity of a girl making a vow to saints. "Goodnight, sirs."

They went on down the street. I stood on the stoop until they turned the corner at Bridge Street, and then I went inside, and shut the door, and leaned against it, and discovered that my hands were shaking.

That surprised me. The fear arriving late, like a guest who'd missed the dinner entirely and showed up for dishes.

Mother was standing in the kitchen doorway. She looked at me for a long time — longer than she'd looked at anyone in years, I think, including herself in mirrors.

"You lied to the watch," she said.

"Yes."

"Easily."

"Yes." And then, because some devil in me wanted her to say something true back, I added: "Was that wrong, Mother? Should I have told them where he really goes?"

She didn't answer. She crossed the kitchen and banked the fire and said, to the coals rather than to me: "Go to bed, Wren."

But she put her hand on my head as she passed — briefly, awkwardly, like someone remembering late how mothers are supposed to touch their daughters — and I lay awake afterward turning that hand over in my memory like a coin of uncertain currency. Approval? Fear? A warning?

I decided, in the end, it was pride. And I decided I liked it. And lying there in the dark with the decision warm in my chest, I understood that something in our house had changed forever, and that the change was me.

* * *

After that, Kestrel and I developed an arrangement without ever discussing it, which is the only kind of arrangement that works between siblings.

He went out. I kept the window. If the watch passed, I doused the candle in the front room twice — our old childhood signal, from the days when he'd sneak back from the harbor with stolen plums and I'd warn him of Mother's footsteps on the stair. Two douses meant *stay out of sight*. One douse meant *clear*.

He never asked how I knew when he needed covering. I never asked what he carried. Once — only once — he paused in the kitchen on his way out and looked at me and said, "Wren. If anyone ever asks you about these nights—"

"I've never seen you leave this house in my life," I said. "You're a ghost who eats porridge and complains about my singing. Ask me tomorrow and I'll swear to it with tears."

Something moved across his face — gratitude and grief fighting for the same ground — and he said, "When did you get like this?" and I said, "I've always been like this. You were just too busy being the important one to notice," and he laughed, actually laughed, the first time since Master Vane died, and went out into the dark, and I sat down at my window to keep watch over him like a lighthouse with a grudge.

I should say something here about whether I felt guilty. People will want to know. Truth-keepers' daughter, sworn family of exactitude, lying to officers of the Admiralty with a smile — surely some part of her recoiled?

No. That's the confession. Not one part. What I felt instead was competence, fierce and total, the way I imagine Mother must have felt the day she first kept a silence so complete even she believed it. Every lie I told fit. They clicked together. Where Kestrel agonized — I could see him agonizing, see it in the set of his shoulders when he thought I wasn't looking — I found I could build a falsehood the way he built a chart, clean lines, sound bearings, every detail supporting every other detail. The corporal's face came back to me for nights afterward: that squint past my shoulder, that slow retreat. *Mine*, I thought. *I made that happen.*

It should frighten me more than it does, writing that down. Perhaps it will later. At fourteen, discovering you have a gift is indistinguishable from joy, no matter what the gift is for.

* * *

Saturday, everything tightened.

I knew it before anyone said a word, from the window, from the street itself. The watch had doubled — pairs instead of singles, and new faces, harder ones, men who walked with their hands resting near their belts rather than swinging. They stopped people at the corner of Bridge Street and spoke to them longer than questions about curfews require. Twice I saw a description passed from one man to another, folded paper, held close.

And once — this is the part that made me leave my window for the first time in weeks — once I saw the young watchman from Thursday, the one whose helmet didn't fit, standing outside our shop and looking up at our windows while his partner talked to the cooper across the lane.

Looking up. At the window seat. At me, probably, though I'd shifted back into the shadow of the curtain fast enough that he'd have seen nothing but dimness and doubt.

I went down to the studio, where Kestrel was bent over his drawing board with that terrible clarity still living on his face, inking coastlines he might never sell, and I told him what I'd seen, all of it, in the flat quick voice I'd apparently been saving for exactly this purpose.

He listened without stopping his pen. Then he said, "How many at Bridge Street?"

"Two pairs. Maybe more by evening. And they've got a paper they keep reading."

"A description." His pen stilled at last. For a moment he stared at the unfinished coast in front of him — Master Vane's last coastline, the one I'd watched him labor over for a month — and I saw him doing arithmetic, his kind of arithmetic, distances and timings and probabilities of death.

"Kestrel." I kept my voice level, because one of us had to. "Whatever it is you're doing Monday morning — whatever's going to the harbor — they're going to be watching for you. Not for a stranger. For *you*. They've been asking about Vane's apprentice all week."

"I know."

"You can't go as yourself."

"I know, Wren."

"Then—" And here I did something I'd been turning over for two days, something that had come to me whole, the way my lies came to me whole, and I laid it out for him the way he'd taught me years ago to lay out a chart: start to finish, no gaps. "Then you need someone at the window who knows the route better than the watch does. You need someone small and forgettable who can walk ahead of you and count the men at each post and come back and draw it for you — because I can draw it, Kestrel, don't make that face, I've been watching you draw my entire life, I know what a survey looks like. You need—"

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"*No.*" It came out louder than he intended, and he lowered it immediately, which was worse somehow. "Wren. Listen to me. Everything I'm doing, I'm doing so that when this is finished — however it finishes — you and Mother are clean. Untouched. If you're seen on those streets, if your face gets into anyone's memory anywhere near mine—"

"My face was already in someone's memory. Thursday night. I gave it to them myself, smiling."

"And it bought you nothing but risk."

"It bought *you* forty minutes," I said, "and it bought Mother a quiet house, and it bought this family one more night of not being dragged into the street. Don't tell me what my own lies purchased. I keep better accounts than you do."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched my brother — the precise one, the truthful one, the keeper of the family virtue — arrive at the same conclusion I'd arrived at alone in the dark a week before: that the Pembertons' truth-keeping had sprung a leak, and the leak was shaped exactly like me, and there was no patching it now.

"We'll talk about it," he said finally. Which from Kestrel meant *never*, but which from Kestrel-with-my-eyes-on-him might mean *Monday*.

He went back to his inking. I went back to my window.

* * *

Sunday evening, the fog came in early and thick, the kind that swallows the harbor whole, and through it I watched a lantern bob along the fence line next door — Hosk's lantern, the one I'd blamed so cheerfully to the watch, except that tonight it was moving wrong, pausing too long at the gap in the hedge where a person could pass from his yard into ours unseen.

And behind it, half-lost in the fog, came a third shape. Not Kestrel. Too heavy, too official, walking with the rolling gait of a man who spends his life on ships' decks.

The lantern stopped. The official shape stopped beside it. And although the fog ate their words before they reached my window, I saw Old Hosk turn and look directly up at our house — look at my window, I'd have sworn it, straight at the seam in the curtain where my eye was pressed — and then shake his head, slow and deliberate, at whatever the other man said.

They stood there a long time. Then the official shape walked on toward Bridge Street, and Hosk's lantern hung motionless by his gate, and I sat in the dark doing sums of my own:

One neighbor who knew too much. One watchman who knew my face. One brother upstairs pretending to sleep. And the morning tide coming — always the morning tide — whether any of us were ready or not.
