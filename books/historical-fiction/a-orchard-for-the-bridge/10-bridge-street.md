## 10. Bridge Street

The body is a traitor long before the mouth learns to lie.

I learned that from Vane himself, though he never said it in so many words. He said it in corrections: *Your wrist is stiff, boy. A mapmaker's line comes from the elbow, not the fingers.* He said it when he caught me holding a pen like a kitchen knife and made me practice the same curve four hundred times until my hand forgot every other way of moving through the world. Four hundred curves. I could draw a coastline in my sleep, and apparently I did, because by nineteen my hands had become instruments — precise, elegant, unmistakable.

Which would have been lovely news if anyone at Bridge Street had wanted to hire an instrument.

What they wanted was Vane's apprentice. And Vane, bless his meticulous ghost, had described me to the Admiralty before he died — or someone had described me for him, or for themselves, and the description was now circulating through Vessing's checkpoints like a bad coin. *Young. Dark-haired. Carries himself quiet. Watch the hands; the boy has a mapmaker's hands.* I knew this because Hosk told me, before the fog and the silver and everything else I owed him, and because Wren had heard two watchmen gossiping outside the bakery with the idle cruelty of men who don't know their gossip is worth a hanging.

So the problem, as I stood in the fish-smelling dark behind Tallowman's smokehouse at half past three in the morning, was not how to get past a checkpoint. It was how to make my own hands into strangers.

* * *

The atlas was not on me. That much I had learned from the customs house, where walking it openly had worked like a charm and left a clerk with my face folded away in his memory like a pressed flower. Never again the same trick twice; Vane taught cartography, but he also taught that no honest survey survives its second pass uncorrected. So tonight the atlas rode inside a barrel of salt herring in the back of Dree Mullet's fish cart, packed between layers of brine-soaked straw, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed under a false lid I'd fitted myself at midnight while Dree snored and his wife counted coins in the next room and pretended very hard not to hear me.

Dree was a porter who moved goods for people who preferred not to be named on manifests, which is a polite way of saying he was a smuggler, which is a polite way of saying he was a businessman. He charged me eight coppers and a look of profound contempt, and he asked no questions, which in Vessing was the most expensive part of the service.

"You want it at the Bridge Street end," he'd said, hitching the cart. "Past the guard post, not before. You're certain."

"I'm certain."

"Because if they open that barrel, your book drowns, and I'm not fishing herring out of brine to save your reading."

"It won't drown," I said. "I built it to survive worse than soup."

He gave me another look, this one almost respectful, and we went.

I walked beside the cart as a porter walks — which is to say, wrong in every way my body had been trained. Porters slouch. They lead with the shoulder, drag one heel, keep their hands loose and low and slightly curled, as though permanently around a rope or a handle. Their knuckles are scarred and their nails are black and they swing their arms when they walk, big lazy pendulum swings, because nobody ever taught them that movement is a language and the whole street is reading.

I had spent the last two days teaching myself to speak porter. I carried actual loads to practice — sacks of grain up from the quay, a crate of lamp oil, once, absurdly, a piano leg, alone, down an alley, for reasons I declined to examine. My shoulders ached in places I hadn't known I owned. Wren watched me drop things on purpose and said nothing, which from Wren was a lecture, a blessing, and a threat all at once.

And now, in the dark behind the smokehouse, I put on the costume of my own betrayal: a borrowed coat two sizes too big, sleeves rolled to hide wrists that were too clean, a rope belt, ash rubbed into my fingernails, and — the masterstroke, if I say so myself, and at that hour of the morning somebody has to — a genuine fifty-pound sack of barley flour across my right shoulder, hired from the smokehouse man for two coppers and a promise to return it.

Fifty pounds of flour. Because here was the arithmetic that had kept me awake: a sergeant at Bridge Street had been told to watch for a mapmaker's hands. But no man watching hands looks past what those hands are carrying. Weight changes everything. Weight drags the shoulder down, shortens the stride, swells the knuckles red, forces the free hand into that loose porter's curl whether it wants to or not. I couldn't disguise my hands. So I gave them a job so heavy and so ordinary that they'd have no time to be eloquent.

The body betrays what the mind conceals. Fine. Then load the body down until all it can betray is exhaustion.

* * *

Bridge Street at dawn is a throat, and the Admiralty had posted a fist in it.

The guard post sat where the street narrows between the chandler's shop and the old tithe barn, a brazier, a barrier, six men, and a sergeant whose name I didn't know but whose posture I already hated: feet planted, weight even, eyes doing the work of a customs ledger. This was the middle checkpoint of three — the customs house behind me, the quay gate ahead — and it was the one that scared me most, because the first had been about parcels and this one was about people. The customs house wanted contraband. Bridge Street wanted *me*.

Dree rumbled up to the barrier with his cart and his lantern and his magnificent bored expression, and I peeled away from him twenty yards out, the way porters do when a queue forms, drifting toward the pedestrian gap with my flour sack riding my shoulder like a second skull.

"Sack down," said a guard, waving me toward the search table. "All loads checked."

"All loads checked," I agreed, in a voice I had also been practicing — lower, flatter, with a harbor-side vowel I stole off Dree and wore badly. I set the flour down. My shoulder sang a brief aria of gratitude that I kept entirely off my face.

A young guard poked it with a knife. Flour came out. He looked disappointed, as guards do when contraband declines to present itself.

"Where from?"

"Quay stores. Up to the baker on Candle Lane." I jerked my chin the way Dree does when he means *obviously, idiot*, and discovered mid-jerk that I had stolen that gesture too, and that theft, done often enough, starts to feel like craft.

The young guard waved me on, and I had the flour halfway back onto my shoulder when the sergeant spoke.

"Wait."

Two syllables, and the morning got colder.

He came around the barrier slowly, the way Vane used to approach a map he suspected of flattering the coastline. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had stopped wasting expressions decades ago. He looked at Dree's cart. He looked at me. He looked at the flour.

"Long night?" he said.

"Every night," I said. "Sir."

"Pick it up again. Slow."

I picked it up again, slow. The sack found my shoulder. My knees took the weight the way I'd trained them to, with a grunt, a stagger-step, a porter's whole grammar of complaint.

The sergeant watched my hands.

I felt it — actually felt it, like weather — his attention settle on my knuckles, my wrists, the way my fingers arranged themselves around the sacking. And here is what I know about being looked at by a professional: you cannot fool him with stillness, because stillness is itself a performance, and performances have tells. So I didn't hold still. I adjusted the load, swore softly at it, shifted it higher, let my hand slip and catch it — clumsy, human, annoyed. The hands of a man carrying fifty pounds are not elegant. They are desperate. I made mine desperate.

"Herring cart yours?" the sergeant asked.

"Not mine. I don't own anything." Which was, that week, true, and truth makes the best mortar for lies; Vane never wrote that down but he drew by it.

The sergeant walked the length of the cart. He looked at the barrels. He tapped one — an empty one, thank whatever gods supervise smuggling — and listened to it ring. Dree chewed something and gazed into the middle distance with the serenity of a man whose conscience, if he ever had one, had been sold years ago at a fair price.

Then the sergeant said, "Open the tall one. Third from the left."

My heart performed a maneuver I will not describe in a document I may someday be forced to account for.

That was the herring barrel. That was *the* barrel, brine and straw and oilcloth and Aldous Vane's life's work sleeping under a lid I had fitted in the dark with glue and prayer. And there was nothing — *nothing* — I could do. Any protest, any sweat, any glance was a signature. The body betrays what the mind conceals, and my body was currently composing a confession in sweat across my lower back.

Dree climbed down without hurry. "That one's full," he said. "You want it opened, you're cleaning it."

"Open it."

Dree looked at me. Just for a moment — a flat, unreadable merchant's look, the kind that prices things. Then he shrugged, knocked the lid loose, and began hauling out herring by the armful, laying them on the cart bed in silver rows, brine dripping, the smell detonating across the whole width of Bridge Street.

The sergeant leaned over the barrel. Straw. Brine. More straw. Herring, herring, herring, an entire drowned choir of them, and somewhere beneath all that silver noise, thirty-one sheets of the finest coastal survey the Vessing coast had ever seen, soaking in pickle juice, waiting.

He reached in. He pulled out straw. He turned it over in his fingers.

And then — I will swear this on any chart you like — a herring flopped. One of the ones laid out on the cart bed, half-dead, brine-shocked, chose that exact moment to arc its whole silver body into the air and slap down against the sergeant's boot.

He stepped back. He looked at his boot. He looked, briefly, like a man rethinking several of his life's decisions.

"Enough," he said. "Reload it. Move along."

Dree restacked herring with the unhurried rhythm of a man paid by the hour, and I stood there holding fifty pounds of flour, watching the atlas disappear layer by layer back under the lid, and understood that I had just survived on a dead fish's sense of theater. Somewhere, I thought, Vane is laughing. He always said the coast keeps its own counsel and reveals what it likes to whom it likes, and if the sea wanted its atlas delivered, evidently the sea would send fish.

The sergeant, though. The sergeant wasn't done.

As Dree clicked the tongue and started the mule forward, the sergeant fell into step beside me — not blocking, not touching, just *there*, walking, the way a shadow walks.

"Candle Lane," he said.

"That's the one."

"Baker's name?"

I had prepared a name. I had prepared it two days ago, rehearsed it, attached it to a real bakery with a real baker who owed the Pembertons for a chart of his ovens' heat-drafts and would, I hoped, cover for a stranger's flour. I opened my mouth to spend it.

And my hands betrayed me.

Not dramatically. Nothing so useful. It was only that, walking with the sack balanced, my free hand rose — absently, automatically, the way it rises a hundred times a day — and sketched a small, unconscious curve in the air. A coastline. A smooth, confident, mapmaker's arc, drawn by fingers that had been drawing coastlines since they were nine years old and drew them now the way other hearts beat.

The sergeant saw it. I watched him see it. Something moved behind his eyes — not triumph, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man confirming a figure in a ledger he'd half hoped was wrong.

"Candle Lane," he repeated softly. "Right. On you go."

I went. Fifty pounds of flour, one street's length of agony, and then the corner, and then the next, walking at porter-speed, not running, *never* running, while my mind ran hard enough for both of us.

He'd seen the hand. He'd seen it and he'd let me walk. Which meant one of two things: either a floating herring had bought more goodwill than any fish deserves, or the sergeant now knew precisely what walked out of his checkpoint wearing a porter's coat, and had decided — for reasons of his own, reasons I couldn't price — to let the ledger stand open a little longer.

There were men like that in the Admiralty's service. Hosk had told me so in the fog, spitting: *Half of them signed the King's papers and half of them signed somebody's debt, and neither lot loves the office.* Maybe the sergeant was the first kind, tired and slow to hang a boy on a hand gesture. Maybe he was the third kind, the kind that serves itself. Either way, I had been seen. Truly seen. And somewhere between Bridge Street and the quay gate, a description was going to grow hands.

* * *

At the chandlery yard past the narrows, per the plan, I took the flour back to the smokehouse man, collected my two coppers' dignity, and met Dree at the lane behind Cooper's Row, where the cart waited and the barrel waited and the morning was turning the color of pewter.

Dree handed the barrel down to me personally, which was not in our arrangement, and held it a moment longer than necessary.

"Whatever's in there," he said, "it isn't herring."

"No."

"And the sergeant knows you."

"The sergeant suspects me. There's a difference."

"There's a difference between dry and damp, too," Dree said, "right up till you're underwater." He spat, professionally, and gathered his reins. "Eight coppers says you don't make the quay gate."

"I only owe you eight coppers total."

"Then consider it advice at no charge." He clicked the mule forward, then paused. "Boy. The fish that jumped? I've carted herring eleven years. Dead fish don't jump."

I stood in the lane with the barrel in my arms and watched him go, and thought about what lives under brine and comes back moving.

The atlas was damp at the corners. Of course it was. By the time I reached the safe dark of the tanner's wall, my fingers — those eloquent, traitorous, irreplaceable fingers — were checking the oilcloth seams, and finding, near the base, a slow seep of brine that had crept a finger's width up the wrapping and touched the edge of the bottommost sheet.

Vane's sheet. The first coastline he ever drew for the atlas, the one he called the spine of the whole work.

The ink hadn't run. Ink like his doesn't run; he mixed it stubborn, the way he did everything. But the paper had drunk the salt, and the edge had begun, very gently, to curl — the sea taking its toll, penny by penny, on the work that belonged to it.

And underneath the atlas, when I lifted it out into the gray light to check the damage, the letter was where I had hidden it in the lining, Vane's sealed accusation against my own family, dry, intact, patient as a knife.

I had until the morning tide. I had two checkpoints' worth of luck already spent. And now I had a new problem, one I hadn't planned for, because planning assumes the enemy stands still:

Sergeant Bridge Street knew my hands. Which meant that by now — unless I was very lucky, and my luck that week had a habit of arriving in the form of dying fish — the captain at the quay gate knew them too.

I wrapped the atlas close, turned toward home through the waking streets, and found, waiting in the doorway of our lodging above the shop, my mother — awake, dressed, arms folded, with Vane's missing seal-wax stub in her open palm and a look on her face that told me the hour of silence was over.

"We need to talk," she said, "about what your father witnessed."
