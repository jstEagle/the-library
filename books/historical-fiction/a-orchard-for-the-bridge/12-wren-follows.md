## 12. Wren Follows

I want it on the record, before anyone tells this story wrong, that I did not set out to follow my brother. I set out to fetch bread. The fact that the bakery lies in the opposite direction from the harbor is a detail I will defend to my grave, and my grave, if things go on as they have been going, may be dug sooner than any of us planned.

Kestrel left the shop an hour past midnight. I know because I was awake, and I am always awake now, which Mother blames on growing pains and I blame on the fact that our family has become a conspiracy, and conspiracies do not sleep well. He came down the stairs on the balls of his feet like a man who has practiced being quiet, which is its own confession — honest people walk loudly because they believe the world owes them the courtesy of hearing them coming.

He wore the porter's coat. Not his own coat, which fits him, but the rough brown one that smells of fish and other men's labor, the one he's taken to wearing these three nights past. And under it, flat against his chest where a book might sit, a shape.

I gave him a hundred-count head start. Then I put on my own coat, took the bread money out of the jar — because I refuse to be a liar *and* a thief — and went after him into the fog.

* * *

Vessing at night is a different city drawn over the old one, the way a chart gets corrected: same coastline, new dangers marked in fresh ink. The fog had come up off the water the way it does in autumn, thick enough that you could lean on it, and every lamp was a smudge of orange like something bleeding through paper.

I kept Kestrel by sound more than sight. He has a particular step when he thinks no one is watching — quicker than his working-day walk, with a little hitch at the end, as though each stride ends in a question. *Should I be doing this?* Every step asked it. Every step answered yes anyway.

We are alike that way, Kestrel and I, except that he asks the question and I skip straight to the answer. Someone in this family has to be decisive, and Mother has decided her role is silence, and Kestrel has decided his is martyrdom, so by process of elimination the decisiveness fell to a fourteen-year-old girl who wanted very much to be asleep.

Here is what nobody tells you about following someone through fog: you learn them. You learn the exact weight of their worry by how fast they walk. You learn what they're carrying by how they hold their shoulders. My brother walked like a boy carrying a cathedral, and I loved him so much in that moment that it frightened me, because love, I was beginning to understand, is not a warm thing in this story anymore. Love is a door left open. Love is the shape of your brother under a porter's coat, visible to anyone who knows to look.

And somebody knew to look. That was the whole trouble with Vessing this week. Vane was dead — they said drowned, they said accident, they said it in the tone people use when they want you to stop asking — and ever since, the Admiralty's men had been walking our streets like surveyors, measuring us for coffins.

* * *

He went down Cooper's Lane and I lost him for four minutes, which felt like four years, and found him again at the corner of Bridge Street because he stopped there, in the shadow of the guard post, and stood very still doing whatever it is mapmakers do — I imagine drawing the place in his head, marking the sentries, counting exits. I watched him watch the checkpoint. Two soldiers, a brazier, a sergeant who moved like a man who'd been cold since childhood.

Then Kestrel stepped out of the shadow, picked up a fish crate from beside Chandler's shuttered door as if he owned it, hoisted it onto his shoulder, and walked through the checkpoint like cargo himself.

The sergeant looked at him. Looked at his hands, actually — I saw it, saw the man's eyes go to Kestrel's hands the way you'd check a horse's teeth — and then waved him through, and my heart started again after a pause long enough to draft a will.

That was when I understood what the crate was for. It wasn't hiding anything. It was hiding *him*. It changed his hands. It made him a porter instead of an apprentice, a body instead of a name. My brother, who can draw a coastline so true that sailors weep at it, had learned to redraw himself.

I should have gone home then.

I would like the record to show that I considered going home. I stood at the mouth of Bridge Street with the fog beading on my eyelashes and conducted a brief internal inquiry, in which I argued both sides with tremendous skill, and in which the side arguing for home pointed out that Mother would kill me, and the side arguing onward pointed out that Mother would kill me either way if Kestrel got caught, and dead is dead regardless of whose fault it's ruled to be.

Also — and here is the true thing, the thing I'll admit only here, in the privacy of my own skull — I wanted to see the harbor. I wanted to see what all this was for. Our family had spent two weeks speaking in a code of looks and half-finished sentences, and I was done being a child kept outside the map. If my brother was smuggling something to save us all, I intended to at least witness the geography of it.

So I followed him down toward the water, keeping to the warehouse wall, and this is where I must confess my second great talent, discovered that night, right after lying: I am good at disappearing. Small, quick, forgettable — I have spent years cultivating invisibility out of pure sibling spite, certain that one day it would come in handy against Kestrel's habit of telling me what to do. The world provides, eventually, for everyone.

* * *

The quay gate was worse than Bridge Street. Worse the way a storm is worse than rain.

There were six men there, not two, and a lantern on a pole, and a captain — I knew he was a captain by the coat and by the way everyone else stood slightly crooked around him, like grass bent by wind. He had a face like a closed ledger. Beside him, holding the lantern, a clerk with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of a man who memorizes faces professionally, and I recognized him — or rather I recognized his *type*, because Kestrel had described him after the customs house: the clerk who looks too long.

They were searching everything. A fishwife argued over her baskets while a soldier turned out each one, herring by herring. An old man's bundle got unwrapped down to his spare shirt. Nobody passed clean.

Kestrel stopped twenty yards off, in the black between two warehouses, and I saw him do the arithmetic — I could nearly hear the pencil scratching. He couldn't carry the crate through; they were opening crates. He couldn't go back; dawn was coming and dawn meant the tide and the tide meant the ship and the ship meant everything.

He crouched. He opened the crate. And he took something out — flat, wrapped in oilcloth — and did something to it that I couldn't see, some folding, some binding, and then he put it inside his coat again, differently, flatter, closer, and abandoned the crate like a man abandoning a decoy he'd already gotten his money's worth from.

Then he walked toward the gate with empty hands and open face, and I thought, *he's going to talk his way through, he's done it once, he can do it again—*

And that was when the clerk said, loud enough to carry across the quay:

"Captain. That one. That's the one from customs. Tuesday."

* * *

I have heard that the body knows it's dying before the mind agrees. I don't know if that's true, but I know my whole body went to iron when the clerk pointed, and every plan I owned evaporated except one, and the one plan was idiotic.

Kestrel stopped walking. I watched him weigh it — run and confirm everything, or stand and gamble — and he stood, and turned around with a porter's dull puzzlement, and said something I couldn't hear, and the captain came forward with the lantern.

The light swung across Kestrel's face. "Name," the captain said.

"Tom Wicker," said my brother, in a voice I'd never heard him use, low and bored and faintly stupid. "Cartage."

"Cartage." The captain looked at the empty hands. "Where's your load?"

"Delivered." A shrug. Perfectly timed. I'd have applauded if I weren't busy planning a felony.

The captain didn't move. The clerk leaned in and said something — I caught the word *apprentice* and the word *hands* — and the captain said, "Show us your hands, then, Tom Wicker."

And Kestrel held them out, and the lantern-light fell on those long, precise, ink-stained fingers, the fingers that had held a pen every day of their life, the fingers that were described in some Admiralty file down to the callus on the middle knuckle, and the captain looked at them for a long, long time, and I understood with total clarity that my brother was about to hang, tonight, on this quay, because of his hands.

Which is when I acted. Because that is the other thing nobody tells you about love: it makes you fast. All those years of being the slow one, the little one, the *wait in the shop, Wren* — apparently I had been storing it up.

I ran.

Not away. Toward. I came out of the dark between the warehouses screaming — and I mean screaming, full voice, the voice I use when Wren wants something badly enough — "*TOM! TOM WICKER, YOU THIEVING BASTARD, THAT'S OUR CART MONEY, YOU GIVE IT BACK—*" and I hit him at a dead run, which knocked him sideways into the captain, which was not part of the plan but improved it, and clung to his arm like a burr and sobbed at him, magnificently, horribly, with genuine tears because terror makes excellent stagecraft.

"You took the cart money, you took it and left me with the mule, Da's going to break your neck—" I rounded on the captain with wet, furious eyes. "Is this him? Is this the thief? Take him, take the whole of him, but I want the coin purse, it's mine, it's got my name stitched in it—"

The captain looked at me. I looked back with the blank outrage of the genuinely aggrieved, which is the easiest expression in the world to fake when you are genuinely terrified and furious at everything, including yourself.

"This your sister?" the captain asked Kestrel.

"No," said Kestrel.

"Yes," I said, louder, at the same moment.

The captain's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "She says yes. You say no."

"She says a lot of things," Kestrel muttered, in the bored-stupid voice, but his hands — I felt them, since I was still attached to his arm — were shaking.

The clerk was frowning. The clerk was *comparing*, I could see it, comparing the description in his head — apprentice, seventeen, alone, mapmaker's bearing — against the evidence before him: a porter being assaulted by a feral little sister in public. Descriptions are drawings, I realized, watching the clerk struggle, and drawings fail when the subject won't hold still. Kestrel alone was a suspect. Kestrel mid-scandal was scenery.

"Search them," the captain said at last, wearily, because captains must.

A soldier patted Kestrel down — and I felt Kestrel's heartbeat through his sleeve, fast as a bird's, while the soldier's hands passed over the flat oilcloth shape against his chest and registered it, apparently, as a shirt seam or a bandage or nothing at all. Then the soldier patted me, found nothing but hunger and fury, and stepped back.

"They're nothing," the soldier said.

"Nothing," the captain agreed. And then, to Kestrel, with that almost-smile again: "Go home, Tom Wicker. Take your sister. And if I see your hands on this quay again, I'll remember them."

* * *

We made it four streets before either of us could speak. He walked fast; I matched him; the fog swallowed the harbor behind us lamp by lamp.

"You," he finally said, "are supposed to be asleep."

"I'm supposed to be a great many things. Obedient. Uninformed. A child." I was shaking now that it was over, the way you shake after, and I hated it, hated that my body waited until the danger passed to admit it had been afraid. "You left the atlas-shaped thing inside your coat where a soldier patted you."

"It was sewn flat. He felt binding, not pages."

"You *sewed* it? When? With what? You can't thread a needle without swearing."

"Focus, Wren."

"I am focused. I'm focusing on the fact that I just saved your life with a lie so good the Admiralty believed it, and you're scolding me about bedtime." We turned onto Cooper's Lane and I grabbed his sleeve and made him stop under the eaves, where the fog was thinnest, so I could see his face. "Who's Marlow?"

His stillness told me everything before his mouth did. "Where did you hear that name?"

"The night Vane died, Mother said it in her sleep. She's said it twice since." I crossed my arms. "I'm not a child, Kestrel. I've been counting what this family doesn't say, and the list is longer than what it does. There's a letter. There's a ship on the morning tide. There's a man named Marlow who can use it, and you're carrying it inside your coat like a second heartbeat, and last night you paid old Hosk in silver instruments, and don't look surprised, Hosk talks in his cups and Hosk's granddaughter talks to *me*."

For a moment he just looked at me, and the look had layers — anger, fear, and underneath both, unwillingly, something like pride, which was the layer that scared me most, because pride means acknowledgment, and acknowledgment means I'm in it now, truly in it, past the point where anyone can pull me out and hand me back my childhood.

"If they'd caught you," he said quietly, "they'd have caught me twice over. One suspect is a mistake. Two siblings is a family. Families get tried together."

"Then we shouldn't have families," I said. "Or we shouldn't do treason."

"We're not doing treason. We're doing the opposite of treason. That's the whole problem — the difference is invisible unless you're holding the proof of it." He rubbed his face. Fog dripped off his nose. "Wren. Go home. Please. What you did tonight — thank you. Now never do it again."

"And if they catch you tomorrow?"

"They won't."

"That's what Vane probably said," I said, and watched it land, watched it hurt, because I was angry enough to want it to hurt. "About drowning. About accidents."

He didn't answer that. There was nothing true to answer it with.

We walked the rest of the way in silence, and parted in the alley behind the shop, him to the cellar hatch, me to the kitchen window I'd jimmied months ago for exactly this kind of evening, because I told you: the world provides. But at the window I stopped, and he stopped, and in the grey beginning-of-not-night-anymore we looked at each other, two liars of the Pemberton line, and I said the truest thing I'd said all night:

"They have your face from the customs house and now they've seen mine. Whatever happens tomorrow — we're both in the drawing now."

He nodded slowly. "I know."

"Do you?" I climbed through the window and stood in the dark kitchen, listening to him go, and finished it aloud to no one, because someone in this family has to say things plainly. "Because the captain remembers hands, and the clerk remembers faces, and by morning they'll have a description of both of us — and I'd wager the tide itself that they'll be standing at the quay gate waiting for us, with Vane's letter in their imagination and a noose in their pocket."

Above me, Mother's floorboard creaked. She was awake too. Of course she was. In this house, lately, sleep was the only thing nobody was smuggling successfully.

Tomorrow the tide would turn, and turn with it everything we had, and I lay down on the kitchen bench without undressing and discovered my third talent of the week, the most useful one yet: I could lie there in perfect stillness and rehearse, over and over, the exact scream I'd use at the quay gate — just in case tomorrow required a second performance.

It would. I knew it the way you know weather.

But that, as they say in Mother's prayers, is tomorrow's trespass.
