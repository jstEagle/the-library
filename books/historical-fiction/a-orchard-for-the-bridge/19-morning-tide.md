## 19. Morning Tide

I have never trusted harbors before dawn. They are places that pretend to be asleep while counting everything you own.

I stood at the edge of the quay with my hood up and my heart going like a fish in a bucket, and I watched the *Merrow's Grace* lie there in the gray water with her lamps lit along the rail, and I told myself the sensible thing. The sensible thing was to go home. The sensible thing was to be in bed when Kestrel came back down the gangway, so that when Mother asked where we had both been all night, neither of us would have to invent anything new.

The sensible thing lasted about as long as it takes a sensible thing to die, which is to say until the tide started to turn.

Because here is what nobody tells you about being the younger one in a family of liars-by-necessity: once you have learned to cover for someone, you cannot stop watching them. It is like learning to see a current in the water. You can stand on the bank and swear you'll stay dry forever, but your eye keeps going back to the place where the surface moves wrong. All night I had watched my brother-sibling-my-Kestrel move through this city like a needle through cloth, in and out, thread after thread, and every stitch they made was a stitch I had helped hide. And now here was the last one, the long pull through the harbor itself, and I was supposed to sit home by the cold stove and *wait*?

I don't think so. I am my mother's daughter and Vane's apprentice's sister, and waiting quietly has never once been in our blood.

So there I stood, third crate from the fish stall, close enough to hear the gulls arguing over the gutting tables, far enough back that the lamplight didn't reach my face. The quay gate was behind me. I could feel it behind me the way you feel a dog that has decided not to like you. Somewhere in that little guardhouse sat Captain Ennett's men with two descriptions now instead of one, and one of those descriptions was mine, and I had earned it honestly, which somehow made it worse.

The sky went from black to the color of dishwater to the color of old pewter, and then, without any ceremony at all, it was morning.

* * *

They brought him out at half past six.

I saw the escort first — four men, Admiralty coats, walking too evenly, the way men walk when they're pretending a prisoner isn't a prisoner. And between them, Kestrel. Hoodless. Hands empty. That was the part that stopped my breath: the hands. Kestrel Pemberton without ink on their fingers and without the map case under their arm looked like a coat with no one inside it. Twenty-two years of my life I have known those hands busy at something, and seeing them swinging loose at their sides was like seeing our house with the roof taken off.

They walked him up the gangway and the crowd on the quay — dockhands mostly, and two women selling pies, and a customs clerk who made my skin crawl because he had been at the checkpoint three nights ago and I would know his squint anywhere — the crowd watched with the mild interest people give anything free. Nobody knew. That was the miracle of it. A family might hang or might not, an atlas twenty years in the making might go to sea or to the fire, and the pie women were arguing about whether the herring would run late this year.

Then a second figure came up the plank, and this one walked differently. Slow, deliberate, a man whose feet were used to courtroom floors rather than decks. Gray at the temples, a lawyer's coat worn well but not new, and when he reached the rail he turned and faced the quay the way a man faces a jury.

Marlow.

I had never seen him. I knew him only as a name said in our kitchen in the low voice Mother used for things she'd sworn never to speak of — Marlow, the advocate, sailing on the morning tide, the one man who can use it. And here he was, real as weather, taking custody — I heard the word come off the deck, *custody*, passed from the captain to him like a parcel — of whatever Kestrel had carried out of our house sewn into the binding of a dead man's atlas.

The captain stood beside him. Big man, weathered, with a face like a cliff that has opinions about the sea. He had searched my sibling's bag with his own hands. He had held the letter, or what lived inside the letter's hiding place, up to lamplight in his own cabin. I knew none of this for certain then; I knew it the way you know things about people you've never met, which is to say I knew it completely and could prove nothing.

Marlow spoke to someone — a harbor officer, a witness, I couldn't tell — and I saw paper change hands, and I saw the harbor officer write, and I saw Marlow sign. My chest did something complicated. Because I understood, in the way you understand things all at once and only afterward find the words, that what was being signed was us. Our names going into a record. Protection, entered before witnesses, the way you enter cargo or debt. The Pembertons, weighed and found worth carrying.

Kestrel did not look at me. Good. Kestrel had spent three days teaching me how not to be seen, and it would have broken something in both of us if they had broken their own rule at the end. But just before they turned to follow the captain aft, they did one small thing. They took off their glove — left hand — and pressed their bare palm flat against the rail, one breath, two, and then took it away again.

That was ours. When we were children and Mother sent us to bed early, Kestrel would press a palm against the wall between our rooms, and I would press mine against the other side, and that was how we said goodnight without giving the watchman downstairs a reason to grumble. One breath, two. *Still here.*

One breath, two. Still here.

* * *

The tide turned at seven minutes past seven. I know because Hosk taught me to read the harbor clock, back when I was small enough to ride on his shoulders, and because yesterday morning, standing in fog with silver instruments changing hands between us, Kestrel had told me the exact minute the *Merrow's Grace* would take her water. "If I'm not on that quay by then," they'd said, "go home and burn nothing and say nothing, and let Mother decide what's true."

Hosk. I owe that old man more than I know how to carry, and I know he'd wave it off and charge me for the waving. They questioned him, after his lantern was seen near our shop. Two hours, Mother said, though she wouldn't say how she knew. They let him go, and he came out of the guardhouse older than he went in, and he still — I saw him, just now, down the quay by the bollards, leaning on his stick with his bad hip cocked — he still came out at dawn to watch the ship go. Not for Kestrel. For the ship, maybe. For the ending of things. Old men come to harbors to watch endings; it saves them the trouble of dying to see one.

The lines came off. The gulls rose screaming as if the whole quay had insulted them personally. The *Merrow's Grace* leaned out from her berth slow as a thought forming, and her sails took the morning wind one after another, and the water that had been holding her all night finally agreed to let her go.

And there was Kestrel at the rail. Small already. Smaller. I put my hand up — not waving, that would be idiocy with the harbor officer still writing things — just up, palm out, at the level of my shoulder. Held it. One breath. Two.

Whether they saw, I will never ask. From the deck it must have been nothing, a smudge of a person among crates and pie sellers and gulls. But I felt the answer anyway, the way you feel the other palm through a wall: *still here, still here, go on now.*

The ship stood out past the mole, past the bell buoy, past the point where the gray water turns the color of steel, and then she was only sail, and then she was only the idea of a ship, and then the morning haze took even the idea.

I stood there longer than I should have. Long enough that my feet went numb in my boots, long enough that the pie woman sold out and packed up, long enough that the customs clerk with the squint went off duty and I watched him go and hated him a little less than yesterday, which is a strange thing to feel toward a man who memorized your brother's face for a living. Relief does that. It makes you generous toward everyone except yourself.

Because underneath the relief — and the relief was real, it filled me up like the tide fills the basin, I could have floated on it — underneath it was this: the family was cleared, yes. Entered into record, protected by an advocate's signature before witnesses, safe. And the price of that safety was that we were scattered like a dropped hand of cards. Kestrel at sea, aboard, a witness required to travel with the evidence — with Marlow, wherever Marlow was bound. Mother in the shop above the street where every neighbor now knew the watch had questions about us. Hosk bought with dead Vane's instruments and paid in shame. And me, seventeen years old, standing alone on a quay with a fabricated alibi in my mouth like a stone I'd swallowed and learned to live around.

We had survived. That was the astonishing thing. We had survived the way a rope survives a storm — by fraying everywhere at once and holding only in the middle, and nobody able to say which strand did it.

* * *

The walk home took eleven minutes. I counted, because counting is what our family does instead of crying.

Vessing in the morning light is almost decent. The fish carts, the bread smell off Baker's Row, the sweeps on the roofs calling to each other in their chimney language. Ordinary streets full of ordinary people who had slept all night without once wondering if the orchard well would give up its secrets. I walked past Bridge Street and did not look at the guard post, and then made myself look, because Kestrel says the thing you refuse to look at is the thing that walks up behind you. The sergeant was there. He glanced at me and away. Either my face wasn't in his description anymore, or it was, and he was letting me pass for reasons of his own. Both possibilities sat in my stomach like bad oysters.

Our shop door stuck, the way it always sticks, the way it stuck the night Vane came to the house, the night Mother burned half the correspondence and swore the rest of us to silence. Some doors you have to lift and shove. I lifted and shoved.

Mother was at the table. Not working — that was the first wrongness. Mother Pemberton at eight in the morning with no work in front of her, no mending, no ledger, no knife and onions. Just sitting, with her hands folded, looking at the middle distance like it owed her money. She'd been there all night, by the look of her. She'd dressed for it, too — her good collar, the one she wears to funerals and rent day.

She looked up when I came in. She read my face in one pass, top to bottom, the way Kestrel reads a chart.

"The tide's turned," I said.

"I know. I heard the bell buoy." Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't; I saw her fold them tighter to make them stop. "Say it plain, Wren."

"They're clear." Plain, then. Plain as I've ever said anything. "Marlow came aboard before she cast off. There was paper, and witnesses, and he signed. The captain let Kestrel stay aboard as witness — that's what they called it, witness — traveling with the advocate. Mother, the captain chose. I watched him choose. Whatever they put in front of him last night, he chose our side of it."

For a moment she didn't move at all. Then she did the thing I have seen her do exactly twice in my life: she put her hand over her mouth, and above it her eyes closed, and she was somewhere else entirely — some night years ago, some room, some thing witnessed and swallowed — and then she came back, and her hand came down, and she was Mother again, only more so.

"Scattered," she said. Not a question. She'd already done the arithmetic herself, of course. She'd probably finished it before I got home.

"Kestrel goes where the inquiry needs them. Weeks, months, I don't know. Marlow's bound for the assizes first, everyone says so — the conspirators' trial. Kestrel will testify. Their name will be in the record next to ours." I sat down across from her because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me. "And Mother — there's more. The magistrate's clerks were at the harbor office already. Asking after the household. After you."

Something moved in her face. Something old and tired and, underneath the tiredness, newly awake. "Asking what?"

"Whether the Pembertons will give testimony of their own. Whether the family witnessed anything." I said it gently, as gently as I've ever done anything, because I knew — the way you know things in this family, sideways and too soon — that I was asking her to open a door she'd nailed shut years ago with her own hands. "They want statements within the fortnight. Mother, they want to know if we'll speak."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the harbor bell rang the hour, and a cart went by, and Vessing went on being Vessing, indifferent and alive. Then Mother Pemberton unfolded her hands, laid them flat on the table like a woman smoothing a chart, and said:

"Then we'll speak."

And the way she said it — plain, no waver in it, the silence of twenty years breaking over that table like a keel coming through ice — I knew the hardest part of our story hadn't ended on any quay.

It was starting right there, at our own kitchen table, with her.
