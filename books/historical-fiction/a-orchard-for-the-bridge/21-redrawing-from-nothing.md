## 21. Redrawing from Nothing

The inquiry ended on a Tuesday, which struck me as appropriate. Treason, in my experience, is a weekday sort of crime — committed in offices, concluded in offices, and mourned by nobody who matters. The conspirators fell the way old scaffolding falls: slowly, then all at once, with a great deal of dust and a few men in fine coats suddenly very interested in their boots.

I did not go to the sentencing. Mother did. She stood in the gallery of the Admiralty court in her good grey dress and gave her testimony in a voice that never rose above the level of a woman discussing the price of mutton, and by all accounts it was that very flatness that undid them. You cannot shout down a woman who is simply telling you what happened. The lead conspirator — a Rear-Admiral whose name I will not dignify here, though I confess I have written it in the margins of several practice charts and then scrubbed it out again, which is its own kind of indulgence — was taken down the court steps to a wagon, and the crowd did not cheer, exactly. They did something quieter and worse. They watched. Vessing is a port city; we know what to do with things we want sunk.

I stayed home that day. I told myself it was because someone had to mind the shop, which was true, and also because I had spent the better part of a year being looked at by men whose job was to look, and I had developed a positive allergy to it.

The shop. That deserves a word. When we came back to it — Mother, Wren, and I, climbing the stairs to the rooms above the studio that Vane's estate had finally, through the good offices of Marlow and a solicitor who billed us for every breath, returned to us — it smelled of dust and cold ash and the particular staleness of a room where a man died badly and then sat unvisited for a season. The Admiralty had seized everything twice: once during the inquiry, once by accident when a clerk with more diligence than sense confiscated our kettle. We got the kettle back. We did not get Vane's instruments back; those had gone to Hosk, and Hosk had gone to his daughter's house in Corrow Bay, and the last I heard of him he was teaching children to read sounding-lines and telling them, I have no doubt, a version of events in which he was considerably braver and I considerably more grateful.

Fair enough, on both counts.

* * *

Here is what I had, when the dust settled and the wagons rolled and the tide of the whole filthy business finally went out: a blank sheet of paper, a pot of ink going slightly thick at the edges, three pens of varying temperaments, and a memory.

That last item is the one I want to talk about, because it took me the better part of a month to understand what it was worth.

They took the atlas. I want that on the record, even if the record is only this page. On the quay, in the grey of that morning, Captain Aldery — a hard man with a ledger-keeper's eyes and, it turned out, a grievance against the Admiralty the size of a ship's hold — held out his hand and I put Vane's atlas into it. Eleven years of that man's life. Every coastline he had ever walked, every sound he had ever taken in a cold dawn with his fingers going numb, every correction he had ever made in that small furious hand of his, the hand that corrected everything, including me, including, in the end, the Admiralty itself, in ink that got him killed. I sewed a letter into its binding and I handed it over like a parcel of fish, and I stood on the quay and watched a stranger's ship carry it out past the mole, and I did not cry, because mapmakers learn early that the sea takes things and crying does not fetch them back.

Marlow signed the protection into the record before six witnesses. The family was cleared by Friday. The atlas was catalogued as contraband charts and, so far as any document will admit, ceased to exist. There are men in the Admiralty who would tell you it was destroyed, and men who would tell you it never existed, and one clerk in the customs house who memorized my face in the space of four seconds and would tell you, if you bought him a drink, that he saw it go aboard the *Meridian's Pride* in the hands of a captain who had no business wanting it.

I have decided to believe the clerk. Belief, like a chart, is only as good as your sources.

* * *

The first thing I drew, that first morning back in the studio with the shutters open and the light doing what Vane used to call "its honest work," was not a coastline. It was a scale bar. Just that: a line, divided, numbered. I drew it because my hand wanted to know if it still remembered how, and because every atlas begins with an admission — *this is how big the world is, and this is how small I am, and here is the ratio between us, and I have agreed to be honest about it.*

Then I sat for a long time looking at the rest of the blank sheet, and the blank sheet looked back, and I will confess — since we are being honest, the scale bar having committed me to it — that for a while I was afraid.

Not of the drawing. Of the remembering.

Here is the trouble with memory, from a mapmaker's standpoint: it is a liar. It rounds the capes. It straightens the rivers. It remembers the lighthouse and forgets the reef that made the lighthouse necessary. Vane used to say that a man's memory of a coast was like a man's memory of a woman — flattering, incomplete, and dangerous to navigate by. He said it to a room full of Admiralty gentlemen once, and the silence afterward had a temperature. He was not a diplomatic man. It is one of the things I loved in him and one of the things, I suspect, that got him a knife in a doorway off Chandler's Row, though the inquiry preferred the tidier fiction of a burglary.

So I sat there, afraid, and then I did what he would have done, which was to get angry and start working.

* * *

I began with the north coast, because the north coast was where he began, and because I had walked it with him the summer I was fourteen and he had made me carry the theodolite and complain the entire way, and complaint, it turns out, is an excellent mnemonic. You remember what you resented. I resented every mile of that coast: the shingle at Harrow Ness that got into my boots, the gull colony at Skern Point that declared war on my hat, the fog that came in on the third day and held us prisoner in a shepherd's bothy while Vane, instead of resting like a sensible man, drew the bothy's interior from memory and made me check his measurements by pacing it in the dark, by feel, to see if memory and floor agreed.

They agreed to within a foot. He was insufferable about it for a decade.

I drew Harrow Ness first. And here is the thing I want to set down, because it surprised me and I do not surprise easily anymore: the shingle came back. Not as a word — as a *sound*, the particular grind of it under a boy's boots, the way it slid and gave, the exact pitch of my own complaining. And under the sound, the shape. I drew the ness and my pen knew where the shoulder of it broke, where the beach steepened, where the old wreck's ribs showed at low water like the fingers of something that had given up. I had not thought of that wreck in years. It had been sitting in me the whole time, patient as a seed, waiting for someone to ask.

That is what I learned that first week, and it is the whole reason I am writing this down: memory is not a warehouse. It is a coast. You have to survey it. And the surveying hurts, and takes longer than you think, and yields more than you dreamed.

* * *

Wren came up on the fourth day, stood in the doorway with her arms crossed — she has learned to stand like Mother, which is to say like a door that has decided not to be a door — and watched me for a while.

"You're doing the north coast," she said.

"I'm doing the north coast."

"The Admiralty has copies. Partial ones. Marlow said the survey office kept working drafts."

"The Admiralty," I said, "has working drafts of a dead man's atlas, annotated by committee, corrected by men who have never been wet above the knee. What the Admiralty does not have—" and I tapped the sheet, where Harrow Ness was taking shape under my pen, the hatching going in the way Vane taught me, close and even on the seaward face, opening out on the landward, so the hill seemed to rise off the paper, "—is the wreck at the base of it. Or the gull colony. Or the fact that the light at Skern Point sits forty feet further east than the survey office believes, because the cliff fell in the winter after they measured and nobody went back to look."

Wren considered this. She is fifteen now and considers everything, the way a magistrate considers evidence. "So you're redrawing it because it's *right*."

"I'm redrawing it because it's *ours*," I said. "The rightness is a bonus."

She stayed, that day, and mixed my ink, and by the end of the week she was holding the scale ruler for me and asking questions that were better than the ones I asked at her age. I have not yet decided what to teach her first. There is time. There is, for the first time in two years, time.

* * *

I will not pretend the drawing came easily after that first coast. Memory is generous with what you loved and miserly with what you merely passed. I lost whole weeks to the middle shores — the flats, the marsh country, the long dull reaches that Vane charted with the same devotion he gave the dramatic ones, because he held the opinion, which he defended like a fortress, that a dull coast is where sailors die. I could remember the shape of the marshes but not the soundings. I could remember the soundings but not which channel silted in the year of the great storm. Twice I drew a stretch of coast, admired it, and then caught it in a lie — a river mouth too wide, a headland too proud — and had to scrape the ink back off the paper, which leaves the sheet scarred, which Vane said was proper, since all honest charts are scarred somewhere by their own corrections.

Some things I could not recover at all. The eastern islands. Vane surveyed them the year before I came to him, alone, and he never talked about them except once, drunk on survey rum, when he said they were "wronger than they look" and laughed in a way I did not like. I have his outline of them in my head and nothing under it. On the new atlas I have left them blank — actually blank, with a note in the margin: *unverified; the sea may keep these until someone goes back.* Vane would have hated it. Vane would also, I think, have approved of the note. He believed a map should confess its ignorance the way a witness confesses under oath: completely, and on the record.

That is what the letter in the atlas binding was, when you come down to it. A confession, on the record. He knew what the family had witnessed and he wrote it down and sealed it, because he believed — this quiet, stubborn, doomed belief that ran through him like a grain through timber — that the truth, properly documented, would out. It nearly didn't. It outed by the width of a tide and the grievance of one honest captain. But it outed.

I am not sure I share his faith. I am sure I share his habit. You write it down. You draw it true. You let the paper do the remembering, because paper, unlike men, can be copied, and unlike the sea, can be argued with.

* * *

Months, now. The atlas grows. It grows slowly, the way an orchard grows, which is a comparison I find myself reaching for lately and cannot fully account for. The north coast is done. The flats are done, or done enough — Vane would have found forty faults and I have resolved to let him, wherever he is, keep them. The harbor approaches are done, and I did those from a memory so sharp it frightened me; I can still close my eyes and see the quay gate, the grey morning, the captain's hand coming out for the atlas, and my own hand letting go.

I drew that morning once. Just once, a small chart in the corner of a practice sheet: the quay, the gate, the *Meridian's Pride* at her moorings, the tide turning. I labeled it, in the old survey style, *position of the atlas, last known.* Then I burned the sheet in the stove and watched the little chart curl and go, and I felt — I want to be accurate about this, because accuracy is the whole trade — I felt *lighter*. Not healed. Lighter. The way you feel after setting down a load you have carried so long your shoulder has grown to fit it.

The sea kept the atlas. Let it. Charts are not the truth; they are the truth's address. I have the truth still, all eleven years of it, folded up in the strangest archive ever kept: a shop-boy's head, scarred by two years of watchmen and checkpoints and one very long winter, and open for survey.

* * *

Yesterday Wren found me at the window at dusk, not drawing, just looking down into the yard behind the shop — the yard with the old orchard, or what the Admiralty's men left of it. They drained the well in the autumn of the inquiry, hunting the map case. They found the case, empty, and they left a hole, and the hole filled with rain, and around it the old trees stand leafless and half-dead, three of them past saving, the rest merely neglected, the way everything of ours was neglected while we were busy not being hanged.

"What are you looking at?" Wren asked.

"The well," I said.

"It's a hole."

"It's a hole with excellent drainage and a shameful history. Most institutions are."

She laughed — my sister, who learned to lie to the watch at fourteen and to tell the truth at fifteen, and who is, of the two skills, still deciding which is harder — and then she looked at the yard a while herself, and her face went thoughtful in a way I have learned to watch for, because it usually precedes either a very good idea or a small fire.

"The trees are dead," she said. "Most of them."

"Most of them."

"You could clear them. Plant new ones." She shrugged, elaborately casual, which in Wren is a shout. "An orchard's no good empty."

I did not answer her right away. I was looking at the well, and at the ground around it, and thinking about what a mapmaker knows that other people forget: that a hole is only a hole until you decide what it's for. Vane's atlas began with a scale bar — an admission of smallness — and it will end, when I redraw the last sheet of it, with a coast I have not walked yet. And between the beginning and the end there is a yard, and a hole, and a sister standing next to me in the dusk with dirt, I would wager, already on her mind.

"Spring's coming," I said.

Wren grinned. "I'll get the axe."

And that is the trouble with sisters, and with plans, and with peace: it never waits for you to finish the sentence. She was down the stairs before I could tell her that the old trees would need burning, not just felling, and that the well would need filling properly, stone by stone, and that I had already — God help me, I had already, weeks ago, in a fit of sentiment I told no one about — written away to a nursery in Corrow Bay for stock. Apple stock. The kind Vane's mother grew, if his rambling stories were true, on a hillside he never drew because, he said, some things are not for charts.

The nursery's reply is due any day now. And when it comes, this atlas will have to share the studio — and the mapmaker — with a hole in the ground that intends to become an orchard.
