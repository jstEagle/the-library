## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker came on a Thursday, in the gray hour after supper when the boarding house smelled of boiled cabbage and lamp oil and the boards of the stairway had learned everyone's weight by heart. Silas heard him from the kitchen, where he was polishing the brass fittings of the Marsh motorcar by lamplight — a task Mrs. Pyle allowed him to do indoors because the cold had cracked his knuckles open like dry kindling.

"United States Census!" the man announced to the parlor at large, and Silas heard Mrs. Pyle's welcome unfold in her best company voice, the one she saved for the rent collector and the minister.

He should have kept polishing. Instead he set down the rag and stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the lamplight behind him, and the census taker looked up from his ledger with the mild, incurious expression of a man doing arithmetic.

The man was perhaps fifty, with a mustache the color of wet ash and a coat too heavy for the season, and he sat at Mrs. Pyle's good table as though tables were made for sitting at. His name, he said, was Mr. Dorsey. He said it the way a man says a thing he expects to be remembered.

"Ladies first," Dorsey said, and took down Mrs. Pyle's particulars — her widowhood, her years in the house, her boarders — and then worked through the others: the two railroad men who were out, the widow Kessler on the second floor, the schoolteacher who took her meals elsewhere. Each answer went into the ledger in a hand so small and regular it might have been printed. Silas watched the pen move and thought, without knowing why he was thinking it, that a page could hold a whole street and still have room left over for judgment.

"And you, sir." Dorsey looked up. "Name?"

"Nakamura. Silas Nakamura."

The pen stopped. It was a small stoppage, less than a breath, but Silas had spent eleven years learning to read pauses the way other men read weather, and this one had a temperature to it.

"Spell that."

Silas spelled it. The letters went down one at a time, each given its own small cell in the ledger, and when Dorsey finished he read the name back aloud, carefully, the way a man handles something he suspects may be loaded.

"Occupation?"

"I drive for Miss Adeline Marsh. Of Elm Street."

"The suffrage woman." Dorsey's eyebrows performed a small neutral maneuver. "That's employment, I suppose. Chauffeur."

"Yes."

"Born where?"

Here it was. Silas had answered this question before — at the immigration office in San Francisco, on the papers folded in oilcloth inside his trunk, on the form for his driver's license, which the clerk had filled out while chewing tobacco and looking mostly at the ceiling. *Japan*, he would say, and the word would land on the desk between them like a coin nobody wanted to pick up.

"Japan," he said. "Kagoshima prefecture. A village you will not find on your map."

"Try me."

"It is called Minamikata. There are persimmon trees."

Dorsey wrote it. All of it, it seemed — the prefecture, the village, the trees — though surely no census required persimmons. Something about the man's thoroughness made Silas's neck prickle.

"How long in this country?"

"Eleven years."

"Citizenship?"

"I am not eligible."

"Not eligible or not applied?" Dorsey looked up, and there was nothing hostile in the look, which was somehow worse. "It's a simple question, Mr. Nakamura. Some men don't apply because they mean to go home. Some because they're lazy. Some because the law won't have them." He turned back to the ledger. "Which are you?"

"The law," Silas said quietly.

"Mm." The pen scratched. Then, without looking up: "Race."

Silas did not answer at once. In eleven years, no paper had ever asked him to say it aloud in his own voice. On forms it was a box, a dash, a clerk's abbreviation — *Jap* in the margin of his license, written in pencil and later gone over in ink. Saying it himself was different. Saying it himself made it his.

"Asian," he said finally. "Japanese."

"Right." Dorsey wrote a long time for such a short word.

Then he laid down the pen and folded his hands, and Silas understood that the census proper was over, because what came next arrived in a different voice — lighter, more companionable, a man leaning back from his work to chat.

"You know," Dorsey said, "I've been across half this county this week. Interesting work. You learn things." He nodded toward the window, toward the dark beyond it, where the river ran somewhere under its lid of ice. "You know the German family over on the far bank? Brandts. Orchard people."

Silas kept his face still. Eleven years of driving a woman whose enemies wrote her letters in anonymous hands had taught him stillness the way the river taught ice — patiently, all winter, from the edges in.

"Heard of them," he said.

"Heard of them how? You deliver over there?"

"I drive for Miss Marsh. She has business on both sides of the river sometimes."

"Sure." Dorsey smiled. He had very even teeth, Silas noticed, for a man whose profession was writing down strangers. "Only, I ask because old Brandt came into town Tuesday. Bought lamp oil and sugar and paid in coin, counted it out twice, like we'd short him. And I got to thinking — man his age, buying sugar like that, he's canning something. Putting stores by. You ever notice how they do that, these Germans? Put stores by. Like they're waiting for something."

"Perhaps he likes sugar," Silas said.

Dorsey laughed — a genuine laugh, warm even, and he slapped the ledger lightly as if the joke had been between them. "Perhaps he does." Then the warmth shut off like a damper. "There's a son, I hear. Grown. Moved off to Cleveland some years back and doesn't write."

"That," Silas said, "is not my affair."

"No. No, I suppose it isn't." Dorsey gathered his ledger, squared it against the table edge twice, three times, a small compulsive percussion. He stood, buttoned his coat, and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering something trivial. "War's coming, you know. Everybody says so. Probably won't come to anything here — Ohio's not Europe. But when it does come, there'll be questions asked that make mine tonight look friendly." He tipped an imaginary hat. "You seem a sensible fellow, Mr. Nakamura. Sensible fellows do fine in wartime. It's the ones with ties that have trouble. Ties across water."

The door closed behind him. The parlor clock ticked. From upstairs came the muffled thump of the widow Kessler moving furniture, or praying, it was the same sound.

Mrs. Pyle appeared in the hall, wiping her hands on her apron. "Well," she said. "He was a friendly sort."

"Yes," Silas said.

"Gave us a good accounting. Whole house, down to the cats." She smiled, satisfied, and began trimming the lamp wicks for the night, and Silas climbed the stairs to his room under the eaves where the roof slope pressed down like a patient hand.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat on the edge of his narrow bed with the oilcloth packet from his trunk spread across his knees — license, alien registration card from 1913, the steamship ticket stub he had never been able to throw away — and he read them by candlelight as if seeing them for the first time. They had always seemed to him like armor. Little rectangles of government approval that said *this man is accounted for; pass on*. He had built his whole philosophy of survival on them. Be accurate. Be quiet. Be useful. Let the papers speak for you, and let them say only what they need to say.

But Dorsey's pen had not wanted accuracy. Dorsey's pen had wanted *ties*. Ties across water. A son in Cleveland who didn't write. Sugar counted out twice in coin.

Silas thought of Heinrich Brandt standing among his bare apple trees in October, breath steaming, pressing a sealed letter into Silas's gloved hand. *When the time comes,* the old man had said. Not *if*. When. He had known, Silas realized now — the way orchard men know frost before the almanac does. And Silas had taken the letter and said yes easily, the way you agree to carry a parcel, and slid it into the false bottom of his tool chest behind the spare tire, and had not thought of it again except at night, sometimes, the way you don't think of a debt until the creditor's face appears in a crowd.

The letter lay six feet away from him now, in the carriage house, wrapped in oilcloth beside his jack and tire irons. It had lain there since October, patient as a seed.

He had told himself it was harmless. Paper was harmless. Questions were harmless — America was full of questions, asked cheerfully, forgotten instantly, filed away in ledgers that no one ever opened again. That was the whole genius of the country: it forgot you unless you gave it a reason not to.

Except that Dorsey hadn't forgotten anything. Dorsey had walked through the boarding house collecting lives the way a magpie collects buttons, and every button went into the ledger, and the ledger went somewhere, and someone, someday, would read it not as a record of a Thursday evening but as a map of who could be trusted when the war came. *Sensible fellows do fine.* The sentence had sounded like kindness. It had been a warning wearing kindness's coat.

And the questions about the Brandts — those had been worse than the ones about himself, and Silas sat turning that fact over like a stone found in a furrow. Why? Because his own answers he had rehearsed all his life; he knew their weight and balance. But Brandt's name had come out of Dorsey's mouth already bent, already shaped into a story — the hoarding, the silent son, the *waiting for something* — and Silas had felt, watching the pen, that his own silence was being written down as part of it. *Nakamura, Silas. Chauffeur. Knows the German family.*

He blew out the candle and lay down and listened to the river. In deep winter you couldn't hear it at all; it moved under the ice in silence, carrying whatever the thaw would eventually deliver downstream. But lately, on nights like this, there had been a sound beneath silence — a groan, a tick, the ice flexing and remembering it used to be water.

Tomorrow he drove Miss Marsh to Columbus for her convention. Tomorrow the letter would ride in the tool chest six feet behind his shoulder blades, across a county full of Dorseys with their little pens and their even teeth.

He slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of persimmon trees, and woke to find frost ferns on the window glass arranged, as far as he could tell in the half-light, exactly like fingerprints.
