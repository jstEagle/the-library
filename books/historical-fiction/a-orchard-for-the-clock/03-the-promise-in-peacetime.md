## 3. The Promise in Peacetime

The letter had been given to him in September, when the apples were still hanging heavy and the word *war* was something that happened across an ocean, to other people, in other people's languages.

Silas remembered it now the way a man remembers a debt only after the collector has knocked — with a lurch of the stomach and a sudden, useless clarity about everything he should have done differently. He stood in his narrow room at Mrs. Ellery's boarding house, the sealed envelope resting on his knee where it had rested for five months, and listened to the wireless downstairs crackle with the news that had already traveled through the town faster than any wire could carry it: war declared. Germany. Us.

The paper in his hands weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

He had been driving for Adeline Marsh since the spring, and it was she who first sent him out to the Brandt orchard — not for apples, though he came back with a basket of them every time, but because Mrs. Marsh believed a woman running for nothing less than the vote ought to buy her fruit from people who needed the money more than the grocery chain did. "The Brandts are good Germans," she'd said, in the offhand way she said most things, as if goodness were a nationality one simply possessed. "Old Heinrich fought for Ohio soil longer than most men born here have been alive."

Silas drove the river road on Thursdays. The orchard sat on the far bank, forty acres of trees terraced up a gentle slope, with a greenhouse at the bottom whose glass panes caught the afternoon light and threw it back in pieces. Heinrich Brandt met him at the gate every week — a broad man going narrow now, the flesh falling away from his frame like snow melting off a roof, though his handshake remained what it had always been, a thing you felt in your elbow.

They spoke little, at first. Silas was not a man who filled silences; Heinrich Brandt turned out not to be either. But two men who do not talk can still say a great deal, and by midsummer they had established between themselves a commerce of small kindnesses: Silas carried out newspapers from town, and Brandt sent back cider pressed from the bruised apples nobody would sell, and once, when Silas's motorcar coughed and died at the gate, the old man crawled under it with him and they fixed the fuel line together without exchanging more than twenty words.

It was Greta who talked enough for all three of them, then — laughing, quick, her English accented but fearless, telling stories about her father's stubbornness while Heinrich pretended not to listen and failed entirely to hide his pleasure. She had been seventeen when they crossed, she said. Her brother Emil had been eleven, and seasick the whole way over, and had sworn on the deck of the ship never to eat herring again, and had broken the oath within a year.

"Emil is in Cleveland," Heinrich said one evening in late August, and it was the first time Silas heard the son's name come from the father's mouth like that — flat, closed, a door shut on a room. "Three years now. He does not write."

Greta looked away. The subject, Silas understood, was a wound the family dressed daily and could not close.

Then came the last Thursday of September, and the smell of the orchard had changed — ripe fruit going soft on the branch, sweetness curdling into rot — and Heinrich Brandt did not meet him at the gate. He was sitting on the porch of the farmhouse, wrapped in a blanket against a chill that was more than autumn, and when Silas climbed the steps the old man waved off the offer of help standing up.

"I have a thing to ask you," Brandt said. "Sit. Sit down. You Japanese sit however you please, I know, but my chairs are here."

Silas sat. Greta appeared in the doorway with coffee and vanished again, and Silas understood she had been told to disappear, and understood also that she was listening from the kitchen with her whole body.

Brandt took an envelope from inside his shirt. It was sealed with wax, which struck Silas as theatrical even then — wax, in 1917, like something out of a novel — and the old man held it in both hands the way a man holds water he's afraid to spill.

"The doctor gives me until the frost," he said. "Maybe Christmas if I am stubborn, and I am stubborn." He said it without self-pity, the way he said everything, a fact stated plainly to another man who could bear hearing it. "When I am gone — when *the time comes*, whatever that time looks like — I want this letter to go to Emil. To Cleveland. Not by post." His thumb moved over the seal. "By hand. By someone I know."

"Why not the post?" Silas asked.

"Because a letter by post is a letter anyone can open." Brandt's eyes were pale blue and very direct. "And because a letter by hand says: my father thought of you at the end. A stamp does not say that."

Silas looked at the envelope. He looked at the orchard below the porch, the trees going gold at their edges, the greenhouse glinting. He was twenty-six years old. He had been in this country four years, and in that time he had learned precisely one doctrine, and he practiced it faithfully: be unremarkable. Be the driver, not the passenger. Be the man history steps around. Carrying letters, delivering messages, holding other people's words in trust — this was the whole architecture of his life, and so the request asked nothing of him he hadn't already given a hundred times to Adeline Marsh.

"Of course," he said. "I'll take it. Whenever you need."

Brandt nodded once, satisfied, and pressed the envelope into his hands, and that was nearly all. Almost an afterthought, the old man added: "There will be a day it must go. You will know the day. And Silas—" he used the name carefully, tasting it, "—when that day comes, it may not be a good day for such errands. Remember you promised anyway."

"I remember," Silas said lightly.

That was the phrase that came back to him now, five months later, sitting on the edge of his bed with the wireless muttering catastrophe downstairs: *he agreed lightly.* He could hear himself saying it — **of course, whenever you need** — the tone easy, unburdened, the promise of a man buying apples. He had taken the envelope the way he took tips from Mrs. Marsh's friends: graciously, and without understanding that some things, once accepted, cannot be set back down.

* * *

Now it was April, and the frost Heinrich had predicted had long since come and gone, and the old man had proved as stubborn as advertised — alive still, the last Silas knew, though the boarding house gossip said the orchard had gone quiet, no more trips to town, the greenhouse dark. And the country had done something no one had quite believed it would do, something that had rearranged the world overnight the way ice rearranges a river: it had chosen sides, and the Brandts were on the wrong side of the line drawn through their own lives.

Enemy aliens. The words were everywhere now — in the papers Adeline read aloud in a voice clipped tight with anger, in the talk at the garage where Silas bought gasoline, in the way the grocer's boy watched the Kessler family leave church on Sundays. Enemy aliens, as though the men who had planted these orchards and built these barns and buried their dead in Ohio ground had been enemies all along, waiting patiently for decades for the chance to prove it.

And Silas Nakamura sat in his room holding a sealed letter addressed to a German name, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding its level, that the promise he had made in peacetime was now worth exactly what it had cost him: nothing. Less than nothing. A promise made cheaply could be abandoned cheaply. That was the arithmetic of the world, wasn't it? That was what the census taker's pencil had already written about him — a foreigner, an alien, a man whose allegiance was a question mark on a government form. No one would blame him for burning the letter. No one would ever know it existed.

Except Heinrich Brandt would know. And Greta. And — the thought arrived unwelcome, unwilled — except Silas himself, who had spent four years perfecting the art of being invisible and had discovered, in the space of one April evening, that invisibility had a price, and the price was this: a man who belongs nowhere can keep no promises anywhere, because promises require a somewhere to stand in.

He turned the envelope over. In the corner, in Heinrich's careful hand, faded now from five months against Silas's skin: *Emil Brandt, Cleveland.*

Downstairs, the wireless announced new measures. Registration of enemy aliens. Fingerprints. Photographs. Papers carried always, produced on demand. Silas listened with the particular attention of a man taking inventory of his own shrinking territory, and his hand closed around the letter.

The frost was coming, Heinrich had said. Maybe Christmas if I am stubborn.

The old man was still stubborn. And Silas, who had agreed lightly, was beginning to understand that the letter in his hands was heavier than paper — that it had been growing weight all winter, ounce by ounce, with every headline, every whispered *foreigner*, every name added to the government's lists — and that soon it would weigh more than a man could carry alone, or perhaps more than a man could refuse to carry.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would drive the river road and see what the war had made of the orchard.

He did not yet know that tomorrow, the marshal's office would make its own plans for him first.
