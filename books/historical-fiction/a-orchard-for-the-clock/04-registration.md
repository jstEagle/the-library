## 4. Registration

The marshal's office smelled of coal smoke and lamp oil and, beneath it, the sweetish rot of old tobacco worked into the floorboards. Silas stood in the doorway a moment longer than he needed to, letting his eyes adjust, letting himself be seen standing there — a man arriving of his own accord, with his hat in his hands and his papers folded square in his breast pocket. He had rehearsed this on the walk over: that he was not being dragged anywhere. That a man who walked in on his own feet kept something the summons could not take.

"Name?"

"Nakamura. Silas Nakamura."

The clerk wrote it out slowly, each letter its own small ceremony. Behind him, two deputies played cards at a scarred table and did not look up, which was worse than if they had. Being watched would have meant he mattered. This was paperwork. He was weather to them.

"Occupation."

"Driver." He hesitated. "I drive for Mrs. Adeline Marsh."

That earned him a glance from one of the deputies — quick, appraising, gone again. Everyone knew Adeline Marsh. Her name had been in the *Gazette* three times that winter, twice for the suffrage meetings at the Methodist hall and once for something a letter-writer had called "unwomanly agitation." The deputy's eyes said he had filed her under something. Silas knew the filing systems of small towns the way he knew the backs of envelopes: by their creases.

"Birthplace."

"Japan. Kagoshima Prefecture."

The pen stopped. The clerk looked up for the first time, and Silas watched the arithmetic move behind his face — the slow sorting of a man into a column he hadn't expected to fill. In the boarding house ledger he had been *Silas, driver*. On the census roll last month, he had become a number beside the word *alien*. Now he was becoming something else again, and the clerk's face told him it was not an improvement.

"You'll need to put both hands flat here."

The fingerprint ink was cold and greasy. The clerk took each finger as though handling a mannequin's, rolling the pads across the card with a pressure that was neither gentle nor rough, merely thorough, and there was a horror in the thoroughness itself — the idea that somewhere in Washington these whorls would live on after him, that his hands had left evidence in a place his hands had never been. When they photographed him, they turned his head left, then right, then full front, and held up a slate with a number on it. He thought: *a number.* He thought: *Adeline says a woman is a person whether or not the law writes her down as one.* He thought: perhaps the law could learn to write things down wrong about anyone.

The marshal himself appeared only at the end — a heavy man with a mustache like a shoe brush, who slid a folded card across the counter along with a printed sheet.

"You carry that on you at all times. All times, you understand. If a deputy asks to see it, you show it. You don't travel more than—" he consulted nothing, recited from memory— "you notify this office before changing county. You understand English good enough?"

"Well enough to understand you."

Something flickered across the marshal's face — annoyance, or maybe the ghost of respect, hard to say in a town where men kept both behind their teeth. "Sign here."

Silas signed. His name looked strange on the line beneath the word ALIEN, the letters small and upright, refusing to slant. He picked up the card. It was heavier than paper should have been, or perhaps he only held it differently now.

Outside, the cold hit him like a hand laid flat against the chest. The street ran white and gray toward the river, and beyond the river, invisible from here but present in him all the same, lay the Brandt orchard, asleep under snow, its rows crossing the hillside like lines on a page someone had stopped reading.

He had promised Heinrich Brandt a letter delivered when the time came. The time had come wearing a uniform and carrying a card.

* * *

Adeline Marsh received him in the parlor with the curtains half drawn, because the light was better for reading petitions that way, she claimed, though Silas suspected she simply liked a room where people had to come close to see her face. She was fifty-one years old and built like the arguments she made — narrow, load-bearing, impossible to dislodge once set.

"You came back," she said. Not gratitude. Observation. She had hired him in November and dismissed him in April with a note that read, in her own hand, *owing to circumstances beyond either of our control* — and they had both known whose control the circumstances were beyond, and it was not hers.

"You said the position was open."

"The position is open because I reopened it." She sat, lacing her fingers. "I want to be plain with you, Silas, because plainness is the only courtesy I can afford either of us this year. My allies are frightened. The National Association has enough trouble without a report going round Columbus that Adeline Marsh employs — " she paused, chose — "*non-naturalized foreign labor* while the country is at war. There are women who have given ten years to this cause and would see it undone by a single newspaper paragraph."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "I don't think you do, and I don't blame you. I think you believe I am ashamed of you. I am not. I am afraid *for* you, which is a different animal, and I am afraid *for* the vote, which is a different animal still, and right now all three of us are in the same field with the same dogs running." She stood, went to the window, stood with her back to him as she sometimes did when the sentence ahead was one she didn't want witnessed. "You will drive me as before. You will say nothing to anyone about my correspondence. And you will be careful, Silas. Careful the way a man is careful walking on river ice — not timid. Careful."

"And if careful isn't enough?"

She turned. For a moment the lamplight caught her eyes and he saw in them something he had never let himself look for in a white woman's face: recognition. The look of a person who had also been told, politely, repeatedly, by reasonable voices, where she might not go.

"Then careful wasn't the point," she said. "But make it cost them something to catch you not being careful. Do you hear me? Never give them anything for free."

He heard her. He carried it out of the house like a coal carried in a bare palm.

* * *

The trouble came, as trouble did, secondhand.

Mrs. Hollenbeck from the feed store told the barber, who told the deputy who bought his tobacco from him, that she'd seen lights at the Brandt orchard after dark. Foreigners, she said. Meeting. Two automobiles' worth of lantern-glow through the trees, voices in German, and Greta Brandt coming out to the road to watch a car pass with a look on her face that Mrs. Hollenbeck did not like and could not describe except that it was *not sorry*.

Silas heard all of this from the barber's chair next over, where he sat getting a trim he did not need, because a man with new registration papers learned quickly to sit in rooms where people talked.

Two automobiles. He almost smiled. The Brandts owned one truck, older than the war, older than the peace before it. But the story did not need to be true. It needed only to be repeated, and repetition, he had learned from reading other people's mail for three years, was how rumors became facts became files.

He drove Adeline home that evening in silence, and after she went in, he sat in the motorcar at the curb with the engine ticking itself cool, and he looked down the dark street toward the river he could not see and felt something settle in him that had been loose since the marshal's office. Humiliation, he understood now, was fuel. It burned dirty and it burned hot, and if a man did not spend it, it spent him.

Tomorrow night he would go to the orchard. He would go the back way, past the millpond, and he would tell Greta Brandt what the town was saying, because she deserved to hear her own danger from a friend rather than a deputy.

And the letter — Heinrich's letter, sealed in brown paper, sleeping in the false bottom of his trunk these six weeks — the letter would need to move soon. Cleveland was a day's drive if the roads held.

He turned the key. The engine coughed, caught, held.

In the dark behind him, unseen, a figure stepped out from the shadow of the Hollenbeck porch and stood watching the taillights diminish down the street, noting the direction, noting the hour, noting everything — for in Ohio in the spring of 1917, everyone had begun keeping notes.
