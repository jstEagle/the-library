## 5. What the Letters Say

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope the color of old bone, with a return address Silas read twice before his hands understood what his eyes were telling him: *Department of Justice, Bureau of Investigation, Columbus, Ohio.*

He had been sorting Adeline's mail at the kitchen table of the boarding house, the way he did every evening after the motorcar was garaged and wiped down and the day's mud scraped from the running boards. It was a ritual he permitted himself to call work. The letters lay in three piles — petitions to sign, invitations to decline, threats to burn — and he moved among them with the ease of a bank teller counting other people's money. He knew Mrs. Marsh's correspondents better than she did. He knew that Miss Ellery in Dayton wrote on Tuesdays and always enclosed a pressed violet, that the man from the *Cleveland Plain Dealer* flattered her in prose he clearly practiced in front of a mirror, that the anonymous writers who called her a harlot and a traitor had lately begun signing themselves *A True American*, as though patriotism were a costume one could rent.

He held the government envelope under the lamp for a long moment. Steam from the kettle ticked against its lid. Downstairs, Mrs. Hobbes was arguing with her cat about a saucer of milk.

Silas opened it the way he opened all of Adeline's mail — cleanly, with a knife, along the top fold so it could be resealed without evidence. He was careful about this. He had never once been caught, not because he was clever but because no one imagined a chauffeur would bother. That was the whole architecture of his life: no one imagining him. A man history passed over could stand inside any room he liked, provided the room believed itself empty.

The letter was typewritten. He read it standing up, because sitting suddenly felt like surrender.

*Dear Madam Marsh,* it began, in the flat courtesy of officialdom, and then: *This Bureau has received information concerning disloyal elements among persons employed by you or residing at your establishment. In the present national emergency, we request your cooperation in furnishing the following—*

There was a list. Names, if known; nationalities; places of birth; dates of hire. A question about whether any employee had expressed opinions hostile to the United States or sympathetic to the Imperial German Government. A question, circled twice in someone else's pencil, about *persons of Japanese birth or descent currently resident within your household or employ.*

Circled twice. Someone had read this before him. Someone had wanted that line found.

Silas set the letter down on the table, and his hands were steady, which surprised him, and then they were not, and he pressed them flat against the wood until the trembling went into the table instead, into the grain, down through the legs, into the floorboards of a house where he had slept three years without anyone learning how he took his tea.

Someone had reported him. Not the census taker — the census taker's questions had been the weather, indiscriminate, falling on everyone. This was different. This was a hand. A hand had written to Columbus. A hand had circled *Japanese* twice and mailed it south like a specimen pinned in a box.

He thought, absurdly, of Mr. Pell, who delivered the morning post and always said *Fine cold one* regardless of season. He thought of the boarders. He thought of the men at the feed store who fell quiet when he walked past with Adeline's parcels, quiet in the new way, the way that was worse than talking. Any of them. All of them. Paranoia is a room with too many doors, and every door opens onto the same face.

* * *

The next morning he lied to Mr. Pell, and it frightened him how little practice it required.

"Anything for me?" Pell asked, halfway up the boarding house steps, mail satchel swinging. He'd never asked Silas that before. Three years of silence between them, broken now by a question shaped exactly like a hook.

"Not today," Silas said, and smiled, and watched Pell watch him. Pell's eyes went to the envelope in Silas's coat pocket — Adeline's ordinary correspondence, nothing in it but a suffrage circular — and lingered there a half-second longer than curiosity requires. Then Pell touched his cap and went on, whistling something martial that Silas didn't recognize, a new song. There were so many new songs now.

He stood on the step until Pell turned the corner. His heart was going like a sewing machine. He catalogued what he'd done wrong: he had answered too fast, smiled too evenly, stood too still. He had spent thirty years being unremarkable, and it turned out that unremarkable was a skill that decayed the moment you needed it.

That afternoon, driving Adeline to a committee meeting on Elm Street, he lied to her too, though she gave him no reason to. She sat beside him wrapped in wool, reading aloud from a newspaper item about the war bonds drive, and asked, "Did anything come from Columbus?"

His hands stayed level on the wheel. "Nothing but the circular about registration deadlines," he said. "For the women's petition."

"Mm." She looked out at the street, where a poster showed Uncle Sam pointing his finger at every passerby. "They've grown fond of deadlines."

He drove. He did not look at her. He was aware, distantly, of the shame settling over him like snowfall — soft, accumulating, covering everything equally — and beneath the shame something colder and more useful: calculation. If the Bureau wrote to her, they might write again. They might visit. And when they visited, they would find a Japanese chauffeur whose papers marked him enemy alien, employed by the most conspicuous dissident in three counties, driving her to meetings where women demanded the vote while their sons died in France. He was not merely a risk to himself anymore. He was a crack in her foundation, and cracks widen.

And yet — and here was the thing he could not say aloud, could barely admit into his own skull — the letter to Cleveland had to go. Heinrich Brandt's seal, Greta's folded hands, the promise made in peacetime when promises were cheap: none of it had dissolved when the declaration came. He caught himself doing it at red lights, stopped at intersections, mapping in his head. South on the river road to Zanesville, then east. Or north through Newark, avoiding the bridge at Dresden where the deputies had taken to stopping cars. Two hundred and seventeen miles to Cleveland. Eleven hours if the roads held. Sleet season. The spare tire well was deep enough for a sealed envelope if you wrapped it in oilcloth.

He was planning a crime, he realized, the way other men planned crops. Calmly. Seasonally. With attention to drainage.

* * *

Thursday night the boarding house smelled of boiled cabbage and kerosene, and Silas sat at the kitchen table with the government letter open before him, reading it a fourth time as though repetition might change the ink. It did not. *Persons of Japanese birth or descent.* Twice-circled. He had copied the Bureau's address onto a scrap of paper and burned the scrap, then felt foolish, then burned the feeling too, or tried to.

Footsteps on the stairs. He slid the letter beneath the newspaper — smooth, practiced — and Mrs. Hobbes appeared in the doorway with her shawl and her suspicion, holding a candle that made her face flicker like a bad photograph.

"There was a man asking after you," she said. "At the door. While you were out with Mrs. Marsh."

Silas kept his voice level. "What sort of man?"

"That's what I asked him." She set the candle down, enjoying her own drama. "Wouldn't give a name. Said he was from the county office. I told him you was employed regular and no trouble to anybody, and he wrote something in a little book." Her eyes narrowed, and for one strange moment Silas thought she was afraid — for herself, for the house, for the cabbage cooling on the stove. "You in some kind of difficulty, Nakamura? Because I'll not have the government tramping through my kitchen. I pay my taxes same as anybody."

"No difficulty," Silas said. "Paperwork. For the alien registration."

"Hmph." She collected her candle. At the door she paused, and without turning around said, "My brother fought at Antietam," which had nothing to do with anything, and left him alone with the letter and the dark coming down outside the windows like a lid closing.

He worked until midnight. Not on the Bureau's questions — those he could answer only with ruin — but on the map he was building behind his eyes. He had driven Adeline to Columbus twice and knew the National Road as far as Springfield. North of that, guesswork. So he reconstructed it from other people's words, the way he reconstructed everything: the traveling salesman who'd complained in the barber's chair about mud on the Newark pike; the deputy who'd told Adeline the Dresden bridge was closed to heavy traffic; a girl at the library who mentioned the Cleveland road ran high above the river through Coshocton and was passable in all but flood. He assembled Ohio the way a blind man assembles a face, from fragments volunteered by people who never suspected they were speaking to him.

That was the gift, he supposed — the thing the census taker's forms had no box for. People talked in front of him. People had always talked in front of him. For thirty years he had used it to know the shape of other lives, a thief of intimacy who stole nothing anyone missed. Now the same faculty turned in his hands and became a weapon aimed at himself: every casual sentence was intelligence, every pause was a warning, every friendly face a possible signature at the bottom of an accusation.

He began keeping watch. He learned that Mr. Pell drank at the Eagle Hotel on Fridays and that the county clerk's son had enlisted. He noticed that the butcher now weighed his order looking past him rather than at him. He noticed that when he garaged the motorcar, the liveryman found reasons to be outdoors, smoking, watching. He noticed everything, and everything meant something, and meaning everything was its own kind of madness, and he embraced it gladly, because madness with a method was the closest thing to safety the winter offered.

On Saturday, Adeline came down early, dressed for the train to Cincinnati, and paused in the hall with her gloves half on. "Silas. You've been strange this week."

"The roads are strange," he said. "Ice."

She studied him with those gray eyes that had stared down judges and editors and husbands, eyes that missed very little and forgave less than they missed. For a moment he thought she saw straight through him to the government letter hidden under the floorboard beneath his trunk, to the oilcloth packet already addressed, to the route drawn in his head from river to lake like a vein of ore.

"Be careful," she said finally, which was what she always said, and left for the station.

He stood in the hall until her cab rattled away. Then he went upstairs and took the letter to Cleveland from its hiding place and held it a long time, feeling through the envelope the ghost of Heinrich Brandt's handwriting, the orchard, the peacetime light, the promise he had made lightly and could no longer carry lightly if he carried it at all.

Two hundred and seventeen miles. Eleven hours. One day off, perhaps, if he invented a funeral, a cousin, a sick aunt.

Downstairs, the front bell rang. Then rang again — impatient, official, twice more in quick succession, the knock of a man accustomed to doors opening for him.

Silas put the letter back beneath the floorboard, smoothed the rug flat, and went down to answer it, arranging his face into the mild, forgettable expression of a man no one need imagine.
