## 6. The Orchard Under Watch

The river kept its own hours. By day it was a gray seam between two counties, crossed by a bridge where men in overalls spat tobacco and talked about the war as if it were weather coming from the east. But at night the river was a country of its own, black and moving under its skin of ice, and Silas had learned that a man could be two things on either bank of it — one thing on the side with the marshal's office and the census rolls, another thing entirely on the side where nobody wrote anything down.

He parked Adeline's motorcar behind the grain elevator on the near shore, out of sight of the road, and walked the last mile along the towpath. The cold came up through his boot soles like water rising in a well. In his coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay Heinrich Brandt's letter — sealed, addressed in a German hand gone shaky at the downstrokes: *Emil Brandt, Cleveland.* He had carried it for three weeks now, telling himself each night that tomorrow he would decide what to do with it, and each morning finding that deciding was a thing other men did.

The orchard announced itself before he saw it. Even in winter, even pruned back to bone, apple trees have a posture — rows standing at attention across the hillside like soldiers waiting out a bad season. The moon was three days off full and gave him enough light to see that half the rows stood unpruned, canes and suckers growing wild at the joints, and that the greenhouse at the foot of the hill had been broken open like a struck clock.

Glass everywhere. He heard it first, actually — his boots grinding through it along the path — before he understood what the glitter was. Someone had put stones through the roof, through the panes on the south wall, methodically, the way boys break windows they know no one will make them pay for. A seedling bench lay overturned, its soil frozen into a dark tongue on the floor. On the whitewashed end wall someone had painted something in tar. Silas did not go closer to read it. He knew the shape of words meant to be read by lamplight by people who wanted you to know they'd come.

A light burned in the farmhouse above the orchard. One window, low and yellow.

He stood in the ruined greenhouse a long time, listening to the river work at the ice below the bluff, and understood that he had not come to deliver anything tonight. He had come because the letter had begun to feel less like paper than like a stone of his own, carried in the pocket, growing heavier by the day. Because a man who reads other people's mail for a living ought, once in his life, to answer some of it.

The door of the farmhouse opened while he was still deciding whether to knock. Not all the way. A crack, a lamp behind it, and a woman's voice pitched flat and unafraid:

"If you've come to finish the greenhouse, the glass is already paid for. If you've come for the deed, there is no deed here worth your trouble. And if you're the deputy again, you'll want to wipe your feet. I just swept."

"I am not the deputy," Silas said.

* * *

Greta Brandt had learned to sort men by their silences. Her father's silence was the silence of a man thinking in two languages. The deputies' silence was the silence of men rehearsing what they would say to her and finding nothing worth the rehearsal. The stone-throwers didn't bother with silence at all; they arrived with noise and left with noise and she swept up between the two noises like a woman sweeping up after a storm that intended to come back.

This one's silence was none of these. He stood at the edge of her lamplight with his hat in his hands — a Japanese man, young, thin as a fence rail, dressed like a chauffeur because he probably was one, everyone in town knew the suffragist's car and the quiet foreigner who drove it — and he looked past her shoulder into the kitchen as though cataloguing it. She saw his eyes find the pail of pruned scions soaking by the stove, the grafting knife open on the table, the ledger lying face-down. She saw him understand, without being told, exactly how alone she was.

"You're the driver," she said. "Marsh's driver."

"Yes."

"Word travels faster than ice breaks." She opened the door wider, because it was ten degrees and holding it shut against a man who hadn't moved an inch toward her felt like cowardice dressed up as caution. "Well. You've walked a long way to stand in a doorway."

"I saw the greenhouse," he said.

"The greenhouse saw itself," Greta said. "It was there. It has eyes now, all those holes in the roof." She stepped aside. "There's coffee. It's been boiled so many times it's mostly opinion, but it's hot."

* * *

The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and apples going soft in a bowl, and Silas sat where she pointed him, at the table's far corner, the way a man sits when he has learned that corners are safer than centers. The grafting knife lay between them and neither of them moved it. On the wall hung a photograph of the orchard in bloom, a white sea under a hard blue sky, and beneath it a smaller frame: a young man in an Army uniform, jaw set like he was posing against an argument. Emil. Silas felt the letter shift in his pocket as if it had a temperature of its own.

"He's in Texas," Greta said, following his eyes. "Camp, training. He enlisted before the declaration. Before anyone asked him to." She said it evenly, but her hand went to the table's edge and gripped it a moment, the way a person steadies themselves against news rather than furniture. "My father hasn't written to him since September. And Emil hasn't written to us. So there's a whole silence crossing the state both directions, and I'm told that's patriotism."

Silas looked at the coffee in his cup. He had driven Adeline Marsh for two years and read every letter that passed through her hands, and he had never once been asked to account for himself this plainly. "I drove Mr. Brandt to town," he said. "In the fall. Many times. To the doctor, to the lawyer, to the steamship office."

"He talked about you. The Jap who drives for the voting lady." She said *the Jap* without heat, quoting, letting him hear how the town said it so he would know she knew better. "He liked you. That was rare. He liked approximately six people on this earth and most of them were dead."

"He asked me—" Silas stopped. Started again. "Before he went into the hospital, he asked me to carry a letter to his son. When the time comes, he said. I agreed."

Greta went very still. Outside, wind moved down the orchard rows, and somewhere in the broken greenhouse a loose pane ticked against its frame like a clock remembering it had a job.

"You have it," she said. "Now. Tonight. You brought it."

"I have it. I have not—" He set the cup down carefully. "The time has come, I think. Your father is worse. The registration, the lists — your name and his are in the newspaper now, under *enemy aliens*. And I am on a list too. A different list. Smaller print." He almost smiled. "But a list."

"And?" she said. "What have you not done?"

"I have not decided that I am allowed."

* * *

That was the thing, Greta thought afterward, the thing she would turn over for years like a stone found in a furrow. Not *I have not decided to do it.* Not fear of the marshal or the deputies or the tar-painters. He had sat in her kitchen with a federal offense folded in oilcloth inside his coat and confessed, with the gravity of a man naming a sin, that he did not know whether he was *permitted.*

Everyone else who had come up that hill since November had known exactly what they were permitted. Permitted to throw stones. Permitted to paint the wall. Permitted to knock on her door at dusk and ask, with terrible politeness, whether any Germans lived here besides herself, as if she might be harboring a cellar full of kaisers. The whole country had become a census taker, going door to door, asking *what are you, exactly,* and writing down whatever answer suited it.

And here sat a man the census had also gotten hold of — she'd seen the notices, *all alien males of enemy blood must register* — and instead of learning cruelty from the lesson plan, he sat holding his hat like a boy at a funeral and worried about permission.

"My father," she said, "spent forty years teaching trees to grow sideways. Espalier. You train them against a wall so the fruit ripens even in a short summer." She nodded at the photograph. "People think it's cruel, bending them. It isn't. The tree doesn't know it's bent. It only knows which way the sun comes from, and it grows toward that." She looked at him straight. "You've decided which way your sun comes from. The rest is paperwork."

Silas was quiet for so long she thought she had offended him. Then he reached into his coat and laid the oilcloth packet on the table between the knife and the ledger, and unwrapped it, and there was the letter, Heinrich's shaky German hand facing up like a face turned toward light.

"It should reach him," Silas said. "Cleveland is four hundred miles. The trains are watched, the roads will be watched more when the thaw comes, and my papers say I may not leave the county without—" He stopped. "They say many things."

"They say them to me too."

"So I am telling you, not asking you, because asking would put the weight on you and it is not yours." His voice stayed level but his hands had gone still in a way that made her think of ice under load. "I will take it myself. I drive Mrs. Marsh to Columbus sometimes; I can learn the roads beyond. I don't know yet how. But I wanted you to know the letter exists, and that it is not lost, and that your father's request did not die with his strength."

Greta looked at the envelope a long moment. Then she got up, took a pencil from the ledge, and on the back of the envelope — over her father's objectionable handwriting, defacing it without ceremony — she wrote in small, firm letters: *Deliver by hand. Trust the bearer.*

"There," she said. "Now it's official. My father always said the family name would root in American soil one way or another. Apparently it roots by way of a Japanese chauffeur and a forged endorsement." She almost smiled. It was the first time her face had tried it in months, and the attempt showed her, briefly, as the girl in the orchard photograph, the one who'd run the rows barefoot. "You understand what happens to you if they catch you."

"I have considered it."

"No. You've feared it. Considering comes after." She poured him more coffee, the bitter boiled-down opinion of it, and sat back down. "Then consider this. There's a man in Chillicothe — a dealer — who owes my father for forty years of nursery stock. He drives a truck north twice a month, nursery orders. Second week of March he goes to Akron. If a man needed to be on that road with cargo no one asks about—"

She let it hang. Outside, down at the bluff, the river boomed once, deep and muffled, like a door closing in a large house — the ice beginning, at last, to talk about breaking.

Silas set down his cup. "Tell me the dealer's name," he said.

* * *

He walked back along the towpath with the letter rewrapped in oilcloth and the dealer's name sewn into his memory alongside the route numbers Greta had recited, and behind him the farmhouse window went dark room by room until only the orchard remained, rows of bent trees holding their shape against the wind. At the grain elevator he cranked the motorcar awake and sat with his hands on the wheel, watching his breath silver the windshield.

Tomorrow Adeline would want the car washed and the route to Columbus planned and her correspondence delivered, and he would read it all as he always did, petitions and threats and love letters, history arriving daily at the Marsh house in stamped envelopes.

But tonight, for the first time, he had written a line in it himself — or Greta had written it and he had agreed to carry it — and the ink was not dry.

Two days later, sorting the afternoon mail on the hall table while Adeline dictated upstairs, Silas slit an envelope with the government's return address and felt the temperature of the house change.
