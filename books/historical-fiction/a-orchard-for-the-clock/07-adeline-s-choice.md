## 7. Adeline's Choice

Adeline Marsh had read ten thousand letters in her life — petitions with trembling signatures, editorials calling her a harlot of the ballot box, love notes slipped into suffrage pamphlets by young women who signed only their initials. She believed there was no letter she could not open without flinching.

She was wrong.

It was the spare tire well that undid her. She had gone to the garage on a Tuesday evening to retrieve her galoshes from behind the seat of the Hupmobile, and the tire iron had been lying wrong — angled across the rim instead of beside it. Silas was careful with tools. Silas was careful with everything: his gloves folded in pairs, his ledger entries ruled straight as fence wire, his silence arranged like furniture no one could knock over. A man like that did not leave a tire iron crooked unless his hands had been shaking when he put it back.

She knelt on the cold garage floor and lifted the spare. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged into the well, lay an envelope. It was not addressed to anyone she knew. The paper had gone soft at the corners, handled often, and the seal had been broken and re-stuck with such delicacy that she might not have noticed if she hadn't spent thirty years steaming open other people's assumptions about what a woman could carry.

She did not open it. She wanted to — God knew her fingers were already asking permission — but she held it up to the bare bulb and simply looked, the way she'd learned to look at a ballot measure for the trap hidden in its third clause.

The return address, faded pencil: *H. Brandt, Orchard, River Rd.*

Brandt. The German orchard keeper whose name had been moving through town all winter like smoke through floorboards. *Enemy alien.* The words had a new weight now; Congress had given them legal bones. Adeline set the envelope back exactly as she'd found it, replaced the spare, laid the tire iron at its proper angle, and stood in the dark of her own garage feeling the floor tilt beneath her.

Because she understood immediately. Not everything — but enough. Enough to know that Silas Nakamura, the most invisible man in Marion County, was planning something that would make him terribly visible. And whatever he was planning, he meant to use her motorcar to do it.

* * *

She let two days pass. This was strategy, though it felt like cowardice. On Thursday she had a delegation arriving from Columbus — three women who had driven four hours through sleet to coordinate the petition drive, women whose names would appear in newspapers if anything went wrong, whose husbands' businesses would be mentioned in the same breath as hers. Adeline had built eleven years of respectability out of mortar made of unimpeachable conduct. One scandal and the whole wall came down, and it would not be her alone buried under it. It would be every woman who had lent the movement her name.

Friday morning she sent the car to be washed and gassed, then told Silas she wouldn't need him until noon. At half past eight she sat in her study with the government inquiry spread before her — the one that had arrived ten days earlier, inquiring after "disloyal elements among household staff," and which she had shown him, because hiding it had seemed a worse betrayal than revealing it. He had read it standing, perfectly still, and thanked her, and gone out to crank the engine, and she had told herself that stillness was innocence.

Now she knew better. Stillness was a man holding a door shut from the inside.

At nine o'clock she heard the motorcar come around the drive and stop, and heard his footsteps on the porch — light, precise, always at the edge of hearing, as if he'd practiced being unheard. She called him into the study before he could knock.

Silas stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands. In the gray morning light she studied his face properly, perhaps for the first time since she'd hired him back after the registration. There was a bruise-colored exhaustion under his eyes, and something else — a coiled quality, like a river under ice. He had been carrying this alone for weeks. She wondered how many nights he'd lain in his room at Mrs. Pruitt's boarding house, listening to the house creak, mapping roads in his head.

"Mr. Nakamura," she said. "Sit down."

"I can stand, ma'am."

"Sit down anyway." He sat on the edge of the chair as though it might be taken from him mid-sit. Adeline folded her hands on the desk. "I found the letter."

The change in him was so small a stranger would have missed it — a settling of the shoulders, a dimming behind the eyes, the particular stillness of a man watching a door close on the last exit. But she had spent her life reading faces that tried to give nothing away. She saw it all.

"Ma'am—"

"Don't." She raised one hand. "Don't tell me you don't know what I mean. Don't tell me it belongs to someone else. I have heard forty years of men telling me things weren't what they plainly were, and I haven't the patience left for it this morning." She drew a breath. "You mean to take Heinrich Brandt's letter to his son. In Cleveland. In my automobile."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, a crow argued with another crow about something neither would concede.

"Yes, ma'am," Silas said finally. His voice was quiet and level and it cost him visibly to keep it so. "I gave my word. Before the war. When it was only a letter."

"A word given in peacetime," Adeline said, "is the most expensive kind. Everything costs more now." She stood and went to the window. Down the street, two men were shoveling snow off the walkway in front of Hartley's Drugstore, and they stopped to watch her window the way men had watched her windows for a decade — not hostile, not friendly, simply *noting*, the way a census taker notes. "Do you know what happens if you're stopped? If a deputy opens that door and finds you, a registered enemy alien, driving toward Cleveland with a sealed letter from another enemy alien in your possession?"

"They won't find it where I've put it."

"They don't need to find the letter, Mr. Nakamura. They need only find *you*. You are the evidence. You're the whole case." She turned to face him. "They'll detain you. They'll question you. And when they're done questioning you, they'll ask who owns the motorcar. And my name will be in the paper next to the word *disloyal*, and every dollar I've begged from cautious widows, every church basement that lets us meet, every legislator who takes my calls because I am respectable — all of it burns in an afternoon. And it will not burn alone. There are four hundred women in this county who have signed their names because I promised them I would never ask them to sign anything careless."

He was looking at the floor. His hands, folded in his lap, had gone white at the knuckles.

"You're right, ma'am," he said. "I'll return the letter tonight. I'll tell Brandt's daughter I couldn't—" His voice caught, a hairline fracture, and repaired itself. "I'll tell her I couldn't do it."

And there it was. That was the sound of a man folding up the last piece of himself, and Adeline Marsh had heard it before — in her own voice, thirty years ago, in a law office in Columbus, when she'd been told, politely, by a man who meant no cruelty, that the world was arranged a certain way and her wanting otherwise was charming but immaterial.

She had said *yes sir* that day. She had walked home and sat by her window and hated herself for a week, and then she had gotten up and done the immoderate thing anyway, and it had cost her, and she had paid, and she would pay again tomorrow, and she had never once regretted the paying.

"Look at me, Silas."

He looked up. And she saw it plain now, what she'd half-seen for months beneath the deference: this man believed history belonged to other people. Believed he was a footnote, a line in a ledger, a name on a registration card. She recognized the belief because she'd been born inside it, had worn it like a corset for the first half of her life, laced by everyone who loved her best.

"Do you know why I hired you back?" she asked. "After the registration. My treasurer advised me against it. She said a Japanese chauffeur was a liability in wartime. She said it kindly, the way people say unforgivable things kindly." She came around the desk and stood before him. "I hired you back because you are the only person in this town who has never once looked through me. Do you understand that? Everyone looks through me, Silas. The grocer. The legislators. Half my own committee. They see the cause or they see the scandal or they see the spinster with the motorcar, but they do not see *me*. You look at me like I'm a person who exists. I have been starved for that look for fifty-one years. I am not going to pretend I didn't notice it just because Washington has decided what you are on a card you carry in your pocket."

"Mrs. Marsh—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice shook and she let it. "I remember what it is to be told where women may not go. Not allowed on the sidewalk in front of the saloon. Not allowed in the courthouse gallery during certain arguments. Not allowed at the polls — *that* one especially, that one every November, standing outside while men I out-thought marched in and voted my life smaller." She took a breath. "Every year of my life, someone has handed me a map with my streets crossed off it, and told me it was for my safety. And every year I believed it a little longer than I should have. I will not hand you a map with your streets crossed off it. I won't do it. Even if it costs me."

Silas had gone very still — but a different stillness than before, and she was beginning to learn his dialects of silence.

"The petition drive is Thursday," she said. "The delegates arrive Wednesday night. I cannot be seen to be anywhere near this, which means I must be seen everywhere, loudly, until Thursday. So here is what we will do." She crossed to the bell pull, then stopped herself — no servants to ring for, no one to arrange this but herself. "Sunday. The deputies go to church; the ones worth fearing sit in the second pew at First Methodist and sleep through the sermon. Take the car at seven, be back by midnight, wash it, and park it exactly where it stands. If anyone asks, I sent you to Columbus for pamphlets. I will swear to it. I have discovered," and here her mouth bent into something too dry to be a smile, "that a woman of my reputation swearing to a thing is very nearly as good as a man doing it, and cheaper than a lawyer."

"Ma'am, if I'm caught—"

"If you're caught, I lose everything I've built." She met his eyes. "If I refuse you, I lose something I built longer, and I am too old to rebuild it. Give me your hand, Mr. Nakamura."

He gave it. His grip was careful, as everything about him was careful, and she held it a moment longer than a mistress holds a chauffeur's hand, and let that be its own sentence.

"One day," she said. "One day, and you come back. That is not a request. Whatever that letter says, whatever you find in Cleveland — you come back, because I did not spend eleven years building a respectable life so I could throw it away on a man who drowns in a river he could have crossed. Do you hear me?"

"Yes, ma'am." His voice had gone rough. "I hear you."

"Then get out of my study and go check the oil. And Silas—" he paused at the door, cap in hands, and for a moment he looked younger than his years and older than the century, "—the tire iron goes back at the correct angle. I noticed. I notice everything. It's how I've survived."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lives. "Yes, ma'am," he said, and went.

* * *

Silas cranked the engine harder than it needed, because his hands needed the work. The motor caught and idled, breathing steam into the cold.

Inside the well beneath the spare tire, the letter waited. Inside his coat pocket, folded in quarters, a map of the Cleveland road with two bridges circled and the hours of the ferries written small. Inside his chest, something that had been clenched since November came loose all at once, and he had to lean his forehead against the steering wheel and breathe until it passed.

She had said *yes*. Not the yes of pity — he knew pity's temperature, and this had been hotter. She had weighed her whole life on one side of the scale and set him on the other, and he had watched the needle move, and she had not flinched.

*You come back,* she had said.

He put the car in gear and drove out onto the street, past Hartley's Drugstore, past the two men who watched him pass, past the marshal's office where his fingerprints lived in a drawer like a confession. The river road opened ahead of him, silver with yesterday's ice, running east toward Cleveland and the son who did not yet know his father was dying.

One day. He meant to use all twenty-four hours of it.
