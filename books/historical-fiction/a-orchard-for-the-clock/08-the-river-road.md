## 8. The River Road

The sleet began before dawn, ticking against the windshield like a clock that had decided to count down something. Silas drove with his collar up and the heater's brass vent turned toward his hands, though it gave little warmth. The motorcar moved through the gray morning like a boat through shallow water — Adeline Marsh's Packard, all polished fenders and borrowed purpose, carrying him east on the river road toward Cleveland with a dead man's letter sleeping in the spare tire well beneath the floor mat.

He had wrapped it in oilcloth first. He had practiced the motion of lifting the mat, turning the wing nut, lowering the letter into the well beside the jack, and closing it again until he could do it in under twenty seconds, in the dark, without looking. He had done it that morning in the boarding house yard at four o'clock, kneeling in frozen mud while the town slept, feeling the letter's small weight in his palms — nothing, really. Paper. Ink. A father's handwriting gone stiff and crooked at the edges of each line.

It weighed more than any cargo he had ever carried.

The road ran along the river, which was still locked in ice but groaning now, deep sounds from underneath, like furniture being dragged across the floors of a house where no one lived. The thaw had come early this year. In the fields, snow lay in rotten patches, and crows walked the fence lines with their heads tilted, listening to the same breaking sounds Silas heard.

He had left a note for Adeline on her kitchen table: *Gone to keep an appointment. Back by nightfall. S.N.* It was not a lie exactly. He had learned in the past weeks that a lie was mostly a matter of what you left out, and leaving out was becoming easier for him than breathing, which frightened him more than the deputies would.

Cleveland was ninety miles. Emil Brandt was stationed at Camp Sherman but home on leave until Thursday. Greta had told him the boarding house on Cedar Avenue where Emil stayed when he came to see the woman he intended to marry, if there still was a woman, if there still were intentions, if the war had not gotten into that too. *Tell him my father did not die hating him,* Greta had said, standing among her broken greenhouse glass with frost on her shawl. *Or tell him nothing. Tell him only that someone kept his word. That might be worth more.*

* * *

The first stop came outside Millbrook, where a deputy had strung a rope across the road and stood beside it with his hat brim dripping and a rifle slung carelessly backward, the way men carry rifles when they want you to know they are not afraid to turn them around.

Silas stopped the Packard and set the brake and put both hands on the wheel where they could be seen. This too he had practiced. Hands visible, eyes level, voice flat. A man who has been fingerprinted learns quickly how much of himself lives in his hands.

"Where you headed?"

"Cleveland."

"Business?"

"An errand for Mrs. Marsh. You know Mrs. Marsh." He said it evenly, not as a shield, just as fact, and watched the deputy decide whether it was one.

The deputy leaned down. His face was young under the hat, chapped red at the cheekbones, and his eyes went over Silas the way a man reads a bill of sale — slowly, itemizing. Hair black. Eyes dark. Skin the color of tea left standing. Not German. Not quite anything the poster in the marshal's office had words for.

"Jap, ain't you," the deputy said. Not a question.

"Japanese-born. Naturalization pending." The phrase felt like a coin he had found on the road and was spending before anyone could ask where he got it.

"Pending." The deputy smiled a little at the word, tasting its uselessness. "You got your papers on you?"

Silas produced them from his breast pocket, unfolding the document the way he unfolded everything now — carefully, showing no eagerness, no fear. His own fingerprints looked back up at him, loops and whorls, the map of a man reduced to ridges. The deputy studied it, then studied Silas's face against it, holding the paper up beside his jaw as though comparing a horse to its pedigree.

"Enemy alien registration," the deputy read aloud, savoring it. "You people and the Huns both. Whole world full of folks need watching these days." He handed the papers back. "Keep to the road. No stopping. And tell Mrs. Marsh the county appreciates her patriotism."

"I'll tell her."

The rope came down. Silas drove through it slowly, not letting his foot shake on the pedal until the deputy was a speck in the mirror, and then letting it shake all it wanted.

His heart was going like a telegraph key. But underneath the terror — he could feel it rising already, shameful and bright — underneath it was something that felt almost like joy. *They stopped me,* he thought. *They saw me. I am on the road, I am in the story, I am worth stopping.*

History, he understood now, was not something that happened to other people. It was a river under the ice all along, and it had simply waited for him to walk out far enough.

* * *

Near Chagrin Falls the sleet thickened to true weather, white ropes of it slanting across the road, and the wipers — Adeline's beautiful new wipers, rubber on glass, a modern miracle — beat back and forth like a metronome counting down the last of his luck.

The second stop was not a rope across the road. It was headlights behind him, gaining, and a hand-crank siren wound up thin and furious, and then a voice shouting over the storm: "Pull it over! Pull it over, damn you!"

Silas pulled over.

There were two of them this time, and neither was young. The older one had a mustache gone gray at the corners and a face built for suspicion, the kind of face that had spent thirty years finding guilt in strangers and had grown comfortable doing it. He came to the driver's window with a lantern held high, and the light came through the glass and filled the car with yellow, and Silas felt every secret in the vehicle begin to sweat.

"Out of the car."

"Is there a problem, officer—"

"Out. Of. The car." Each word placed down like a stone.

Silas stepped out into the sleet. It struck his neck and melted down his collar. The younger deputy, round-faced and windburned, was already walking the length of the Packard, running a hand along the fenders, pausing at the rear.

"Registered owner?" the older one asked.

"The car belongs to Mrs. Adeline Marsh, of—"

"I know whose car it is. I asked who owns it." He let that sit, watching Silas try to find the difference. "A Jap driving a suffragist's Packard to Cleveland in a sleet storm. Alone. At speed." He shook his head slowly, a man admiring the audacity of the world. "Boy, do you know what we've been pulling off this road all week? Deserters. Draft dodgers. Two Germans with a trunk full of dynamite near Zanesville — *dynamite*, boy. And here comes you."

"I'm delivering correspondence for Mrs. Marsh."

"At speed. In a storm." The lantern rose again. "Open the boot."

The boot held a tool roll, a blanket, a tin of sandwiches Greta had pressed on him at dawn — rye bread and hard sausage, food from another country, another life. The deputies went through it with the thoroughness of men who enjoyed their work. Then the younger one straightened up by the rear bumper.

"Paw," he said. "Spare tire well's got a false bottom look to it. Wing nut's scratched."

The world narrowed. Silas heard the river, three fields away, groaning under its ice. He heard his own blood. Ninety years old, his grandmother had been, and she had once told him that in the last moments of a thing you would hear very small sounds very clearly, and she had been right about everything else.

The older deputy turned the lantern on him full, and the light took away the road, the storm, the world, and left only the two of them.

"What's in the well, boy."

"A jack," Silas said. "And a spare."

"Lift the mat."

And Silas understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that he could refuse — and be beaten, or worse, and searched anyway. Or he could lift the mat, and the letter would be found, and Heinrich Brandt's last words to his son would pass through the hands of two men with a lantern and a rifle and a county's permission, and whatever happened after that, the promise would be broken in a way no distance could mend.

He bent toward the car.

That was when the younger deputy, circling back around the fender with the lantern, stopped.

"Paw," he said again, slower. "Paw, look at the radiator ornament. Look at the plate bracket. That's the *suffrage* car. That's the lady's car — the one in the *Gazette*. Gold star on the door. She drove Senator Hardin's wife to Columbus in it. My ma voted for school board because of that lady." He said it all in a rush, a boy defending something he hadn't known he cared about. "Ma's got her picture on the parlor wall. Cut out of the paper."

The older deputy did not lower the lantern. For a long moment the sleet ticked on the roof of the Packard and the lantern hissed faintly in the wet, and Silas stood between them with his hand on the mat he was supposed to lift, frozen mid-motion, a man caught between two versions of himself — the enemy alien on the paper, and the chauffeur of a woman whose photograph hung on a stranger's parlor wall.

"Mrs. Marsh," the older deputy said finally, and the name came out grudging, like a coin pried from a cold fist. "She vouches for you?"

"She hired me back herself. After the registration." Silas heard his own voice steady, and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a bridge holding weight you were sure would fell it. "You can wire her office. She'll confirm. She confirms everything, officer. That is rather her profession."

Something moved at the corner of the old man's mouth. Not a smile. An admission.

"Put the mat down," he said.

Silas put the mat down. The wing nut stayed unturned. The letter slept on in its oilcloth, six inches beneath a deputy's boot heel, and Silas felt the whole of his body begin to shake only now, only after, the way a house shakes after the earthquake has passed.

"Get out of the county before dark," the older deputy said, turning away. "And boy—" He paused at the tailgate, rain streaming off his hat brim. "Whatever you're hauling, it'd better be worth a woman's name. Because that's the only thing saved you today. Her name. Not yours."

"Yes sir," Silas said.

They pulled away, taillights drowning in the sleet. Silas sat in the Packard with the engine ticking and his hands flat on the wheel and did not drive for a long time. Through the windshield the road went on east, gray and endless and open, and somewhere at the end of it a soldier in army wool was waiting to learn what his father had written him, and whether love, arriving late, still counted.

Silas started the engine.

By the time Cleveland's smoke rose on the horizon, purple against the late afternoon, he had stopped shaking. He found the Cedar Avenue boarding house as the streetlamps came on, gaslit halos in the falling wet, and parked the Packard in the shadow of a church whose bell was ringing five o'clock.

On the third-floor landing a tall young man in a uniform that fit him loosely, as if he were still growing into it or shrinking out of it, was coming down the stairs with a duffel bag over his shoulder. Blond, blue-eyed, a Brandt jaw on him plain as a signature. He stopped when he saw Silas — a stranger, Japanese, standing in the stairwell of his boarding house — and his hand moved instinctively toward the duffel strap.

"You're looking for somebody?" Emil Brandt asked. "Because I'm shipping out tomorrow at dawn, friend, so whatever it is, it had better keep till never."

Silas reached for his breast pocket, and stopped, and instead said the only true thing he had:

"It won't keep. And it isn't mine to carry much longer."
