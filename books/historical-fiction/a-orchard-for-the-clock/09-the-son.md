## 9. The Son

The house on Cedar Avenue had a porch that sagged in the middle like a tired horse's back, and a flag hanging from the second-floor rail — forty-eight stars, sun-faded to the color of weak tea. Silas stood at the foot of the steps with the letter inside his coat, against his chest, where it had ridden for two days and two hundred miles, and he found that his feet would not carry him up.

Cleveland was louder than home. Not bigger — he had expected bigger — but louder in a way that pressed against the ears: streetcars shrieking on their rails, a newsboy crying something about Russia, a hundred conversations braided together in English and Polish and Italian and a tongue he did not know at all. In Marietta, a man could be invisible by standing still. Here, invisibility required motion. Everyone moved, and so no one looked twice at a Japanese man in a good coat walking with purpose. Purpose was a kind of citizenship. He had learned this on the drive up, stopped twice on the river road, and he wore it now like Adeline wore her white sashes.

He checked the address again, though he knew it by heart. Heinrich Brandt had written it out for him in October, in pencil, pressing hard enough to tear the paper: *Emil Brandt, 114 Cedar Avenue, Cleveland, Ohio.* A soldier's address now, or it would be soon. Greta had confirmed it — her brother wrote when he could, short letters full of nothing, the way men write from camps.

Silas climbed the steps. The boards gave under his weight and gave again.

A woman opened the door before he knocked a second time. Middle-aged, flour on her knuckles, eyes that went from his face to the street behind him and back with the quick arithmetic of a landlady counting trouble. Behind her, somewhere in the house, a wireless played march music.

"I'm looking for Emil Brandt," Silas said.

"Nobody here by that name."

"He rents a room. He'd be tall. Fair hair. He'd have—" Silas stopped himself from saying *an accent*, because the woman's own vowels were thick with somewhere else, and because he understood, suddenly and completely, what it cost to say *nobody here by that name* in the spring of 1918.

She studied him. Whatever she found — the coat, perhaps, or the steadiness, or simply the exhaustion of a man who had driven through sleet since dawn — she stepped back and called into the hall: "Emil! Someone for you."

* * *

Emil Brandt came down the stairs in his uniform.

Silas had imagined him a dozen times on the road north, and every version had been wrong. He had imagined the son as an echo of the father — broad, weathered, slow-moving, a man built for orchards. But Emil was lean and quick-eyed, and the Army had planed him down further, and there was nothing of Ohio farmland in the way he stood except, perhaps, the stillness of his hands, which hung at his sides the way a man's hands hang when they have spent years holding ladders and pruning knives and have not yet learned what to do instead.

The khaki sat on him like a fact that could not be argued with. On the left sleeve, a chevron. Over his heart, nothing yet. Silas thought: *this man may be shipped west to fight Germany while his father dies across the river from me, declared an enemy of the country whose cloth he wears,* and the thought was so heavy he nearly sat down on the porch.

"You're not from the post office," Emil said. His voice was flat Ohio German, the accent worn smooth by drill sergeants.

"No."

"Then who are you?"

"My name is Silas Nakamura." He said it plainly. He had learned, these past months, that names given plainly were harder to suspect. "I work for a Mrs. Marsh, in Marietta. Before that I drove fruit for your father. Apples, mostly, across the bridge on market days." He paused. "I've come to bring you something. From him."

Something moved through Emil's face like wind through a row of trees — fast, bending everything, gone before you could name it. He glanced past Silas to the street, then stepped out onto the porch and pulled the door shut behind him.

"Not in the house," he said. It wasn't an apology. It was geography.

* * *

They walked three blocks to a small park where the snow had rotted into gray slush and a statue of some forgotten judge stood green with age. Emil bought two coffees from a cart without asking if Silas wanted one, which Silas took for courtesy rather than command — it was how farmers bought things, one for each hand, no ceremony.

For a long time neither spoke. A tram rattled past. Children ran along a path, chasing something invisible.

"He's alive," Emil said finally. It wasn't a question either, but it wanted an answer.

"Yes. Thinner. His hands bother him — the cold gets into them, Greta says. He can't prune anymore. She does it."

"Greta." Emil looked into his coffee. "Is she all right? Truly. Not the way people say *all right* when they mean *alive*."

"The greenhouse has stones through it. Half the town crosses the street when they see her. But the trees are pruned, and the cellar is stocked, and she stands at the gate like she owns the road." Silas hesitated. "She does own the road. Nobody's told her otherwise."

Emil almost smiled. Then the smile went where smiles go in wartime, and he said: "I enlisted in May. Before the declaration even. I figured — better to choose than be chosen for." He turned the paper cup in his fingers. "You understand what that makes me, over there. If they ship me to France, I'll be shooting at men who talk like my father. Maybe cousins. There are Brandts in Baden. My father used to get letters." He laughed once, without any humor in it. "Not anymore, I suppose. Now the letters come from strangers."

"From me," Silas said.

"Yeah." Emil looked at him then, really looked, the way deputies had looked at him on the river road, and Silas braced for the question underneath the look — *what are you, where are you from, why does a Jap carry mail for a suffragist in Ohio* — the whole census-taker catechism he had answered twice already with his papers sweating in his coat pocket.

But Emil only held out his hand. "Give it to me. Please. Before I lose my nerve and start talking about myself again."

* * *

The letter was heavier than paper had any right to be. Silas had felt its weight change on the journey — first the weight of a promise made lightly, then the weight of a crime, and now, drawing it out from inside his coat, the weight simply of itself: an envelope sealed with wax, softened and re-hardened, marked with a thumbprint in the wax that could only have been Heinrich's, big and blunt as a stone.

Emil broke the seal with his thumbnail and read standing up, in the cold, in the slanting afternoon light. Silas watched him read. He had spent six years reading other people's faces over other people's letters — Adeline's flicker of triumph at a favorable editorial, the tightening around her mouth at the threats — but he had never watched a man read a letter meant for himself alone, and he found he could not look away, and could not decipher it either. Emil's face did what soldiers' faces do: it held.

Twice his jaw worked. Once he exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the way a man breathes when lifting something too heavy.

When he finished, he folded the letter along its creases, once, twice, precisely, and put it inside his tunic, against his chest, where Silas had carried it. Then he stood looking at the statue of the forgotten judge for a long moment.

"There's a deed in it," Emil said. "The orchard. Signed over to me." His voice came out strange, flattened at the edges. "And instructions. He wants the family name kept on American soil. Wants me to hold it, work it, after." He shook his head slowly. "It's not forgiveness, Mr. Nakamura. You should know that. I argued with him two years ago about the war, about Germany, about — God, about whether I was an American or a German first, and I said things a son shouldn't say, and he said things back, and we both let the silence grow because silence is easier to tend than truth." He looked down at his cup. "This letter isn't him saying I was right. Or that he was. It's him saying: *the trees don't care who quarreled. Plant.*"

"That sounds like him," Silas said.

"It does." Emil laughed — a real one this time, brief and startled out of him. "It does sound like him. Damn him."

* * *

They walked back toward Cedar Avenue as the light went coppery between the buildings. At the corner where they would part, Emil stopped.

"Why?" he said. "Why did you come? Two days' drive. Sleet. Deputies stopping a Japanese man driving a white woman's car with a letter from a German enemy alien in his coat. You must have known what would happen if they searched you." He wasn't accusing. He was genuinely asking, the way a man asks when the answer might rearrange something in himself. "My father isn't your blood. I'm not. Why?"

Silas considered lying, or half-lying — *your father paid me, I owed him for seasons of work, I was going to Cleveland anyway* — the comfortable fictions, the paperwork of motive. But the man had just read his father's whole soul on a park bench, and something in Silas was tired of carrying papers.

"Because someone had to," he said.

Emil waited. It was not enough, and both of them knew it, and Emil had the patience to let it stand until more came.

"In October your father asked me, and I said yes the way you say yes to carrying a package. Lightly." Silas looked down the street, at the tram wires stitching the sky. "Then the war came, and the census man came, and I lost my job, and I got papers that say I'm an enemy in the country where I've paid taxes for nine years, and I understood that your father and I are — " he searched for the word and refused the easy ones — "neighbors. That's all. Neighbors. And a promise to a neighbor made in peacetime is worth exactly what it costs in wartime. Otherwise it was never a promise. It was a pleasantry."

He shrugged, embarrassed by his own eloquence, which arrived only ever at moments like this and never when Adeline needed a speech at a rally.

"So. Someone had to. And I could."

Emil put out his hand. They shook, the soldier's grip, firm and brief, and then Emil held on a moment longer.

"When I get leave again — after, if there is an after — I'll come to the orchard. Tell her that. Tell her I read it four times on the walk here and I'll read it four hundred." He released Silas's hand. "And tell my father—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell him the name stays planted."

* * *

Silas walked back to where he had parked the car, in a lane off Cedar, under a gas lamp not yet lit. The sky was doing what March skies do in Ohio, bruising toward evening, and the wind had shifted and come up colder, smelling of the lake and of snow that hadn't fallen yet but intended to.

He was twenty feet from the car when he saw them.

Two men stood beside it. Not leaning, not idling — standing, the way officials stand, hands visible, coats open. One wore a city policeman's badge; the other wore no badge at all, which was worse. The one without a badge had a telegram flimsy in his gloved hand, and as Silas came closer, unhurried, purposeful, purpose being citizenship, the man raised the flimsy slightly, like a man holding up a card in a game, and said:

"Silas Nakamura? We'd like a word about your trip north. Seems somebody wired ahead from Marietta."

Behind them, the spare tire well where the letter had ridden sat empty, and Silas made himself keep walking, keep his face pleasant and blank, and began — quickly, silently, in the language of his childhood — to decide what he would say, and what he would never say, and how much of himself a telegram could weigh.
