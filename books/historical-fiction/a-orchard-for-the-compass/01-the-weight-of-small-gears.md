## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The road to the observatory was a spine of switchbacks stitched into the mountain's shoulder, and Jude Nakamura climbed it the way she did everything: carefully, counting.

Four hundred and eleven steps from the town gate to the iron archway, if you took the long path. Three hundred and eighty if you cut through the orchard, but the orchard path was Marta's, and Marta had opinions about people who moved through her trees without invitation. So Jude took the long way, pack heavy on her shoulders with tools wrapped in flannel, each weight in its own pocket accounted for — the jeweler's screwdrivers, the brass pliers with the velvet jaws, the oil in its stoppered vial that smelled faintly of pine and something older, like the inside of a church.

The morning was doing what autumn mornings did up here: burning off the fog in slow ribbons so that the valley below appeared piece by piece — first the slate roofs, then the river's silver thread, then the market square where women were already setting out cabbages in pyramids, arranging them the way everything in this town was arranged, neatly, defensively, as though tidiness itself were a kind of armor.

Jude had lived here five years and she still caught herself looking at the town this way, from above, as if checking on it. As if it might need her.

Her mother would have laughed at that. Her mother had never needed anyone, right up until she had, and then there had been no one, and that was the arithmetic Jude carried in her chest like a swallowed stone. Five years since the funeral. Five years since she'd come up this hill for the first time with nothing but her mother's toolbox and a letter of introduction written in a hand so fine it looked like engraving.

*The girl has my trust. Give her work and she will give you back your time.*

Marta Ilves had read the letter twice, folded it along its original creases, and said only: "Can you true a escapement?"

"I can true an escapement," Jude had said, which was not quite the same as yes, and Marta had known it wasn't, and had hired her anyway, and that — that particular mercy, the hiring of someone who was almost enough — was the shape of every kindness Jude had received since.

She came through the iron archway into the observatory yard. The building rose ahead of her, white-walled and dome-crowned, its copper roof gone the green of deep water. It had stood on this hill for a hundred and forty years, keeper of the region's official time; every clock in every office and schoolhouse and station within two hundred kilometers answered to the master clock in the room beneath the dome, and by extension to the men and women who tended it. There were three of them now besides Marta. Tomas, broad-shouldered and quick to laugh, who could rebuild a striking train blindfolded and who pretended he couldn't hear when the others teased him about his singing. Anneli, oldest of the apprentices, precise as a ruler, who kept the observation logs in handwriting so even it looked printed. And Petra, youngest, who talked to the telescopes like they were horses she was gentling.

And there was Jude, who had learned, over five years, the particular grammar of this place — which silences meant *leave me be*, and which meant *stay near*, and which one, the rarest, meant *I am telling you something true by saying nothing at all.*

"Late," Anneli said, not looking up from her logbook when Jude came into the clock room. It was seven minutes past six. The word was a greeting. In another house it might have been an accusation; here it was simply the weather.

"The fog on the switchbacks."

"Marta says the fog is no excuse. Marta says time doesn't fog." But Anneli's mouth did something at the corner that was almost a smile, and she slid a cup of coffee across the table toward Jude without being asked, and Jude understood this too: *you are late, and you are forgiven, and I will never say the second part aloud.*

The clock room sat at the heart of the building like a heart indeed, walled in stone, kept at a temperature so constant that Marta checked the thermometer twice daily and recorded it in red ink. Along the walls stood the regulators — eight of them, tall cases of dark walnut, their pendulums breathing in and out of phase like a row of sleeping things. And against the far wall, behind a door most visitors never noticed, the master itself: a case of blackened oak taller than two men, built by hands dead a century, keeping the seconds for everyone below.

Except, as of four days ago, it wasn't.

"It stopped again last night," Tomas said, emerging from behind the case with grease to his elbows and frustration all over his face. "Eleven minutes past two. Just — stopped. Not wound down, not jammed. Stopped, like it thought about it and decided against."

"Clocks don't decide."

"This one does. This one's been deciding things all week. I've cleaned the train twice, checked the suspension, re-poised the bob until my arms ached, and every night it quits at some new hour like a man walking out of his own wedding." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago. "So Marta sent for you."

"She didn't have to send for me. I'm always here."

"You're the clock doctor," Tomas said, grinning. "We're just nurses. She wants the doctor."

It was a joke, mostly. But under it ran the thing that had been running under everything for four days: the master clock did not stop. In a hundred and forty years it had been cleaned, adjusted, struck by lightning once, and shaken by an earthquake that had cracked wine bottles in cellars across the valley, and it had not stopped. Its stopping was not a malfunction. It was an event. And events, in this town in this year of 1938, made people look at one another a little too long across the supper table.

Footsteps on the stone stair — the particular unhurried tread that could belong to no one else — and Marta Ilves came down into the clock room carrying the morning's barometric readings on a clipboard she had owned longer than any of her apprentices had been alive.

She was not a large woman. She seemed, somehow, larger than the room she stood in, the way certain trees seem larger than the forests around them — not tall but *present*, drawing the eye, insisting on her own coordinates. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned with mathematical severity. Her eyes were pale as winter light and missed approximately nothing.

"Nakamura." She said the name correctly, always, every syllable weighted properly, in a country where half the officials in the valley could not or would not manage it. "You've heard about our patient."

"Tomas tells me it stops at night and refuses to explain itself."

"Mm." Marta set down her clipboard and regarded the great black case the way a physician regards a relative — fondly, warily, with old history between them. "I've known this clock forty-one years. When I was younger than you, I oiled its pallets under old Keeper Brandvold, God rest his suspicious soul. It has never stopped unbidden. Never. Not once." She turned those winter-light eyes on Jude. "Which means either something inside it has changed, or something outside it wants us to believe something inside it has changed. Your task is to find out which."

"And if it's the second?"

A pause. Small, but Jude had spent five years learning to measure Marta Ilves's pauses the way other people measured pendulums.

"Then we will want to know who benefits from a broken clock," Marta said, "before we announce to the world that ours is fixed." She handed over a ring of keys, iron worn smooth and bright at the grip. "The master key is the small one. The case hasn't been opened fully — a full opening — in nineteen years. Brandvold's successor sealed it after the cleaning of 'nineteen-nineteen and considered the matter closed. You'll find the seals intact. Break them."

"Break them?"

"You are the clockmaker summoned to repair the master clock. That is your paper, your warrant, your permission. No one will question the apprentice who opens a broken clock. That is exactly why it must be you." Marta held her gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Whatever you find in there, you bring to me before you bring it anywhere else. Do you understand me, child?"

*Child.* She was twenty-three. Marta had called her *child* since the day she arrived and would call her *child*, Jude suspected, at sixty. It should have stung. Instead it settled somewhere warm behind her ribs, and she hated how much it settled, because warmth was a thing that could be taken away, and she had already lost the one person whose warmth couldn't be.

"I understand," Jude said.

"Good." Marta gathered her clipboard. "Petra has breakfast going. Eat first. A trembling hand ruins more movements than rust ever did."

* * *

Breakfast was taken at the long table in the keepers' quarters, where the windows faced east and the light came in thick and gold through glass that had never quite been replaced after the earthquake. Petra had fried eggs and dark bread and the apple preserves that were the single undisputed triumph of the observatory kitchen, made from fruit of Marta's orchard, the rows of gnarled trees visible from this very window, standing bare-shouldered now in the October cold.

"Eat," Petra commanded, sliding the plate over. "You look like a photograph of yourself."

"That bad?"

"Worse. Photographs don't have shadows under their eyes." Petra was seventeen and had no talent whatsoever for silence, which in this household was less a flaw than a public service. "Tomas says the clock's possessed. Anneli says it's temperature drift. Marta says nothing, which means Marta knows something. What do you say?"

"I say eat your eggs before they go cold."

"Coward."

"Careful student," Jude corrected, and Petra laughed, and across the table Anneli looked up from her logbook with that expression she wore — the one that disapproved of laughter in principle but permitted it in practice — and for a moment the room was exactly what it had been for five years: the safest room Jude Nakamura knew.

That was the treachery of belonging, she thought, spearing her egg. You stopped noticing you were safe. Safety became air — invisible, unexamined, necessary. You only remembered it existed when you imagined losing it, and lately, in the autumn of 1938, imagining losing things had become a national pastime. The newspapers in the town kiosk spoke in careful language about careful matters. Men in uniform had begun appearing on the valley roads, first in ones, then in pairs, then in lorries. At the market, Frau Hessler sold her cheese without meeting anyone's eyes. The silences had changed key. Jude knew silences the way musicians knew intervals, and these were minor ones.

"Jude." Anneli, quietly, over the rim of her cup. "The audit clerk was in town yesterday. Asking questions at the records office. Official-looking fellow. Brandt, they said his name was."

The name landed on the table like a dropped gear. Petra's fork paused midair.

"Asking what kind of questions?" Tomas asked.

"The usual kind. Who lives where. Who works where. Whose papers are in order." Anneli set down her cup with deliberate care. "He asked about the observatory. About who keeps the official time."

"And what did the records office tell him?" Marta asked from the doorway, though no one had heard her enter, which was ordinary.

"That we keep it very well," Anneli said.

Something passed around the table — not spoken, only felt, the way a change in barometric pressure is felt before the storm arrives. Marta stood in the doorway a moment, reading her apprentices' faces the way she read her instruments, and whatever sum she arrived at, she kept it behind her eyes.

"Eat," she said again. "Nakamura. The clock."

* * *

The clock room was quietest in the middle of the morning, when Tomas was up under the dome cleaning the transit instrument and Anneli had gone down to the telegraph office to receive the Paris time signal. Petra stayed in the quarters, ostensibly studying star charts, actually napping. Jude stood alone before the great black case with the ring of keys in her hand and the small bright iron key cold against her palm.

The seals were wax, crimson, impressed with the observatory's mark — the crossed circle of the meridian — and they had not been broken in nineteen years. She broke them the way her mother had taught her to break anything that mattered: slowly, with attention, acknowledging that some acts do not undo.

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath released.

The door swung open on darkness and machinery — the great going-train descending through the case like a skeleton of brass, wheels within wheels, the weight lines taut and gleaming, the escapement's anchor rocking with that sound she had loved since childhood, that patient tick-tock that was, her mother used to say, the closest thing humans had ever built to a heartbeat you could trust.

But the clock was not ticking. It had stopped at 2:11 the previous night, and it hung in its own silence like a diver suspended underwater.

Jude worked. She worked the way prayer is supposed to work — with total attention and no expectation of answers. She examined the escapement, the crutch, the suspension spring. She tested the weight lines for fouling. She checked the pallet stones under magnification, found them perfect, found everything perfect, found the entire movement in a condition that should have run another fifty years without complaint — and that perfection itself became the mystery. Clocks failed by wearing. This one hadn't worn. Something else had happened to it.

It was nearly noon when she saw it. High in the case, above the strike train, where the mechanism gave way to the architecture of the case itself — a shadow that was too regular. A seam where no seam should be.

She fetched the lamp and held it close. The panel was fitted so finely that from any normal distance it vanished into the oak grain entirely. Only at this angle, in this light, did its edge declare itself. There was no hinge. There was a catch — a small brass lever, sprung, recessed into wood, designed by someone who very much did not wish it to be found.

Jude stood on the ladder with her fingers on the hidden catch for a long moment. Below her the dead pendulum hung motionless. Through the stone walls she could hear the observatory breathing — wind at the dome, Petra's distant laugh, the world going on below in the valley where the clocks all told the time this machine was supposed to give them.

*Marta first,* she reminded herself. *Whatever you find, Marta first.*

Her thumb pressed the catch.

The panel swung inward, silent on leather hinges, and revealed a cavity perhaps a foot square, lined in green baize gone grey with age. Inside lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and beside it — loose, as if placed there recently, as if someone had meant to come back and had not — a single pressed flower, flat and papery, its petals the color of old blood.

An apple blossom.

Jude reached in with both hands. The oilcloth bundle had weight, texture, the soft resistance of a book. She lifted it down into the lamplight, untied the cord with fingers that had suddenly become someone else's fingers, stiff and far away, and opened the wrapping.

A ledger. Leather, cracked at the spine, swollen with use. On the flyleaf, in a hand she did not know, an inscription:

*For the ones who were counted out. Let this count them in.*

She turned the pages with the care owed to relics. Names. Dates. Columns of entries in a dozen different hands spanning decades — names she half-recognized from gravestones, from shop signs, from the whispered histories of the valley. Beside many of the names, pressed flat between the leaves, flowers. Blossoms. Each entry marked, each life noted, each flower kept.

And then, a third of the way through, a date from the summer twenty-two years ago, and an entry in a handwriting she knew better than her own — a handwriting she had seen on grocery lists and letters and the margins of her childhood books, a handwriting that had stopped existing five years ago and here it was, alive on the page:

*Voss, Aldric. Age 9. Sheltered in the clock room, June–September.*

And beside it, in her mother's hand, smaller, added later, the words that stopped Jude's breath in her throat:

*Witnessed: E. Nakamura. My daughter Jude played with him. She remembers him. She promised.*

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below in the valley, the church bells rang noon — telling a time this clock had not granted them.

Jude sat down on the ladder step with the open ledger in her lap, and the silence of the stopped master clock filled the whole stone room, and she understood, finally, why it had stopped — not how, not yet, but why — because some machines know when the truth has been sleeping inside them, and refuse to tick over it a moment longer.
