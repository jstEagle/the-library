## 2. What the Case Conceals

The master clock did not want to be opened.

Jude understood this the way she understood weather, or bread dough, or the moods of certain customers — not through argument but through the hands. The case door had swollen against its frame as though the wood itself had been told to keep a secret and had taken the instruction literally. She worked a thin blade of boxwood along the seam, easing it, whispering to it in the low coaxing voice she used on frightened mechanisms and frightened animals, and gradually, by degrees no larger than a hair's width, the door gave.

Inside, the smell hit her first: oil gone amber with age, walnut, beeswax, and beneath all of it something older — dust that had settled undisturbed for years, carrying the faint mineral sweetness of the mountain itself. The clock's heart hung before her in the lamplight: the great brass pendulum, the escapement with its jeweled pallets worn to a soft shine, the weight-driven train of gears stacked like the vertebrae of some patient animal that had been standing very still for a very long time.

She should have begun with the escapement. That was procedure — Marta Ilves had drilled procedure into her from her first week, when Jude was sixteen and still flinched at raised voices. *Diagnose before you touch. Touch before you trust your diagnosis. And write everything down.* But her lamp, angled into the case to throw light across the movement, caught instead on something at the back wall of the housing, behind the weight channels, where no mechanism had any business being.

A seam. Too straight.

She knelt on the boards and looked again, telling herself it was a shadow, an old repair, a knot in the oak. Clock cases were built to hold clocks. Everything in them served the train, the escape wheel, the strike. A straight seam meant intention, and intention meant hands, and hands meant someone had stood where she was kneeling, decades ago perhaps, and hidden something inside the body of the thing that kept the town honest.

Her fingers were cold. She noticed this the way one notices a distant bell — abstractly, then all at once.

The panel gave with a sound like a held breath released. It was fitted flush, invisible unless you knew to press the lower left corner while lifting, and it came away into her palms lighter than she expected, a rectangle of oak planed thin as a book cover. Behind it: darkness, and within the darkness, the shape of a book.

* * *

She did not take it out at once.

This was the discipline five years in this place had taught her — the silences that kept people safe were also silences that kept herself safe, and the first rule of any silence was *wait*. Wait to see if the floor above creaked. Wait to hear whether Tomas was still arguing with young Petra about the barometric corrections, their voices drifting down through the observatory's bones like weather through a house. Wait to feel, in her own chest, whether what she was about to do was curiosity or trespass.

It was both. She took it out anyway.

The ledger was bound in leather the color of strong tea, cracked along the spine, its corners rubbed round as river stones. No title. No clasp. When she opened it on her knees, the pages exhaled a scent she knew before she could name it — dried flowers, pressed flat, dozens of them, their pigments faded but stubborn: cornflower blue gone to gray-lilac, yarrow yellow gone to parchment, a sprig of mountain ash whose berries had left small rust-colored ghosts on the page beside them.

And names. Names in ink, in pencil, in the careful slanted hand of someone taught penmanship under a strict schoolmaster and in the hurried scrawl of someone writing by candlelight with cold fingers. Each entry the same architecture: a name, a date, sometimes a second date, and always, tucked into the gutter of the page like a pressed wing, a flower.

*Ilse Brandtner, March 1911 – June 1911. Yarrow.*

*Aro Kask, winter 1919. Cornflower.*

*Two sisters, surnames withheld by request, autumn 1923. Mountain ash.*

She turned the pages slowly, and the room seemed to tilt around her, gently at first, then not gently at all. This was not a record of repairs. This was not a log of the clock's rate, the windings, the adjustments — the kind of book every keeper kept and every apprentice copied out. This was a census of the hidden. Forty years of names going back before her own birth, each one a person who had at some point needed to be nowhere, and had been nowhere here, inside this hilltop room, under the tick of the town's official time.

The observatory did not only tell time, she thought. It told *shelter*. Time and shelter, wound on the same weights.

Her thumb was moving down the entries faster now, and she hated herself for the hurry even as she could not stop it. Because there was a name she was looking for without admitting she was looking for it. There is always a name. She had learned that too — that everyone who opens another family's papers is hunting for their own face in the glass.

* * *

The handwriting stopped her like a hand on her shoulder.

She knew it before she read a single word — the particular forward lean of it, the way the ascenders on *d* and *l* curved back over themselves like grass bending in wind, the little flourish that turned every terminal *e* into something almost smiling. Her mother's hand. She would have known it in the dark, in ash, dissolved in water. She had spent the first year after the funeral writing letters to that hand and burning them, and she still saw it in dreams, always mid-sentence, always about to say the thing she'd never said aloud.

There it was, dated October 1930, in ink gone brown:

*Jude Nakamura, age nine. Arrived with her mother, Hana. To be entered under our care until circumstances allow. She is afraid of the dark; Marta has promised her the lamps stay lit.*

Beside her childhood name, pressed so long it had gone translucent as skin, a single flower she recognized with a jolt that went through her ribs: edelweiss. Not from the garden below. From the high scree slopes you could only reach by a full day's climb, the ones her mother had loved and called *stubborn flowers*, because they grew where nothing was supposed to grow, close to the stone, holding on.

Her mother had climbed somewhere, sometime in the autumn of 1930, and picked this flower, and carried it down, and pressed it here, beside the written proof that Jude herself had once been one of the hidden.

She had known, of course, in the way children know things they are never told — that their arrival in this town had not been simple, that the word *circumstances* sat over her early childhood like snow over a buried fence. She knew they had come up the mountain road at night. She knew her mother had spoken softly for years afterward in a way other mothers did not, listening past doors, counting strangers in the square. She knew the silences. She had been raised inside them, taught to live in them the way fish are raised in water: *some questions are not questions, Jude. Some answers are heavier than the asking.* For five years since her mother's death, those silences had been the walls of her life — sad, perhaps, but solid. Load-bearing.

Now she sat on the cold floorboards of the clock room holding a book that showed her the walls had always been paper.

The safe silences didn't crack all at once. They went the way ice goes on the reservoir in spring — a single report, then a long groaning, then everywhere at once, all the old certainties shifting and grinding and letting in the black water underneath. Because if her mother had written in this book, then her mother had known the book existed. Had known the compartment, the panel, the corner to press. Her mother had knelt where Jude knelt. And in five years of grief, in five years of *tell me nothing and I will ask nothing*, her mother had never once said: *there is a room in the clock where we are all written down.*

Unless she had said it, in her way. Unless every silence had been an instruction Jude hadn't known how to read.

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from under the door. Down the corridor, Tomas's voice rose and fell, arguing about something harmless. Somewhere below, the town went on believing the time, believing the hilltop, believing the ordinary story of an ordinary building. And Jude knelt in the middle of forty years of rescued lives, her mother's handwriting under her thumb, feeling the vertigo of a woman who has just learned the floor of her life was a trapdoor all along.

She read her own entry again. *She is afraid of the dark; Marta has promised her the lamps stay lit.*

Marta.

Marta Ilves, head keeper, who had summoned her up the hill with two dry sentences about a failing escapement. Marta, who watched people the way lighthouse keepers watch water. Marta, whose hand appeared nowhere in these pages — and whose absence from the book was somehow louder than anyone's presence in it, because whoever maintained a book like this for forty years did not need to sign it.

The panel lay beside her on the floor, its inner face bare oak, planed smooth. Someone had made it carefully, lovingly even, fitting it so perfectly that three generations of apprentices — herself included — had cleaned this case, oiled this case, slept within arm's reach of this case, and never found it.

Jude closed the ledger. The pressed edelweiss shifted against her mother's ink, translucent petals over brown letters, the flower lying across the words like a hand laid over a mouth — or like a blessing. She couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding felt suddenly like the truest thing she knew about her family, about this house, about the whole steep town below.

She should put it back. She knew that. Whatever this was, it belonged to the clock's keepers, to whatever covenant had built the panel, and she was — what? An apprentice. A guest. A name in the book, which might make her family and might make her evidence.

But her hands did not move toward the compartment. Her mother's handwriting had done something to her obedience, loosened it at the root, and she sat there in the ticking half-dark with the ledger in her lap like a bird too frightened to fly and too warm to release, and she read on. Entry after entry. Flower after flower. Dates she began to recognize from the town's old troubles — the border closures, the deportations everyone described in the passive voice, *people were moved*, as if the valley had hands. Every hard season, the book thickened. Every quiet year, the pages thinned. Whoever kept it had recorded the town's conscience the way the great dial above recorded the sun: faithfully, without comment, whether anyone looked or not.

Then footsteps on the stair.

Not Tomas — she knew his uneven gait, the left heel he refused to have seen to. These steps were slower, deliberate, spaced like punctuation. A keeper's walk. Marta's walk, the one Jude had heard a thousand times coming down the dormitory corridor at lights-out, and had never once heard approaching a room where she was hiding something.

There was no time. There was only the panel, six feet away, and the ledger open in her lap, and the door beginning to swing inward, and Jude sitting in the exact center of a secret that had apparently been waiting thirty years for her to find it.

She did not move. Some instinct older than fear told her that scrambling now would be worse than being found — that the book in her lap was hers by blood, if it was anyone's — and so she stayed kneeling among the gears, the lamp burning steady, as the door opened and the lamplight from the corridor fell across the floor between them.

Marta Ilves stood in the doorway. She looked at Jude. Then, without hurry, without surprise, without anything so merciful as shock, she looked at the open panel in the clock case — and Jude understood, with a lurch like a weight dropping through a broken train, that the head keeper had known exactly what she would find here, and had perhaps sent for her for no other reason at all.

"You opened it," Marta said. Not a question. Her voice was quiet as snowfall, and just as impossible to read.

"I was repairing the escapement," Jude said, and heard how thin the silence sounded when it was finally spoken aloud. "The panel was—" She stopped. There was no lie in this room that would hold weight. The whole case was built of truths.

"Yes," Marta said. "It usually is." She came into the room and closed the door behind her, and the latch clicked home with the soft finality of a clock being shut. "Before you say anything else, child — before you decide what you've found, or what it makes you, or what you'll do with it — I need you to swear me something."

The ledger lay open in Jude's lap, her mother's ink facing the ceiling, the edelweiss trembling faintly with the pulse in Jude's hands.

"Swear me," Marta said, kneeling down onto the old boards with a stiffness Jude had never seen her show the apprentices, "that until this clock runs true again, you tell no living soul what is in that book."
