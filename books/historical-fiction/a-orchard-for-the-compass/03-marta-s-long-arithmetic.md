## 3. Marta's Long Arithmetic

Marta Ilves had spent forty years learning the weight of a closed door.

She knew them all by their hinges: the observatory's iron door that groaned like a man waking, the door to the clock room that wanted lifting at its latch, the little cedar door inside the clock case itself — the one Jude Nakamura had been staring at for eleven minutes now, though Marta had not counted aloud and would never count aloud, because counting was a habit you learned to keep in your chest in this town.

The girl stood with her sleeves rolled and her hands still raised from the ledger as if she had touched a stove and could not decide whether it had burned her or blessed her. Her mother's hands. That was the cruelty of it. Elke Nakamura had stood in this exact square of lamplight twenty-two years ago with a sleeping boy wrapped in a wool blanket, and she had held her hands exactly so — lifted, trembling, asking permission without words.

"Sit down, child," Marta said. "You'll want to be sitting."

Jude sat on the repair stool, which was too low for her and made her knees rise like a girl's. Good. Let her feel small. The ledger did not care how old you were when it opened itself to you; it made everyone nine years old again, the age when you first understood that adults lied beautifully and constantly, and called it keeping safe.

Marta took the ledger across her own lap, both palms flat on the cover. Leather gone soft as ear skin, corners rounded from forty years of being held just this way. It smelled of tallow and dried flowers and the particular cold-sweetness of paper kept in darkness.

"Do you know what an observatory is for?" she asked.

Jude blinked. "Time. Official time. The master clock is regulated against the transit observations, and everything in the valley — trains, court sessions, birth certificates —"

"Yes. Everyone knows what we give." Marta turned the cover open with a thumb worn smooth for the purpose. "Nobody asks what we keep."

* * *

She began, as she always did in her head, with arithmetic. Forty years was easier to hold if you broke it into numbers.

Forty years. Nine hundred and some entries, though she had stopped counting precisely after the war, because precise counting was the first thing they taught you to fear. Eleven keepers before her, four of them dead now, two vanished, one who drank himself off the cliff road one February — grief, most likely, though the coroner wrote *ice*. Three hundred pressed flowers between the pages, each one matched to a tree planted in the orchard below, each tree a name, each name a life that had passed through this building like light through glass and gone on somewhere else, bent but unbroken.

And every entry had been written in official time. That was the whole genius of it, the thing Marta had inherited like a second skeleton: the observatory did not merely record time, it *was* time. When the keeper wrote *March 3rd, 11:47 p.m., family of four, arrived via the mill road*, that timestamp carried the authority of the state itself. No one audited the audit. Who checks the clock against the clock?

"You found your mother's hand," Marta said. It was not a question. She had watched the girl's face when the pages fell open — watched recognition arrive like weather.

"She wrote my name," Jude said. Her voice came out thin. "Under the date. Under the flower. There's a flower — a plum blossom — and then —"

"Then your name, and the word *sheltered*, and the number of nights. Fourteen." Marta had not needed to look. "Your mother brought you here the winter your father's shop was burned. You were five. You slept in the pendulum room for two weeks while men walked the town with lists. Your mother sat where I'm sitting and did the same arithmetic I do: what is the cost of silence, what is the cost of speech, which one can a child survive."

"I don't remember any of it."

"Good," Marta said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it. "Forgetting is a mercy the young are given and the old must manufacture by hand."

She turned pages slowly, letting the girl see without touching. Names in six different hands across forty years — keepers' hands, each generation adding its entries in the careful script people use when they understand that ink might someday be read by enemies. Dates. Numbers of nights. Little pressed flowers browning at the veins: plum, apple, quince, a sprig of rosemary once because someone had arrived on the night before a wedding and there had been nothing else in the vase.

"This one," Marta said, stopping at a page near the middle. A man's name, a date from the year of the border troubles, a sprig of apple blossom gone the color of old tea. "He came over the pass with frostbite in both feet. We hid him in the coal cellar for nineteen days. Dr. Amrein came twice a week and told his wife he was visiting a mistress, which was the only lie that woman would have forgiven. When he left, we planted an apple tree for him. It still bears. Sour apples. He was a sour man." She smiled despite herself. "He sent a letter for ten years afterward. Always unsigned. Always the same joke about the apples."

Jude's fingers hovered above the page, not touching. "How many?"

Marta let the question sit. Some questions deserved a moment of silence before answering, the way a bell deserves the pause between strikes.

"One hundred and twelve names," she said. "Some are whole families. Some are single nights — a stranger at the door in a storm, fed and gone by morning. Some stayed months. Two were born here, in the east room, during the worst year. Their births are recorded nowhere else in this country. Only here. In official time."

"And the army doesn't know."

"The army suspects everything and proves nothing, which is how armies prefer it. Suspicion is cheaper than proof and lasts longer." Marta closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin lid, the way you close a book you intend to open again tomorrow. "But suspicion is why the clock must run, child. Do you understand me? An observatory with a broken master clock gets *visitors*. Inspectors. Auditors. Men with clipboards who count our stores and read our correspondence and ask why a building that keeps time cannot keep its own machinery alive. While the clock runs true, we are boring. Boring is armor."

"The escapement needs a new pallet arbor," Jude said, grateful — Marta could see the gratitude — for something technical to hold onto. "I can cut one from the stock steel in two days. Three if the temper fights me."

"Take four," Marta said. "Precision is a virtue. Haste is a confession."

* * *

They went down through the building together, because Marta wanted the girl to see it whole, the way you want a bride to walk the length of her own church. Through the instrument room with its brass telescopes shrouded in canvas like sleeping animals. Past the east room, where Marta did not stop but slowed, just slightly, and saw Jude notice the slowing. Down the spiral stair into the orchard side of the hill, where the evening air came through the high windows smelling of mown grass and stone.

The orchard lay below the observatory terrace in long rows, older than Marta's tenure and younger than the building itself. Forty years of pruning showed in the shapes of the trees — trained low and wide, hedged against wind, each row aligned to a bearing that had nothing to do with sun or drainage and everything to do with the fact that Marta's predecessor had been a surveyor before she was a keeper, and had laid out her rows like a map of somewhere no one could arrest you for remembering.

"Count the trees," Marta said.

Jude counted under her breath. Marta watched her lips move. The girl got to ninety-something, faltered, started again, and finally gave up and looked up.

"There aren't enough trees for the names."

"No." Marta folded her hands. "There aren't enough trees for the names, and there are trees for names that aren't in the ledger anymore, and there is one stump by the north wall that matches no living page at all. An orchard is a ledger too, child. It's just one that grows, and drops fruit, and can be cut down by anyone with an axe and a bad afternoon." She heard her own voice go hard and made herself soften it. "We plant one for every person. That was the covenant. Paper burns; wood remembers differently. If ever the ledger were seized, the orchard would remain — a testimony no clerk could carry downstairs in a box."

"But a stump can't testify."

"A stump testifies to whoever knows how to read it." Marta looked at the north wall, at the pale circle of the old cut, already softening back into the grass. "That one I'll tell you about another day. Not today. Today has carried enough."

It was the truth, and it was also a kindness, and Marta had learned that the two were usually the same object viewed from different ends.

* * *

Back in the clock room, the lamplight had gone amber and the great pendulum swung its patient arc, and Marta did the thing she had done three times before in forty years and had hoped never to do again: she put the ledger into a young person's hands and waited for them to understand what they were holding.

It was heavier than paper. Jude felt it — Marta watched her feel it, watched her forearms adjust, watched her set it carefully on the workbench as though setting down a sleeping animal.

"Marta," she said. "Why show me? You could have taken it out of the case and told me the compartment was empty. You could have told me anything. I'm the newest here. Tomas has been with you six years."

"Tomas has been with me six years, and Tomas is kind and clever and frightened in the way young men are frightened, which is loudly." Marta pulled the second stool close and sat, knee to knee with the girl, close enough to smell the machine oil on her hands and, beneath it, the faint camphor of the Nakamura house, which had soaked into that family's clothing the way grief soaks into a coat. "I am showing you because your mother's handwriting is in these pages. Because a ledger is not a secret if no one left alive remembers what's in it — it's just trash. Secrets need two people: one to hold the knowledge and one to be worth holding it for." She paused. "And because I am sixty-three years old, and the next pair of hands to open this case should belong to someone who will still be opening it when I am not here to explain."

Jude's throat moved. She didn't cry. Marta noted that with approval and a small private sorrow — the girls who didn't cry had usually learned it early, and never for good reasons.

"Now swear," Marta said.

"Swear what?"

"That you'll tell no one. Not the other apprentices, not the priest, not a diary, not a lover, not your own reflection. Not until the clock runs again. Not one word outside this room until that escapement is cutting true time and this building is boring again." Marta held up a finger. "And hear the terms before you agree, because I am not offering you a gift. I am handing you a debt with interest accruing. Every day you know and say nothing, the knowing gets heavier. It will change the taste of bread. It will make you lie fluently to people you love. One day you'll catch yourself performing innocence so well that you frighten yourself, and you'll come to me at midnight and ask if it was worth it, and I will tell you yes, and you will hate me a little, and we will both go on. That is what I am giving you. Say yes anyway or leave the ledger on the bench and I'll put it back in the dark and we'll speak only of gears until one of us dies."

The pendulum ticked through eleven full swings. Marta counted them in her chest, as always.

Jude looked at the ledger. At the compartment in the clock case, its cedar walls worn glossy by decades of careful hands. At the window, where the valley lights were coming on one by one, each one a household that owed its shape — maybe its existence — to the quiet arithmetic done in this room since before she was born.

"My mother swore this oath too," Jude said. "Didn't she."

"She did. On this stool. In this light."

"Did she ever regret it?"

Marta considered lying, decided the hour was past for it, and chose instead the harder mercy: "Once. The night she signed your name and realized she'd written it in a book that could hang her. She regretted it for about an hour. Then she asked me for a pen and went back and added your birth date, because a record should be complete." Marta smiled, and the smile hurt her face, which is how she knew it was real. "That was your mother. Accuracy even at the gallows' edge. I see her hands in yours every time you touch a gear."

Jude bowed her head. When she spoke, her voice had steadied into something Marta recognized from mirrors: the voice of a person accepting weight.

"I swear. Until the clock runs, no one hears it from me."

"Not even hints."

"Not even hints."

"Not even silences that point."

Jude looked up sharply at that, and Marta held her gaze and let her work it through — the way a pointed silence could hang a man faster than a spoken word, the way the town had its own grammar of what went unsaid, and a novice's silence could betray more than a traitor's tongue.

"Not even those," Jude said. "I've had practice."

"I know," Marta said softly. "That's partly why I chose you."

* * *

Later — much later, after the lamps were trimmed and Jude had gone down the hill with the ledger's weight visibly riding her shoulders like a yoke — Marta climbed the iron stair to the observation platform alone, as she did most nights, and stood under the open shutter with the cold pouring in like water.

Below, the town slept its arranged sleep. Above, the stars kept their own indifferent time, and somewhere between the two ran the thin wire Marta had walked for forty years: the wire between the truth and the telling of it.

She thought of the ledger's last section. She thought of the page that had been there in spring and would not be there forever, the entry she herself had written in a hand steadier than her heart — a boy's name, a summer's dates, a sprig of cherry plum, the word *promised* written small beside it because Elke had made her write it, made her bind the observatory to a vow on behalf of a frightened child who had no idea what he was costing anyone.

*If he ever comes back,* Elke had said, gripping Marta's wrist hard enough to bruise, *the book speaks for him. Swear it.*

*I swear it.*

Forty years of arithmetic, and Marta had learned this much: the sums always grew larger than you planned, and the carrying column — the part you held in reserve, the part that changed everything if it surfaced — was always the last figure entered, and it was always the one that kept you awake.

She pulled the shutter closed against the cold and went down to check the pendulum, though the pendulum needed nothing, because checking was the prayer she knew.

In the clock room, the case stood open still, the cedar compartment breathing its flower-and-tallow smell into the dark, waiting — as it had waited through wars and audits and the deaths of four keepers — for whatever the morning would demand of them all.

Two days, Jude had said. Three, if the temper fought her.

Four, Marta had told her.

She found, standing alone in the ticking dark, that she was praying for the steel to fight.
