## 4. A Boy Called Aldric

The summer the boy came up the mountain, the observatory smelled of hot brass and cut hay, and Jude was eleven years old and knew everything about time except how to make it pass.

She remembered it afterward in fragments, the way a clock remembers its maker: not whole, but in parts that fit together only when you held them to the light. The smell of the boy's fear — she had not known then that fear had a smell, sour and mineral, like water standing in iron. The sound of his breathing behind the pendulum case, quick and shallow as a bird's. Her mother's hand pressing down on her shoulder, hard enough to leave a bruise shaped like five small moons, and the word her mother said without moving her lips at all: *nothing*.

*Nothing*, meaning: you have seen nothing. *Nothing*, meaning: if anyone asks, this room has always been exactly what it appears to be.

Jude had learned her silences early, but that was the summer they stopped being lessons and became load-bearing walls.

* * *

It began, as most things at the observatory did, with the clock.

The master clock stood in the heart of the building like a patient animal, taller than her father, its case of black walnut polished by forty years of hands. It governed everything — the town bells below, the railway schedule, the observatory's own telescopes and their hungry, precise appetite for exact seconds. When it faltered, the whole valley faltered with it. Marta Ilves used to say the clock was the town's conscience: it did not care who you were, it only cared whether you were true.

That June it had begun to lose time. Not much — four seconds a day, then seven, then eleven — but Marta treated every lost second as a wound. She summoned Jude's mother, who kept the town's clocks the way a priest keeps souls, and her mother brought Jude along because Jude's hands were small enough to reach the places grown hands could not.

So Jude was lying on her back inside the clock case, cheek against cool walnut, when she heard the knock.

Not a knock, really. A scratching. Three fingernails dragged once down the outer door, soft as a moth against glass. Her mother went still above her, a wrench suspended mid-air, and Jude watched through the gap between two gears as her mother set the wrench down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown.

"Stay where you are," her mother said, in the voice she used for customers, pleasant and empty as a cleared table.

Jude stayed. She stayed for a long time. She counted the tick of the escapement to keep herself company — she could count to a thousand in perfect rhythm back then, a trick that made the apprentices call her *the metronome* — and somewhere around six hundred she heard low voices in the entry hall, and around nine hundred she heard boots on the stairs, and at a thousand exactly the door opened and her mother knelt down beside the open case and said, very quietly:

"There is a boy in the pendulum room. You will be kind to him. That is all I will say, and you will ask nothing else."

The pendulum room lay behind the clock chamber, a narrow stone vault where the great rod swung free of dust and draught. Jude slid out of the case, wiped her hands on her apron because her mother's eyes flicked to them, and followed.

He was smaller than she expected. Later she would understand that he was probably fourteen or fifteen, half-starved and folded into himself to seem smaller still, but at eleven everyone older than ten seemed made of different timber entirely. He sat on the floor with his back against the stone, knees drawn up, wearing a coat too heavy for June and shoes gone soft at the soles. His hair was pale as winter wheat and his eyes were the color of water over slate, and he looked at her the way a wild thing looks — deciding, all at once, whether she was danger or weather.

"This is my daughter," her mother said. "Jude."

The boy said nothing.

"She won't tell," her mother said, and it was not a boast; it was an instruction, delivered to both of them at once.

Jude looked at the boy. The boy looked at Jude. Somewhere above them both the master clock sent its long silver shadow sweeping across the floor, slow as the hand of God, and Jude heard herself say the first true thing she ever said to him:

"You're standing in my favorite place."

His eyes flicked up, startled. "I'm sitting," he said, in accented but careful speech.

"That's why there's room for me."

And something in his face loosened — not a smile, nothing so generous, but a crack in the wall through which a person might eventually be reached. Her mother saw it, nodded once, and left them alone with the pendulum and the dark.

* * *

His name, he said that evening over bread and goat cheese in the keeper's kitchen, was Aldric. Just Aldric, for three days. The surname came later, whispered like contraband, when trust had put down roots: Voss.

He was from over the eastern passes, from a country whose name changed twice in Jude's childhood depending on which army had last drawn the map. His father had printed things — pamphlets, newspapers, letters — and printing things had become, in the space of one bad autumn, a way to disappear. Aldric's father had put him on a cart, then a train, then a truck, then the feet of a smuggler who took payment in whatever the family had left, and the smuggler had walked him over the mountains by night until they came to the town under the observatory, where the smuggler had knocked on a certain door and spoken a certain sentence and been fed and paid and sent away.

"The man who owns the bakery," Aldric said. "He knew where to send me. He said — " Aldric hesitated, testing the words before trusting them to the air — "he said there are people here who have done this before. For a long time. He said the mountain remembers."

Jude's mother, kneading bread at the far bench, did not look up. But her hands paused, one full beat, and resumed. Jude understood even then that the pause was the whole answer.

"Why here?" Jude asked Aldric. "Why the clock?"

"Because no one watches a clock," Aldric said. Then, catching himself: "I mean — everyone watches a clock. But nobody looks at it. Nobody wonders what's inside. It just tells the truth and everyone believes it." He turned his water-colored eyes on her. "Your mother says the best hiding place in the world is inside a thing everyone trusts."

Jude thought about that for the rest of her life.

* * *

He stayed nineteen days. She knew the number precisely because she counted them, and she counted them because each one felt borrowed, and borrowed things demand accounting.

By day he was invisible — folded into the pendulum room, or the archive loft, or on the brightest afternoons, when the trails filled with hikers, up among the telescope domes where no civilian had business climbing. He learned to move the way Marta Ilves moved, unhurried and unremarkable, a body that drew no second glance. By night he emerged, and those were the hours Jude lived for.

He taught her constellations from the catwalk, his breath fogging in air too cold for July, his finger tracing lines she never quite saw until years later, alone, she looked up and found them waiting. He taught her three words of his mother's language and made her swear never to use them where windows could hear. She taught him the escapement — how the wheel wants only to spin free and fall forever, and how the pallet fork catches it, holds it, releases it one tooth at a time, so that falling becomes measuring.

"It's cruel, isn't it," he said, watching her small screwdriver ease the fork into place. "To hold something back like that."

"It's kind," Jude said. "Falling isn't going anywhere. Measured, it becomes time. Time is the only thing we get to keep."

Aldric looked at her for a long moment, and she felt the weight of being seen by someone who had recently lost everything worth seeing him by.

"You're strange," he said finally, and it sounded like a medal being pinned on.

* * *

On the twelfth day the soldiers came to the town.

Not for him — or not knowingly for him. A customs patrol, her mother called it later, routine, bored men checking freight manifests and looking at girls. But word traveled uphill faster than lorries, and Marta Ilves appeared in the kitchen doorway with her face arranged into weatherproof calm, and Jude's mother said one word — *down* — and Aldric was gone, folded into the space beneath the pantry stairs where the preserves stood in ranks like a small glass army, and Jude was given a basket and told to sit on the cellar door and shell peas.

The soldiers walked up the observatory road because the road was there and walking it cost nothing. Two of them. One asked Marta, in the flat courtesy of authority, whether she had seen anyone passing through. Marta said that she saw stars and clouds and occasionally a marmot with poor manners, and that people were rather beneath the observatory's notice. The soldier laughed the way men laugh when they cannot tell if they have been insulted. They drank the water Marta offered them — offered, Jude understood even then, because refusal requires a reason and hospitality permits none — and they left, and the peas were shelled, and Aldric came up out of the dark under the stairs gray-faced with his hands shaking so badly he could not hold the cup Marta gave him.

Jude held it for him. Their fingers touched around the clay. Neither of them mentioned it, then or ever, and she carried the warmth of it for nineteen days and then for thirty years.

That night she could not sleep. She climbed the inner stair past the sleeping apprentices' rooms, past her mother's door with its thread of candlelight beneath, up into the clock chamber, because the ticking was the only lullaby she trusted. And Aldric was already there, sitting on the floor with his back against the case, knees drawn up, listening.

"I count the ticks," he said, without turning. "When I can't sleep. Did you know it's never the same? Even a perfect clock. There's always a little unevenness. The maker's hand in it."

"Then it's not perfect."

"It's better than perfect," Aldric said. "Perfect doesn't prove anybody was here."

They sat a while in the dark and the enormous patience of the machine. Then Aldric said, in a voice scraped thin: "If they find me. If someday they come with papers and questions and they take me — is there anything — "

He didn't finish. Jude, eleven years old, exhausted, terrified in the secondhand way children are terrified for the people they love, heard the unfinished question land in the dark between them like a dropped coin.

She stood up. She went to the clock case — she knew its secrets the way she knew her own teeth, having spent that June elbow-deep in its works — and she pressed the hidden catch her mother had shown her in confidence, the one beneath the strike-train bracket, and the false back breathed open an inch, and inside, in the spill of starlight through the east window, she saw the edge of a leather ledger, old and fat and soft-cornered, tied with a string gone gray from handling.

She had never been allowed to touch it. She had never been told what it was. But her mother had once, drunk on plum brandy and grief for a friend buried without a name, murmured against the top of Jude's head: *everything we are is in the clock.*

"If they ever come for you," Jude said, "if they ever say you were never here, that you're a lie, that nobody sheltered you — this book will say otherwise. This book will say your name and the day you came and the day you left. My mother writes in it. She'll write yours."

Aldric stared at her. In the dark his eyes had no color at all, only shine.

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm going to ask her to," Jude said. "Tomorrow. And she'll do it, because she does everything the book needs done. I've watched her." She pushed the false panel closed until it clicked, soft as a held breath released. "You're in there now. Even before the ink. Being in there starts tonight."

Aldric Voss, fourteen years old and eighty miles and a whole war away from home, put his face in his hands and cried without any sound at all, which Jude understood was a skill like the escapement — holding back, holding back, releasing only measured amounts — and she sat beside him on the cold flagstones with her shoulder just barely touching his coat, keeping time with him, until the tower bell rang two and they crept down to their separate beds.

Her mother wrote the page the next morning. Jude watched her do it: the date, the name *Aldric Voss*, the route over the passes, the number of days, and beside it — Jude leaned close and saw — a small pressed flower, blue as the hour before dawn, laid flat into the paper like a seal.

"For every tree we plant," her mother said, catching her looking. She did not explain further. Marta Ilves explained nothing that did not need explaining. But she touched the flower once with one fingertip, the way you'd touch a child's head, and Jude understood that the ledger was not a record.

It was a garden.

* * *

He left on the twentieth morning, before the sun cleared the ridge, in a fishmonger's cart bound for the coast city and a shipping clerk there who owed the mountain a favor older than any war. Jude was not supposed to be awake. She was awake anyway, standing at the orchard fence in the gray light with dew soaking through her socks, and Aldric came down the path with his ruined shoes and his father's coat and stopped on the other side of the fence from her, because the fence was easier than the goodbye.

"They'll write your page," she said. "I'll see to it myself if I have to."

"I know," Aldric said. And then, awkwardly, formally, like a boy reciting something learned from a book of old customs: "If I ever need it — the book — you'll speak for me?"

"I'll speak for you," Jude said. "Even if the book burns. I remember things."

Something crossed his face then that she was too young to read and would spend decades learning to read: gratitude and dread in equal measure, the expression of a person handed both a shield and a prophecy. He put his hand through the fence slats — the only touch either of them ever initiated — and gripped hers once, hard, dry, brief.

"Keep the clock true," he said. "Whatever else happens. Keep the clock true."

Then he was gone down the switchback road, smaller and smaller, until the trees took him, and the mountain closed over the place where he had been the way water closes over a stone.

Jude stood at the fence until her mother came to fetch her in for breakfast, and neither of them spoke of it, and that silence too was a kind of ledger entry.

* * *

Thirty years later — or five, depending on which life you counted — Jude Nakamura stood again in the pendulum room with the ledger's leather warm under her hands and her own childhood handwriting staring up at her from the page her mother had written, and the pressed blue flower had faded almost to gray, the way all evidence fades, the way promises fade, unless someone keeps re-inking them.

She thought: he is somewhere past the border now, older than our mother was then, and he thinks of this room, or he doesn't, and either way the page exists, and the tree — she did not yet know about the tree, about the stump in the orchard's third row, about the things the seasons do to testimony —

She thought: the promise holds. Promises are the only clocks that never need winding.

And downstairs, faint through the stone floors, she heard the first engine of the first army lorry turning off the valley road onto the observatory track, gravel popping under tires like slow applause, and Marta Ilves' voice calling her name from the foot of the stair — not afraid, worse than afraid, *careful* — and Jude slid the ledger back into its hiding place and pressed the panel home and started down toward the sound of the world arriving.
