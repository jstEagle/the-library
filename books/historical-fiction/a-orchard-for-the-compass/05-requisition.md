## 5. Requisition

The lorries came on a Tuesday, which felt like a betrayal in itself. Tuesdays were for winding the sidereal clock and sweeping the gallery; they were not a day for engines.

Jude heard them before she saw them — a low grinding hum climbing the valley road, the sound of something heavy being dragged uphill against its will. She was at her bench on the third floor of the observatory, fitting a new escape pallet into the regulator's deadbeat escapement, and she stopped, tweezers hovering over the jewel, because the sound did not belong to anything that had ever come up this road before. The mountain road took carts and mules and once, memorably, a circus wagon whose axle screamed the whole way to the summit. It did not take lorries.

She went to the east window and looked down through the wavy old glass.

Three of them, olive-grey, crawling up the switchbacks with the patient menace of beetles, their canvas backs lashed down, their hoods crowded with helmets. Behind them, far below, the town lay in its morning haze — chimneys just lit, bread smoke rising, the market square a scatter of awnings — all of it small enough to cover with a thumb. She thought, absurdly, of covering it. Of pressing her thumb against the glass until the lorries could not reach anyone.

Behind her, the master clock ticked on, indifferent and magnificent, keeping the region's time as it had for eighty years: every train schedule, every court summons, every birth certificate and death notice stamped by the town clerk bore seconds that had fallen out of this room, down through the telegraph line, into the world below.

"Marta," she said, though Marta was two floors down. The word simply left her, the way a warning leaves you before you have decided anything.

* * *

They assembled on the observatory steps without anyone calling them there — the way a household gathers when a strange dog comes up the lane. Marta Ilves stood at the top of the stone stairs in her grey work coat, hands folded, silver hair pinned back so tightly it drew faint lines at her temples, as though even her hair refused to be disarranged by what was coming. Tomas stood one step below her, sleeves rolled, a smear of brass polish still bright on his left forearm. Anneli hovered near the door with a rag in her hand she seemed to have forgotten she was holding. Jude found herself beside Tomas, close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

The lorries halted in the gravel forecourt with a hiss of brakes and a shudder Jude felt through the soles of her shoes. Engines died. For a moment there was only ticking — the great clock audible even out here, through stone, through morning air, patient as tide — and then the soldiers began to climb down.

There were perhaps twenty of them. They were young. That was the first thing that lodged in her and would not come loose: how young they were, chins soft above their collars, one of them squinting around at the mountains with an expression dangerously close to wonder before he remembered himself and fixed his face into blankness. They arranged themselves without being told, two files flanking the stairs, and an officer came forward.

He was not what she expected either. She had expected the captain from the posters — jaw, glare, gloved fist. This man was older, slight, with spectacles and a leather document case held under his arm the way a schoolmaster holds a roll book. He climbed three steps and stopped, leaving a step of distance between himself and Marta, and Jude understood that the distance was calculated. Courtesy, deployed like calipers.

"Madam Keeper," he said. "Officer Brandt. Records Audit Division." He produced papers, unfolded them with small precise motions, and read aloud in a voice pitched exactly for clarity and nothing else — no warmth, no threat, only the terrible neutrality of printed language given breath:

"By order of the Regional Command, effective this hour, all civic records of this municipality are placed under requisition for audit. Census registers. Residence permits. Naturalization files. Correspondence archives. Telegraph logs." A pause, the paper lowered half an inch. "And the official time records of the Observatory."

Beside her, Tomas made a sound low in his throat, quickly swallowed.

"The observatory is a scientific institution," Marta said. Her voice was level. She had the particular stillness of people who had learned that stillness was armor. "Its instruments are calibrated to tolerances measured in thousandths of a second. Moving them—"

"Instruments remain," Brandt said. "Records travel. You may keep your telescopes, madam. It is your paper we've come for."

"And the clock?" Jude heard herself ask.

It was out before she could weigh it, and Brandt turned to her then, and she felt the full weight of his attention settle on her like a hand on the back of the neck. His eyes went to her face first, then — briefly, deliberately — to her name as it must appear in whatever list he carried behind those spectacles. Nakamura. He did not say it aloud. He didn't need to. The whole town knew which names the audit meant when it said *foreign elements*, and hers sat at the top of every list anyone had drawn since the war began sharpening itself across the border last autumn.

"You are?"

"The apprentice," Marta said, stepping fractionally sideways so that she stood between them, a woman of sixty making herself a wall four inches wide. "Jude Nakamura. She maintains the master clock. Which is a machine, Officer, not a record."

"A machine that generates records," Brandt said mildly. "Every second it strikes is entered somewhere, is it not? Every correction, every adjustment. Timestamps are testimony, madam Keeper. Testimony requires auditing like everything else." He refolded his paper along its original creases, exact to the millimeter, and tucked it away. "You will provide access to the archive rooms, the clerks' quarters, and the telegraph office. My men will begin inventory this afternoon. Nothing will be damaged. I give you my word as an officer."

He said it the way a man gives his word about weather.

* * *

They started at the bottom of the hill, in the town, and worked upward, and from the gallery windows the apprentices watched it happen the way sailors watch a storm approach across open water — unable to intervene, able only to measure its speed.

First the municipal building. Soldiers went in empty-handed and came out carrying boxes, grey archive boxes with brass corner fittings, stacked and roped and ferried to the lorries in pairs. Jude knew those boxes. She had helped carry a dozen of them up this very hill two winters ago when the clerk's cellar flooded — boxes of residence permits going back fifty years, each card bearing a photograph, a signature, a date of entry into the valley. Somewhere in those stacks was the card that had let her mother stay. Somewhere in those stacks was the card that had let *her* stay, age nine, listed under her mother's name with a clerk's hurried annotation that had always struck Jude as almost tender: *dependent child, admitted.*

Now those cards were evidence. That was the word Brandt kept using, softly, to the town clerk, visible through the window below: *evidence.* Evidence of what, he never finished saying. Foreign elements. Irregularities. Persons of interest. Words that did not accuse but prepared accusation, the way a chisel prepares a joint.

By mid-afternoon the lorries were heavy-bellied, riding low on their springs, and the soldiers reached the parish house, and then the school, and then — Jude saw Anneli grip the sill — the small yellow building on Weaver's Lane where Frau Hessler taught piano and, everyone knew, kept letters for half the valley's families, mailed to addresses abroad that people preferred not to write on their own envelopes.

"They can't take private correspondence," Tomas said. "There are laws. There are—"

"Tomas." Marta's voice was quiet. "Watch."

He watched. And so they all watched Frau Hessler come out onto her own doorstep with her hands raised, palms outward, not in surrender but in the universal gesture of a woman saying *I am old and this is my home,* and watch the young soldier with the wondering chin look away from her, ashamed, while another went past her into her rooms.

"She keeps nothing," Anneli whispered. "Everyone knows she burns everything every month. Everyone knows."

"Frau Hessler knows it," Marta said. "The army does not know it. So they will take ash and feel victorious, and she will stand on her step tomorrow exactly as today." Marta's eyes stayed on the street below, and her voice did not change at all, and Jude understood suddenly that she was not describing Frau Hessler. She was teaching. She was standing at the window giving a lesson in survival, in the grammar of occupation, and the lesson was: *let them take ash. Learn what to burn. Learn what cannot be burned.*

Jude thought of the ledger in the clock case. Forty years of names, dates, pressed flowers. Paper that could not be allowed to become ash and could not be allowed to become evidence, and that now sat thirty feet below her feet inside a housing that three soldiers were at this very moment walking toward, because Brandt had said *the official time records of the Observatory* and meant, sooner or later, everything this building contained.

Her mouth went dry.

* * *

They came up the hill at four o'clock.

Marta met them at the door with the observatory's visitor register open on a lectern, and she made them sign it — all of them, officer and soldiers alike, name, unit, purpose — invoking some clause from the observatory's founding charter, and Brandt signed with a fountain pen in a small neat hand, and if he recognized the maneuver for what it was (a keeper establishing, in ink, that this was a visitation upon an institution and not yet possession of it) he gave no sign. Perhaps he filed it away. Jude had the growing sense that Officer Brandt filed everything away, including her.

Then the soldiers spread through the building, and the apprentices learned what helplessness weighed.

It weighed exactly as much as an archive box. Jude discovered this by lifting them. Because there was no rule she could invoke and no lie she dared tell and no door she could bar, she did the only thing left: she inserted herself into the work, volunteering to carry, to stack, to point out which cabinets held what, and so she spent the afternoon handling her own family's history like a porter, box after box, feeling through rope and cardboard the mass of other people's saved lives.

The telegraph logs went first. Forty years of daily transmissions — *Observatory standard time, 12:00:00, error nil* — the dry heartbeat of the valley, seized in eleven boxes. Then the meteorological ledgers, taken because they contained station notes and station notes contained signatures. Then the clerk's duplicate registers, hauled up the hill from town on a handcart by a sweating private who apologized twice for bumping the stair rail, as if the rail were the thing at risk.

Anneli cried in the linen closet, silently, and came out with red eyes and said it was dust.

Tomas argued. That was worse somehow than the crying — watching him follow Brandt from room to room citing statutes, permits, the charter, the 1911 accord on scientific neutrality, his voice cracking upward with each citation, and Brandt listening with genuine attention, nodding, saying *yes, quite right, I'll note your objection,* and noting it, and taking the boxes anyway. Tomas had believed the law was a wall. Jude watched him learn, citation by citation, that it was a ledger too — and that someone else held the pen.

At dusk Brandt came to the clock room.

Jude was there, ostensibly oiling the great train, and she did not stop oiling when he entered, because stopping would mean something and continuing meant she belonged here, that she was furniture, that she was safe. The master clock towered between them, its pendulum swinging in long grave arcs, its case of black walnut rising to the ceiling like a monument.

"So this is the father of us all," Brandt said, tilting his head at it. "Every clock in the valley obeys this one."

"Every clock consults it," Jude said. "Obedience is not how machines work."

Something flickered behind the spectacles — not displeasure. Interest. "You repair it?"

"I keep its rate. It has drifted four seconds fast since spring. I'm correcting it gradually, so the town doesn't feel the change."

"Gradually," Brandt repeated, tasting the word. He walked slowly around the case, trailing one finger near — not touching — the walnut paneling, and Jude's heart performed a slow cold revolution, because his finger passed within a hand's width of the hidden seam, of the compartment, of forty years of pressed flowers and her mother's handwriting. "A responsible keeper corrects gradually. Doesn't alarm anyone. Lets the truth arrive gently." He turned. "You'd be surprised how often, in my work, I meet men who adjust clocks the other way, Fräulein Nakamura. Who make time say what they need it to have said."

"I wouldn't know anything about that, sir."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you wouldn't."

He wished her good evening. At the door he paused. "We return Thursday for the observatory's internal registers. Maintenance books, staff records, correspondence. Have them accessible." A small bow, precisely measured, and he was gone, his boots ringing down the iron stair.

Jude stood alone in the clock room with the pendulum sighing beside her and her hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the cold walnut until they stilled.

Thursday. Two days.

* * *

That night none of them slept well, and all of them ended up in the kitchen, which was how grief always traveled in that house — downward, toward warmth, toward the stove Marta never let go cold. Nobody spoke for a long time. The kettle ticked as it heated, a small domestic clockwork among giants.

Below them, in the yard, the lorries sat loaded under guard, their canvas covers dark shapes against the gravel, waiting for morning and the road down. Everything the valley had written about itself was in those beds. Tomorrow it would be driven away to be read by strangers looking for crimes.

"We should have—" Anneli began.

"We should have what?" Tomas asked bitterly. "Hidden it? Fought? With what, Anneli — the charter?"

"With ash," Marta said.

They all looked at her. She sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and firelight moved on her face, and for a moment she looked older than Jude had ever seen her, and also harder, the way wood hardens.

"Paper burns faster than soldiers climb stairs," Marta said. "Remember that. Not tonight — tonight we do nothing, tonight we are only tired keepers who cooperated fully. But remember it." She set the cup down. "They think they came to take our memory away. Let them carry the boxes. Memory was never in the boxes."

Jude looked at her hands. In them, invisibly, she already held the weight of what she'd have to do before Thursday: open that seam, decide, move what could not burn and must not be found. Her mother's handwriting. Her own childhood name. The page that would someday, if a frightened boy named Aldric Voss ever came home asking, have to speak for him.

Helplessness sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. But stones, she thought, can be built with.

"Thursday," she said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "Before Thursday."

Marta looked at her across the kitchen, and in the keeper's eyes Jude saw that she had been understood — and that the burden, which was not a gift, had just changed hands.

Above them, through the floor, the master clock counted out the midnight hour, steady as ever, keeping true time for a town that might soon have none.
