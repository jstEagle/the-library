## 6. The Missing Pages

Tomas Reiner had counted things his whole life. His father, a customs clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like a filing drawer, had taught him that a man who could count could survive any audit — of goods, of grief, of guilt. *Count what remains,* his father said, *and you will always know what was taken.* It was the only inheritance Tomas had ever valued, and on the third morning after the lorries came down from the pass, he stood in the observatory's record room with a pencil behind his ear and an inventory sheet trembling slightly in his cold fingers, counting what remained.

The soldiers had been gone two days. Their boot-prints still ghosted the stairwells in gray flour-fine dust, and the smell of them — leather, machine oil, cheap tobacco — clung to the banisters like a guest who would not leave. They had carried forty-one archive boxes down the hill, each one stenciled with the black eagle of the audit office, each one heavier than the last. Tomas had watched from the window of the apprentices' room, his breath fogging the glass, and had done the arithmetic no one asked him for: at four boxes per trip and six men per detail, the removal had taken three hours and eleven minutes. He knew because he had timed it against the observatory's wall chronometer, which was itself now suspect, which was itself now evidence.

*Everything is evidence now.* The thought sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"Start with the shelf ledgers," Marta Ilves had instructed that morning, her voice level in the way that ice is level — smooth, load-bearing, hiding depth. "Then the correspondence files. Then the instrument logs. Jude will take the master clock room; you take everything else." She had paused at the door, her broad back filling its frame the way it filled every frame, and added, without turning: "Count honestly, Tomas. Whatever you find."

He had not understood then why she said it that way. He understood by noon.

* * *

The record room occupied the observatory's eastern ground floor, a long chamber with windows fogged by altitude and age. Shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks, and the light came through sideways and amber, thick with motes that turned slowly like planets in their courses. It was, Tomas had always thought, the warmest room in the building — not in temperature but in temperament. Other rooms held instruments, mechanisms, the cold precision of brass and glass. This room held people. Forty years of names in Marta's square handwriting, in old Keeper Amsel's spidery one, in a dozen apprentice hands that had come and gone like weather. Deliveries logged, visitors recorded, coal accounted for, bread baked, hours kept. The town's official time lived here, and so, folded invisibly into the margins of civic diligence, did the town's unofficial mercies.

Tomas worked methodically, as his father had trained him. Shelf by shelf. Volume by volume. He wrote accession numbers in his small even hand, noted condition, noted absence. The army's officers had been thorough but not loving in their seizure; they had taken whole runs of files, entire years of correspondence, the visitor registers from 1919 through 1931. But they had missed things, too — missed the bundle of seed catalogues behind the stove pipe, missed the false-backed drawer where Marta kept the observatory's founding charter, missed, apparently, the fact that some volumes had gaps in their numbering that predated the audit by decades. Gaps were normal. Paper rotted, pages tore, apprentices made mistakes. Tomas noted the gaps without alarm and moved on.

It was past two when he carried the final armload into the center table: the loose folios and unbound papers that had lived in the clock room's cabinet, the ones Marta had called *the working file* — documents too current or too delicate for the shelves. Among them, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, he found the ledger.

He recognized it before he unwrapped it, and his hands went still.

He had seen it exactly once. Five years ago, his first winter at the observatory, he had come up to the clock room after midnight with tea for Marta and found her at the workbench with this same oilcloth open, this same book beneath the lamp, and she had looked up at him with an expression he had spent five years trying to forget — not anger, not fear, something older than both, the look of a woman measuring whether a thing could be unseen. She had closed the book. She had said, *Knock next time, Tomas.* And he had knocked, always, ever after, and never seen it again, and had told himself the shape under the oilcloth was account books, was star charts, was nothing.

Now it lay under his hands in the amber light, and the oilcloth was open, and the book was open to its end.

The ledger was bound in leather gone soft and dark as chestnut, its spine cracked into segments like the bark of an old tree. The pages were heavy stock, cream-colored, ruled faintly in blue. Each entry followed the same liturgy: a name, a date range, a place of origin written small, and beside most entries — pressed flat between the leaves like specimens — a flower or leaf, browned to sepia ghosts of themselves. Tomas turned the pages with the care owed to relics. *Elena Marchetti, 1901–1903, Genoa.* A sprig of almond blossom. *Yosef Brandeis, 1912–1914, Kraków.* A maple leaf. *Anneliese Vogel and child, 1926–1927.* A pressed violet, its color almost miraculously survived. Names in three hands — Amsel's spider-scrawl, Marta's square print, and a third, flowing and slanted, that appeared in the earliest entries and then stopped, and that Tomas did not recognize but somehow felt he should.

He reached the final numbered section — entries 214 through 231 — and the paper changed.

Not in quality. In edges. The remaining stubs were rougher there, torn rather than cut, and the tear-lines were clean, deliberate, vertical, following the binding gutter with a precision that no accident produces. Someone had opened the ledger to its last gathering of signatures and removed six leaves — twelve pages — pulling each free along the sewn spine with a knife or a fingernail or a patience that made Tomas's skin crawl. The stubs remained, and on the stubs, ghosting through from the excised pages' reverse sides, faint mirror-image impressions of ink: the tails of letters, the bottom curves of numbers, the shadow of a date. Entry 213 ended cleanly. Entry 232 began cleanly. Between them, nothing. A silence in the record shaped exactly like a person.

Tomas sat down without meaning to. The chair scraped, and in the fogged-window hush the sound seemed enormous.

*Count what remains,* his father said, *and you will know what was taken.*

He counted. Twelve pages. Six entries, if the pattern held — six names, six dates, six flowers, six lives filed away in the observatory's secret mercy. And he knew — the knowledge arriving whole, the way dread arrives, without process — whose name had headed the first torn page. Because entry 232, the first surviving entry of the final section, was dated June 1933, and everyone at the observatory knew what the summer of 1932 had sheltered, even those who had never been told. Even Tomas, who had only been told in pieces, in the way this house told all its truths: obliquely, in silences with shapes. A boy had hidden in the clock room. A boy with a foreign name. A boy who was now, according to the newspapers nailed to the post office board, an officer of an enemy army, declared, denounced, wanted.

Aldric Voss. His page was gone. Whoever tore it out had known precisely which pages, had gone straight to the end of the book like a reader who already knew the story.

And that meant it had not been the soldiers. The soldiers had seized archives by the crate, blind and greedy, taking everything and understanding nothing. They had not stood in this room with a knife and a steady hand and surgically removed six entries while leaving the binding intact. This had been done by someone who knew the ledger's anatomy. Someone who knew which names mattered. Someone inside.

* * *

Tomas did not go immediately to Marta. He told himself it was procedure — establish the full inventory first, report completely or not at all, his father's doctrine — but he knew the deeper truth as he climbed the stairs to the clock room: he needed to look at their faces first. All three of them. He needed to see who could not meet his eyes.

Jude was at the master clock's open case, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe pushed up into her dark hair, her small tools laid out on chamois in their strict order. She glanced up when he entered, and her face did the thing it always did — the quick assessment, the slight settling of weight onto the back foot, the face of someone who had learned early that doors open both ways. Five years in this house had softened it but not erased it. Tomas had watched that softening happen and had loved her for it, the way you love a plant that decides, against all advice, to grow toward your window.

"Find anything?" she asked.

"Boxes," he said. "Forty-one accounted. Some gaps in the shelf volumes, old ones." He heard his own voice and hated how carefully casual it was. He was performing calm, and Jude Nakamura, of all people, could hear a performed calm the way a tuner hears a flat string.

She looked at him a moment longer. "You've got your counting face on."

"I always have my counting face."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You have your father's face. There's a difference."

Marta was below, in the transit room, re-leveling the meridian circle that a lieutenant with muddy boots had leaned against. Tomas watched her through the doorway for a long moment — her gray braid, her forearms bare despite the cold, the sure economy of her hands on the adjusting screws. Forty years of entries in that square handwriting. Forty years of pressed flowers. If anyone in this house had reason to remove Voss's page, it was the woman who had written half the book. To protect the boy grown into the enemy officer? Or to protect the house from him?

And yet — no. He could not make it fit. Marta burned nothing and hid everything; that was her whole doctrine, the one she'd built the ledger on. *Paper survives people,* she liked to say. *So we write it down.* Destroying pages was against her religion.

Which left the others. Which left — his stomach turned over slowly, like a gear engaging — himself. Because he had been alone in the record room for six hours, and the ledger had been in his hands, and there was no witness to what it had looked like when he found it except the finding itself. If suspicion fell within this family, it would fall in a circle, and he was standing in the arc of it.

He went back down and finished the count. It took until dusk. When he finally climbed to Marta's study, where she and Jude sat either side of the small stove with tea going cold between them, he laid the inventory sheet on the desk and then, on top of it, the ledger, open to the torn stubs.

"The final section," he said. "Entries 214 through 231. Twelve pages. Removed along the gutters, deliberately, recently — the fibers are still pale in the tear. Before the soldiers came. The dust on the oilcloth was undisturbed except for one set of fingerprints, small ones, and—" he stopped, because he had already said more than he meant to about the dust, about how closely he had looked, about what looking that closely implied.

Jude had gone very still. He watched her look at the stubs, and he watched her understand, and he saw her hand rise halfway toward the torn edges and stop, as if the paper might cut her.

"That's his section," she said quietly. "That's where—"

"Yes," Marta said.

"You knew?" Jude's voice cracked on it. "You knew the pages were gone?"

"I knew the book was in the cabinet. I did not know this." Marta's face, Tomas thought, was doing what it always did — holding. But her thumb was pressing hard against the edge of the desk, whitening at the nail, and he had learned to read Marta in her hands. She was frightened. Marta Ilves, who had faced down customs men and border police and one memorable colonel, was frightened, and the fear was not for herself.

"Someone in this house," Jude said. The words came out flat, stripped of question. "Someone got there first."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came off the glacier field and moved through the orchard below the windows, and Tomas thought of the rows of trees out there in the dark, one for every name, and wondered which row had a gap in it, and whether the gap in the trees matched the gap in the book.

"We don't say that yet," Marta said at last. "We say: someone with access. That is a wider door." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "Tomas. Your inventory. Is it complete?"

"It's complete."

"Then keep it where no one finds it. Not in your room. Somewhere only you would think of." Her eyes held his a moment longer than instruction required, and in that moment Tomas felt the cold draft move through the warm house — the specific chill of being trusted and suspected in the same glance, of knowing that Marta's *only you would think of* was both faith and test.

He took the inventory sheet and left them, and stood a while on the landing where the stairwell window showed the town below, its lamps coming on one by one, obedient to the time the observatory gave them. Somewhere down there the army clerks were sorting forty-one boxes by lamplight, hunting foreign elements in other people's paperwork. And somewhere in this house — in one of these rooms, behind one of these familiar faces — was a person who had knelt at this book with a blade and taken six lives out of the record, and the house was warm around him, the stove ticking, the tea cooling, the clocks keeping their careful time, and none of it, none of it, felt warm at all.

Below, in the yard, a figure crossed from the woodshed toward the kitchen door, moving quickly, shoulders hunched against the wind. Tomas leaned closer to the glass, telling himself it was habit, only habit, only counting — and saw that the figure paused at the orchard gate, just briefly, just long enough to look back at the observatory windows, before slipping through into the dark rows of trees.

It was not Jude. It was not Marta.

It was the third apprentice, Pieter, who had been at the observatory longest of any of them — and who, Tomas realized with a cold that started in his teeth and spread downward, had been the one sent to fetch the working file from the clock room cabinet, two days before the soldiers ever came.
