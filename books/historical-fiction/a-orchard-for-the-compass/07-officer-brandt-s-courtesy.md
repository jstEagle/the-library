## 7. Officer Brandt's Courtesy

The summons came at nine o'clock precisely, which Jude would think about later — how the army kept time by the observatory it had come to dismantle.

A corporal delivered the note on a clipboard, standing in the doorway with his boots leaving wet half-moons on the flagstones, and Marta took it without reading it in front of him. She waited until his footsteps had faded down the hill path before she unfolded the paper under the lamp.

"Interview," she said. "Ten o'clock. The map room." She read it twice more, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder. "You, me, and the other two apprentices, separately. They're going through us like pages."

"Like what?" Pieter asked from the workbench, not looking up from the escapement he was cleaning. His hands were steady. Jude noticed that about him lately — how steady his hands were, how careful, how nothing about him trembled anymore the way it had when he'd first arrived, thin and coughing, five years ago.

"Like pages," Marta said. She folded the summons along its original crease and set it beneath the inkwell, a habit so old it was practically liturgy — nothing important left lying open in this house. "Jude first. They've asked for her by name."

The room went quiet in the particular way this house knew how to be quiet. Not empty silence — inhabited silence, the kind that had walls and a floor and people standing inside it. Tomas set down his file. Even the pendulum of the regulator clock seemed to swing more softly, though Jude knew that was impossible; a clock did not care who was listening.

"They asked for me by name," Jude repeated.

"Your name is on the census. Your mother's name is on the census." Marta's voice was level, but she was looking at Jude the way she looked at a movement in the works that didn't belong — not alarmed yet, only attending. "That's all it is. A name on a list. You'll go down, you'll answer what they ask, and you'll come back up before noon."

"And if they ask things I shouldn't answer?"

Marta crossed the room and took Jude's chin in her hand, tilting her face up to the lamplight, the way she had when Jude was fourteen and had just arrived with frostbitten fingers and no luggage. "Then you'll do what you were taught. You answer slowly. You answer small. A person who answers quickly has something ready, and a person with something ready has something hidden. Give them weather. Give them gears. Let them grow bored." She released her chin. "Boredom is the best lock we have."

* * *

The map room had been the observatory's heart once — Jude remembered it from childhood visits with her mother, the great tables where surveyors bent over the valleys, the smell of ink and cold tea. Now it smelled of boot polish and cigarette smoke, and the maps were gone, rolled and stacked against the wall in canvas tubes like bodies wrapped for burial.

Officer Brandt stood at the window with his back to the door.

She had expected a different animal. The soldiers in the square were loud, young, careless with their rifles; she had built herself a picture of the auditor out of them, some red-faced captain who would shout and slam his palm on wood. But Brandt was slight, gray at the temples, wearing his uniform the way a librarian wore a cardigan — correctly, unremarkably, as if he had been issued it rather than chosen. He was looking out the window at the town below with an expression of mild scholarly interest, and for one absurd moment Jude thought: *he's counting chimneys.*

"Fräulein Nakamura." He turned. His voice was soft, unhurried, pitched for reading rooms. "Thank you for coming up. Please sit."

There was one chair placed facing the desk, and one behind the desk, and the geometry of it was its own kind of question. Jude sat. Her hands settled in her lap the way Marta had drilled into all of them during the first winter — folded, visible, still. *Hands tell the truth before mouths do,* Marta used to say. *So give your hands nothing to say.*

Brandt did not sit behind the desk. He sat on its edge instead, angling himself beside her rather than above, and produced a leather folder that he laid closed upon his knee. Everything about him said: *I am not in a hurry. I have nowhere else to be. We are simply two people talking.*

"You've been at the observatory five years," he began. "Apprenticed to Frau Ilves. Before that—" He opened the folder a hand's width, consulted it without appearing to read it, closed it again. "Before that, the Nakamura family lived in the valley. Your father a watchmaker. Your mother—"

"My mother kept the accounts for the co-op," Jude said.

It came out smoothly. Too smoothly, maybe. She heard the polish on it and hated herself for the polish, because polish was preparation and preparation was a thing Brandt could see. She made herself add, slower, "I was eight when my father died. Nine when—" She let the sentence stand unfinished, a door left ajar, and watched whether he would walk through it.

He didn't. That frightened her more than pursuit would have.

"The co-op," Brandt said. "Yes." He rose, strolled to the window again, hands clasped at the small of his back. Below, the lorries stood in the square in a gray row, and soldiers were carrying archive boxes down the observatory steps two at a time, the way men carry anything heavy and official. "Remarkable view from here. You can see the whole valley. Every road in, every road out." He glanced back at her with something almost like an apology. "You understand why the army values such places. Sightlines. Time. Records." He smiled faintly. "An observatory is really three instruments, isn't it? An instrument for seeing far, an instrument for measuring time, and an instrument for remembering. The third one concerns me most."

"I'm only an apprentice clockmaker, Officer. The stars are Herr Vossel's old department, and the archives were never mine. Gears are what I know."

"Gears." He tasted the word. "Then you'll appreciate precision, and I'll be precise with you." He returned to the desk and, this time, sat behind it, and the shift in geometry landed exactly as designed: the courteous man had been the preface; this was the text. "This town has harbored persons of foreign origin for many years. Some registered. Some not. The audit will establish which names appear in civic records, which records are complete, and which records may have been—"

He paused. Chose his next word the way a surgeon chooses a blade.

"—amended."

Outside, wind moved through the orchard. Jude heard it, the long sigh of branches that had been planted before she was born, and she fixed her eyes on a point just left of Brandt's shoulder, on the rolled maps stacked against the wall, and performed the silence she had been taught since she was old enough to understand why it was taught.

Not blankness — Marta had been explicit about that too, years ago, by lamplight, after the first census taker came. *Never look blank. Blank faces invite digging. Look like someone with nothing interesting in her, which is harder and safer. Think about gear trains. Think about oil viscosity. Let your face rest on something dull.*

Jude thought about the fourth wheel of the master clock, and the way its teeth needed dressing after fifty years of wear, and how the pitch of its whine changed with humidity, and her face became the face of a girl thinking about gear trains, which is what she was.

"Amended," Brandt repeated, letting it hang. Then, lightly, as if discarding it: "But you wouldn't know about records. You've told me so." He opened the folder fully now, and she saw — deliberately, she was sure she was meant to see — a single sheet at the top, handwritten, old, the ink gone brown. She could not read it upside down at that distance. It did not matter. Her pulse climbed anyway, a slow stair, one step at a breath.

"Your mother," Brandt said. "Lena Nakamura."

"Yes."

"She died four years ago. Illness?"

"Pneumonia. In the spring."

"A shame." And here was the terrible thing, the thing Jude would turn over at night for weeks afterward: Brandt sounded like he meant it. There was genuine gentleness in it, or something indistinguishable from genuine gentleness, which amounts to the same weapon with a different maker's mark. "She was well regarded, according to these papers. Generous. The sort of woman who took in strays, I imagine. Cats. Birds with broken wings." He tilted his head. "People."

"My mother liked animals," Jude said. "We had a cat. It was mostly bad-tempered."

Something flickered at the corner of Brandt's mouth — appreciation, perhaps, of a well-made thing, even if the thing was a lie. Or especially if. He made a small note, unhurried, and Jude understood that every word she gave him was being weighed somewhere behind those mild eyes, sorted into piles she could not see: *useful, useless, interesting.*

"Tell me about the clock," he said then, and the pivot was so smooth she felt it only afterward, like realizing you'd been steered downhill while believing you'd chosen the slope. "The master clock. You're repairing it. Why does the region's official time need repairing, Fräulein? Has it been failing?"

"Worn. Fifty years of service wears anything." True. All true, and she let the truth carry her, because Marta had also taught her that the safest lie is the smallest one, worn over truth like a coat worn over skin. "Escapement wear, dirt in the train, the usual. I'm re-bushing the pivot holes and replacing two wheels."

"And while it's disassembled — while the official time of this whole district sits in pieces on a bench — who verifies the timestamps? The record entries? The register signatures?" He said it idly, examining his fingernails. "For instance. If an entry were dated Tuesday, and the clock had been running slow, and Tuesday's entry had in fact been written Thursday — who would ever know?"

The question went through her like cold water finding a seam.

She thought of the ledger hidden in the clock case, forty years of dates pressed among dried flowers. She thought of the torn stubs where pages had been. She thought — and hated herself even as the thought formed — *does he know about the ledger? Is this fishing, or does he have the fish already?*

"I suppose no one would know," she said slowly, and let confusion onto her face like opening a curtain. "I don't understand what timestamps have to do with me, Officer. I fix clocks. If the army wants to verify our records, the army has the records. You took the boxes yesterday. I carried two of them down myself."

"I know you did." He smiled. "Diligent."

The word sat between them like a coin on a table, and neither of them picked it up.

* * *

Afterward — it could not have been more than twenty minutes, though it aged her like a decade — Jude walked out past the corporal at the door, down the corridor that smelled of paraffin and cold stone, and made it as far as the coat rack by the entrance before her knees informed her what her performance had cost.

She gripped the rack. The coats hung around her — Marta's gray wool, Tomas's patched overcoat, Pieter's too-good jacket that had come with him from wherever he'd come from — and she breathed the ordinary smells of her family and counted, the way she counted seconds, until her hands stopped shaking.

They had asked for her by name. Her mother's name had been in his mouth, handled gently, like evidence.

And there had been a moment — near the end, as she stood to leave, when Brandt had shaken her hand with cool courtesy and said, "Thank you, Fräulein. We may speak again" — a moment when his gaze had drifted past her shoulder, toward the clock room, toward the tower, and rested there for exactly one second too long.

She stepped out into the white morning. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, sifting down through the pines along the hill road. Below, the lorries waited. Above, behind her, the dome of the observatory held its blind silver eye against the clouds, and somewhere inside it the master clock lay open on her bench, heart exposed, waiting for her hands.

She was halfway up the stairs when the thought arrived, complete and freezing, the way the truest ones always do:

*Brandt hadn't asked her a single question she couldn't survive.*

Which meant either that he had learned nothing —

— or that everything dangerous was being asked elsewhere, right now, in the map room, of someone else. Marta. Tomas. Pieter.

She took the last stairs two at a time.

In the workshop, Marta stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the snow, and she did not turn when Jude came in. "Well?" she said.

"He was polite," Jude said. "That's what frightens me." She pulled off her coat with numb fingers. "Marta — he asked about timestamps. About whether anyone could tell if a record was written on a different day than the day it claims."

Marta was silent a long moment. When she finally turned, her face was composed, but her eyes had gone somewhere Jude had never seen them go — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. An arithmetic running fast behind them.

"Show me the clock," Marta said. "All of it. Every wheel. Now, before they come back."
