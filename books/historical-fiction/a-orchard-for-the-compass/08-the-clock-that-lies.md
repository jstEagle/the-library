## 8. The Clock That Lies

Tomas Reiner had been raised to believe that a clock could not lie.

His father had said it the way other men said their prayers, standing in the doorway of the shop on market mornings, wiping his hands on his apron: *A clock is the only honest thing a man will ever own, boy. It has no opinion of you. It does not flatter you. It simply goes.* And young Tomas had believed it absolutely, the way children believe the ground will hold them up — not because he had tested it, but because he had never once needed to.

He was thinking about that sentence now, at two o'clock in the morning, with a jeweler's loupe screwed into his eye and the master clock's escapement open before him like a chest cavity, because he had found something in it that should not be there, and he did not know yet whether what he felt was horror or awe.

* * *

It had started three days after the lorries came.

The army had taken the archive boxes down the hill in good order, stacked like cordwood, each one chalked with a white number by a clerk who worked with the calm of a man sorting laundry rather than dismantling forty years of a town's memory. Officer Brandt had stood on the observatory steps afterward and thanked Marta Ilves for her cooperation in a voice so mild it might have been an apology, and then he had posted a notice on the door: *By order of the Provisional Audit Authority, all civic timekeeping instruments are subject to inspection and verification.*

*Verification.* The word had sat strangely with Tomas from the first moment he read it. He turned it over during meals, during the endless inventory work, during the nights when the observatory creaked under wind off the ridge. Verification implied doubt. Doubt implied suspicion. Suspicion of what? A clock was not an opinion. A clock was not a foreign element. A clock simply went.

Except.

Except that for eleven days now, Jude had been making her careful comparison runs against the chronometer signals they still received over the wire from the capital relay — the one instrument Brandt's men had left untouched, perhaps because it was small enough to overlook, perhaps because taking it would have made their audit look like vandalism rather than procedure. And every evening Jude copied the accumulated error into her notebook in her small, even hand, and every evening the number was the same shape: the master clock running slow. Not erratically slow, the way a tired mechanism drifts when its oil thickens or its pendulum gathers dust. Slow *evenly*. Slow by almost exactly four seconds a day, day after day, as regular as rain.

Jude had frowned at it and muttered about temperature compensation, about the barometer falling, about the cold snap settling into the tower stones. Tomas had nodded along. It was a reasonable explanation. It was the explanation he wanted.

But tonight, unable to sleep, he had come up alone to check it himself, because his father had also taught him this: when a mechanism behaves too well, too consistently, in exactly the way a fault should not behave — then the fault is not in the mechanism. The fault is in the intention.

* * *

The clock room at night was a country of its own. The great regulator stood against the east wall, nine feet of walnut and brass, its case dark as river stone, its face a pale moon above the staircase. Around it the lesser clocks ticked in their ranks — the sidereal clock, the mean-time slave dials, the old transit-clock that had outlived three keepers — and beneath them all ran the master's voice, that deep, patient heartbeat that Tomas had slept inside since he was fourteen years old. He knew its sound the way he knew his own pulse. Which was, he understood now, precisely why he had never heard what was wrong with it.

He lit the work lamp low, hooded it with his hand so the glow wouldn't leak through the slit windows where a sentry on the lower road might notice light moving at an hour when no keeper had business moving, and opened the lower case.

The movement was clean. That was the first thing wrong with it. Someone had cleaned it recently — recently meaning within weeks, within the season — and not merely cleaned but *tuned*, the pivots bright, the oil laid in threads fine as spider silk. Tomas felt his stomach tighten as he looked, because he knew whose hands had done that work. There were only four people in the building who could produce a finish like that, and one of them was dead, one was under arrest pending nothing in particular, one was Jude, and one —

One was him.

But he hadn't done this.

The rating nut on the pendulum bob was next. He checked it with fingers gone suddenly clumsy. Seated firm, undisturbed. The suspension spring, then — the thin steel tongue from which the whole swinging weight hung, the component whose stiffness decided, more than anything else, how long the clock thought a second was. He examined it through the loupe, tilting it into the lamplight, and there he stopped breathing.

The spring had been altered.

Not broken, not bent crudely, not sabotaged in any way that would ever appear in a court-martial exhibit or an auditor's report. It had been *thinned*. Delicately, surgically, along its working length, with a stone finer than any whetstone in the workshop — polished down just enough, just so much, so that the spring flexed a fraction softer and the pendulum swung a fraction longer and each second the clock counted became a fraction heavier than a true second. Four seconds a day. Perhaps a minute and a half a month. Nothing. Less than nothing, to anyone who did not compare the master against the wire signal every single evening and write the difference down.

To anyone who did, it was everything. Because every timestamp the observatory certified — every arrival logged, every departure recorded, every document dated and sealed with the town's official time — was being minted from a false second. The records the army had carried away down the hill in their numbered boxes were records whose times could be made to disagree with the truth, slowly, cumulatively, in ways no casual inspection would catch and only a sustained daily comparison would reveal.

Someone inside this house had been shaving minutes off history.

Tomas lowered the loupe and sat back on the work stool, and the clock went on beating above him with its deep unhurried heart, lying to him, lying to the town, lying to the soldiers sleeping in the requisitioned schoolhouse, and he put his face in his hands.

* * *

He tried, in the manner of honest men confronted with dishonesty, to construct an innocent explanation.

Accident. Wear. But wear thins a spring unevenly; this was uniform, finished, deliberate. Temperature; but temperature affects the pendulum rod, not the spring's temper, and no natural force polishes steel to a mirror along six centimeters of its length. Jude — could it have been Jude, working some scheme of her own? No. Jude had spent three days chasing the error as if it were a rat in the pantry, annoyed, baffled, writing figures. You do not hunt a thing you planted yourself, not with that particular frown, the frown of a craftsman insulted by her own instrument.

Marta, then. Marta Ilves, who had run this observatory for thirty years, who knew every screw in the tower better than she knew the lines of her own face. Marta, who had watched the archive boxes go down the hill without changing expression, who had answered Brandt's courtesy with courtesy so exact it was indistinguishable from contempt, who had begun — Tomas had noticed, everyone had noticed — teaching the apprentices, in the gentlest possible way, which questions deserved slow answers.

And yet. The work on that suspension spring was not Marta's work. Marta was seventy years old and her hands, though steady enough for the daily winding, had not held a burnisher against watchmaker's steel in a decade. Tomas knew this because he maintained her tools, because he was the one who kept her bench in order, and her finest stones had lain unused behind their leather wraps since before the war's first winter. He checked them that same night, before dawn, while the house slept: dry, clean, unworn.

Which left him sitting in the grey hour with a truth he did not want, turning it like a coin he could not spend.

Because there was one other person in this building whose hands were that good. One other person who had access to the clock room at hours when no one else was watching. One other person who had been restless lately, preoccupied, watchful in a way that Tomas had read as grief or fear of the audit and now recognized as the specific vigilance of someone maintaining a secret under occupation.

Elias.

Old Elias Venn, third apprentice, twenty-six years old, quiet as furniture, the one who wound the tower dial and swept the stairs and said less in a week than Tomas said before breakfast. Elias, whose sister had married a printer from across the border and vanished into a country that no longer issued exit papers. Elias, who had once, drunk on festival wine two summers ago, told Tomas that a man could serve a cause with his hands instead of his mouth, and had laughed, and had never mentioned it again, and Tomas had filed the sentence away as festival nonsense the way you file away all the things friends say when the lamps are low.

Four seconds a day. Tomas began, despite himself, to do the arithmetic that Jude would have done faster. The spring had been altered — when? The finish on the movement suggested sometime this past autumn, before the frost. Say September. Five months. At four seconds a day, five months was roughly ten minutes. Ten minutes of official time, minted falsely, distributed across hundreds of documents — arrival slips, transit permits, custody transfers, the timestamps by which the army intended to reconstruct who had been where, and when, and therefore who had helped whom move things and people that the state had declared immovable.

Ten minutes was a life. Ten minutes was the width of a border crossed at night, the distance between a checkpoint opening and closing, between a lorry held at the depot and a lorry waved through. Somewhere in those falsified minutes, Tomas understood, people had slipped through. People were alive right now, in rooms he would never see, eating bread he would never taste, because a quiet man with a burnishing stone had stolen seconds from the empire's clock one at a time.

*Protection,* he thought. *It's protection.* And then, immediately, in his father's voice: *It is treason.* And both sentences were equally true, and they lay side by side in his chest like two blades crossed, and he could not separate them, and he did not want to.

* * *

He went up the tower stair without deciding to, his feet carrying him on the old morning rounds while his mind stood still. Through the slit windows the valley was coming up out of the dark, the town's roofs greying, woodsmoke standing straight in the cold air, and on the far side of the square the schoolhouse where the officers billeted showed one lit window — Brandt's window, Tomas thought, or the clerk's, someone awake over paperwork, someone adding columns, someone whose columns were about to stop agreeing with themselves.

That was the thing Tomas kept circling and could not land on: the sabotage could not hold. It was beautiful and it was doomed. Sooner or later Brandt's auditors would make their own comparison run — they had seized the records, they would seize the standards next, it was only a matter of weeks, perhaps days — and when they compared the master clock against any independent standard, the drift would surface, and it would not read as accident. A deliberately altered spring was evidence. Evidence of intent. Evidence of a hand inside the machine. And when they found the hand, they would not stop at the hand. They would take the whole arm, the whole shoulder, the whole body that the arm belonged to — and arms and shoulders and bodies meant the household, the family, Marta and Jude and Elias and, God help him, himself, the apprentice who had touched that spring with his own fingers tonight and left prints in the oil.

Unless it was fixed. Unless someone restored the spring, or replaced it, quietly, before the audit reached the tower — and then the drift in the seized records would have no visible cause, no smoking steel, nothing provable. Or unless the drift was never discovered at all, which required that the audit never verify the clock, which required luck, and luck was not a plan.

Or unless — and here Tomas's mind went to a place he immediately regretted going — unless the drift was *meant* to be discovered. Meant, but late. Meant to surface after the pages that mattered had already been explained, excused, buried under the noise of a scandal. He thought of Jude's nightly comparison log, neat as scripture. He thought of her saying, only yesterday, in her flat practical voice, *I'm going to correct the rate as soon as I isolate the cause. We can't certify false time, Tomas. Not now of all times.* And he remembered the cold that had gone through him at the word *correct*, and he had not known why until this night.

If the clock were corrected — truly corrected, brought back to true time — the drift would stop, yes, but the *record* of the drift would remain in Jude's notebook, and in the seized archives' internal inconsistencies, and in whatever reconstruction Brandt chose to build from them. Correcting the clock was confession by another route. Preserving the lie was complicity by another route. Every road out of the clock room led through fire, and Tomas stood on the tower stair in the freezing dark and understood that he was watching, from the inside, the exact machinery of the age itself: ordinary people, decent people, keeping honest instruments and being forced to choose, daily, in small increments, between honesty and mercy.

His father had been wrong, he thought. Or his father had lived in a country where the sentence was still true. *A clock is the only honest thing a man will ever own.* His father had never imagined a world in which honesty itself would need to be smuggled, hidden in a suspension spring, weighed out in stolen seconds.

* * *

Below him, a floorboard sighed.

Tomas went still on the stair, lamp shuttered, breath held. Footsteps — light, familiar, avoiding the fourth step the way everyone in the house avoided the fourth step — crossing the lower landing toward the clock room. Then a pause. Then the soft scrape of the case door he had left ajar, the click of the hooded lamp being adjusted.

He descended two steps, silently, and looked through the balustrade into the clock room, and saw Elias Venn standing before the open movement with a folded scrap of paper in one hand and the rating chart in the other, checking the drift against the evening figures, his face in the lamplight calm and concentrated and utterly unafraid — the face of a man doing necessary work — and Tomas felt the strange doubled motion in his chest again, admiration rising like warmth and fear rising like floodwater, meeting somewhere behind his ribs and cancelling into a silence he did not trust himself to speak out of.

Because Elias looked up. Of course he looked up; he was a clockman; he heard the house the way the house heard itself. Their eyes met through the balusters, across the ticking dark, and neither of them moved, and the master clock beat its false, faithful heart above them both.

"Your father," Elias said at last, quietly, "was right about clocks."

"I never told you about my father."

"No," Elias agreed. "You didn't." He closed the movement panel with a click soft as a held breath. "But you're going to have to decide soon, Tomas, what you'll tell the ones who ask. Because they're going to ask. Brandt requested the rate logs an hour ago."

Tomas's mouth had gone dry. "The comparison notebooks?"

"Everything." Elias banked the lamp. "Tomorrow morning. Officially. With receipts." He paused at the foot of the stair, and in the last of the lamplight his face was neither guilty nor afraid but only very tired, the tiredness of a man who has been walking a tightrope for months and can see the end of the rope approaching from both directions at once. "Whatever you decide, decide it fast. The clock can't lie much longer for any of us."

He went out. Tomas stood alone in the clock room with the dawn coming grey through the slits and the great pendulum swinging its measured, corrupted arc — four seconds a day, four seconds a day — and understood that the question was no longer what the clock was doing.

The question was what *he* would do, and there were perhaps twelve hours left in which to know.
