## 9. Orchard Rows

The orchard lay on the western slope below the observatory, where the ground fell away toward the river and the fog came up in the evenings like a tide returning to a shore it had never truly left.

Marta Ilves walked it the way she did everything now — slowly, deliberately, with the air of a woman counting something that could not be counted aloud. Behind her, Jude came down the path with her hands in her coat pockets and her shoulders drawn up against the wind, and Marta heard in her footsteps the particular hesitation of someone who had been summoned and did not yet know why. She knew that walk. She had invented it herself, forty years ago, on this same path, when old Keeper Anselm had led her down here for the first time and said only, *You should see what we keep besides time.*

The apple trees were past pruning age and past apology. Their trunks leaned at angles no orchardist would have permitted, their branches crossed one another like people talking over each other in a crowded room. Moss climbed the northern faces of the oldest limbs. In spring they still flowered — extravagantly, recklessly, as if trying to make up in blossom for all they lacked in order — but the fruit ran small and sour, good only for pressing, and no one pressed it anymore except for the cider Marta made each October and shared with the apprentices in the clock room, one glass each, never more.

Forty-one trees. Marta counted them without looking, the way she counted the seconds between the master clock's escapement beats. Forty-one trees, though anyone passing would have seen only a neglected slope of crooked apples, half-wild, hardly worth the wood.

She stopped at the gate — a low thing of wire and larch posts, older than she was — and waited for Jude to catch up.

"You'll want to know why I brought you down here," Marta said.

"I assumed it wasn't about the pruning."

A small smile escaped before Marta could govern it. The girl was learning to be careful without becoming small, which was the whole difficult art of survival in a town like this one, in years like these. "No," Marta said. "It isn't about the pruning." She unlatched the gate. "But we will talk about the ledger, and I'd rather not do it under a roof."

Jude's face changed — Marta watched it happen, the way weather changes over water, the light going from clear to uncertain in the space of a breath. Since the night they had opened the clock case together and found the leather book with its columns of names, its dates, its small pressed flowers laid between the pages like sentences in another language, Jude had carried the knowledge carefully, as one carries water across ice. She had asked questions twice, then stopped asking, waiting for Marta to be ready. That patience was her mother in her. Elke Nakamura had possessed the same quality: an ability to hold silence the way other people held tools, knowing exactly when to set it down.

"Under a roof," Marta said, pushing the gate open, "there are walls, and walls have ears even when rooms are empty. Wood carries sound. Stone remembers. Out here—" she gestured at the slope, the fog gathering below them along the river bend, the town's roofs just visible through it, "—out here the wind takes what we say and spends it somewhere over the mountains."

* * *

They walked the first row together, and Marta began, as Anselm had begun with her, not with explanation but with a tree.

"This one," she said, resting her palm against a trunk gone silver-grey with age, bark peeling in long curls like paper left out in rain. It was an apple of no name she knew, a seedling raised from a pip some traveler had once spat into her keeping. "Planted in the autumn of 1898. Do you want to know by whom?"

Jude said nothing, which was answer enough. She was looking at the tree the way she looked at movements in a watch — reading structure, reading intent.

"A man called Ferenc Halas," Marta said. "A surveyor. He came through the valley in the summer of '98 carrying papers that were not his, fleeing a border that had moved overnight and taken his family's land with it. He stayed three weeks in the cellar beneath the clock room — you've seen the hatch, though you may have thought it was for coal. When it was safe to move him on, Anselm planted this tree and entered Ferenc's name in a book, and beside the name he pressed one of the blossoms from this very branch." She let her hand fall away. "He said the tree was so the record would have roots. Words can be burned. A living thing must be cut down in front of witnesses, and even then—"

"Even then it grows back," Jude said quietly.

Marta turned to look at her. "From the stump. Yes. If the roots are deep enough." She had not told the girl that part. She wondered, briefly and not for the first time, how much of what Jude knew had come from her mother, and whether Elke had stood on this same slope in some other autumn and heard the same lesson from Anselm's mouth. There were so many things Marta had meant to ask her and had stored instead in the place where she kept everything she could not afford to say.

They moved down the row. Marta named names as they went — some she gave fully, some only in fragments, testing as much as telling, watching Jude's face for the flicker of recognition or grief. A schoolteacher from the north who had printed pamphlets in a language the province had forbidden; a Jewish family of four who had wintered in the observatory's lower rooms while their neighbors' loyalties sorted themselves; a deserter from the last war who had simply walked uphill one night and refused ever to walk back down. Each name had a tree. Each tree had a page. Anselm's system, refined over decades, then hers: the ledger in the clock case recorded the person, the season, the danger, the departure; the orchard recorded the rest — the part that could not be written, the weight of a human life continued past its crisis.

"Your mother has a tree," Marta said, when they reached the fourth row. "Third from the wall. The one that leans east."

Jude stopped walking.

"She came to us in '26," Marta went on, because stopping now would be crueller than going on. "Pregnant, unmarried, disowned, with a language in her mouth nobody in this valley spoke. Anselm took her in the way he took everyone — without ceremony, without questions he didn't need answered. You were born in February, in the blue room, with me holding your mother's hands and old Anselm downstairs winding the clock because he couldn't bear to listen from closer." Marta heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used for readings that must not tremble. "In the spring, we planted her tree. And in the autumn, when you were six months old and asleep against her shoulder right about where you're standing now, she pressed a blossom from its first flowering and laid it in the ledger beside both your names. Hers and yours. Because you were sheltered too, Jude. Before you could walk, before you could speak, this hill kept you."

Jude had put her hand against the trunk of the leaning tree. Her fingers spread on the bark the way they spread on the brass of a movement — finding the grain of it, the places where growth had fought itself and won. She did not cry. Marta had expected tears and understood, when none came, that this was worse and better at once: the girl was filing the information into the deep drawers where she kept everything that would have to be acted upon later.

"Why trees?" Jude asked at last. "Why not just the book?"

"Because the book can lie," Marta said. "Or rather, the book can be made to lie. Pages torn out. Entries inked over. A record is only as honest as the last hand that touched it. But a tree—" she gestured at the slope, the whole crooked congregation of them, silvered and mossy and blooming anyway, "—a tree is testimony that doesn't know how to stop testifying. Anyone who wanted to erase Ferenc Halas would have to stand here with an axe and do it in daylight, and everyone on this hill would see, and the stump would remain, and the stump would remember." She paused. "Love leaves physical evidence, child. That is its great inconvenience and its great mercy. We plant because forgetting requires effort, and we mean to make forgetting expensive."

* * *

The fog was climbing the slope by mid-morning, thinning as it rose, wrapping the lower rows in gauze. They worked their way westward, and Marta found herself doing what she always did in this orchard: measuring the years by the trees' heights, the way other people measured them by children. Here the row where the trees were all of a height — the hard winter of 1912 that had stunted everything planted near the wall. There the gap where two trees had died of blight and been replaced by saplings that now stood taller than their dead elders, the way the second generation of anything stands.

And there — she had known they would come to it, had been walking toward it since the gate, had perhaps been walking toward it for five years, since the day Jude Nakamura arrived at the observatory with her father's tool roll and her mother's careful eyes —

There was the stump.

It sat in the fifth row like a missing tooth. Wide as a cartwheel, grey as ash, softening already at the crown where rot had gotten in despite everything. Around its base, pushed up through the grass in a ragged ring, grew the suckers — dozens of them, thin whippy shoots rising straight and green from the rootstock below ground, the tree's stubborn insistence on continuing. Someone had cut them back over the years, roughly, angrily, and they had come again each spring, and been cut again, and come again. Marta had stopped cutting them four years ago. Let them grow wild, she had decided. Let the evidence be ugly if it must be evidence at all.

Jude saw it and stopped. Marta watched her read the scene the way she read a mechanism — stump, suckers, the absence where a tree should be — and arrive, slowly, at the wrong conclusion first, as everyone did.

"Which tree is that?" Jude asked. "Did it die?"

"It didn't die," Marta said. "It was cut."

"When?"

"Eighteen years ago. On an April morning, before dawn." Marta folded her hands at her waist. She had rehearsed nothing, but the facts arranged themselves in order anyway, the way they always did, because she had carried them so long they had worn grooves. "Anselm cut it himself. I held the lantern. We did it in the dark so that only the two of us and God would witness, and God, we judged, could be trusted to forget what He preferred not to know."

"Why?" And then Jude's voice changed, dropped, caught on something — because she was clever, because she had spent a childhood among people who hid other people, because some part of her had known since the ledger opened that every name had a tree and every absence had a reason. "Whose tree was it, Marta?"

Marta looked at the stump for a long moment. The fog had reached them now, beading cold on her eyelashes, silvering the wool of her sleeves. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice in the town and fell silent, and the silence after it was enormous.

"There was a boy," she said. "In the summer of 1920. Eleven years old. His father had been an officer on the losing side of the war, and the winning side had decided that officers' sons make convenient hostages, or inconvenient examples — the paperwork used both words at different times. The boy's mother got him over the mountains to us with a letter of introduction and a tin of sugar, and he stayed in the clock room fourteen months. Fourteen months, Jude, in a room four meters square, with the pendulum swinging above him day and night. He learned to sleep inside its rhythm. He learned to tell time by the sound of the escapement alone. He helped Anselm clean the gears — small hands, you understand, hands that fit where grown men's wouldn't." Her throat tightened around the next part, as it always did, as it had for eighteen years. "When it was safe — when the amnesty came through and the boy could be sent to an uncle abroad — Anselm planted him a tree. A young apple, third row from the fence, in good soil. And he wrote the boy's name in the ledger, and pressed a blossom beside it, and told the boy before he left: *This tree is yours. As long as it stands, this hill says you existed, and that we kept you, and that keeping you was right.*"

"The ledger," Jude whispered. "The pages in the back. The ones that were there when I found it, but—" She stopped. Marta watched her arrive at it fully now, the way one arrives at the end of a long calculation: not the final section's emptiness, which they had discovered days ago, but this older absence, this first one.

"Eighteen years ago," Marta said, "soldiers came to the observatory. Not many — a lieutenant and four men, with an order signed in a provincial office, authorizing the seizure of records pertaining to persons of enemy origin. Anselm met them at the door with the visitor register and the correspondence files and every innocuous document on the premises, and he was courteous, and he was slow, and he gave them tea, and by the time they left they believed they had taken everything worth taking." She drew a breath. "What he could not give them, because it lived in the locked case inside the clock, was the ledger. So while I kept them at the table, Anselm went upstairs with a knife, and he tore out every page that named the boy, and he burned them in the stove, and then — because he was Anselm, and he understood that a record with a hole in it invites the question of what filled the hole — he went down to the orchard in the dark and he cut the tree, so that no stump, no sucker, no blossom on any branch would ever testify to a name the ledger no longer contained."

"But he missed the stump."

"He missed the roots." Marta crouched, stiffly, her knees reporting their decades, and laid her hand flat on the earth between the suckers. "Cut a tree and the roots go on living for years, sending up shoots, spending themselves in green insistence. Anselm knew it. He cut the suckers back every June until he died, and then I cut them, until four years ago, when I stopped." She looked up at Jude through the fog-beaded air. "Do you know why I stopped?"

Jude shook her head slowly.

"Because I am getting old, child, and I have begun to think about what remains when I don't. And I thought: perhaps someday someone will come asking. Perhaps the boy himself, grown, will come back to this hill and stand at this fence and look for his tree. And when he does, I wanted him to see this—" she lifted her hand from the earth, from the ring of green shoots standing straight as minute-hands around the dead wood, "—I wanted him to see that the hill never agreed to forget him. That the roots went on. That everything done to erase him was done against the ground's own objection."

Jude had gone very pale, or perhaps it was only the fog. Her lips moved around a question she didn't manage to ask.

"His name," Marta said gently, "was Aldric Voss."

* * *

They stood a long time without speaking. Below them the fog thinned and reformed, and the town appeared and disappeared in it — roofs, chimneys, the square's dark plane trees — as though the valley itself were deciding what could safely be shown. Marta thought: this is what I have done my whole life. Decided what can be shown. Held the fog or lifted it, hour by hour, name by name, and called the work keeping.

"I knew him," Jude said finally. Her voice came out strange, flattened, the voice of someone reading a measurement off a dial and not trusting it. "When I was small. There was a boy who stayed one summer — Mama said he was a cousin from the coast. He taught me to whistle through a blade of grass. He let me hold the little gears." She turned to Marta, and her eyes were wet now after all, though her face remained composed, and somehow that composure was the most heartbreaking thing on the whole grey slope. "That was him. That was Aldric Voss."

"That was him."

"And the promise—" Jude's hand went to her collarbone, an unconscious gesture, touching the place where a pendant might hang, where a promise might be worn. "Mama made me promise. She said, *if that boy ever comes back and needs us, the book will speak for him.* I was seven. I didn't know what book. I thought she meant a storybook, I thought—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, steadier. "She meant the ledger. She meant this. She was preparing me for something I wouldn't understand for twenty years."

Marta felt the full weight of it settle onto her then, heavier than any archive box: the shape of a promise made by a dying woman to a child, sealed in ignorance, coming due eighteen years later on a fog-bound hillside between a stump and its insistent roots. She thought of Anselm burning the pages and cutting the tree, believing he was closing the account forever. She thought of how badly he had miscalculated — how love, once planted, behaves exactly like an apple rootstock, indifferent to axes, patient under sod, willing to wait decades for its moment.

"The army has the archives now," Marta said. "And someone reached the ledger before we did — the final section is gone, torn out, Voss's entry with it. Whether they took it or it was removed for other reasons, I don't yet know. But hear me, Jude." She took the girl by both shoulders, feeling the fine bones of her, the trembling in her that was not fear exactly but voltage, current looking for a circuit. "The ledger can be reconstructed. Names can be remembered. Dates can be reasoned backward. But a torn-out page proves nothing to a tribunal, and a memory proves less. What cannot be argued with—" she nodded at the stump, at the ring of suckers standing in their imperfect circle, "—is what grows. Wood doesn't perjure itself. Rings don't change their story under questioning. If it comes to proof — if it comes to a man's neck or a man's freedom hanging on whether this hill ever sheltered him — then this orchard is the only witness we have left."

Jude looked at the stump for a long time. Then she crouched, the way Marta had, and did something Marta had not foreseen and would remember for the rest of her life: she took out the small knife she carried for trimming pegwood, and she selected one of the strongest suckers — thumb-thick, straight, two seasons of growth behind it — and cut it cleanly at the base with a single pass.

"For grafting," Jude said. "Apple stock will take onto almost any root. And this root—" she stood, holding the green shoot against the fog-light, "—this root has survived being erased once already."

Above them, faint through the mist, the observatory bell rang the half hour, and the sound came down the slope and settled over the rows like a blessing neither tree nor girl acknowledged. Marta counted the strokes out of habit. Twelve and six. Correct. True.

Then, distantly, from the road below the orchard wall, came a sound that did not belong to the hour or the fog: engines. Low gears laboring up the hill road, more than one, heavy-laden, and beneath them the thin metallic rattle of something Marta had hoped never to hear again on this mountain — the sound of a truck bed filled with iron.

Jude turned her head toward it, the sapling cutting still in her hand.

"Marta," she said. "Someone's coming up."
