## 10. A Letter Under Pendulum Glass

The pendulum case had not been opened in eleven years.

Jude knew this because Marta told her so on her first morning at the observatory, the way Marta told most things — flatly, without ceremony, as though reciting a fact of geology rather than a small confession. *We don't open it. The clock keeps better time when we leave it alone.* And for five years Jude had obeyed, the way she had learned to obey all the quiet rules of this house: don't ask about the third chair at the kitchen table that no one ever sat in; don't wonder aloud why Tomas's letters from his brother came with the stamps soaked off; don't hum the songs your mother sang in a language no one in this valley claimed to speak anymore. Silence was a craft here, practiced with the same devotion as escapement adjustment and lens polishing. She had become fluent in it. Some nights she dreamed in it.

But the master clock was sick, and sick clocks demanded honesty. That was the whole religion of the trade — her mother had taught her that before anything else, before songs, before stories: *a clock is a machine that cannot lie, and if it lies, someone made it lie.* So on the fourth morning of her repairs, with the army still eleven days away and the town below still pretending to be an ordinary town, Jude knelt before the pendulum case with a screwdriver, a lamp, and a growing conviction that the illness lived behind that glass.

The case stood taller than she did, walnut gone amber with age, its glass door clouded at the corners where breath had touched it over decades. She worked the screws slowly. They turned with the reluctant shriek of metal that had never been asked to move in living memory, and each one sounded, in the hush of the clock room, like something being woken against its will.

*Leave it alone.* She heard Marta's voice and kept turning.

The last screw gave. The glass door swung open on hinges that had somehow been oiled recently — within the year, she judged, running her thumb along them. Recently oiled but never opened past a crack. Someone had tended this door without entering it, the way you might tend a grave.

Inside, the pendulum hung motionless, its bob a great disc of brass polished to a dull fire. The rod rose into darkness. Dust lay on everything in a fine grey felt, undisturbed except along one narrow path near the base of the case, where the dust was thinner, brushed away by air — air that moved only when the case was opened. Someone had opened this door often enough, and briefly enough, to leave only a current, not a mark.

Jude crouched and put her eye to the gap beneath the pendulum's arc, where the bob swung closest to the floor, and there she saw it: a corner of paper, cream-white in all that grey, wedged flat between the floorboard of the case and the iron bracket that anchored the suspension spring. It had been slid in from the back, through a seam she hadn't noticed, and pushed forward until only its edge showed. Positioned, she thought, so that anyone servicing the pendulum — and who else would open this case? — would find it. Positioned for her, or for whoever came after her.

Her hands were steady. She noted that about herself, distantly, the way she noted barometric pressure. Her hands had learned steadiness in rooms where shaking meant ruin.

She drew the letter out with two fingers.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, folded twice, tied with waxed thread gone brittle. The oilcloth had kept it dry and dark; the pendulum's slow breathing above it had kept it company. She untied the thread and unfolded the paper, and the smell rose up out of it — cedar and ink and something faintly of smoke, of a world elsewhere — and the handwriting stopped her heart for one full second before her mind caught up to her eyes.

She knew the hand. Not well. Once, briefly, a long time ago, in pencil, in the margins of a book about stars: *this one is Cassiopeia, see how she sits wrong on her throne.* A boy's careful capitals. But handwriting matures the way trees do, keeping its early habit in the grain, and the tall ascenders were the same, and the way he crossed his T's with a single impatient stroke was the same.

*Aldric,* she said, though not aloud. In the clock room you didn't say things aloud unless they were true and necessary. The name stayed behind her teeth, tasting of a summer eleven years dead.

She carried the letter to the window, where the morning light came down the mountain clean and cold, and read it standing, as one reads a verdict.

---

*To the keeper of the observatory clock — or to the hand that finds this,*

*I write from the coast, where the fog comes in like a debt collector. I am told letters may still cross the border if they are brief and dull, so I will be both.*

*You will not remember me clearly. I was the boy in the clock room the summer the mills burned. You were smaller than I was and braver than any of us deserved. You brought me bread with plum butter and told me the clock ate time and that was why some days went missing. I have thought about that sentence for eleven years. It has become more true the older I get.*

*I need you to know what I am now, so you can decide what I was then. I am a clerk. I translate shipping manifests. I have never carried anything heavier than a ledger, and I intend to keep it that way. Whatever the newspapers decide to call my country and yours, I was a boy who hid behind your mother's skirts and ate your bread, and I am going to remain that boy as long as paper and distance allow.*

*They are closing the borders. Everyone says it won't come to that and everyone is buying train tickets, which tells you what everyone believes. If the borders close, I may not be able to write again. So I am writing this now, while the fog is thin:*

*I am coming back. Not soon — perhaps years — but I am coming back, and when I come back I will need my page.*

*You'll understand what that means. Or the person holding the ledger will. Tell them Aldric Voss remembers the orchard. Tell them he remembers which row.*

*I trust the promise. I have kept it on my side of the sea every day since, and it has weighed nothing and everything, the way good promises do.*

*Wind the clock. Keep the rate true. Time is the only country left that doesn't check papers.*

*— A.V.*

---

The date at the bottom was nine years old.

Jude stood at the window for a long time with the letter in her hands, reading it a second time, a third, the way you reread a telegram hoping the words will rearrange themselves into mercy. Nine years. He had written it nine years ago, sealed it, smuggled it or mailed it or carried it himself across whatever distances then existed between here and the coast — and hidden it here, under the pendulum, where only the clock's own servant would ever find it. Where *she* would find it.

Had he been here? Had he stood in this room as a young man, sliding oilcloth into the dark while the pendulum breathed over him? Or had he sent it to someone — to Marta — who had put it where it needed to be and told no one, adding it to the long arithmetic of silences this house kept?

Either way, the meaning arrived with the same terrible weight: the promise had not died with the summer. It had been lying in wait, patient as a seed, and now it had germinated in her hands.

*He trusts her promise.* The sentence from the letter turned itself over and over. He trusted *the* promise — hers, the one she had made at twelve years old with flour on her wrists and a frightened boy's eyes fixed on her face: *if you ever need it, the ledger will speak for you.* She had said it because adults said such things to children to make them brave, and she had meant it because children mean everything. And then the war had come, and the borders had closed like a book snapping shut, and for nine years Aldric Voss had been a name she did not say even inside her own head, because names that heavy could crack a person if lifted too often.

Now the name had weight again, and hands, and a deadline.

Because she knew — everyone in the valley knew, the way you knew weather in your knees — that the army was coming. Lorries on the north road, requisition orders already typed in some provincial office, an audit of records, of persons, of *foreign elements*, that phrase already circulating in the market like bad coin. When the soldiers reached the observatory they would take the archives, the registers, the ledgers. Everything written would become evidence, and evidence cut both ways depending on who held the knife.

And somewhere in that future was a man walking toward this town — or trying to — who believed that a piece of paper under a pendulum would be waiting for him, and that the girl who fed him plum bread would be standing behind it.

*

She found Marta in the orchard, as she had known she would. Marta went to the orchard the way other people went to church, alone and without explanation, and came back with her face arranged into its usual administrative calm. The rows ran up the slope in their old disorder — forty years of planting, tree after tree, many of them gnarled past fruiting now, their labels lost to weather, their reasons known only to the woman walking among them.

Marta was pruning. She didn't stop when Jude's boots crunched on the frost-stiffened grass, but she angled her head, which for Marta was a full welcome.

"A letter," Jude said. No preamble. There was no room left for preamble; the words had been climbing her throat since the clock room.

The shears stopped.

"Under the pendulum glass." Jude held out the oilcloth bundle. "Nine years old. His hand."

For a moment Marta looked at the bundle the way she looked at instruments that had been dropped — assessing damage, calculating repair. Then she set the shears down on the wall beside her, wiped her hands on her apron with unnecessary thoroughness, and took the letter. She read it once, quickly, the way she read barometric charts. Then again, slowly, her lips moving slightly around certain phrases, and Jude watched the woman's face do something it almost never did in company: soften and harden at the same time, like wax cooling over a flame.

"He came at night," Marta said at last. "Spring of 'twenty-nine. Young man, rain-soaked, standing at the gate like the boy he'd been had been stretched to fit the body. He wouldn't come inside. Said the fewer people who saw him enter, the fewer who'd remember him leaving." Her thumb moved across the signature. "He'd walked from the rail head. Forty kilometers. He asked to see the clock."

"You opened the case."

"I opened the case." Marta handed the letter back. "He put this in himself. Sealed it himself. Then he stood there a while with his hand flat on the glass, like a man touching a sleeping animal, and he said—" she paused, retrieving it exactly, and Jude understood that Marta Ilves forgot nothing, had never forgotten anything, that memory was the second ledger this woman kept, "—he said, *keep the rate true, and keep my page safe, and one day the arithmetic will balance.* Then he walked back down the hill and I watched him until the fog took him, and that is the last time anyone in this town saw Aldric Voss."

"And you told no one. Not even me. Five years, Marta. I've cleaned every part of that clock but the pendulum, and you let me think—"

"I let you think what was true: that opening it wasn't yet your work." Marta's voice stayed level, but something underneath it moved, tectonic. "A letter is a fuse, child. It does nothing until someone strikes it. He wrote *I am coming back* — do you understand what that means with the border closed? It means he is either dead, or in prison, or he is finding a way through anyway, and all three of those men are dangerous to know about. I carried that sentence for nine years so that no one else had to." She looked down the orchard rows, at the bare winter trees standing like a congregation. "That is what keeping means here. You carry it until it's your turn to pass it, and you pray the passing happens in daylight."

Jude held the letter against her chest, over her sternum, where she could feel her own pulse knocking. "He needs his page. And his page is—" She couldn't finish it. She didn't have to. They both knew the ledger's final section, the entries of the last decade, the pages that had been whole when the clock first fell ill and that Jude had not yet inventoried since beginning her repairs. A cold thought moved through her that she refused, for the moment, to look at directly.

"The audit," Marta said quietly. "Yes. I've done my own arithmetic." She picked up the shears again, considered a dead branch, and left it. Even grief, Jude thought, obeys the seasons. "If he comes, he cannot come to an observatory full of soldiers. If he comes, he must find us before they find the ledger. Which means—" she met Jude's eyes, and for the first time in five years Jude saw fear in them, unmasked, plain as a lit window, "—which means we have less time than the clock says. Maybe less than the clock *can* say."

*

That night the clock room was cold enough that Jude could see her breath drift through the lamplight, and she worked anyway, because working was the only prayer she trusted. She stripped the escapement, cleaned the pallets, measured the drop with calipers whose brass had warmed in her palms. And all the while the letter lay in her breast pocket, over her heart, a small rectangular warmth that should have been impossible — paper holds no heat — but that she felt anyway, the way amputees feel weather in absent limbs.

*Nine years.* She tried to build him out of that span and failed. The boy in the clock room had been all elbows and watchfulness, fourteen maybe, with a burn scar on his forearm he wouldn't explain and a habit of flinching at the sound of boots on gravel. What grew out of that boy in nine years of fog and manifests and a closing border? A clerk, the letter said. A man who translated cargo into language. But the letter also said *I trust the promise*, and trust like that doesn't survive in clerks. It survives in people who have spent nine years walking toward something.

She thought about her mother's handwriting in the ledger, beside her own childhood name. She thought about the pressed flowers, each one a person, each person a tree. Somewhere in those rows — she was nearly sure, though she hadn't counted yet, hadn't dared — there was a stump where a tree should be. She had seen it her second week at the observatory and asked no questions, because asking no questions was the dialect she'd been raised in.

*Tell them he remembers which row.*

She wound the clock. She set the pendulum swinging, and the tick resumed its ancient patient argument with silence, and she stood listening to it, and the sound had changed for her forever. Every tick was now also a countdown. Every swing of the bob passed over the place where a man's hope had lain in oilcloth for nine years, waiting for the right pair of hands.

Outside, far down the valley, on the north road, she saw lights. Headlamps, plural, moving in convoy formation, too orderly for farmers, too late for mail.

Eleven days, the town had whispered. Maybe fewer.

Jude blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with the ticking and the letter, and understood that the past had not merely arrived — it had arrived with a schedule, and she was the only one who knew how to read it.
