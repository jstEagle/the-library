## 12. Tomas's Bargain

Tomas had always measured his life in the sounds of the observatory. The tick of the master clock through the floorboards, steady as a heartbeat under the whole hill. The creak of Marta's boots on the iron stairs at half past six, never a minute late, because a man who kept time for others could not afford to be late to himself. The whisper of the wind through the orchard rows below the windows, carrying up the green smell of apples and wet stone.

For eleven days since the soldiers had come down from the ridge, those sounds had been wrong. He heard them now the way a deaf man reads lips — straining, suspicious, translating everything into threat. The clock's heartbeat sounded like a countdown. Marta's boots on the stairs sounded like an arrest. And the wind in the orchard sounded like someone walking through it who should not have been there.

He sat in the workshop past midnight with a lamp turned low and the torn edge of a memory in his hands — not paper anymore, he had sold that away, but the shape of what the paper had been. Four pages. Forty years of names compressed into their final section, ending with a boy called Aldric Voss and a pressed sprig of apple blossom flattened thin as a wafer. He could still feel them: the soft give of old leather, the way the ink had browned like tea left standing, the small dry weight of flowers that had once been alive on trees planted for people saved.

He had taken them on a Tuesday. That was the detail that would not leave him alone — how ordinary the day had been. Rain in the afternoon. Marta downstairs correcting the sidereal tables. Jude somewhere on the roof with her ear against the dome, listening to the great telescope settle as the temperature fell. He had opened the clock case with the same brass key that hung on its hook by the door, the key any of them used, the key that meant *this house trusts you*, and he had lifted four pages out of a ledger older than his father, folded them into his coat, and walked down the hill through the rain to meet a man in a gray overcoat outside the baker's shutters.

His brother was in the army's custody by then. Luka, twenty-three, arrested at the border crossing for a stamp missing from a transit card — a stamp no clerk had ever told him he needed, a stamp that existed only so that men like Officer Brandt could hold young men in rooms without windows and call it procedure. Three weeks now. Three weeks of nothing: no charges, no letters allowed through, no word whether he was fed or beaten or simply forgotten in some requisitioned schoolhouse with wire on its windows.

The gray-coated man had named his price before Tomas finished speaking. Pages for the brother. Not copies — originals, because Brandt wanted leverage, and leverage does not survive as a copy. And Tomas, who had spent five years learning which silences keep people safe, had learned in one evening that silence has a price too, and that he could pay it with someone else's name.

*You did it for Luka,* he told himself, the way he had told himself every night since, kneeling at the washbasin, looking at a face in the dark glass that looked older than his thirty-one years. *You did it because they were going to take him anyway, and pages are paper, and paper is not a person.*

But paper here was a person. That was the whole terrible architecture of the ledger — Marta had explained it to Jude in the clock room while Tomas stood in the doorway pretending to check the escapement, and he had understood even then, even before the theft, that those pressed flowers were gravestones and birth certificates both. Each tree in the orchard. Each name. Forty years of the observatory saying *you were here, you were sheltered, you mattered*, written in ink because memory is not enough when the state decides memory is a crime.

And he had sold four of them for his brother's freedom.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first snow of the season came down through the cone of light beneath the window, slow and enormous, each flake turning twice before it vanished into the dark. Tomas watched it and thought about how snow erased things. How kind it looked doing it.

He heard her on the stairs before he saw her — not Marta's metronome tread but something lighter, more hesitant, a rhythm that stopped twice between the second landing and the workshop door. Jude. He had known her steps for five years; you learn a person's walk the way you learn a tune. She stood in the doorway with her coat still on, snow melting in her dark hair, and she looked at him sitting alone with the low lamp, and something in her face said she had come looking for exactly this.

"I couldn't sleep," she said, which was a courtesy, a lie offered gently, the way this household lied to one another out of respect.

"Neither could I," he answered, which was true, and they both knew the difference.

She came in and sat across the workbench from him, on the stool where apprentices ground lenses, and for a long moment neither spoke. The master clock ticked through the wall, patient, indifferent, keeping perfect time for a town whose official records now lied about it. Tomas found he could not look at the wall. He looked instead at Jude's hands, chapped from the roof work, resting palm-down on the bench the way she rested them when she was waiting for someone else to be brave.

"The inventory," she said finally. "I've done it three times. The final section of the ledger — Voss's entry, the last four pages — they're gone. Torn out close to the spine. Whoever took them knew the binding." Her voice stayed level, but only just; he could hear the effort underneath, like ice holding a man's weight. "Marta says the case was locked. The key hangs by the door. Anyone in this house could have—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I keep telling myself it was a soldier. That someone got in during the requisition."

"Jude."

"No. Let me finish. I need to say it all before I lose my nerve, because I've been losing my nerve for three days, and Marta taught us that a question answered slowly is better than a question answered never, so I'm going to answer slowly, and you're going to let me." She breathed. The snow ticked faintly against the window glass. "Was it you?"

There it was. Not an accusation — worse. An offering. She had climbed the hill at midnight in the first snow to give him the chance to say it himself, before suspicion did what suspicion does inside a small warm house: freeze everyone in place, turn every glance sideways, make family into a list of possible thieves.

Tomas looked at his hands. They were good hands; he had always believed that. Hands that could true a balance wheel thinner than a hair, that had rebuilt the transmission gear when he was nineteen while Marta watched and said nothing, which from Marta was thunderous praise. He thought of all the hours those hands had spent in service of this place, and how four pages had undone all of it in the space of one walk down a rainy hill.

"Yes," he said.

The word came out smaller than he intended, and larger than he feared. It hung in the lamplight between them like a struck bell that would not stop sounding.

Jude did not move. He had expected anger — wanted it, almost, the way a man with a rotten tooth presses his tongue against it, wanting the pain made definite. Instead she went very still, the stillness of someone recalculating everything at once: every shared meal, every joke in the lens room, every time Tomas had covered for her mistakes or she for his, all of it passing under review behind her eyes.

"Why," she said. Not *how*. Not *how could you*. Just *why*, and her voice cracked open on the single syllable like a seam giving way, and he saw that she already knew, or guessed, or had spent three days assembling the same terrible arithmetic he had.

"My brother," Tomas said. "Luka. You met him two summers ago — he carried the new pendulum weights up the hill, remember? He complained about it for a week." He laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughter, and hated it. "They took him at the border. No charge. A missing stamp. Brandt's people. And a man came to me — gray coat, soft voice, knew my name, knew Luka's cell number, knew things about our father's shop that no stranger should know — and he said the captain would consider a trade."

"The pages."

"He wanted the originals. He said—" Tomas stopped, swallowed. "He said the last section was the valuable part. He said names near the end were names still living. I didn't understand what he meant until later, and by then the papers were gone and so was my brother's file number from the detention list, and there was a letter in my pocket saying Luka would be released pending review." His voice frayed. "Pending review. Do you know what those words do to a person? They let you eat again. They let you sleep, a little. They are the cruelest kindness ever invented, because they mean they can take it back whenever they want."

Jude was quiet a long moment. Snow filled the window behind her, erasing the orchard row by row.

"So they're not free yet," she said.

"No."

"And if Brandt decides he needs more—"

"Then he'll ask for more. And I don't know what's left to sell." Tomas put his head in his hands. "That's the truth of it, Jude. I thought I was making one trade, one payment, clean and finished. But men like that don't buy things. They buy *people*. And once you've sold yourself once, you find out you were only renting yourself to them all along."

He heard her stand. He flinched, expecting her to leave — to go wake Marta, to carry his confession down the hall like contraband and hand it over, and God forgive him, part of him wanted her to, wanted the choice taken out of his hands, wanted to be caught and sentenced because the weight of carrying it alone had grown heavier every night until he could barely stand under it.

Instead she came around the workbench and sat beside him, on the same bench, shoulder almost touching his, the way they used to sit as apprentices grinding glass side by side in the years when the world was simpler and the worst thing either of them faced was a scratched mirror.

"Marta will have to know," Jude said softly.

"I know."

"Not tonight. But soon. Because if Brandt comes back for more pages, and we don't know what you've already given him, then we're defending a house with holes in it we can't see." She paused. "And because she'll understand. Better than me, maybe. She's kept this ledger forty years, Tomas. She knows exactly what fear makes people do. Why do you think she wrote everything down? So that fear couldn't win by forgetting."

"She'll never trust me again."

"Maybe not. Trust isn't a thing you get back. It's a thing you rebuild, gear by gear, the way we'd rebuild the escapement after someone dropped it. Slowly. With your hands where everyone can see them." Jude looked at him then, and her eyes were wet but level. "You should have come to us. You should have told us about Luka the day they took him. We would have found another way. There's always another way — slower, harder, but it doesn't cost anyone else's name."

"I know that now." His throat closed. "I tell myself I did it for family. My blood family. And I destroyed—" He gestured helplessly at the walls around them, at the ticking heart of the building, at the orchard invisible under the snow. "This is my family too. That's what I can't make sit straight in my chest. Both things are true. I love him, and I betrayed you. Both at once. In the same hour."

Jude reached over and took his hand — his good hands, his ruined, ordinary hands — and held it the way you hold a wound closed.

"Beg me," she said quietly. "You were going to. I saw it in your face when I came in. So say it."

Tomas bowed his head. "Don't tell Marta tonight. Give me until the audit — let me try to get Luka properly out, through the lawyer in the valley, through whatever's left of honest channels. And don't tell Brandt that we know. If he thinks the pages vanished without a trace, he may believe he got away clean, and a man who believes he's won stops digging." He squeezed her fingers, desperate. "And don't hate me. I can survive Marta's anger and the tribunal and all of it, but not that. Not from you."

"I don't hate you." She said it steadily, and he understood she had decided it, deliberately, the way you decide to true a wheel — not because the feeling arrived but because accuracy demanded it. "Hate would be easier. What I feel is worse. I understand you. I'd burn this whole hill down for my mother if she were alive, and I know it, and knowing it means I can't throw stones. But Tomas—" her grip tightened, "—listen to me. Whatever Brandt asks next, you bring it to this room before you answer him. Whatever letter, whatever whisper, whatever price. You come up these stairs and you say it out loud. Because the next page he asks for might be mine."

The words landed like cold water. Tomas looked up sharply. "Yours? Your entry's in the middle sections, the early ones—"

"That's not what I mean." Jude rose, pulling her coat closer, and at the doorway she paused, silhouetted against the snow-glow, and for a moment she looked less like an apprentice and more like a keeper already — like the woman the observatory had been growing all these years without anyone noticing. "Voss is coming back. I received a letter. He needs his page, and his page is in your pocket history, sold to the man who wants him declared an enemy. So whatever bargain you think you've finished, Tomas — it hasn't finished you. It's just begun."

She slipped out. Her footsteps faded down the corridor, unhurried, deliberate, answering slowly.

Tomas sat alone with the lamp and the ticking wall and the snow coming down over the orchard, over the stumps and saplings and the felled tree whose page he had sold, and he understood at last what he had traded: not paper. A promise. A promise made by a child to a frightened boy in this very room, and promises, he knew now — holding the ledger's ghost in his empty hands — were the only currency in this house that could never be counterfeited, only spent.

Downstairs, very faintly, the front gate latch clicked in the wind.

Someone was standing in the snow below the window, at the edge of the light, watching the observatory.
