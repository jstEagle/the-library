## 13. Midnight Adjustment

The observatory at midnight was a different country.

Jude had learned its borders by heart in five years: the creak of the third stair that meant Marta was awake; the way the dome's iron ribs ticked as they cooled, contracting around the day's heat like a fist slowly unclenching; the smell of the clock room itself — machine oil and old walnut and, beneath it all, the faint green ghost of the pressed flowers that had once lived inside the case. She knew which silences kept people safe. She had built her whole life on them, floor by floor, the way her mother had taught her to build a wall: stone against stone, each one chosen for what it could bear.

Tonight she sat alone before the master clock with a lantern turned low and a decision in her hands heavier than any gear she had ever lifted.

The clock ran slow. Not broken — slowed. She had proven it three nights running, carrying her father's old pocket chronometer up from the workshop and setting it against the wireless time signal from the capital, then holding both to her ears like two hearts that disagreed. The master clock lost eleven seconds a day. Eleven seconds, deliberate, patient, shaved off by a governor weight adjusted a half-turn out of true — a sabotage so gentle it would take an auditor weeks to find it, and so total that every official timestamp the observatory had issued since spring was wrong. Every record Brandt's men had carried down the hill in their grey archive boxes bore times that did not match the sky.

Someone inside had done it. Someone who loved this house.

She thought she knew who. She hoped she was wrong.

* * *

The lamplight made small gold islands on the workbench. Jude laid out her tools the way she always did — files left to right by fineness, screwdrivers by size, the brass drift and the mallet with the worn leather head her mother had owned before her. Ritual first. Ritual was how you told your hands what your heart was afraid to say.

The governor assembly came apart under her fingers in eleven movements, each one memorized, each one a kind of prayer. The regulating weight sat where it should not have sat, threaded three turns deep into the slow side of its arc. Whoever had turned it had used a proper key, had not scratched the brass, had wiped the housing clean afterward. This was not vandalism. It was craftsmanship. It was care.

That was what made it unbearable.

If a soldier had done it, she could have hated it cleanly. If Tomas had done it out of spite — but no, Tomas's betrayals were loud and desperate and came with his brother's name attached, and this was quiet, this was patient, this was someone thinking in years. Only one person in the house thought in years. Only one person had kept the ledger for four decades and understood exactly what a falsified timestamp could do: make a rescue look like it happened after curfew, or before an amnesty, or on a date when a certain border post had changed hands. Make forty years of mercy into forty years of crimes that could be proven.

Marta Ilves had slowed the master clock to protect the dead and the disappeared whose names filled its case.

And if Jude corrected it — if she restored true time — then the moment the army compared the clock against the seized records, the discrepancies would surface like drowned things. Brandt would have arithmetic where he now had only suspicion. He would have proof that the town's official time had been lying, systematically, deliberately, in one direction only, for months. He would have a thread, and Brandt pulled threads.

But if she left it — if she let the lie run — then the lie was hers. Every hour it ticked false, she became its keeper. And worse, smaller, meaner: the clock would keep lying about her mother too. Her mother's name was in that ledger beside her own childhood handwriting, and the timestamps around that entry, the dates of the shelter records, the arrival logged for a frightened boy called Aldric — all of it lived downstream of the truth, and the truth was that time is time, and a clock that lies is not protecting anyone. It is only deciding whose version of events gets to be real.

Her mother had taught her that. *A clockmaker's first oath,* she'd said, wiping Jude's small hands with turpentine after a day of filing, *is that you don't get to choose what the hour says. You only choose whether you serve it.*

Jude sat with the open clock until the lantern burned low enough that she had to trim the wick.

* * *

She went down to the kitchen for oil and found Marta there, which she had known she would, because Marta slept the way cats sleep — lightly, in installments, always with one ear aimed at the stairs.

The older woman stood at the stove in her shawl, warming milk she would not drink, and did not turn around. "You've found it," she said. Not a question. In this house they had stopped pretending long ago that anything stayed hidden between them.

"The governor weight. Three turns." Jude set the oil can on the table. "Eleven seconds a day."

"Twelve, in damp weather." Marta finally looked at her, and her face in the stove light was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she despised. "I started in April, when the requisition orders were drafted. I thought — if the audit comes, let the records disagree with themselves. Let nothing line up. A wall of small errors. Errors are hard to hang a man with."

"And easy to hang a woman with, once they're found."

"Yes." Marta said it without flinching, which was somehow worse. "Yes, that's the other end of the lever. I know how levers work, child. I've been pulling this one for forty years."

Jude wrapped her hands around the warm mug Marta pushed toward her, though she didn't want milk. "If I correct it—"

"You'll be telling the truth." Marta's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the terrifying thing about her; her anger and her love occupied the same flat, level register, like water finding its mark. "You'll be telling the truth, and the truth will cost us. It may cost me my freedom. It has already cost me sleep since April, which I counted as cheap." She turned back to the stove. "I won't stop you. I want you to understand that. I will stand in this kitchen and let you walk up those stairs and undo my work, because you are right, and being right is the whole point of everything we built here. But I'm allowed to tell you what it costs."

"Tell me, then."

Marta was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the pendulum swung through its arc, faithful and false, faithful and false.

"It costs me the last thing I had," she said at last. "For forty years I believed that silence was a tool, same as a file or a lathe. You use it to shape something. Shelter. Time. Safety. Tonight I have to consider that maybe I shaped myself into it instead. Maybe I slowed more than the clock." She looked at Jude then, fully, and her eyes were dry and very old. "Correct it. Correct it tonight, while your hands are steady. And when they come — and they will come — you remember that whatever happens to me, you chose the hour over the lie, and that is the only inheritance worth leaving anyone."

Jude drank the milk anyway. It tasted like obedience and like farewell, and she could not have said which.

* * *

Back in the clock room, she worked.

Not quickly. Never quickly — speed was for people who wanted a job finished, and Jude wanted a job *true*. She disassembled the governor completely, degreased it, inspected every tooth of the escapement by lantern light with her loupe screwed into her eye socket until the socket ached. She checked the pendulum's suspension spring for wear, the crutch for play, the beat — listening, listening, the way her mother had taught her, letting the tick and the tock fall evenly across her heartbeat like two feet walking a road.

Then she began the adjustment.

Three turns of the regulating nut. She gave it back one-eighth at a time, closing the case, starting the clock, waiting a quarter hour, comparing against the chronometer, opening the case again. Each eighth-turn was a sentence in a confession she hadn't written yet. The room was cold enough that her breath showed, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the dome wall, a giant bent over a small brass heart.

Somewhere past two in the morning, her hands began to shake.

She set down the key and pressed them flat against her knees and let herself feel it, all of it, because her mother had also taught her that a shaking hand forced to work makes mistakes, but a shaking hand that stops and admits the shaking can begin again honest. She was afraid. Not of Brandt — or not only of Brandt. She was afraid of the shape of her own life if she did this. Every safe silence she had ever learned, every wall she had built stone by careful stone, and now she was going to take out the keystone with her own fingers because the wall had been built around a lie, and a house around a lie is just a larger cell.

They'll take Marta, said the fear. They'll come with their lists and their questions and they'll take her, and it will be your hands that handed her over, your corrected clock, your true time.

Maybe, answered the other voice, the one that sounded like her mother and like the ledger and like a boy hiding in the clock room twenty years ago, breathing through his mouth so she wouldn't hear him cry. But if you don't, then everyone in that ledger stays guilty forever, and guilt is the one thing Brandt can grow like mold in a dark room. Truth doesn't save them. Truth just gives them somewhere to stand while they fight.

She picked up the key.

The last eighth-turn went in at ten minutes past three. She closed the housing, wound the barrel, set the pendulum swinging from rest, and held the chronometer against the master clock's strike train and waited for the top of the hour the way you wait for a verdict.

At four o'clock precisely, the master clock struck four.

Four notes, even, unhurried, falling through the cold air of the clock room and down through the floors of the observatory and out into the sleeping town below — the true hour, spoken for the first time in months, and Jude stood in the middle of it with tears she refused to acknowledge running down her face, because grief and relief had the same salt and there was no one to see the difference.

The clock ran true. Whatever came next would come next on honest time.

* * *

She was cleaning her tools when she heard the gravel.

It came from the orchard side — the soft, irregular crunch of footsteps trying not to be footsteps, and beneath it, lower, the sound of the orchard gate easing shut on hinges that Tomas had sworn he'd oil in September and never had. Jude went still, loupe still screwed in her eye, one hand resting on the workbench among her files like a pianist finding home position.

Through the tall south window, where the glass was oldest and thickest and slightly green, she watched the treeline at the bottom of the orchard, where the pines stood black against a sky just beginning to think about grey.

A figure detached itself from the trees.

Gaunt. Coatless despite the cold, or wearing a coat so thin it amounted to the same thing. It moved along the fence line with the particular economy of someone who had learned to cross open ground fast and low, and it stopped at the gap in the fence where the root cellar door lay hidden under the espaliered pear — stopped, and straightened up, and looked directly at the window.

Even at eighty meters, even in the dark, even after twenty years, Jude knew the way he tilted his head when he was deciding whether to trust someone.

Aldric Voss stood at the edge of the observatory grounds like a man returned from the dead to collect a debt, and the debt, she understood with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear, was a page in a ledger — and dawn was less than two hours away, and downstairs, in the east bedroom, Officer Brandt slept behind a door with his boots polished and his warrant signed.

Jude blew out the lantern.
