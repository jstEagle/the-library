## 14. The Man at the Treeline

The dark came back like a held breath released.

Jude stood in the orchard gate with the dead lantern in her hand and counted her own heartbeats, because that was what she had been trained to do — count, always count, let the numbers hold the place where panic wanted to live. Ten heartbeats. Twelve. In that time the figure at the treeline did not move, and neither did she.

The last light had gone off the western ridge, and the mountains had become a single black shape against a sky still bruised violet, and between her and him lay forty meters of orchard rows that Marta's hands had planted and Marta's hands had kept, one tree for every name, every tree a testimony written in wood and fruit. The army had not touched the orchard yet. Brandt's clerks catalogued paper; they had no vocabulary for trees.

"Aldric," she said, barely above breath.

He crossed the distance the way he must have crossed borders for years — low, unhurried, patient as water finding its level — and when he stopped an arm's length away she could finally see him properly in the blue remnant of evening, and seeing him was worse than guessing at him.

He had been a boy with quick eyes and hair the color of wet river stones. He was thirty now, perhaps thirty-one, and he looked fifty. His cheekbones stood out like tent poles under canvas. The civilian coat he wore had belonged to some larger man once; it hung from his shoulders as if he were its memory rather than its owner. There was mud to the knee on both trousers, and his hands — she remembered those hands, clever with knots and paper boats — were cracked at every knuckle and stained with something old and ground-in.

"You knew I'd come," he said.

His voice was rusted. She shook her head before honesty caught up with her mouth. "I hoped. That's different."

"It's more than most people give." He glanced past her shoulder toward the observatory, its dome a pale skull against the first stars, its windows dark except for the east bedroom where — they both knew it, the knowledge passed between them like a cold coin — Officer Aldous Brandt slept with his warrant folded on the nightstand. "You have guests."

"The audit is staying in the house."

"Then I've come at exactly the wrong time or precisely the right one." Something flickered across his ruined face that was almost the smile she remembered, the one that used to make the summer of her ninth year feel like a conspiracy. "I need an hour, Jude. One hour of roof. Then I'm gone over the pass before first light and you never saw me."

"An hour is nothing."

"An hour is everything. An hour is longer than I've been indoors in four months." He said it without self-pity, the way a man reports weather, and somehow that made it land harder than weeping would have.

Jude turned and looked up at the observatory — at her home, her found family asleep inside it, at the master clock she could hear through stone walls even now, that deep slow heartbeat she had spent five years learning to mend — and understood with total clarity that whatever she did in the next sixty seconds would be the shape of the rest of her life.

Her mother had hidden people. Her mother's handwriting sat in a leather ledger behind a panel of walnut, beside Jude's own childhood name, beside a pressed sprig of apple blossom. Marta had taught her which silences keep people safe. And now the silence itself had walked out of the ledger and put its cracked hand on the orchard fence.

"Come," she said.

* * *

She took him not through the house but around it, along the north wall where Tomas's herb beds had gone leggy and fragrant in the dark, through the woodshed door that she oiled herself twice a year because Marta said a squeaky hinge is a confession shouted to the whole valley. Inside the shed she lit no lamp. She knew it by touch: the stacked cordwood, the barrels of apples sweating sweetness into the cold air, the narrow stair that rose into the observatory's underbelly, built by some keeper a century ago for hauling mercury and machinery and never once, until tonight, for hauling a fugitive past an officer's sleeping door.

At the top of the stair, behind the pendulum case's blind side, there was a space. Every clock has one. The space where the maker admits the mechanism cannot be perfect and leaves room for the human hand. It was barely wider than a coffin laid upright, smelling of oil and dust and brass, and when Jude pressed the panel aside Aldric Voss folded himself into it without complaint, without hesitation, with the ease of a man who had slept in worse places this year and would sleep in better ones someday, God willing.

"The clock runs loud," she whispered. "It'll cover you. Don't move if you hear boots."

"Jude." His voice through the gap in the panel was already going distant, already becoming memory. "My page. You promised."

"I know what I promised."

She closed the panel, and the master clock swallowed him — tick, tick, tick — and she stood alone in the dark with her palm flat against the warm wood, feeling the escapement release its seconds into the room, feeling, absurdly, that she had just locked a living man inside time itself.

* * *

Downstairs the kitchen held the banked heat of Marta's evening fire and the smell of yesterday's bread. Jude did not light a lamp here either. She moved by the amber glow of the coals, and she had just set the kettle on — because her hands demanded work and her mind demanded a lie to hide behind — when the floorboard at the head of the stairs announced Marta Ilves.

Marta descended in her nightgown and shawl with a candle in a tin holder, and she did not ask why Jude was awake, and she did not ask why the kettle was on, because Marta had spent forty years reading the silences of frightened people and she could read this one like large print.

"There's someone upstairs," she said. Not a question.

Jude's throat closed. She nodded.

"In the clock room."

Another nod.

Marta set the candle on the table. The flame stood perfectly straight, as if even fire understood the gravity of the hour. For a long moment the head keeper of the observatory looked at her apprentice, and Jude braced for the arithmetic — the risk weighed, the household counted, the cost per life computed in the currency of survival — but what Marta said was:

"Is it Voss?"

"How did you—"

"Because you went pale in the orchard this afternoon looking at the stump of the third tree, and because you've been sleeping with your boots by the door for two weeks, and because you're my girl and I watch you." Marta lowered herself onto the bench with the small grunt of her knees. "One hour?"

"He asked for one."

"He can't have it." Jude's heart dropped like a dropped weight — and then Marta continued, "He'll take three, because Brandt sleeps like the dead after his wine and wakes like the dead too, and because no one crosses the pass in the dark. We move him at moonset, down the root cellar stair and out through the drainage culvert, and he's at the treeline before the valley can see him. Until then—" she lifted her chin toward the ceiling, toward the great clock overhead whose heartbeat filled the whole building like blood in a body, "—he is a page in our ledger, and our ledger does not surrender its entries to men with warrants."

Jude sat down across from her. The kettle began, softly, to sing.

"Marta. If they find him—"

"They won't find him. But they might find *evidence* of him, which is nearly as bad, so listen." Marta's voice dropped into the register she used for teaching, calm and exact, each word placed like a gear into its seat. "Brandt takes tea at seven. He walks the grounds at half past with the corporal, always clockwise, always the same route — a man who audits time trusts routine more than instinct. Between six and half past, the kitchen door is unobserved. You will bring him food at six. Bread, cheese, the apples from the second barrel — not the first, the first are counted. And you will speak to him quietly, because sound travels down the pendulum shaft like water down a well."

"And if someone else wakes? If Tomas—"

"Tomas," said Marta, and the name sat between them with all its unspoken weight — the missing pages, the cold draft of suspicion that had been moving through the warm house for weeks, "Tomas will be watched. By me. Go on, then. Take him his bread while it's still dark. A man who's crossed a mountain should eat before he hears bad news."

"What bad news?"

"That the pages he came for aren't here." Marta met her eyes over the candle flame, and in them Jude saw the thing Marta almost never showed: grief, worn smooth as river stone from long handling. "That's yours to tell him, child. You made him a promise. Promises include their consequences."

* * *

She brought the food up through the woodshed stair at ten past five, when the windowless dark was at its deepest and the house above them breathed with the particular silence of people pretending to sleep. When she pressed the pendulum panel open, lamplight shielded under her hand, Aldric unfolded himself stiffly, joints cracking like green twigs, and took the plate in both hands as though it were something holy.

He ate the way starving men eat — fast, then forcing himself slow, then fast again — and Jude sat on the floor beside him with her back against the clock case, feeling the mechanism breathe through the boards, and waited until the bread was gone before she told him.

"The pages are gone, Aldric."

His chewing slowed. Stopped. He did not look surprised, she realized. He looked confirmed. As if he had carried the possibility across three borders and a war and had only been waiting for someone to hand it its true weight.

"Torn out?"

"The final section. Everything from the autumn of '18 forward." She hesitated. "Someone in this house did it. We don't know who. We don't know why."

"I know why." He set the plate down with terrible care. "Everyone wants something. Even the people who love you. Especially them, sometimes." He rubbed his face with both hands, and in the shielded lantern light she saw how the tendons stood in his wrists, rope over bone. "But the page isn't only paper, Jude. Tell me about my tree."

"My tree?" — and then she understood. "The orchard. One tree for every name."

"Mine was the third row, near the wall. A plum. Your mother planted it with me — she put my hands in the dirt over the roots and said, *now the ground knows your name too, and the ground keeps better records than governments.*" His voice caught on the words and steadied itself, an escapement releasing after a jam. "Is it alive?"

Jude looked at the floorboards. She could not make herself look at him.

"It was cut down," she said. "Years ago. Before I came back to the observatory. Marta won't say who ordered it or why. There's a stump. This afternoon I found—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Under the bark at the base, someone carved initials. A.V. They've healed over mostly. The tree tried to close the wound and couldn't quite."

Aldric was silent for a long time. Above them the master clock gathered and released its seconds, indifferent and faithful, the only honest witness either of them had left.

"Then we build the page another way," he said at last. "From memory. Mine and yours. Names, dates, the flowers — Marta pressed flowers in the book, didn't she? Apple blossom for me. I remember watching her press it in the big iron press on the shelf by the window while I pretended to study the star charts. I remember everything from that summer. It's the only summer I own outright."

"We can reconstruct it. Enough of it. Enough to stand in a tribunal and say *this man was sheltered, this man was sworn for, here are the names who swore.*"

"And the tribunal sits when?"

"Day after tomorrow." The word *tribunal* in the clock room felt like ice water down the pendulum shaft. "Brandt's audit closes then. Whatever he has, he presents it then."

"Then we have tonight and tomorrow." Aldric leaned his head back against the case, and the great clock's vibration ran through his skull and hers both, their two heartbeats offered a third rhythm to keep. "Jude. Why did you restore the clock? I heard — word comes up the valley, slowly, but it comes. The official timestamps changed. Brandt has proof now of falsified civic time."

"Because it was lying," she said simply. "And because I thought truth would save us. I'm beginning to wonder if I chose the right weapon."

"You chose the only weapon that doesn't rot in your hands." He closed his eyes. "Rest. I'll wake you if—"

A sound stopped them both.

Not boots. Not voices. Something smaller: the far door below, the woodshed door, easing open on its oiled hinges — the hinge she herself kept silent, the hinge only family used — followed by footsteps on the lower stair. Unhurried. Familiar. The particular tread of someone who had climbed these stairs ten thousand times and had no need to fear them.

Tomas's silhouette rose into the lantern light, and his face, when he saw them — the fugitive unfolded from the clock's blind side, the bread crumbs, Jude's hand frozen on the panel — went through three expressions in the space of a breath: recognition, horror, and then something worse than either.

Calculation.

"Tomas," Jude said, rising, putting herself between him and the pendulum case, her voice low and steady and absolutely clear. "Before you decide anything — remember that whatever you do next, you do it in front of me, and I am in that ledger too."

Behind her, inside the ticking dark, Aldric Voss did not move. Outside, far down the valley, the first grey suggestion of dawn was assembling itself along the mountain's shoulder, hours early in her terror, hours late in fact — and somewhere below, in the east bedroom, Officer Brandt turned over in his sleep with the warrant beneath his pillow, waiting for morning to tell him what it knew.
