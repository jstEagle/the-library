## 16. Discovery

Tomas had not slept. He had lain in his narrow bed in the apprentices' wing listening to the observatory breathe — the tick of the regulator clock down the corridor, the wind working at the shutters, the small animal sounds of a house that believed itself safe — and all the while the pages he had sold sat in Officer Brandt's dispatch case like a stone in his own chest, heavy and cold and growing heavier with every hour.

He had told himself it was arithmetic. That was how he had learned to survive after his father died: reduce everything to sums. His brother Emil was a number — a cell number, a trial date, a sentence that could be measured in years. The ledger pages were numbers too — names, dates, entries that had slept behind clockwork for decades without anyone bleeding from them. He had stood in Brandt's office three days ago with the torn leather in his hands and made the trade the way a man signs for a delivery of brass stock, and he had walked home through the dark believing the sum balanced.

But sums did not account for Jude's face. Sums did not account for Marta Ilves setting out four cups every morning as she had for twenty years, pouring tea for people who would be arrested by Thursday if the audit went the way Tomas had helped it go. Sums did not account for the fact that last night, passing the pendulum case, he had seen Jude kneeling before it with her sleeve rolled up, cleaning glass, and she had looked up at him with such unguarded trust that he had to leave the room on the pretense of fetching oil.

And sums could not account for what he had seen this evening, from the window of the tool room: a man at the orchard fence. Gaunt, civilian-coated, hat pulled low, standing in the blue dusk with the patience of someone who has walked a long way and cannot afford to seem eager. Tomas had watched Jude cross the grass toward him, watched them speak — briefly, urgently — and then watched her glance back at the observatory windows before letting him past the fence line.

Aldric Voss. It could be no one else. The name Tomas had carried to Brandt in exchange for his brother was already here, inside the walls, under the same roof as the auditors billeted in the east room, eating Marta's bread at the long table downstairs.

The knowledge sat in him like swallowed ice.

* * *

He tried not to decide anything. That was the trick of it — he told himself he was only lying awake, only thinking, and thinking was not yet doing. But his body had already decided. At half past two he rose without lighting a lamp, dressed in the dark, and stood in the doorway of the apprentices' wing listening to Jude's breathing through the wall — slow, even, the breathing of someone who had spent the day hiding a man and had finally let herself sleep. Downstairs, faintly, the master clock struck the quarter hour. Even slowed as it had been, even falsified, it kept its rhythm, faithful to whatever time it had been told to keep.

*Faithful to whatever time it had been told to keep.* Tomas thought of himself that way and had to press his hand flat against the cold plaster until the thought passed.

There was a telephone in Brandt's office at the requisitioned schoolhouse. There was also a sentry at the schoolhouse door, but the sentry knew Tomas — knew him as the apprentice who ran errands between the observatory and the audit, who fetched parts lists and carried certified copies, whose manner was so correct, so punctiliously helpful, that no one had thought to wonder why. Half-loyalty bought access; that was the whole of its economy.

He told himself he would only confirm the timing of the search. They were going to search anyway — Brandt had said so twice, had said it almost gently, *we will come to the observatory within the week, Herr Reiner, and I would prefer you not be surprised by it*. If the man Voss was there, then the search would find him whether Tomas spoke or not. All Tomas would do was choose the hour. Dawn, when the household was asleep and no one would do anything foolish. Dawn, when resistance was still tangled in dreams.

That was the lie he carried down the hill through the wet grass, and he recited it the whole way like a rosary: *I am not betraying anyone, I am choosing the hour, I am sparing them the noon raid, the market-square arrest, the guns in front of the children.* By the time he reached the fence around the schoolhouse the rosary had worn thin enough to see through, and he could read the truth printed beneath it plain as a parts invoice: he was afraid. Not of what Brandt would do to the others — or not only of that — but of what would happen to Emil if the search failed and Brandt concluded his informant had given him less than everything. Fear had a voice, and Tomas had discovered it sounded exactly like his own conscience.

The sentry saluted him through. In the office, a green-shaded lamp burned over papers stamped in red. Brandt looked up from a report with the unhurried courtesy that Tomas had come to understand was worse than anger — anger you could fight; courtesy you could only endure.

"Herr Reiner. At this hour."

"There's something you should know," Tomas said, and heard his own voice come out level, which seemed an obscenity. "The man you're looking for. He came tonight."

Brandt set down his pen. He folded his hands. For a moment he simply looked at Tomas with pale, patient eyes, and Tomas felt the whole architecture of his justification collapse into rubble, because those eyes were not surprised. They were not even particularly interested.

"He came tonight," Brandt repeated. "To the orchard fence." A pause, delicate as a watchmaker's tweezers. "You understand, of course, that we have had a man watching the treeline since Tuesday."

The floor tilted. Tomas gripped the back of the chair.

"Then why—"

"Why have we waited?" Brandt smiled faintly. "Because a bird in the hand is worth nothing if you frighten the flock. Because we wished to know who would shelter him, and now we know. Because—" and here his voice softened into something almost kind, "—because your brother's file crossed my desk this afternoon, Herr Reiner, and I am pleased to say the review went favorably. You may consider your obligation discharged."

Discharged. The word dropped into Tomas like a plumb weight and found no bottom. He had sold the pages, sold the name, sold the hour of dawn — and it had purchased nothing that wasn't already bought, changed nothing that wasn't already decided. He had betrayed his family for a door that had been open all along.

"The search," he said. His mouth was dry. "At dawn, as I—"

"At first light," Brandt agreed, returning to his papers as though Tomas had brought him a cup of coffee rather than a household of sleeping people. "Thank you for your diligence. Go home, Herr Reiner. Sleep well. You've done your duty."

Tomas walked back up the hill in the grey hour before morning, past the dew-heavy orchard, past the fence where a man had stood in the dusk asking only for one hour of shelter, and he stopped once, in the middle of the path, and was sick into the grass. Then he straightened, wiped his mouth, and went inside to wake no one, to warn no one, to sit in the dark kitchen like a stone while the house he loved slept around him on the last morning of its innocence.

* * *

They came at first light, exactly as promised: two lorries grinding up the switchback road with their engines deliberately loud, and twelve soldiers, and Brandt in his pressed uniform stepping down onto the gravel with the air of a guest arriving slightly early for dinner.

Marta Ilves met them on the front steps in her keeper's coat, buttoned to the throat, silver hair pinned with mathematical precision. She did not look at the lorries. She looked at Brandt the way she looked at a clock brought in for repair that some fool had oiled with machine grease.

"You'll wipe your boots," she said. "This is a civic institution under continuous operation since 1871. The master clock is never left untended, and I am presently tending it. If your men are to move through my observatory, they will move in single file, they will not touch the instruments, and they will sign the visitors' register, which has been maintained without a gap in eighty years and will not acquire one today."

For a moment Brandt only blinked. Then — Tomas saw it from the shadows of the kitchen door — he inclined his head, amused, and gestured to his sergeant. Boots were wiped. A soldier with a clipboard was produced and presented to Marta like a duelist offering his second. She marched him to the hall stand, uncapped the pen herself, and dictated the entry: *Inspection party, twelve plus officer, 6:14 by observatory standard time.* She did not say — and Tomas felt the irony land in his chest like a splinter — that observatory standard time was currently a fiction, shaved minutes deep.

What followed was ceremony weaponized. Marta accompanied each detail personally. When soldiers moved toward the instrument rooms, she recited the calibration protocols for the transit telescope until the sergeant's eyes glazed and he ordered his men out rather than risk disturbing "the alignment schedule." When they reached the archive shelves — already thinned by requisition — she demanded inventory receipts for every box touched, in duplicate, and stood correcting their paperwork in red pencil while they worked. When Brandt himself paused at the pendulum case, she stepped between him and the glass with the smoothness of long practice.

"The pendulum is rated weekly," she said. "It was rated yesterday. Any handling before next week's rating invalidates the certificate, and the certificate is filed with your own bureau in the capital, Herr Officer. I can send them a copy of today's irregularity, if you prefer."

Brandt studied her for a long moment. "You run a very orderly house, Frau Ilves."

"I run the town's time," Marta said. "Order is the entire profession."

But Tomas, watching from the kitchen where he had stationed himself like a piece of furniture, saw what the soldiers did not: Jude slipping out the back with a lantern hooded under her coat, moving along the orchard rows in the grey light, and behind her, bent low, a gaunt figure in a civilian coat. They went row by row, tree by tree, pausing in the shadow of each trunk while a patrol passed along the garden wall, and then crossing the open ground between the apple rows in the ten-second gaps of darkness. Toward the old root cellar at the orchard's north corner — the one from before the observatory piped water, the one with the collapsed stair everyone had forgotten except, apparently, the people who had needed it forgotten.

Jude moved him like she moved through the clock works: precisely, unhurried, trusting the mechanism. And Tomas stood in the kitchen with his guilt rising through him like floodwater through a cellar stair, understanding that the only reason she had those ten seconds of darkness was that Marta was upstairs spending procedure like coin, and the only reason the patrols' routes were predictable was that Tomas himself had drawn them for Brandt a week ago, and the only reason any of it might hold was luck — luck that was not going to hold forever, because Brandt had not come for a search. Brandt had come for a failure. A search that found nothing was not an ending for a man like that; it was a beginning.

In the clock room, the master clock ticked on, keeping its beautiful, corrupted time. Below the orchard, in the dark of the root cellar, a man who had crossed a border and a war for one hour of shelter crouched among old roots and waited. Above both, Marta signed the soldiers out of the register at 8:03 and closed the book with a snap like a shot.

Brandt paused on the steps, buttoning his glove. "No irregularities today, Frau Ilves," he said pleasantly. "How disappointing. We'll try again soon. One does hate to leave a job unfinished."

His gaze traveled, unhurried, over the orchard — the rows, the north corner, the ground above the old cellar — and rested there a half second longer than it rested anywhere else.

Then he was gone, and the lorries ground away down the hill, and Marta Ilves turned from the steps and looked directly through the kitchen window at Tomas Reiner, sitting alone in the dark with his untouched tea, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic of her own.

"Emil's release papers arrived at the schoolhouse yesterday," she said quietly, to no one, to everyone, to him. "Signed off Tuesday. Before any of this." She let that stand in the cold air between them. "Whatever you think you bought, boy — check the date on the receipt."

She walked back inside. And Tomas put his face in his hands, because he understood at last that the search had failed, and that this — Marta's voice, quiet and terrible; Jude somewhere below the orchard with a fugitive; the family fracturing around him like ice underfoot — this was not the end of what he had done.

It was only the sound of it beginning to come apart.
