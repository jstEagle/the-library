## 17. The Arrest of Marta Ilves

The soldiers came back at noon, when the light was flat and honest and there was nowhere for anyone to hide — not in the clock room's brass shadows, not in the orchard's bare rows, not under the great dome where winter sun fell through the slit like a blade laid open on the floor.

Jude heard the lorries first. She had learned their sound the way she had once learned the particular cough of her mother's lungs: a low diesel grumble climbing the switchback road, growing heavier as it grew nearer, until it arrived at the observatory gates with a squeal of brakes that set her teeth on edge. She was standing at the master clock with an oil cloth in her hand. She did not move for three full seconds. In those seconds she thought, absurdly, of the pendulum — of how it swung without opinion, indifferent to armies, keeping its own counsel in iron and inertia. Then she folded the oil cloth into quarters, because her hands needed something lawful to do, and went down to meet them.

There were six this time, not four. Officer Brandt came last up the gravel path, his boots crunching in a rhythm so even it might have been regulated by the very clock they'd come to audit. He wore his courtesy like a pressed uniform. Behind him, two clerks carried a wooden case she recognized from the tribunal notices posted in the square — a portable typewriter, its keys wrapped in oiled paper against the cold.

"Fräulein Nakamura." He removed his glove to shake her hand, which somehow made it worse — the bare skin, pale and dry, briefly clasping hers. "A formality today. Nothing more."

*Nothing more* was a phrase Jude had come to distrust above all others. It was the phrase her mother had used the summer Aldric Voss slept in the clock room — *nothing more than a boy passing through* — and the phrase Marta had used about the ledger — *nothing more than a record of gardening*. The world's cruelties traveled disguised as small things.

Behind Brandt, soldiers were already moving through the ground floor with a list. She watched one run his finger along the spines of the almanacs in the reading room. Another stood at the foot of the stairs to the clock room, not blocking it exactly, simply occupying it, the way weather occupies a valley.

"The search is concluded?" she asked. Her voice came out level. Five years of apprenticeship had taught her hands to be steady; apparently it had taught the rest of her too.

"It is concluded," Brandt said. "And thorough. Your cellar is remarkably well built. Pre-war stonework. The kind of thing a man could wait out a season in, if he were patient." He smiled faintly, watching her face for the flicker he wanted. "We found no one. I want you to know that I reported that accurately. No one."

The *no one* landed like a stone dropped down a well. Jude counted the seconds before the splash and heard none.

"But," Brandt continued, turning toward the reading room windows, where the town lay below in its gray morning haze, chimneys and rooftops and the frozen river, "a search that finds nothing is still a search. And searches generate paperwork. And paperwork—" he tapped the clerks' case lightly, almost affectionately— "paperwork generates questions. You understand. I am only the instrument. The audit requires—"

He stopped. Downstairs, in the entrance hall, there had been a sound. Not loud. A chair scraping. A voice — Marta's voice, saying something in that tone she used for correcting apprentices' arithmetic, mild and absolute at once.

Jude's heart performed something irregular.

She followed Brandt down. It took everything she had not to run, and everything she had not to be slow.

* * *

Marta Ilves stood in the entrance hall in her gray work dress and her good shawl, the one with the knotted fringe she wore for official occasions and funerals, and Jude thought with a lurch that she must have chosen it deliberately, knowing which of the two this would be. Her white hair was pinned with the plainness of a woman who had decided long ago what mattered and what did not. Beside her, Tomas stood with his hands at his sides and his face the color of paper left out in rain, and behind him the younger apprentices — Anneli and Piet, fifteen and seventeen — were pressed against the coat rack like moths seeking a wall.

Brandt unrolled a document on the hall table with the ceremony of a man laying out instruments before surgery.

"Marta Ilves," he read, "keeper of the regional time standard, you are charged under emergency statute eleven with falsification of civic records, specifically the deliberate misregulation of the official time standard over a period of no fewer than fourteen months, resulting in the corruption of timestamps upon documents now held in evidence." He looked up. His eyes were not cruel. That was the terrible thing about them; they were merely attentive, the way a scale is attentive. "You are further charged with harboring undocumented persons, count unspecified, duration unspecified. The specification will follow investigation."

"I keep the clock," Marta said. Her voice did not rise. It never rose; it descended, when anything, settling onto a sentence like a bird onto a branch. "I have kept it for thirty-one years. If it ran slow, it ran slow the way all clocks run slow. Friction. Temperature. Age. Ask your own horologists. Time is not a moral instrument, Officer. It does not testify."

"Time testifies constantly," Brandt said. "That is rather its profession. Fourteen months, madam. An average deviation of ninety seconds per day, always in the same direction, always corrected in bursts — corrections made, I note, at irregular intervals, as though someone wished the drift to look natural." He folded the document. "Someone in this house understood exactly what those minutes were worth. Someone gave every stamped receipt, every register entry, every border log a shadow of doubt wide enough to drive a family through."

Jude felt the words go through her like a gear tooth through a fingertip — that specific, intimate, mechanical pain. Because it was true. Because she had found the sabotage and understood it, and then she had undone it, minute by minute through a midnight in February, alone among the weights and wires, restoring true time because truth was the only thing she had ever been taught to serve. And true time had become the ruler against which every falsified timestamp could now be measured, cleanly, irrefutably. Her honesty had built the gallows' frame. All Brandt had to do was hang the arithmetic on it.

She looked at Tomas. Tomas was looking at the floor tiles, counting them, perhaps, or praying to whatever god presided over men who had sold pages of a ledger to buy a brother back. His jaw worked once. He said nothing. The silence between him and Marta was a whole conversation, and Jude, who had grown fluent in silences in this house, read every word of it.

"You'll come with us," Brandt said to Marta. Not a question. "For questioning. The charges permit detention pending specification."

"And my house?" Marta asked.

"Will remain staffed. Under supervision." A glance at the soldier on the stairs. "Your apprentices may continue their work. The clock will be kept running. The state has an interest in accurate time, as you know better than anyone."

Something moved at the corner of Marta's mouth — not quite a smile; more the ghost of one, the way a tree's shape remains in a stump. She turned to Jude, and for one moment the hall, the soldiers, the cold, all of it receded like tide going out, and there were only the two of them standing in the entrance where five years ago a frightened girl had first climbed these steps smelling of coal smoke and river.

"Jude," Marta said. "The third-week winding. Don't let Piet rush the great weight. It likes to be lowered slowly."

"Yes, Marta."

"And the greenhouse glass on the south wall of the orchard — there's a crack over the fig. Patch it before the next frost or you'll lose the grafts."

"Yes, Marta."

That was all. That was everything. Instructions for clocks and fig trees, delivered in front of six soldiers and a typewriter, and each one a key turning quietly in a lock: *the winding schedule tells you when the officers change shift; the orchard path is still yours; keep the living things alive.* Jude received them the way she had learned to receive everything in this house — with a steady face and a heart going like a flywheel.

They put no handcuffs on her. They didn't need to. Marta Ilves walked out of her own front door between two soldiers with her chin level and her fringe-knotted shawl straight, and at the bottom of the steps she paused, just once, to look back — not at the house but upward, at the dome, where the transit telescope waited under its canvas hood for a clear night that might be a long time coming.

Then the lorry door shut, and the engine caught, and the sound climbed away down the switchback road until it was indistinguishable from wind.

* * *

The house after she was gone was unbearable in the way that a stopped clock is unbearable — not broken, precisely, but emptied. Jude moved through it doing the tasks Marta had named, because tasks were the only language her grief spoke fluently. She patched the greenhouse glass over the fig, pressing the putty along the crack with her thumb until the seam disappeared. She supervised Piet's lowering of the great weight, hand over hand, slow, slow. She wound the regulator in the corridor and listened to its tick rejoin the master clock's tick, the two voices slightly offset, like people talking in different rooms about the same sorrow.

Tomas did not come near her. He worked in the archive room with the supervised clerk watching him, sorting boxes with exaggerated correctness, and twice Jude passed the doorway and saw him stop mid-motion, one hand full of papers, staring at nothing, and then force himself back into motion. Once their eyes met. He opened his mouth. Whatever he meant to say, the clerk cleared his throat, and Tomas swallowed it, and Jude walked on, and the cold draft that had entered the house weeks ago — the draft of torn pages and secret bargains — blew through her again, colder now, because Marta had known. Jude was suddenly certain of it. Marta had known what Tomas did, had perhaps always known, and had said nothing, had burned nothing, had confessed nothing, because that was the discipline she had kept for forty years: you did not hand the state a single thread more than it could pull out of you by force.

And now the state had pulled out Marta herself.

Anneli found Jude in the clock room at dusk. The girl stood in the doorway hugging her elbows, and in the brass half-light her face looked older by years.

"They're saying in the town," Anneli whispered, "that they're questioning everyone who ever signed the visitor registers. Everyone whose name is in any box they carried down the hill. Herr Weissmann the ferryman was taken this morning. And the Kessler woman who runs the boardinghouse." Her voice thinned. "Jude — my aunt sent flour to the Ilves house every month for ten years. Is that a name? Would that be a name?"

Jude closed the cabinet she had been pretending to clean. She thought of the ledger, hidden now where no search would think to look — not in the clock case, which the army had photographed down to its screws, but sewn into the horsehair ticking of the spare-room mattress, flat and patient, forty years of names sleeping inside a thing designed to be slept upon. She thought of the reconstructed page in its oilcloth envelope beneath a floorboard, and of the man those pages belonged to, who had walked out of the root cellar four nights ago and into the pine dark beyond the treeline, carrying a cutting grafted from his own felled tree.

"Yes," Jude said. "It would be a name. But names are only evidence if someone reads them aloud, Anneli. Until then they're just ink." She heard herself say it and recognized, with a strange double vision, both Marta's voice and her own mother's layered underneath it — the inheritance of careful speech, passed hand to hand like a lamp in a draft.

But the lamp was guttering. Every day the interrogation schedules tightened; the town crier posted them now outside the church, columns of surnames and hours, and Jude had begun to study them the way she studied gear trains, looking for the load-bearing tooth. Marta's name appeared for Thursday, ninth hour. Below it, in the same column: *Nakamura, J. — Friday.*

Her own name. Printed. Scheduled. As though she were a component already installed in some vast machine, awaiting only her turn on the bench.

Piet brought supper up to the clock room that night — bread, hard cheese, tea gone lukewarm on the stairs — and ate his share in silence across the workbench from her. He was seventeen and tried to be brave the way boys do, by being busy, and he had spent the day polishing brass that did not need polishing.

"They can't hold her," he said finally. "She hasn't done anything. Keeping a clock isn't a crime."

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't."

"So they'll have to let her go."

Jude looked at the master clock, at its honest restored pulse, sixty beats to the minute, exact as a conscience. *You did this,* said a voice in her that sounded like fear wearing her own face. *Every minute you gave back, you gave them a measuring rod. If you had left the lie running, the timestamps would still be fog, and fog shelters everyone.* And another voice, quieter, deeper, the one that lived below the waterline of her thoughts where the truth kept its own time: *No. The lie was already found. Brandt knew before you touched it. The only question was who would stand in front of it when it broke — and Marta stepped there herself, on purpose, the way she has stepped in front of everything for forty years.*

Still. Knowledge did not blunt the blade; it only told you which way it was falling.

* * *

Friday came with snow, fine and dry, ticking against the windows like sand poured through an hourglass. Jude dressed with care — the dark wool dress, hair braided tight, the small competence of her appearance assembled piece by piece like a movement going into its case. In the mattress, the ledger slept. Beneath the floorboard, the page. On her mother's grave down in the terraced cemetery, snow lay equally on all the stones, erasing distinctions the law had never managed to erase.

At the gate, a sergeant with a clipboard checked her name against his list. Inside the customs-house annex that had become the audit's offices, corridors smelled of wet wool, ink, and boiled coffee, and doors stood numbered like cells in an enormous mechanism. A clerk led her to a room with one table, two chairs, and a window high enough to show only sky.

Officer Brandt arrived with a folder and no typist. He sat, aligned the folder's edges with the table's edge, and regarded her for a moment with that scale-like attention.

"Fräulein Nakamura. Thank you for your punctuality." Almost gently: "Your keeper taught you that, I imagine."

"She taught me everything about time," Jude said.

"Did she." He opened the folder. "Then you'll appreciate the precision of what follows. We have established, to within four seconds per event, the pattern of drift in the civic time standard covering fourteen months. We have receipts, border logs, ration stamps — all bearing timestamps now demonstrably false. We have witness testimony placing undocumented persons at this observatory across three decades. And we have a charge sheet for Marta Ilves that will, I regret to say, proceed to tribunal unless mitigating evidence is presented." He folded his hands. "Mitigating evidence, Fräulein. That is a term of art. It means: things that lessen blame. It can also mean things that transfer it."

The snow ticked at the high window. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, softly, the way doors close around here — carefully, as if even the building had learned which silences keep people safe.

"What are you asking me to do?" Jude said.

"I'm asking you," Brandt said, "to tell me about a boy named Aldric."

The name hung in the room like the smell of smoke after the fire is out. Jude sat very still, hands folded, breath measured, the apprentice's stillness perfected over five winters — and beneath the stillness, something turned over and began, with terrible steadiness, to swing. Because she had promised a frightened child, years ago, that the ledger would speak for him if he ever needed it. She had promised a dying woman's memory that silence would protect the living. She had promised Marta, in effect, nothing at all — Marta had never once asked for her silence, only taught her its cost.

Thursday, ninth hour, they would question Marta. Friday, ninth hour, they had questioned her. The machine was assembled. Every gear was cut. There remained only the choice of what force to apply, and in which direction.

"I never knew a boy named Aldric," Jude said slowly — and watched Brandt's pen hover, waiting — "but I know where you can find the proof of everything else you've asked about."

Brandt looked up.

"Bring me the ledger," Jude said, "and I'll bring you the truth. All of it. Forty years of it. With names."

Outside, the snow went on falling, fine and dry, marking time.
