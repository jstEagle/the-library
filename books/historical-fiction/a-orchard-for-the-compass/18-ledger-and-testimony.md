## 18. Ledger and Testimony

The night before the tribunal, Jude did not sleep. She sat in the clock room with the ledger open on her knees, and she read it the way her mother had taught her to read scripture — slowly, aloud, letting each name have its weight of air.

*Ilves, Marta — keeper. Forty years.*

*Kovac, Elena — seamstress, sheltered 1921, winter of the border closures.*

*Nakamura, Hana — sheltered 1919 through 1933, fourteen years, until papers came.*

And beside that entry, in her mother's small, slanted hand: *Jude, b. 1911.* Her own name, entered like a flower pressed between pages before she was old enough to know she needed saving.

She had spent five years learning which silences kept people safe. She had learned them so well they had grown into her bones, into the way she held her shoulders, the way she answered questions with questions, the way she could stand in a room full of soldiers and be nothing at all, a glass of water on a table, furniture that breathed.

Tomorrow she would break every one of those silences in a single morning.

She closed the ledger and pressed her palm flat against its leather cover, worn soft as a horse's muzzle from forty years of hands. Somewhere below her, in the town, Marta Ilves sat in a cell no larger than the pendulum case, charged with falsifying civic time and harboring persons without documents. Because of Jude. Because Jude had wound truth back into the master clock, minute by minute, alone in the dark, believing honesty was a clean thing, a repair, like truing a wheel or leveling a beat. She had not understood until too late that a clock does not only tell time. It testifies. Every timestamp on every seized record now stood corrected against the observatory's rate, and the corrections pointed like fingers at the woman who had bent time to protect the names.

*I did this,* Jude thought, and the thought did not crush her, which surprised her. It sharpened her. It made the choice simple in the way that terrible things are sometimes simple: there was one person in a cell who did not deserve it, and there was one book on her knees that could take her place, if Jude was brave enough to let it speak.

She rose, wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, and took down the reconstructed page — Voss's page, rebuilt over one hour among the gears from his memory, from the stump of his felled tree still showing its ring-count in the orchard, from a pressed sprig of apple blossom Tomas had missed when he tore the section out. She put both inside her coat, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could keep them company.

Then she sat back down and waited for dawn, because the tribunal opened at nine, and she had promised herself she would not arrive early, desperate, breathless. She would arrive the way Marta answered Brandt's questions: slowly, precisely, on her own schedule.

The pendulum swung. The escapement ticked its patient arithmetic. Jude counted beats until the windows went grey, then gold, then the color of cider.

* * *

The courthouse had been a granary before the war rearranged the town's purposes, and it still smelled faintly of rye and dust, as though justice here were something stored against winter. Soldiers flanked the doors. Inside, the chamber had been furnished with borrowed chairs and a long table draped in army green, and at that table sat the three officers of the audit tribunal, with Officer Brandt at their right hand, precise as a drawn line, his hands folded, his face arranged into an expression of courteous neutrality that Jude had learned to read as hunger.

The room was half full. Townsfolk who dared — old Elena Kovac with her shawl the color of wet slate; the baker's widow; two of the apprentices, pale as candles. Tomas was not among them. She had not seen him since the search, and part of her grieved that he might never see what she was about to do, and part of her knew he was the reason she had to do it.

Her name was called. Not requested — called, the way you call livestock or weather.

She walked the length of the room with the ledger against her ribs and set her hand on the rail of the witness box, and the wood was cold, and she thought: *my mother touched colder things than this and survived fourteen years of hiding in plain sight.*

"State your name for the record," said the presiding officer, a colonel with a voice like a file drawn across iron.

"Jude Nakamura," she said. "Apprentice clockmaker. Keeper-designate of the hilltop observatory."

"You are aware that the observatory's records have been seized under the audit of foreign elements."

"I am aware."

"And you are aware that providing false statements to this tribunal carries penalty."

"I am aware of penalties," Jude said. "I have been aware of them since I was seven years old. That is rather the point."

A murmur moved through the room like wind through wheat. The colonel's eyes narrowed. Brandt's hands, folded on the green cloth, went very still.

"What is that you carry?" the colonel asked.

Jude unwrapped the oilcloth. She laid the ledger on the rail where all of them could see it — the worn leather, the swollen spine, the ghost-smell of apples and machine oil rising from it like a memory given form. Then she set the reconstructed page beside it, edges imperfect, ink still sharp.

"This is the ledger of the observatory," she said. "Forty years of it. It records every person this town sheltered, fed, hid, and freed. Immigrants. Dissidents. Children without countries. It records dates, names, and the tree planted in the orchard for each soul saved. It is the true civic record of this town, and I am entering it into evidence, and I am prepared to answer for every page of it."

Silence. The kind of silence she had spent her life perfecting, turned inside out and handed back to the men who thought silence was theirs.

Brandt spoke first, softly. "Miss Nakamura. You must understand what you're doing. This document implicates dozens of your neighbors. Possibly hundreds, across four decades. You would be naming names."

"Yes," said Jude. "That is what testimony means. My mother taught me that silence keeps people safe. She taught me so well that I nearly let an innocent woman hang for my own caution." She looked at Brandt directly, and found, strangely, that she was not afraid anymore — or rather, that the fear was there but had stopped being in charge, the way pain stops being in charge once you decide what the wound is for. "So let me name them. Let me name all of them, starting with mine."

She opened the ledger.

"Elena Kovac. Sheltered the winter of 1921, when the border closures left her stranded with two children and no papers. The observatory fed them in the clock room for five months. There is an apple tree behind the east wall with her name at its root — go count its rings, gentlemen, and match them to the year."

"Elias Brandt's own clerk, Anselm Roth — yes, your clerk, Officer, the man you arrested Tuesday in the square — appears on page thirty-one. Sheltered 1904. He was six years old. He was carried up the hill in a flour sack during a purge, and the keeper of that day logged it in this book beside a pressed violet, because that keeper believed that mercy deserved documentation, that kindness should leave evidence, that someday someone would come asking and the town should be able to say: *yes, we did this, we are glad of it.*"

Her voice did not shake. She had tuned it like a movement, like a train of wheels, and it ran true.

"Hana Nakamura. My mother. Fourteen years in this town under a false surname, sheltered from 1919 until her papers came through. She learned watchmaking at this observatory. She taught me everything I know about silences, and everything I know about gears. And beside her name, in her handwriting, is mine. *Jude, b. 1911.* This book saved my life before I could walk. And I hid it, and I guarded it, and I let it sit silent while soldiers carried our archive down the hill in boxes, because I thought silence was safety." She lifted her chin. "It is not safety. It is just slow surrender, done politely."

"Enough," said the colonel.

"No," said Jude, "it is exactly enough, and it is not yet all." She turned to the last intact section. "There is a gap in this book. Pages torn out — I will come to who tore them, though I'd ask the tribunal to note that the man who did it acted to free his brother, and that desperation is not the same as treason, whatever your regulations say. But the missing entry has been reconstructed, from living memory, from physical evidence, and from the confession of the man himself. Aldric Voss. Entered into this ledger as a boy in the summer of 1924, sheltered eleven weeks in the clock room while men searched this town for him. A tree was planted for him. It was cut down — by whom, we were never told — but its rootstock lived, and lives still, and I hold a cutting of it in my keeping. He was our guest. We swore him our protection. This book is the proof of the promise, and I am the last witness to it, and I will not unmake him with my silence."

Brandt rose. For the first time since she had known him, something moved beneath the courtesy — not anger, she thought. Recognition. The look of a man watching a mechanism run that he had assumed was broken.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that by your own testimony, you have confessed. To harboring. To concealment. To obstructing this audit. You have named yourself."

"Yes," said Jude. "I noticed. I did it anyway."

"On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that a ledger is a promise made in writing," she said, "and a promise made in writing to a frightened child does not expire because borders move. On the grounds that your audit calls these people foreign elements, and I call them the reason this town has an orchard. And on the grounds—" her voice finally caught, and she let it catch, let the room hear it— "on the grounds that the woman in your cell taught me that some things are worth answering for slowly, carefully, and completely. So ask me your questions, gentlemen. Slowly. I have forty years of answers, and I intend to give you every one."

The colonel looked at Brandt. Brandt looked at the ledger — at the pressed flowers, the faded inks, the small slanted hand of a woman dead five years who had trusted a clockmaker's daughter with the arithmetic of mercy. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its polish.

"The tribunal will recess," he said. "The document will be examined. Miss Nakamura — do not leave the building."

"I wouldn't dream of it," said Jude. "For once in my life, I intend to be exactly where I said I'd be, at exactly the time I said I'd be there. Ask anyone. I keep very good time."

They took the ledger into custody — gently, she noted; even soldiers handled evidence more carefully than they handled people — and she sat in the witness box through the recess with her empty hands open in her lap, feeling lighter than she had felt in five years, lighter and more terrified, the way a clock feels after you've opened its case and can no longer pretend you don't know what's inside.

Through the high granary window, she could see the hilltop observatory, small and white against the sky, its dial catching the noon light. Somewhere in that building was a letter under pendulum glass, a cutting wrapped in damp cloth, a sapling row waiting to be replanted. Somewhere below the town, in a cell, Marta Ilves was sitting the way she answered questions — slowly, precisely, giving nothing away.

The recess stretched past one o'clock, past two. Then the door at the front opened, and the colonel returned with Brandt behind him, and Brandt was carrying the ledger, and his face was the face of a man who had lost an argument with his superiors and won something larger instead, and Jude's heart began, very quietly, to hope.

"The tribunal reconvenes," the colonel announced. "In light of evidence received this morning, the scope of this audit is under review by regional command." His eyes found Jude's. "Your testimony, Miss Nakamura, is not finished. Neither, I suspect, are your troubles. But before we continue—" he paused, and something almost like respect crossed the iron of his voice— "you should know that a messenger arrived an hour ago. A man was taken at the eastern treeline at dawn, traveling without papers toward this district. He claims, as his sole defense, to be named in a ledger he has never seen."

Jude closed her eyes. Against her ribs, where the cutting rode in its damp cloth, the rootstock of a felled tree seemed to warm.

"He is being brought here," the colonel said. "Under guard. Tomorrow morning."

"And his page?" Jude asked. Her voice was steady. She had tuned it herself. "Does he get his page?"

The colonel looked at Brandt. Brandt looked at the ledger in his hands, and then at her, and for a long moment nobody in the granary-courthouse said anything at all — and for once, Jude thought, it was the good kind of silence, the kind that comes before a thing is decided rather than buried.

"That," Brandt said at last, "will depend entirely on what happens tomorrow."
