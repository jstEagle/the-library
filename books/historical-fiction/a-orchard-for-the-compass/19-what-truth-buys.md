## 19. What Truth Buys

Marta Ilves had spent sixty years learning the grammar of waiting, and she had never once been taught the word for it that meant anything but fear.

The tribunal chamber had been a magistrate's court before the war rearranged its furniture. Now it was a room of borrowed authority: a long table draped in army green, three officers whose ranks she could not be bothered to distinguish, a clerk with ink-stained cuffs who wrote everything down twice because once was apparently not obedient enough. The windows had been papered over with muslin so that no one inside could see the town and no one in the town could see what was done here. Marta thought it was the most honest thing about the place — they had at least admitted that light would embarrass them.

She sat where she had been told to sit, which was nowhere in particular, a wooden chair set slightly apart from the benches like a piece of furniture under suspicion itself. Her hands were folded on her knees. Her back was straight. Sixty years of leaning over clockwork had curved her spine only in the gentlest way, an old woman's question mark, and she had decided long ago that she would not let this room turn even that against her.

Jude was speaking.

Marta listened the way she listened to a movement in the escapement — not to the sound itself but to what the sound implied, the health of everything behind it. The girl's voice did not waver. It was not loud. It carried anyway, the way small precise things carry: a tick through a wall, a name spoken clearly into a room built for mumbling.

"—and the entry is dated the twelfth of August, 1919," Jude said. "The pressed flower is a plum blossom. There were four persons sheltered in that year alone. I have counted them. The ledger records forty years of it. Four hundred and eleven names."

"Four hundred and eleven," repeated the officer in the center, whose name was Oberst Kessler and who had the face of a man who had spent his life being disappointed by maps. "You have counted."

"I keep clocks, sir," Jude said. "Counting is the whole of the trade."

Someone at the back of the room made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Marta did not look. Looking was a currency and she spent nothing.

They had brought her in from the cell that morning with clean hands and no explanation, and she had understood immediately — the way one understands a change in barometric pressure — that something above Brandt's head had moved. Officers do not wash their prisoners' hands out of kindness. They wash them when the prisoner is about to be shown to company.

Brandt sat at the end of the green table with his papers squared and his pen capped, and he wore his courtesy like a coat two sizes too small now, buttoned anyway. He had not expected the ledger. That much had been visible from the first hour of the hearing: the flicker in him when Jude produced it, wrapped in oilcloth, entered into evidence page by page while the clerk's pen scratched like a mouse in a wall. He had expected silence. He had budgeted for silence. Silence was the cheapest material in the world and everyone sold it to him willingly.

Instead the apprentice Nakamura had stood in front of three officers and read names aloud for two hours. Her mother's name among them. Her own.

*That child*, Marta thought, watching her, *was raised in my house and has learned to tell time better than any of us.*

"The audit," Kessler said, turning a document with distaste, as though it had arrived damp, "was initiated under provisions concerning foreign elements and civic falsification. You are charged with falsifying official time."

"I am charged," Marta said, "with keeping time badly."

"You kept it falsely."

"I kept it slowly." She unfolded her hands, laid them flat on her knees. "For nine months the master clock ran four minutes slow per week. I adjusted the rate myself. The timestamps on your seized records are therefore later than the true moments they describe. If you wished to know what actually happened in my observatory, you needed only to subtract. Clocks are honest about arithmetic even when their keepers are not."

"And why," Kessler said, "would the keeper of the region's official time falsify it?"

"Because the men who came to consult those records were not consulting history," Marta said. "They were hunting people. A record delayed is a door held shut a moment longer. I held every door I could."

The muslin over the windows glowed faintly with the grey afternoon. Somewhere below, in the square, a cart passed; the sound reached them muffled, as if the whole town were underwater and this room the only dry pocket, and the driest thing in it.

Kessler looked at Brandt. It was a brief look, administrative, the look of a man confirming with a subordinate that a certain file may now be closed, and Marta watched Brandt receive it and understood that whatever verdict was coming had already been written somewhere above all of their heads — in a capital, in a ministry, in a language of budgets and embarrassment rather than truth. Truth had merely provided the excuse.

But she found, sitting there with her old heart going steadily in its case, that she did not mind as much as she should have. Truth does not require that it be loved. It requires only that it be entered into the record. The rest — the motives, the politics, the faces of the men who allowed it — was weather.

"The tribunal finds," Kessler said, and the clerk's pen began its double work, "that the audit conducted by Officer Brandt exceeded its mandate; that the seizure of civic records was disproportionate; that the charge of civic falsification, while technically sustained, was motivated by considerations outside the public interest—" here Brandt's jaw tightened by a millimeter, the smallest mechanical failure, "—and that further proceedings are dismissed. Marta Ilves is released. The records are to be returned to the observatory."

He paused. Papers turned.

"Aldric Voss," he read, "formerly a national of a state with which we are now at war, is cleared of charges of espionage. There being no evidence of espionage. However—" and here the *however* came down on the room like a weight settling onto a train, "—he remains a national of an enemy state present without documentation, and deportation orders stand. He will be escorted to the border within fourteen days. This is not a punishment. It is a regulation."

*A regulation*, Marta thought. How they loved that word. It was the word for everything they broke and everything they hid behind, both at once, a hinge that swung whichever way the wind required.

Tomas's brother was named next — released, effective immediately, a clerk who had spent five weeks in a cell for having once, twenty years ago and aged nineteen, slept in a hayloft that appeared on page thirty-one of a leather book. And then Tomas himself.

"Tomas Reiner," Kessler said, "is found to have removed documents from evidence and attempted to negotiate their sale to an officer of this command." The pause stretched. In the bench beside her, Marta felt Tomas go rigid, a young man braced for the axe the way a sapling braces for frost — all at once, everywhere, uselessly. "In view of his cooperation, his youth, and the circumstances regarding his brother, he will not be prosecuted. His apprenticeship at the observatory is terminated. He is struck from the register of civic trades."

Struck from the register. Marta heard it and thought: we strike a bell, we strike a match, we strike a name. The same verb for making a sound and for erasing one.

The gavel — or whatever this army used instead of one, a knuckle rapped twice on green baize — fell, and the room exhaled into motion.

* * *

Outside, the muslin had been peeled from the windows by some orderly's efficiency, and the late light lay along the street in long amber bars. The town had come out to watch them leave the tribunal the way towns watch anything: from doorways, from the fountain's rim, from the steps of the church with its crooked copper spire. Not cheering. Cheering was still expensive. But standing, which in 1938 in that town was its own kind of speech.

Frau Halden, who sold bread and had sold bread to every soul in that ledger whether or not she knew it, stood at her door with flour to her elbows and looked at Marta as she passed, and nodded. One nod. Marta returned it and felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs and settle there, warm as a stone from a hearth.

Jude walked beside her. The girl had gone quiet since the reading of the verdicts — not the trained quiet Marta knew well, the quiet of concealment, but the other kind, the heavy kind, the quiet of someone carrying more than they can set down yet. Marta let half the street pass before she spoke.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it before it sets like plaster."

"He's being deported." Jude's voice came out flat and careful, each word placed like a gear into its seat. "Cleared and deported. They cleared him in order to deport him. Fourteen days."

"Yes."

"That's what truth bought?"

Marta stopped walking. Around them the street flowed, ordinary and oblivious, a boy chasing a hoop, a dog asleep in a doorway with its paws twitching after some dream of rabbits. She took her apprentice's chin — no. She did not take her chin. She was not a mother in that way and never had been, and Jude flinched from hands near her face the way anyone raised on sudden slaps will flinch forever. Instead Marta stopped, and turned, and gave the girl the full weight of her attention, which was the only inheritance Marta Ilves had ever possessed in quantity.

"Come here," she said, and drew her into the shadow of the baker's awning, out of the flow of the street. "Let me tell you what truth buys, since you've just paid for it and deserve an accounting."

Jude waited. Good girl. She always waited. That was the trouble with her and the salvation of her both.

"It buys exactly what it bought today," Marta said. "It bought me out of a cell. It bought Reiner's brother out of a worse one. It bought forty years of names a legal existence — do you understand what that means? Those pages are evidence now, not contraband. No officer can burn them without burning his own record. It bought Voss his life, which I grant you is the smallest coin on the scale, since his life was never lawfully theirs to take. But hear the rest." She held up one finger, gnarled at the joint, a finger that had adjusted ten thousand escapements. "Truth does not buy what you want. It buys what is real. What is real today is that a man who should have been shot in a ditch will instead stand on a platform in fourteen days and board a train to somewhere the war has not yet learned his name. That is not victory. That is an amputation performed cleanly instead of a death by sepsis. Learn to tell the difference, because you will be offered the confusion your whole life. People will tell you that unless truth buys everything, it bought nothing. They are liars, or they are tired, and you must forgive the tired ones and never listen to either."

Jude's eyes had gone bright and wet and furious, and she blinked none of it away. "His tree," she said. "We planted the cutting. It's in the orchard. It'll bloom next spring and he won't—"

"He'll know it blooms," Marta said. "That is what the ledger is *for*. Not proof for tribunals — though it served, didn't it, better than any of us dared hope. Proof for the person. So that no one who was ever hidden in that house can say, in some bad decade of their own life, *it was a dream, no one ever loved me that much.* The tree blooms. The page exists. The distance between them is just geography, and geography is the one thing clocks cannot fix and orchards don't care about."

She heard herself and paused, surprised. Sixty years of keeping time and she had never once said a sentence that long about feeling. She blamed the girl entirely, and intended to keep blaming her for decades if the world permitted it.

"Come," she said. "We have a household to reassemble and a clock to thank."

* * *

The observatory received them at dusk the way it received everything: without comment, its dome holding the first star in the slit of its shutter like a cupped palm holding water. Inside, the master clock was running — Jude's midnight adjustment still holding true, the pendulum swinging its long patient arc, the tick falling into the rooms like a heartbeat into a sleeping house.

Tomas had not come home. Marta had known he would not — a man struck from the register does not walk back up the hill that evening to eat soup among the people he sold pages to — but she had left his place set at the table anyway, plate and spoon and the chipped blue cup he favored, because a table that stops setting a place teaches the remaining diners that belonging is conditional, and she would die before teaching these children that lesson.

Jude saw the place setting and said nothing, and washed the blue cup and dried it and put it back in the cupboard where it lived, and Marta pretended not to notice that her hands shook doing it.

Later — the lamps lit, the soup eaten, the younger apprentices sent up to their cots with the particular reluctance of children who know the adults need to talk — the two of them sat in the clock room with the ledger open between them on the workbench, and Marta poured the plum brandy she kept for occasions, of which this was somehow both and neither.

"They're returning the boxes tomorrow," Jude said. "All the archive. Two lorries, the sergeant said. He apologized." She said it with a strange note, wonder braided into contempt. "An army sergeant apologized to me."

"Take the apology," Marta said. "It costs nothing and it's the last one you'll get from that uniform."

"Marta." Jude turned her glass, not drinking. The lamplight moved in it. "When I testified. When I said my mother's name in front of them. I kept waiting for it to feel like betrayal — of her, of the silences she taught me. It never came. It felt like introducing her. Like saying, *this is my mother, she fed people, look what she did.*" She looked up. "Was that wrong? Tell me if that was wrong."

Marta considered the question seriously, because the girl deserved seriousness, and because she herself had spent five nights in a cell asking a version of it against the wall.

"When your mother came to me," Marta said at last, "she was nineteen and pregnant and her own family had put her out, and she asked me — the first thing she asked me, before food, before a bed — *will you write it down? Will someone write that I was here?* Everyone thinks the hiding is the mercy. The hiding is nothing. Anyone can hide. The writing-down is the mercy. To be hidden is to survive; to be recorded is to have existed. Your mother understood that before I did." She touched the ledger's cover, worn leather gone soft as ear. "So no, child. It wasn't betrayal. It was the family trade. We are keepers of time. Time is just the name of things that happened, written where the forgetting can't reach them."

The pendulum swung. Below them the town lights pricked on one by one, and beyond the town the treeline stood black against the last violet of the sky, and Marta found her eyes going there despite herself, to the fence, to the gap in the hedge where a gaunt man had slipped through into their keeping one dusk and would slip out through another in thirteen days.

"Thirteen days," Jude said quietly. She had followed Marta's gaze without seeming to. Of course she had. The girl read people the way she read dials.

"Thirteen days," Marta agreed. "And a station platform, and a cutting in a pot, and a page in oiled paper." She closed the ledger. "Which means we have thirteen days to teach this town how to say goodbye without saying surrender. I have never done it. I suspect there is no correct rate for it — no regulation. We will simply have to keep it by hand."

Jude almost smiled. Almost. It was there and gone, like a star behind cloud.

But later that night, when Marta climbed the stairs past the clock room, she stopped in the doorway and saw her apprentice standing at the open casement, looking down the dark hill toward the treeline, one hand resting flat on the cold stone sill — resting there the way a hand rests on a pulse.

And far down, at the edge of the orchard where the felled tree's rootstock still sent up its stubborn shoots, something pale moved in the moonlight. A figure, upright, waiting. As if he too had counted the days, and had come, thirteen days early, simply to stand within sight of the house that had once written him down.

Jude did not call out. Neither did Marta. Some appointments, Marta thought, turning back down the stair, are best kept in silence until it is their scheduled hour —

but she took the ledger off the workbench as she went, and tucked it under her arm, because in thirteen days that page was leaving this house, and she intended to see it delivered properly, on time, to the minute.
