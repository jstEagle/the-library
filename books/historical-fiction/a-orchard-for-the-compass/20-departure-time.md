## 20. Departure Time

The morning of the departure was the kind of morning that made the observatory's work feel holy — high thin cloud, the sun a pale coin behind it, and the air cold enough that every breath arrived visible, like a small confession. Jude rose before the master clock finished striking six. She had not truly slept. She had lain in her narrow bed listening to the tick of the regulator on her wall, that smaller faithful cousin of the great clock on the hill, and she had counted seconds the way other people counted sorrows: one by one, without hurrying them.

There were three hours until the train. She knew this without looking at anything but the light. Five years in an observatory teaches you to carry time in your body like a second heartbeat, and this morning she felt it doubly — the ordinary pulse of the hours, and beneath it, running faster, the countdown to the platform at the valley station where Aldric Voss would board a train going west, out of the country, out of her life, precisely as the tribunal had ordered.

She dressed in the dark. She did not light a lamp because lamps invite questions, and this morning there was one thing she wanted to do before anyone else in the house was awake.

* * *

The orchard wore its autumn plainly now. The rows Marta had planted across four decades stood bare-limbed and patient, their leaves mostly fallen, lying in the frost-stiffened grass like letters no one had gotten around to reading. Jude walked the rows slowly, her boots leaving dark prints in the silvered grass, counting trees the way she had once counted names in the ledger. Each tree was a name. She understood that better now than she ever had. Somewhere in this orchard was a tree for her mother; somewhere, a tree for herself, planted small and crooked the summer she'd first climbed the hill with her hand inside Marta's rough one. And near the north wall, set apart from the others as if by some old shyness, stood the stump of Aldric Voss's tree — felled years ago by someone who feared what growing things could testify to — and from its rootstock, stubborn as any promise, the new shoots had come.

Jude knelt in the frost. Her breath hung over the stump like incense.

The grafting knife was warm from her pocket. She had chosen the cutting two days ago, a finger-length whip of last spring's growth, healthy bark, two good buds. The graft itself she had already made — a splice onto a hardy seedling root, bound in waxed tape, potted in a clay vessel that had once held lamp oil — but this morning she came to cut one more thing: a sprig of the shoots themselves, still green against the season's surrender, to wrap in damp cloth beside the potted graft for the journey. A tree could not cross borders whole. But a cutting could. A cutting was a name folded small enough to travel.

She worked quickly, her fingers numb, her cuts clean. Her mother had been the one who taught her that a wound made cleanly heals more honestly than a wound made raggedly, and she thought of her mother then — of the handwriting in the ledger, the small pressed flowers, the silence kept so long and so well that Jude had mistaken it for peace. She thought: *You gave me your silences. I am trying now to give away something better.*

When the sprig was wrapped and the clay pot settled into a basket lined with straw, she stood and looked back down the rows. Forty years of rescue, standing in orchard formation. She would be keeper here soon — the register was signed, the rate was set, Marta's hands were finally learning to rest — and someday she would walk these rows with apprentices of her own and tell them which tree belonged to whom, and none of it would be secret anymore, because truth had turned out to be the only soil in which anything survived being transplanted.

The master clock on the hill struck the quarter hour. She had forty-five minutes to make the station.

* * *

Marta met her at the door of the keepers' house, wrapped in the grey coat she wore to funerals and tribunals alike, as if she owned only one garment for occasions when the world required gravity. Her face was composed, but her eyes — those eyes that had measured forty years of arrivals and departures from the top of the hill — were wet at their rims, though nothing fell.

"You'll take the cart down?" Marta asked.

"I'll walk," Jude said. "The road is good. And I want the time."

Marta nodded as if this were the most reasonable sentence in the world, which, between the two of them, it was. Then she reached into her coat and produced a small parcel, twine-tied, and pressed it into Jude's free hand.

"For him. Don't open it. Don't ask."

"Marta—"

"He'll know what it is." The old woman's voice was steady, but her jaw worked once, twice, around whatever else wanted out. What finally emerged was quieter: "Forty years I've watched people leave this town. Some ran. Some were taken. This is the first one we are sending off *properly*, with his own name in his own pocket and a piece of his own tree in his hands. Let me have that. Let us all have that."

Down at the gate, the other apprentices waited in a loose, awkward line — Tomas among them, hollow-eyed, stripped of his post but not expelled from the family, a distinction everyone was still learning how to live inside. He held his cap in both hands. When Jude passed him he said, not quite meeting her eyes: "Tell him—" and stopped. Started again. "Tell him I'm glad it was enough. The page. That it was enough."

"I'll tell him," Jude said.

And it was true, she reflected, walking down the hill road with the basket on her arm and the parcel in her pocket, that it had been enough — barely, narrowly, at terrible cost, the way most true things arrive. The reconstructed page had gone into the record with the original ledger. Voss had walked out of the tribunal a cleared man and a deported one, freedom and banishment pronounced in the same breath, and he had looked at her across the courtroom and neither of them had flinched, because they had made their promise years ago under different stars, and promises made in hiding do not break easily in daylight.

The town was quiet in the way towns are quiet after armies leave — swept, watchful, ashamed of its own squares. In the market a woman was stacking winter cabbages with fierce concentration. A boy chased a hoop along the gutter. Life insisting on itself. Jude walked through it carrying a tree in a pot, and no one looked twice, and that too was a kind of grace.

* * *

The station sat at the valley's throat where the river bent, a low stone building with a slate roof and a clock above the platform doors. The clock was wrong. It had been wrong since the army took the town — running fast, or slow, or simply abandoned to its own drift — and Jude stopped beneath it for a moment and looked up at its face with the professional grief of her trade. Someone should regulate it. Someone should climb up with oil and keys and patience and give these people back their true time. Perhaps, she thought, that was the whole of the work she had been born to: not the grand testimony, not the ledger entered into history, but this — standing under broken clocks in small stations, knowing exactly how many minutes the world was lying to itself.

Aldric Voss was already on the platform.

He stood near the bench at its far end, civilian coat, one valise at his feet, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man who has learned to wait in places where waiting is dangerous. The war had written itself into him — the gauntness, the new lines around his eyes, a scar at his hairline that hadn't been there the summer he hid in the clock room — but when he turned and saw her, something in his face unsealed, briefly, like a window opened in a house long shut.

"You're early," he said.

"The observatory keeps the official time," Jude said. "I couldn't very well be late."

He almost smiled. It was strange, she thought, how much of intimacy survives in almosts — almost smiles, almost touches, words begun and redirected, whole sentences carried in the space between two people who have agreed not to say the largest thing aloud.

"Your friends," he said, nodding toward the station road, where Marta and the apprentices had appeared, walking down in a straggling, deliberate procession, as if they had agreed among themselves to arrive neither too eagerly nor too formally. Marta led. Tomas trailed.

"They insisted."

"I didn't expect—" He stopped. His voice, when it resumed, was lower. "I spent seven years believing no one remembered. That the page was ash somewhere, or worse, that it existed and meant nothing. And instead—" He gestured vaguely: the platform, the procession, the woman with the basket. "Instead there is an orchard."

"There is an orchard," Jude said. "There always was. You just weren't shown it."

The train came while they still had words left unsaid — of course it did; trains are punctual even when everything else fails — its smoke rising over the valley rim, its whistle a long iron note that scattered pigeons from the station roof. The porters began their unhurried business. Somewhere a conductor called a destination in a bored voice, naming a city Jude had seen only on maps, a country that had declared this man an enemy and now, having been argued out of it, merely wanted him elsewhere.

They had twenty minutes. They used eleven of them for the giving.

Jude set the basket down on the bench and lifted out the clay pot. The graft looked small against the enormity of what it meant — a stick, tied and waxed, sleeping in dirt — but Voss took it in both hands the way men take infants or communion, with a care that seemed to surprise even himself.

"From the rootstock," she said. "Your tree wasn't killed. Whoever cut it didn't dig. The roots held. Everything growing from them is yours — genetically, botanically, however you want to say it. Plant this, and it's your tree continuing. Different ground. Same roots."

He looked at the little green sprig wrapped in damp cloth beside the pot, then at her. "And the flowers in the ledger," he said slowly. "The pressed blossoms. One per name. That was never just record-keeping, was it. It was planting. Every person saved, a tree. Every tree, a witness."

"Marta's arithmetic," Jude said. "Forty years of it. She says love that leaves no evidence isn't love, it's rumor."

That earned a real smile this time — brief, tired, real. Then Jude drew the ledger page from her coat.

She had copied it fair, in her best hand, onto paper of matching weight: his name, the dates, the room behind the clock case, the small notation in her mother's writing that had started everything — *vouched for, Nakamura* — and at the bottom, where the original had been torn, she had written the reconstruction's provenance in full, because a page that hides its own history is just another silence. Beside it she laid the pressed blossom she had taken from the original evidence file, returned to her after the tribunal with the rest of the ledger: a five-petaled flower, browned with years, that had once grown on a tree now reduced to a stump that refused to die.

"Your page," she said. "All of it. Not a copy to hide — a copy to *keep*. You testified on our soil. You should carry your own record home. Whatever home turns out to be."

Voss held the page without reading it. He had memorized its contents decades ago, she suspected; what he was holding was not information but absolution made physical, a document saying *you were here, you were known, you were sheltered,* in ink that would outlast every border.

Then he reached into his valise and brought out an envelope, worn soft at the corners from handling.

"I wrote this the night before I crossed back over," he said. "I was going to mail it if I lived. Since I'm handing it to you myself, open it whenever you want — today, next year, when the graft blooms. There's no deadline on it. That was the whole point of writing it."

Jude took it. Their hands did not linger — that was part of the promise too, though neither had ever spoken the promise's terms aloud. They had made it as children: *if you ever need it, the ledger will speak for you.* Neither asking. Neither keeping. Love expressed as letting go, on schedule.

Marta came forward then, and gave him the parcel, and whatever was in it made him go very still and look at her for a long moment before bowing his head over it. Tomas shook his hand and said his rehearsed sentence and then ruined it by adding, hoarsely, "I'm sorry," and Voss said, "I know. So am I. We were both trying to save someone. Only one of us picked the right method," and somehow that was forgiveness, or near enough to travel on.

The whistle blew the second call. Porters swung the valise aboard. The engine breathed steam into the cold air, and the platform clock — wrong, drifting, abandoned — showed a departure time that Jude knew to the minute was false.

"Two minutes," she said.

"Two minutes," he agreed.

They stood facing each other while the seconds ran, and neither asked the other to stay, and neither said goodbye, because goodbye was a word for endings and this was not an ending — it was a departure, scheduled and precise, a train leaving on time from a life that had nearly run out of time altogether. What they did instead was this: he pressed his lips briefly, gravely, to her forehead, the way one honors a witness; and she straightened his collar with both hands, the way one tends something entrusted; and then the conductor touched his shoulder, and he stepped up onto the step of the carriage, the clay pot cradled in one arm, the page in his breast pocket, and turned.

"Keep the rate," he said.

"Always," she said.

The train moved. It gathered speed along the valley floor, smoke unwinding behind it like thread pulled from a spool, and Jude stood on the platform with Marta's arm coming around her shoulders and did not wave, because waving was begging, and they had promised. She watched until the train bent around the river bend and was gone — gone the way a pendulum passes the bottom of its arc, at greatest speed, committed, irreversible — and then she took out her pocket watch and noted the actual time of departure in the small notebook she carried for exactly such purposes, because the station clock lied, and someone, somewhere, ought to know the truth of when he left.

* * *

They walked back up the hill in the long gold light of afternoon. At the turn where the road climbed steeply, Marta stopped and looked back at the valley, at the smoke of the departed train still faintly visible, a grey ribbon dissolving into blue.

"You didn't cry," Marta observed.

"No."

"Neither did I. Forty years of practice." She paused. "But you will. Tonight, or in a week, or the first spring morning you walk the orchard and see his row. Grief keeps its own timetable. It's the one clock nobody regulates."

Jude said nothing. In her coat pocket, the envelope sat unopened, patient as a seed. She found she was in no hurry. Some letters, like some trees, ask only to be given time.

By the time they crested the hill, the light had gone amber and long, and the master clock was preparing for the evening wind — the great weights to be raised, the escapement checked, the day's error recorded and corrected. Jude went in alone, as she now did most evenings, and stood in the familiar hush of the clock room, where a frightened boy had once hidden behind the case and a ledger had slept in its compartment for forty years.

She raised the weights. She checked the beat. And then she opened the register to the current date and wrote, in her careful keeper's hand, the day's entry — temperature, barometer, rate, correction — and beneath the figures, unprompted, her pen added a line no regulation required:

*Departure, 14:37, observed and verified.*

She blotted it. She closed the book. And outside the tall south window, the orchard stood in the fading light with its rows of witnesses, and in her pocket the sealed letter waited with the whole of the night ahead of it — and the first hint, faint on the wind from the valley, of rain coming, and with the rain, she would think later, the smell of earth turning over, ready to receive whatever was planted next.
