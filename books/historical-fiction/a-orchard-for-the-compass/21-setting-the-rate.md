## 21. Setting the Rate

The frost came early that year, silvering the orchard grass on mornings when the town below still smelled of woodsmoke and bread, and on one such morning — six weeks and three days after the tribunal, not that Jude Nakamura was counting, except that she was, because counting was now her profession and her prayer — she climbed the observatory hill before dawn to set the rate.

She had the keys on her own ring now. That was the thing she kept noticing, the small absurd miracle of it: the iron key to the east door, the brass key to the clock room, the little steel key that opened the register's clasp, all of them swinging at her hip with every step, singing their small metal song against her thigh. For five years she had walked this road as an apprentice, hands in her pockets, unburdened, unentrusted. Now the keys rode against her body like a second heartbeat, and she found she did not want them to be quiet.

The hill was hers to climb alone at this hour. Marta Ilves no longer rose before the light. Her hands had grown stiff since the weeks in custody — the cold cell, the interrogation schedules that had fractured the found family like a dropped plate — and the doctor in the valley had prescribed rest, warmth, and no clocks before breakfast, which Marta had received with a look that suggested she intended to obey none of it. But she slept later now, and Jude rose in her place, and the town below had learned to expect the observatory's voice at the same second it had always spoken, no earlier, no later.

That was the point. That was all of it, really.

* * *

The clock room smelled of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of the cedar shavings Tomas had been sweeping up for a month as he rebuilt the case's interior — the hidden compartment now emptied of its secrets, its false back replaced with honest joinery, though Jude had asked him to leave the compartment itself. *Leave it open,* she had said. *A clock that hides nothing.* He had looked at her for a long moment with those bruised, careful eyes of his, the eyes of a man who had sold a piece of his own soul to buy his brother's freedom and was still learning how to live in the house of what he'd done, and then he had nodded and fitted the new panel without a lock.

She lit the lamps. The master clock stood where it had stood for eighty years, a tower of walnut and brass as tall as two men, its pendulum swinging in its long glass case like a slow silver breath. Jude stood before it the way other people stood before altars.

Setting the rate was the keeper's most sacred work. Anyone could wind the barrels, oil the escapement, dust the dial. But setting the rate — determining, day after patient day, whether the clock ran fast or slow, and by how many fractions of a second, and correcting it by the weight of a single gram moved along the pendulum's rod — that required the kind of attention that could not be faked. It required you to give the clock your mornings and your midnights. It required you to *listen*.

For six weeks she had listened.

The sabotage was long corrected — the extra weight someone had secreted in the pendulum's bob, the minutes shaved daily off the town's official time so that the seized records would bear falsified timestamps, the treason that had been protection and the protection that had become evidence. She had removed it herself, alone at midnight, minute by minute, choosing truth even as it cut her own family open, even as it handed Officer Brandt the very instrument he used to arrest Marta. She did not regret it. She had decided, in the terrible clarity of that night, that she would not regret it, and she had held the decision the way one holds a hot iron — by committing fully, and quickly, and not looking at the palm.

But correcting a sabotage and setting a true rate were different labors. The first was surgery. The second was tuning an instrument to the earth itself.

She opened the glass case and took up her notebook — a new notebook, plain, its first page bearing the observatory's register number and, beneath it, in her own hand: *Rate log, resumed under J. Nakamura, acting keeper.* Then she took up the transit telescope's reading from the night before, the star-sights Elias had logged at the meridian, and she compared them against the clock's error, and she did the arithmetic she had done forty-one mornings running.

The clock was slow. One and six-tenths seconds over twenty-four hours. It had been slow by almost exactly that amount for nine days, which meant the rate was stable, which meant the time had come to correct it.

She opened the drawer of small weights. Each was labeled in Marta's spidery hand, generations of them, grams and half-grams and one tiny quarter-gram that Marta swore had been placed on the pendulum by the keeper before her, in another decade, another trouble. Jude chose the half-gram, held it a moment in her palm — it weighed less than a pressed flower — and screwed it to the pendulum rod, one third of the way up, where its effect was gentle.

Then she closed the case, and sat down on the keeper's stool, and waited for the next beat to fall.

*Tick.*

The sound moved through the floor, through the stone, through the soles of her boots, into the bones of her. Somewhere below, the town was waking to a clock that would now, within the week, be telling the truth to within a tenth of a second a day. Somewhere below, a clerk in the record office would stamp a document with a time that could be trusted. Somewhere below, a mother would send a child to school and know, without thinking of it at all, that the bell that rang was honest.

*Tick.*

She thought of her mother, whose handwriting she had found in the ledger beside her own childhood name, and who had taught her, in the years before the fever took her, that silence could be a kindness or a cage and that the whole art of a life was knowing which silence you were standing in. Her mother had never told her about the ledger. Had never told her about the boy called Aldric, or the forty years of names, or the orchard planted one tree per soul saved. She had simply taught her daughter which silences keep people safe — and had trusted, perhaps, that one day the daughter would learn which silences to break.

Jude had broken the great one. She had stood in the tribunal hall with her voice shaking and her hands steady and had read the names aloud — her mother's name, her own name, the names of the dead and the scattered and the saved — and had entered the ledger into the record, and had watched Captain Aldric Voss, gaunt and civilian and cleared of everything except the accident of his birth, stand a little straighter as forty years of quiet courage became, at last, a matter of public fact.

Truth had not bought everything. Truth had amputations of its own. Voss was exiled all the same, deported as a former enemy national, and Jude had put his page in his hand at the station and a grafted cutting from his felled tree's surviving rootstock, and neither of them had asked the other to stay, because the promise they had kept since childhood was precise, and precision was the whole religion of this hill.

But truth had bought *this*: the keys at her hip, the stool under her, the rate log in her own hand.

*Tick.*

She stayed until the sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid a long gold bar across the clock room floor, and then she locked the case, and banked the lamps, and went down to sign the register.

* * *

The register lay on its lectern in the front hall, a ledger of office older than any of them, its vellum spine cracked and mended twice. Every keeper since the observatory's founding had signed it on the day of their appointment — name, date, and the clock's measured rate, entered in ink that was meant to outlast them all.

Marta was waiting. She had dressed with ceremony, in the dark green coat she saved for funerals and inspections, her white hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had faced down an army officer in her own hall and had burned nothing and confessed nothing. Her hands were folded on the lectern's edge. Her eyes, when they found Jude's, were dry and very bright.

"The rate?" she asked.

"One and six-tenths slow, corrected with a half-gram at the third station. It should settle to true within the week." Jude heard her own voice and was surprised by its steadiness. "I'll log it daily. If it drifts, I'll adjust."

"If it drifts," Marta said, "you'll adjust. Yes." She turned the register to the blank page and uncapped the ink, and then — an old teacher's habit, and Jude loved her for it — she stepped back. "It's your hand that goes in the book. Not mine. Not anymore."

The hall was not empty. The apprentices had gathered the way they always gathered, badly and warmly: Tomas by the door with his cap in his hands, Elias still in his nightshirt with a coat thrown over it, little Anka perched on the stairs where she was not supposed to be, and old Ferenc the groundsman at the edge of the lamplight, smelling of the orchard. None of them spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them the master clock beat its steady second, the true second now, the honest one, and Jude understood that they had all come to hear it.

She took up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. That was the strangest part. She had testified before a tribunal with less composure than this; she had faced Brandt's courtesy and Brandt's cage of precise questions with terror wearing the mask of competence; and now, signing her own name into a book that would outlive her, she felt only a great and quiet rightness, like a pendulum finding its center.

*Jude Nakamura. Acting Keeper. Rate: 1.6 sec slow, corrected.*

She wrote the date beneath it. She blotted the ink. And when she looked up, Marta Ilves was watching her with an expression Jude had never seen on that stern face — something between grief and harvest, the look of a woman counting a long arithmetic and finding, at the end of the columns, that the sum had survived her.

"You understand," Marta said, and her voice was rough, "that the title is *acting*. The council must confirm it in the spring. There will be a hearing. There may be questions about—" she gestured vaguely, a gesture that contained the tribunal, the ledger, the arrest, the whole terrible autumn, "—about everything."

"Then I'll answer them," Jude said. "Slowly, if they're the wrong questions."

Marta laughed — a short, cracked sound, quickly mastered, but a laugh — and the hall's tension broke with it. Elias whooped. Anka came down the stairs at a clatter and was not scolded. Tomas put his cap back on his head, and when he caught Jude's eye he did not look away, and she did not look away either, and between them passed the wordless treaty they had been drafting for weeks: *I know what you did. I know why. We are both still here.* Compassion was not the same as forgetting. She was learning that the two could live in the same house, the way the clock and its hidden compartment had lived in the same case for eighty years.

"Enough ceremony," Marta said, recovering her sternness the way one pulls a coat back onto a shoulder. "The frost will lift by nine. The saplings won't plant themselves."

* * *

The orchard had been Marta's revelation, that evening among the neglected rows: the pressed flowers in the ledger matched the trees, one tree planted for each person saved, forty years of love leaving physical evidence in wood and leaf. And one sapling with no ledger page — Voss's tree, cut down years ago by someone who had known or guessed, its stump still bleeding dark rings into the soil at the row's end.

They had begun the rebuilding in October, as soon as the tribunal released them back to themselves. Now, in the hard bright cold of late autumn, the work went on. The ground was too frozen for true planting — everyone knew the saplings would wait for spring — but the holes could be dug, the rows staked and strung, the rootstock bedded in straw against the frost. So they dug.

Jude worked beside Tomas in the row they had named for the rescued, driving the spade into ground that fought back like a living thing. The earth came up in dark clods that steamed faintly in the cold. Ferenc had marked the rows with lime, straight as meridian lines, and at the head of each row stood a wooden stake bearing a strip of paper in Jude's careful hand: a name. Her mother's row was the third. Voss's was the last, at the orchard's western edge, where the light went last.

They had grafted the cutting from his tree's surviving rootstock — one of three Jude had taken before the tribunal, one given to Voss at the station, one planted here, one still in the cold frame as insurance against winter. The grafted sapling stood in its pot at the row's end, thin as a whip, three buds sealed tight as secrets, waiting for a season kind enough to let it speak.

"Forty-one names," Anka announced, counting the stakes with a child's ruthless accuracy. "Forty-one holes by spring. Ferenc says the far row has to come out — the apple trees are dead anyway — and we can put in plums."

"Plums," Elias said, "for the ones we haven't saved yet."

The words dropped into the cold air and everyone went quiet for a moment, because they all heard what the boy had said without meaning to say it: that the ledger was not finished. That the orchard was not a memorial but a promise, still accruing interest. That somewhere in the world there were people not yet sheltered, not yet fed, not yet hidden, whose trees were not yet in the ground.

Jude straightened, her back aching, her gloves black with soil, and looked down the rows of stakes and names and holes waiting in the frozen earth. The sun stood high enough now to warm the orchard's western edge. The town below rang its noon bell — one second early, still, the clock's correction not yet settled, but honest in its error and logged as such in her own hand — and the sound climbed the hill and passed over them like a blessing.

Marta stood at the orchard gate, not working, only watching, the way she had watched Jude sign the register. Her breath made small clouds. Her stiff hands were folded on top of her cane.

"Acting Keeper," she said, when Jude came near. She said it the way she said everything, dry as August, but her eyes were not dry at all. "Do you know what the old keepers called this work? Setting the rate. Not *correcting* the clock. *Setting the rate* — as if the clock had a nature, and your work was only to help it be what it already was."

Jude looked at the rows, at the stakes, at the thin grafted sapling waiting in its pot at the western edge. "And what's my nature, do you think?"

Marta considered her for a long moment, the wind moving the white hair at her temple.

"Stubborn," she said. "Truthful at the worst possible moments. Given to keeping promises with a precision that would frighten a tribunal." The ghost of a smile. "You'll do."

* * *

They came down from the orchard at dusk, mud-froze to the knees, hands cracked with cold, and the kitchen fire and the smell of Marta's dark bread received them like arms. It was while they were eating — Anka asleep against Elias's shoulder, Tomas telling the story of the spade and the buried stone for the third time, each telling larger — that Ferenc came in from the gate with the evening post.

"Two for the observatory," he said. "The council's confirmation notice, by the look of the seal. And this."

He set the second item on the table, and Jude's hands went still around her cup.

It was a letter. Thin, travel-worn, its envelope soft at the corners from a long journey. It bore no return address. The postmark was foreign, the ink of the cancellation half-legible, a country she had never written to and no one here had reason to.

But it was the corner of the envelope that stopped her breath — the corner where, beneath the flap, something small and flat and pressed showed through the paper's thinness. A blossom. Dried, pressed with care, a flower from a tree that could only have grown from a cutting she herself had carried, wrapped in damp cloth, to a station platform on the worst and best morning of her life.

The kitchen went on around her — Tomas's voice, the fire's tick, the master clock above them beating its true and settling second — but Jude sat in the middle of it all without moving, her eyes on the envelope, her heart keeping its own time now, quick and hard, like a pendulum at the start of a swing.

The letter was addressed to her. The handwriting was his.

She reached for it as the firelight trembled, and broke the seal.
