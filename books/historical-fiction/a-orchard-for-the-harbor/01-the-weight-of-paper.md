## 1. The Weight of Paper

The snow came down over Vessany like ash from a fire no one would admit had started.

Freya Fairweather stood at the window of the courier house on Delmare Street and watched it settle on the rooftops of the capital, on the shoulders of the statues in the square below—generals and poets alike wearing the same white shroud—and she thought, not for the first time that winter, that a city under siege learned to be beautiful in the way that dying things are beautiful. The lamplighters still made their rounds at dusk, though half the lamps were dark for want of oil. The bells still rang the hour, though the foundries had taken two of them for cannon metal. Everything continued because continuing was the only instruction left.

Behind her, in the long room where the couriers waited between assignments, someone was boiling chicory over a spirit stove. The smell of it—burnt, bitter, faintly sweet—had become the smell of the Republic's last season. She could hear Major Ostrevant's voice through the door of the inner office, low and clipped, speaking to someone she couldn't see. He had summoned her an hour ago. She had been made to wait an hour more. This too was part of the instruction: wait, continue, do not ask.

Her hands were steady. She noticed them being steady the way one notices a held breath—not the act itself but the effort underneath it. Twenty years a courier, first for her mother's trading house and then, after the borders closed, for the Republic's diplomatic service, and her hands had never once shaken in front of a superior officer. They were not going to begin now, on what everyone understood, though no one said, might be the last assignment of the kind there would ever be.

The door opened. "Fairweather."

* * *

Ostrevant's office had once been grand—a merchant's parlor, with a plaster ceiling worked into garlands of fruit—but the Republic had furnished it with army surplus and the fruit overhead now hung above a scarred trestle table, a map case, a brazier burning green-tinted coal that gave off more light than heat. The map on the wall behind him had been updated so many times with inked corrections that the original coastlines had nearly vanished beneath the revisions. The harbor towns were a smear of red pins. One by one they were being pulled.

He did not offer her a chair. He never did; it was a courtesy he reserved for people he intended to insult gently.

"You'll go south," he said. "Through the Verren Pass before it closes—I give it ten days, perhaps less, before the snow takes the road for the season. You'll carry sealed orders to Harbor Command at Callowmere. Three packets." He laid them on the table as he spoke, three envelopes in gray wax, each stamped with the seal of the Provisional Secretariat: the lighthouse and the broken chain. "You will deliver them into the hands of Commandant Levasseur personally. No deputies. No adjutants. If Levasseur is dead, you come home. You do not pass them to his successor."

"And if there is no successor?"

Ostrevant looked at her then, fully, and she saw how much weight he'd lost since autumn, how the skin had begun to hang at his jaw like a flag with the wind gone out of it. "Then the orders are moot, aren't they."

It was not a question. She did not answer it.

"The third packet concerns disposition of records," he went on, as if she hadn't spoken. "Harbor Command is to prepare the coastal registry for evacuation or destruction, per annex. You are authorized to witness the burning. In fact—" and here he paused, and drew from a drawer a fourth envelope, thinner than the others, worn soft at the corners as if it had traveled far already, which it had, "—there is this."

She knew it before she touched it. She knew it by its weight, or by the particular way he held it, two fingers, at arm's length, the way one holds something that has already been grieved.

"The Fairweather correspondence," she said.

"What remains of it. The customs letters your mother filed with the port authority at Callowmere, twenty years of them—the originals came to us when the trading house was dissolved. There are also personal papers. Letters retained in the family file." His voice was flat, procedural, and she understood that the flatness cost him something, because Ostrevant had known her mother, had eaten at their table in the years when the orchard still bore fruit and the harbor still traded freely with three nations. "Standing order, effective yesterday. All correspondence bearing on foreign nationals is to be destroyed. Enemy-adjacent persons are not to be identifiable in our records. If the coast falls, nothing on paper is to tell anyone who helped whom, or who owed whom, or who was promised anything."

"Who was promised anything," Freya repeated quietly.

"I'm reading the language, Fairweather. I didn't write it." He set the envelope down between them, and neither of them touched it for a moment. On the wall, the map's red pins seemed to lean toward the harbor like men leaning into wind. "You're the last living officer with authorization over those files. It should be you. It should be done properly, witnessed, entered in the log. That's all."

"That's all," she said.

He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile, because it contained everything the flatness had been holding back. "You were four years old the first time I saw you," he said. "Your mother carried you up the gangway at Callowmere in a basket of quinces, and the customs man made her declare you as produce. Four years old, and already contraband." He straightened. "The packet goes with the others. You burn it at Harbor Command, in their presence, and you log it. Or you burn it here tonight, before you leave, and log it here. Your choice. But it burns, Fairweather. I'm sorry. Those are the orders."

* * *

The spirit stove was still lit in the long room when she came out, and the room was empty except for young Perrin, asleep in a chair with his boots off and a dispatch case locked to his wrist by a chain, dreaming whatever couriers dream—probably of open roads, she thought, of borders that opened instead of closed. She took the fourth envelope to the corner by the stove, and she sat down across from the sleeping boy, and for a long time she simply held it.

The paper was soft with age. Her mother's hand was on the front of it—Freya knew the hand without looking, the strong slanted script with its impatient crossbars—but she looked anyway. *Renn, Ilse. Retained at request of A. Renn, customs, per standing arrangement.* Twenty-two years old, that notation. Older than the war, older than the border closures, older than every red pin on Ostrevant's map.

Inside there was only one letter. She had known there would be only one. The rest had gone into the fire over the years, one by one, as the arrangements of peacetime were dismantled piece by piece—the trade agreements, the mooring rights, the sister-city friendship of Callowmere and Vellmark across the strait. What remained was the one thing her mother had refused to surrender even to the dissolution of her own house: a private letter, written in the summer of the last good year, promising a thing.

Freya read it. She had read it a hundred times, and she read it now the way one touches a scar, to be sure of its shape.

*To Aldous Renn, customs officer, from Maren Fairweather, of Fairweather Orchard, Callowmere district:*

*You have asked me, in your careful way, to put in writing what we agreed under the trees, so that it survives us both. I am happy to. Let it be recorded that so long as I live, and so long as any roof of mine stands, there is a place in my house and at my table for your sister Ilse, and that she may claim it without notice and without explanation and without shame, in any season, in any weather, whatever else has changed. My daughter Freya stands witness to this promise and inherits it with the orchard. A promise kept only by one generation isn't a promise; it's a loan. Tell her that. Tell her the trees expect her.*

There was more—some gentle joke about the quince trees, a reference to a harvest dinner Freya remembered only as warmth and lantern light and Ilse Renn laughing with cider on her chin, seventeen years old and fearless—but the heart of it was that paragraph, and Freya had carried the heart of it in memory for years already, because a courier learns early that paper can be taken from you and memory cannot. She closed her eyes and recited it silently, start to finish, checking herself against the page the way a musician checks tuning. *A promise kept only by one generation isn't a promise; it's a loan. Tell her that. Tell her the trees expect her.*

When she opened her eyes, Perrin was watching her from his chair, awake without seeming to have moved.

"Burning?" he asked softly.

"In a minute."

"My grandmother's letters burned in the spring," he said. "All the ones from her brother, in Vellmark. Before the strait closed. My mother did it. She read each one aloud first, though. To the kitchen. Like—" he fumbled for it, "—like returning them somewhere."

"Yes," Freya said. "Something like that."

She read the letter aloud then, quietly, there in the empty room, to the chicory steam and the sleeping stove and the boy who pretended not to listen. She read it in her mother's cadences, the way Maren Fairweather had delivered bad news and good identically, level and warm, and when she finished she folded the letter along its old creases and held it over the stove until the corner caught.

The paper went quickly, the way old paper does, eager for it. She watched the flame take her mother's careful words and turn them to a lace of orange, then black, then nothing, and the ash rose past the plaster garlands of painted fruit toward a ceiling that had forgotten what orchards were. She felt nothing, which was itself a feeling—the professional calm holding, holding, like ice over water, and beneath it the quiet dread moving slowly, patiently, the way cold moves under ice, in all directions at once.

*Tell her the trees expect her.*

Ilse Renn had crossed into the occupied territories eleven months ago to care for an aunt. The border closed behind her. Since then: nothing. No letters—that channel had been severed first, as such channels always are. No word through intermediaries, though Freya had spent a small fortune trying. The aunt's village had appeared on a list, in autumn, of places whose names had stopped appearing anywhere at all.

And tomorrow she would ride south with sealed orders, one of which ordered the detention, upon the Republic's withdrawal from the coast, of all persons with enemy ties—a category into which the name Renn fell like a stone into a well—and another of which required her to certify that no trace remained of who had ever been promised shelter, or by whom, or why.

She brushed the last ash from her fingers. Perrin had gone back to sleep, or was courteous enough to seem to.

Outside the window, the snow went on falling over the capital, covering the roofs, the statues, the darkened lamps, falling with the terrible impartiality of weather and of history, erasing streets she had walked all her life, and Freya Fairweather sat with her hands flat and steady on her knees and thought about the weight of paper—how little it weighed, an ounce, less, and how it had just become the heaviest thing she carried.

Three packets for Harbor Command. One promise, memorized, unburnable.

In the morning she would ride south through the Verren Pass while it was still open, toward the harbor town where her family had once kept an orchard, carrying orders that might already be a death warrant for the woman she had sworn, under the trees, in another country entirely, to keep alive.

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the city a bell rang the hour, one of the ones they hadn't yet melted down, counting out the Republic's remaining time like a patient clerk.

Freya put on her coat, checked the seals on her dispatch case, and went down into the snow.
