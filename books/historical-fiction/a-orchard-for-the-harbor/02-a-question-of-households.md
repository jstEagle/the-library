## 2. A Question of Households

The snow had been falling on Vessany's capital for three days, and Aldous Renn had learned to read it the way other men read ledgers. The flakes came down fine and gray, sifted through the smoke of a city burning its furniture to stay warm, and they settled on the shoulders of everyone alike—the clerk and the colonel, the breadline and the barricade. Snow did not distinguish households. That was, in theory, his job.

He carried the census satchel under his coat, pressed against his ribs like a second heart. Inside: the forms, the carbon paper, the ink pad gone stiff with cold, and the brass stamp that made him, in the eyes of the Directorate of Population, an instrument of the state. *Aldous Renn, Assistant Enumerator, Fourth District.* It was not a title that inspired fear in peacetime. In the final winter of the Republic it had become something else—a key that opened doors, and behind some of those doors, people who should not exist.

His route that morning took him up Tanner's Hill, where the houses leaned together like conspirators and the gutters rang with meltwater that refroze by noon. The address he sought was a narrow gray house with a blue door, third from the corner, and he knew before he knocked that the woman inside would make him lie.

He had known it since Tuesday, when he'd cross-checked her registration against the harbor customs manifests—an act of diligence, nothing more, or so he'd told himself at the time. Freya Fairweather, courier, licensed under the Republic's Emergency Transit Act. Her papers were immaculate. That was the first thing wrong with them. Nobody's papers were immaculate anymore; the war had chewed the edges off every document in Vessany, left them water-stained and amended and stamped by three different authorities who hated each other. Hers were clean as scripture.

And there was the matter of the handwriting.

He climbed the steps and knocked. From within: footsteps, measured, unhurried—too unhurried for a city where unannounced knocks meant conscription or worse. A pause at the door long enough for whoever stood behind it to compose herself. Then it opened.

"Good morning," Aldous said. "Aldous Renn, Directorate of Population. A routine enumeration of households."

Freya Fairweather was smaller than her file photograph suggested, and older around the eyes, though she could not have been past thirty. She wore a plain wool dress the color of wet slate, and her hair was pinned back with the practical severity of a woman who did not waste minutes on mirrors. But it was her hands he noticed first—the way they hung at her sides, loose and ready, the way a courier's hands do, trained to drop a satchel and run or to strike and keep running. Hands that had already calculated the distance to the back window.

"A routine enumeration," she repeated. Her voice was level, faintly amused, the voice of someone who found bureaucracy tedious rather than frightening. "In this weather."

"The weather doesn't excuse the state from knowing its own." He said it flatly, the standard line, and watched it land without effect. She stepped aside and let him in, which surprised him. Most people made him stand on the step while they fetched their papers, as if the threshold itself might protect them. She invited him into the room where all her secrets lived, and that confidence frightened him more than evasion would have.

* * *

The house smelled of lamp oil and dried apples. There was a table by the window, two chairs, a stove with one iron ring glowing dull orange, and very little else. He had enumerated a hundred rooms like it—spare, temporary, the furnishings of people who kept their lives packed near the door. A safehouse. He knew the architecture of impermanence when he stood inside it.

"You'll forgive me if I don't offer tea," she said. "The rationing has reduced hospitality to a theory."

"The Directorate understands." He set the satchel on the table and began the ritual: form 11-C, Household Enumeration, Winter Revision. Name. Age. Occupation. Place of birth.

"Fairweather. Freya Margit Fairweather. Twenty-nine. Courier, licensed. Born in Corren Bay, on the coast."

Corren Bay. He wrote it down and did not look up, though everything in him wanted to. The manifests. The ledger line he'd copied out Tuesday night by lamplight until the words stopped being ink and became a sound in his ears: *Fairweather & Sons, Fruit Merchants, Corren Bay*—and beside it, in a different hand, a different name, entered year after year like a heartbeat: *Renn.*

"Household composition?" he asked. "Do you reside alone?"

"I reside alone."

"Any lodgers? Persons sheltering temporarily? The Winter Revision requires you to declare anyone taking refuge in your household, regardless of duration."

"No one." She said it easily. Too easily. He had spent eleven years listening to people answer questions, and he had learned that the truth came in two textures: rough and smooth. Rough truth stumbled, corrected itself, overshot. Smooth truth was rehearsed, and rehearsed truth was almost always a lie wearing good clothes.

He wrote *resides alone* in the box and moved down the form. Property held. Income declared. Ration class. And then the question—the one added that autumn by directive 44-B, the one that had turned his clipboard from an instrument of counting into an instrument of hunting:

*"Has any person claimed kinship with you, or asserted a familial connection to your household, whether by blood, marriage, or prior agreement, within the past five years?"*

It was an absurd sentence, bureaucratic lace over a barbed hook. What it meant was: *Is anyone hiding with you? Has anyone tried to? Are you the kind of person others run to?*

He asked it because he had to ask it. His supervisor, Dravic, a man built entirely of procedure, had made the district's position clear: every form completed to the letter, no exceptions, no mercies. *We are not counting souls, Renn,* Dravic had said. *We are accounting for them. There is a difference, and the difference is treason.*

"Has any person," Aldous said, and heard his own voice go quiet, "claimed kinship with you? Blood, marriage—or prior agreement?"

The pause lasted less than a second. To Dravic it would have been invisible. But Aldous had spent eleven years measuring the silences between answers, and this one was vast. In it he saw the whole interior weather of the woman across the table: the flicker toward the window, the stillness of the hands that had been ready a moment ago going deliberately, carefully slack, the small recalibration behind her eyes as she decided what kind of liar to be for him.

"No," Freya Fairweather said.

He wrote *no* in the box. His pen moved smoothly. His hand did not shake, and he despised it a little for that—for the steadiness, the professionalism, the way eleven years of service had built him into an instrument that could record a lie without flinching.

Then she made her mistake. Or perhaps, he thought later, it was not a mistake at all; perhaps some part of her wanted him to see it, wanted to know what manner of man had come through her door. Because when she passed him her document folder—registration, license, transit permit, the immaculate papers—he felt, beneath the stiff official envelopes, the corner of something else. Older paper. Thicker. Deckled at the edge the way legal documents used to be printed before the war ate the good mills.

She saw his fingers stop. Neither of them moved.

"May I?" he said, and it was not really a request, and they both knew that too.

* * *

What he drew out was a deed. He knew deeds; his father had kept theirs folded in a tin box on top of the wardrobe, and as a boy Aldous had unfolded it a hundred times, tracing the printed borders of land he'd never seen, land his father spoke about the way other men spoke about the dead. This deed was creased into eighths, soft with handling, and when he opened it the smell rose up out of the folds—dust and apple and something older, something like summer preserved against all evidence.

*Deed of Grant, Orchard Parcel Fourteen, Corren Bay District.* Seven hectares on a hillside above the harbor. Planted, according to the margin note in faded violet ink, in rows of Winesap and Winter Banana and a variety called Harbor Perfume that existed nowhere else in the world except in the memory of the people who had eaten it.

And in the lower corner, in the same violet ink, a signature. *Margit Fairweather.* Her mother's hand—he knew that now, knew it the way you know a face in a crowd, because he had spent Tuesday night and half of Wednesday studying that same handwriting in a customs ledger thirty pages thick, where it appeared beside his own family's name, season after season, year after year, two women trading fruit and letters and, evidently, trust.

*Renn.* His sister's name. Ilse, who had crossed into the occupied zone two winters ago with a suitcase and their mother's ring, and who had not written since the border closed. Ilse, whose last letter had ended with a single line he had reread so many times the paper wore thin: *If anything happens, find the orchard people. They promised.*

He looked up. Freya Fairweather was watching him with an expression he would carry for the rest of his life, however long the Republic allowed it to be. Not fear. Not defiance. Something quieter and far more dangerous: recognition. She had understood, in the space of that pause, exactly what he was holding and exactly whose brother stood in her kitchen holding it.

"It's not on the declaration of assets," he said. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, as though someone else were enumerating.

"I keep some things off the forms," she said. "Everyone does, now. You must know that better than I."

"I'm required to record all property holdings."

"Are you also required to read them aloud?"

The stove ticked. Outside, somewhere down the hill, a tram bell rang with the hollow optimism of a city that hadn't run a full schedule in months. Snow ticked against the window in dry little grains, like sand, like seeds.

Aldous Renn sat with the deed in his hands and understood, with terrible clarity, that he had arrived at a door he could not walk back through. On one side of the moment lay his duty, clean and simple as a form: *Property undeclared. Kinship question evaded. Subject recommended for verification detention.* Three lines of carbon copy. By Friday she'd be in the processing center at the old grain depot, and by spring—if the word meant anything anymore—the file would be closed, and he would be commended, and Dravic would shake his hand with his dry procedural grip, and somewhere in that handshake Aldous would finally become the thing the stamp in his satchel said he was.

On the other side of the moment lay a hillside in Corren Bay he had never seen, and a woman named Ilse walking toward it through the snow with a suitcase, trusting a promise made among apple trees before either of them was born into this winter.

He thought about his father unfolding the deed in the lamplight. *Land remembers,* his father used to say, tapping the printed borders. *Men forget. Paper forgets. Land keeps the count.* His father had died believing the family orchard would come back to them someday. His father had never imagined his son would be the one counting souls instead of trees.

"The deed is old," Aldous said finally. "Pre-war grant. The Winter Revision concerns current holdings." He slid it back across the table, face down, and watched Freya Fairweather's breath change—the smallest catch, quickly mastered, but he heard it. "Current holdings appear to be in order."

"That's very literal of you," she said.

"I'm a literal man. It's why they gave me the stamp." He gathered his forms, squared their edges against the table, performed the whole pantomime of the efficient functionary while inside him something came loose and drifted, slow as ash. At the door he stopped with his hand on the latch and asked the only question left in him, the one that had nothing to do with directive 44-B, the one that was just a brother standing in the snow-smelling dark of a stranger's kitchen:

"Do you know what happened to Corren Bay? After the border closed. Whether anyone—" He couldn't finish it. Eleven years of precise language and he could not finish a sentence.

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face, it was enough, or it wasn't, and she chose—as she would choose again and again in the weeks to come—to give him only as much truth as he could carry.

"I haven't been to the coast in two years," she said. "But orchards are patient things, Mr. Renn. Things planted deep have a way of coming up when you least expect them. Even in winter."

She closed the door behind him, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

* * *

He walked back down Tanner's Hill through the falling snow and did not open the satchel once, though three times his hand went to it—to the blank form, the carbon paper, the place where a recommendation for detention ought already to have been drafted. Each time, he let his hand fall.

At the district office, Dravic looked up from his desk, a lamp-lit island of order in a room full of clerks stamping the Republic's last inventory of itself.

"Fourth District complete?" Dravic asked.

"Complete," Aldous said, and laid his forms on the desk, every box filled, every answer recorded, *no* written fair and square in the kinship column in his own impeccable hand.

Dravic leafed through them, nodded, and reached for his stamp. Then he paused, one page between finger and thumb, and frowned—not at what was written, but at what wasn't.

"Renn. This household here. Fairweather, F.M., courier." He tapped the form twice, a small dry metronome of suspicion. "Her transit license shows coastal origin. Coastal registrations are flagged for secondary review under directive 51. Why is there no annotation?"

Because I held her mother's deed in my hands and smelled apples in it, Aldous thought. Because my sister may be alive on a hillside above Corren Bay, waiting under trees that remember our name. Because I have spent my life counting what I was told to count, and today, for the first time, I counted something else.

"There was nothing to annotate," Aldous said. "The household is exactly as declared."

Dravic studied him a moment longer than necessary, then stamped the form, and the thud of the brass went through Aldous like a nail driven into his own coffin—or perhaps, he thought, walking home through the gray and settling snow, like a seed pressed into frozen ground. He could not yet say which. He only knew that he had lied to the Directorate for the first time in eleven years, that the lie had tasted of apples, and that tomorrow he would have to go back to the customs ledger and find out what else his mother's handwriting had kept beside the name *Fairweather*—and how much of it the Republic had already read.
