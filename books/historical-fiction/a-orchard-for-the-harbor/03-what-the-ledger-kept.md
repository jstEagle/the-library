## 3. What the Ledger Kept

The customs house on Candlemaker's Row had been a granary once, then a chapel for a sect that believed God lived in numbers, and now it was neither. Its shelves bowed under the weight of ledgers the Republic could no longer afford to read. Dust hung in the shafts of light like flour milled from old years.

Freya had come for a single volume.

She told herself it was routine—a courier verifying provenance for a manifest she might someday need to authenticate—but her hands knew better. They shook as she signed the requisition slip, and the clerk behind the desk, a boy of perhaps seventeen with a chilblain on his ear, looked at her hands and looked away again with the practiced blindness of people who survived capitals by not seeing what passed before them.

"Third mezzanine," he said. "Ledgers of the coastal trade, years twenty-one through thirty-four. You'll want a lamp. The windows on that side were boarded after the shelling."

The shelling. As though the war were weather, as though artillery had opinions about architecture. She took the lamp because he offered it, and its oil smell followed her up the stair like a dog that had decided she was its person.

* * *

Her mother's handwriting was unmistakable even in a ledger meant to record other people's commerce. It slanted forward the way a runner leans into wind—eager, always eager, as if every entry were a departure rather than an arrival. Freya found it on the second shelf of the third case, in the autumn pages of the year she turned eleven: **Fairweather Orchard — 40 crates winter pears, consigned Renn & Son, Harbor District. Payment rendered in full.**

Renn & Son.

She stood so long beneath the lamp that the flame guttered twice, and each time the dark came back it seemed to have rearranged itself around her, closer than before.

There were more entries. Of course there were more entries—the orchard had shipped fruit downriver to the harbor for three generations, and the Renns had been their factors for two of them. But knowing a thing in the abstract and seeing it inked in her mother's hand, page after page, season after season, *Fairweather beside Renn*, was different. It was like hearing your own heartbeat amplified through a wall you had always assumed was solid stone.

And beside one entry—not the first, but early enough, in the summer pages—a smaller notation, cramped because the margin was narrow, in the same leaning hand:

**Ilse Renn, b. this spring. Maren Fairweather to send cuttings for the christening.**

Cuttings for the christening. Freya pressed her palm flat against the page as though she could warm the ink back into speech. She remembered those cuttings—or she remembered being told of them, which by now amounted to the same thing. Three scions of the Winterness pear, wrapped in damp sacking, carried downriver by her mother herself because some gifts were too alive to trust to carriers. Her mother had come back smelling of river and woodsmoke and had said, laughing, that the harbor woman had wept over bare sticks of wood as though they were infants.

They were infants, in a way. That was the whole point of an orchard. You planted what you would not live to eat.

Freya closed the ledger. The sound of it was very small in the vast dusty room, and yet it seemed to echo, seemed to carry all the way down the stair and out into the frozen streets where a census taker named Aldous Renn had stood in her doorway eleven days ago asking his quiet questions, and among them, the one that was not standard at all:

*Has anyone claimed kinship with you?*

He hadn't guessed. He had *known*. Not from surveillance, not from a denunciation slipped under a ministry door—he had known because he was a man who kept records in his head the way his family kept them on paper, and somewhere in that head lived the fact that Fairweather orchard fruit had crossed his father's desk for twenty years, and somewhere in the ministry archives—she saw it now with terrible clarity—lived the household census of a harbor district in which a girl named Ilse Renn was listed at the same address as a boy named Aldous, brother and sister, and somewhere in *her own* file, the file every licensed courier carried like a second spine, lived the deed to an orchard in a name that matched the ledger.

He had put the pages together the way any careful man does. And then he had looked at her across the width of a safehouse table and asked his question softly, giving her room to lie, and when she hadn't taken it—when she'd hesitated just one breath too long—he had written something in his book and closed it, and left, and reported nothing.

Why?

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the boy clerk dropped a stack of folios and swore with the mild obscenity of someone who has never been truly afraid. Freya realized she was cold to the bone, that her breath made ghosts in the unheated air, and that she had been standing here long enough that leaving would look like what it was: a woman fleeing a piece of paper.

She did not leave. Not yet. There was one more thing to find, and she owed it to whoever she still was to find it properly—to see the whole shape of the trap or the gift, whichever it proved to be.

She pulled the next volume. Summer pages. Year twenty-two.

* * *

That was the summer the war was a rumor, and rumors were the only crop that failed nowhere.

Freya was nineteen. The orchard was still theirs—still Fairweather land, though the blight had taken the north rows and her father's cough had taken most of his strength, so that the work fell to her and to the two hired men who stayed out of loyalty and were paid out of a tin box that grew lighter every week. The pears that summer were small and fierce with sweetness, the way fruit is in a bad year, as if the trees had decided to spend everything at once.

Ilse Renn came upriver in the last week of June.

She was twenty-three, four years older, and she arrived the way her family's paperwork always arrived—precisely, without fuss, already correct. Her father sent her to audit the accounts and count the crates, and she did both in a morning, standing in the packing shed in a grey dress with her sleeves rolled, writing figures in a book identical in spirit to the ones Freya would one day flee through in the customs house. Then, having nothing else to do until the boat returned, she took off her shoes.

"I beg your pardon," Freya said.

"The grass," Ilse said, as if that explained anything. "I've been on a counting stool since March. My father thinks I'm inspecting the cold store." She looked at Freya with pale, level eyes that held either mischief or arithmetic—it would take Freya the whole summer to learn they were the same thing. "Are you going to report me?"

"To whom?"

"Exactly." And she walked out into the orchard barefoot, and after a moment that Freya would replay for years, Freya followed.

What was there to do in an orchard in high summer but walk the rows? They walked them. Ilse asked the names of things—every graft, every rootstock, the difference between the Winterness and the Old Harbour variety—and Freya answered, and discovered that she knew more than she'd thought, that the knowledge had been sinking into her all her life the way rain sinks into ground that looks dry but isn't. Ilse listened the way few people listen: completely, without preparing her reply while you spoke. By the time the sun stood overhead they were lying in the grass between the third and fourth rows with their heads nearly touching, looking up through leaves that moved green light across their faces, and Ilse said:

"Your mother sent my mother cuttings when I was born. Did you know that?"

"Three Winterness scions. She carried them downriver herself."

"They're bearing now. Small trees still, but bearing. My father won't let anyone pick the first fruit off them—says they're yours, really, that we're only keeping them until—" She stopped. Freya waited. Below them in the valley the river ran silver, and far downstream, invisible but present the way a headache is present, lay the harbor, and beyond the harbor the border, and beyond the border the country whose name everyone had begun saying in a lowered voice.

"Until what?" Freya said.

"Until anyone asks." Ilse plucked a blade of grass and split it along its seam with a thumbnail, a nervous habit Freya would later find in her brother's hands too, the same precise fidget, and something about that recognition went through her like a needle finding a vein. "My father says the world is going to tilt. He doesn't say it at home. He says it at the countinghouse, to men who nod and don't meet his eyes. He says if the border closes, half our clients become the wrong kind of names overnight."

"He thinks we'd be the wrong kind?"

"I think," Ilse said carefully, "that he thinks paper is the only country that lasts. Names in ledgers. Debts that survive regimes. He's been moving our accounts for a year, quietly, into forms a new government can't seize." She turned her head; the grass ticked against her ear. "And he says—if it happens—if the worst happens and we're the wrong kind of name—your mother promised him the orchard would remember us."

Freya sat up. A leaf-shadow lay across Ilse's face like a blindfold, and she lifted her chin so her eyes came clear of it, and those eyes were not playful anymore. They were the eyes of someone who had done sums no one her age should have to do.

"What exactly did she promise?" Freya asked.

"She said—" Ilse recited it, and the cadence was her mother's cadence, learned and kept: "'Any Fairweather tree will shelter a Renn. If ever there's trouble, come to the orchard. We'll hide you among the rows if we must, in the cellar if we must, in plain sight if we must. An orchard is full of places, and none of them are on any map.'" A pause. "Did she mean it?"

Freya thought of her mother, who measured promises the way she measured fertilizer—by what they would yield in five years, ten, a lifetime. Who had once driven back through a storm to return a customer's overpayment of four coppers because a debt wrongly in her favor was still a debt.

"She never says anything she doesn't mean," Freya said. "But it wasn't her promise alone to give."

"No," Ilse agreed. "So give yours."

The river ran. The hired men's voices drifted up from the far rows, arguing amiably about ladders. Somewhere beyond the horizon a border was still open, a republic still stood, a word like *detention* still belonged mainly to grammar.

"If it comes," Freya said, "if the border closes and your name becomes the wrong kind—you come here. Whatever the risk. However it looks. You come to the orchard, and I will keep you, and I don't care who I have to deceive to do it." She heard her own voice go flat and formal and understood that she was speaking the way her mother wrote in ledgers: leaning into wind, making it a departure. "That's my promise. Witnessed by the trees."

Ilse laughed then—quick, startled, real—and reached out and pressed her palm flat against the trunk of the nearest Winterness, as though taking an oath on it. "Witnessed," she said. "By all of them."

They were lying in the grass again within the hour, talking of nothing, of river boats and blight and whether pears ripened faster in company. But something had been planted between the third row and the fourth that day, something dormant and patient, and Freya had gone to sleep that night without knowing she had done the most consequential business of her life, without signing anything, without a single figure entered in a book.

* * *

Now she stood in the granary-chapel of numbers, seven years older and one republic poorer, holding a lamp over the proof that it had all been written down.

Not the promise itself—that existed nowhere but in two memories and the rings of trees. But the *adjacency*. Fairweather beside Renn, year after year, in her mother's unmistakable hand. Enough for a careful man to follow. Enough for a careful man with a sister named Ilse to understand, the moment he saw the deed in her papers, exactly what kind of promise he was standing next to.

She thought of him in her doorway, pen poised, asking *has anyone claimed kinship with you*—and she understood at last that the question had been a door held open, not a net thrown. He had given her the chance to say *no one*, cleanly, and be done. She had hesitated instead. And he had closed his book and walked away carrying her secret like a crate of glass through a crowd.

Aldous Renn had chosen, in that doorway, not to be a hunter. The knowledge should have comforted her. Instead it frightened her more thoroughly than suspicion would have, because choices like his were not private. Every falsified line, every question unasked, every discrepancy unreported—they accumulated somewhere. In a functioning state they accumulated in files. In a dying one they accumulated in memory, and memory could be subpoenaed by anyone who won.

And there was another thing, colder, that she made herself look at straight on, the way you make yourself touch a wound to learn how deep it goes:

If Aldous could trace her to Ilse through a ledger, so could the ministry. So could Harbor Command. Her mother's handwriting sat on a shelf on Candlemaker's Row, public as weather, linking the Fairweather name to the Renn name in ink that would not burn. When the orders came down—and they were coming, everyone felt them coming the way animals feel frost—the lists would be drawn from exactly such paper. Households. Kinships. Claims.

Somewhere in a ministry archive, a clerk she had never met might be pulling these same volumes, running a finger down these same columns, pausing at the same notation: *Ilse Renn, b. this spring.*

Freya returned the ledgers to their cases. Her movements were slow and exact, a courier's movements, a woman putting evidence back in order so that no one would know it had been disturbed. But inside her chest something had come loose and was swinging like a lantern in wind, throwing shadows of the orchard against every wall—rows of trees, a barefoot woman laughing, a palm pressed to bark, *witnessed by all of them*.

Nostalgia, she thought, descending the stairs past the boy clerk, out into the street where the snow was beginning again, fine as sifted flour. People spoke of nostalgia as if it were soft. It wasn't. It was the sharpest instrument she owned, a blade honed on seven lost summers, and fear was only what happened when you finally looked at its edge.

She turned toward the safehouse, and her mind was already working the problem it would not stop working now that it had been given all its pieces: the ledger, the deed, the list that did not yet exist but would. If Ilse Renn's name was going to surface, it would surface soon, and through paper, and the only question left—the only one that mattered, the only one worth the word *promise*—

was whether Freya would reach the paper first.

Three days later, the dispatches came down from Harbor Command, and she learned how little time paper needed to outrun her.
