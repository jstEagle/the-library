## 4. Orders Come Down

The rain had been falling on Vessany Harbor for three days without conviction, a thin gray drizzle that seemed less like weather than like the sky itself losing interest. Freya Fairweather stood at the window of Harbor Command's dispatch room and watched it silver the masts of the fishing fleet, which had not gone out in a week. The boats knocked against their moorings like restless animals, and beyond them, out past the breakwater, the sea was the color of unpolished pewter.

Behind her, the stove ticked as it cooled. Someone had fed it peat that morning, someone whose name she didn't know, and now the room smelled of burnt earth and lamp oil and the particular staleness of paper kept too long in damp air. She had been in this room for six hours. The dispatch case sat open on the table behind her, its wax seals broken, its contents spread out in the careful order she had arranged them: military traffic first, then civil, then the thin folder marked with the black diagonal stripe that meant *internal security*.

She had read everything twice. That was her discipline, drilled into her across four years of carrying other people's decisions through other people's dangers: read it twice, burn it once, remember forever. The words were already beginning to arrange themselves in her memory the way they always did, into shapes she could carry without paper, and she hated how easily her mind did this work now, how little friction there was left in her.

The third dispatch was the one she was avoiding.

She knew it was the third one the way you know which tooth aches before you touch it. Command's correspondence had a grammar, and she had learned to hear the sentences before she read them. The first dispatch—*consolidation of naval assets, redeployment northward by month's end*—was an obituary written in the passive voice. The second, with its schedules and tonnages and its careful accounting of what would be evacuated and what would be left to the tide, was the inventory of an estate. And the third, the thin folder with the black stripe, was always either mercy or murder, and in the last year of the Republic it had almost never been mercy.

Outside, a gull cried once and gave up. Even the birds were tired here.

Freya turned from the window. Her boots left wet prints on the flagstones; she hadn't bothered to wipe them, another small dereliction, and she noted it with the detached interest of a woman watching herself come apart in increments. Four days ago she had stood in this same room while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs read her credentials aloud, and she had thought: *I am still good at this.* Now she looked at the thin folder and thought nothing at all, which was worse.

She sat down at the table. She broke the seal on the third dispatch.

*By order of the Provisional Directorate for Coastal Security,* it began, in the cramped hand of some undersecretary who would never see this harbor, who would never smell the peat smoke or hear the idle boats, *all persons resident within the designated coastal zone found to hold ties of blood, marriage, correspondence, commerce, or sympathy with forces of the occupation are to be taken into protective detention pending further directive.*

*Protective detention.* The Republic had gotten very good at those two words. They were a coat pulled over a cage.

There followed eleven pages of names.

Freya read them the way she read all lists—quickly, professionally, letting the names pass through her like water through a net—and then, on the fourth page, in a column of surnames sorted by district, she stopped as if she had walked into glass.

*RENN, ILSE. Occupation: none recorded. Residence: last registered, District Nine. Status: entry of residence lapsed. Note: sister of subject RENN, ALDOUS, census official, District Seven. Detain on location.*

She read it again. The letters did not rearrange themselves. The name did not dissolve into the list around it. It sat there in the middle of the page, in the undersecretary's cramped hand, small and black and absolute, and the rain went on ticking against the window as though the world had noticed nothing whatsoever.

Ilse Renn.

Freya set the dispatch down very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off. Then she got up and walked to the window again, because standing was easier than thinking, and watched the fishing boats knock against their moorings, and made herself do the arithmetic she did not want to do.

The border had closed in the autumn. Ilse had been across it when it closed—had been visiting her aunt in the river towns, everyone said so, the whole district had said so—and no word had come since. No word came from across the border about anyone. But a list is a living thing in wartime; it grows in the dark, it feeds on paperwork, and somewhere a clerk had entered Ilse Renn's name because her brother held a government post and her residence had lapsed, and lapsed residence plus enemy kinship equaled *detain on location*, and the machine did not care whether the woman named existed, or where, or whether she was already dead in a ditch outside Torvane. The machine only cared that the line be filled in.

*Detain on location.*

Except no one knew her location. Except Freya did not know her location, and that should have been a comfort and instead felt like a debt.

She pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass. Below her, a soldier crossed the yard leading a limping dog, and the dog looked up at the window with the flat incurious gaze of animals, and Freya thought: *four years ago I carried letters about seed stock and shipping rates, and I promised a girl under flowering trees that if war ever came to her door, mine would open first.*

The promise had been made in May. She could still feel that May on her skin—the warmth coming up off the orchard rows, the white petals drifting down like a snow that wanted nothing from anyone, Ilse's bare feet in the grass between the third and fourth row of pippins, laughing at something Freya's mother had said about grafting. *If it ever comes to war,* Ilse had said, and she'd said it lightly, the way young people say such things, throwing the word away like a stone into deep water, *you'll hide me, won't you? In your cellar, with the apples.* And Freya's mother had answered before Freya could, in her dry way, not looking up from the cutting in her hands: *She'll hide you in the house, girl. The cellar's for my brandy.* And they had all laughed, and the petals had come down, and the promise had settled over them like the blossoms did, soft and total and seemingly weightless.

Nothing was weightless now. Every word had a tonnage. Somewhere in the capital a man with cramped handwriting had written *detain on location*, and the words weighed exactly as much as a human life, which is to say they weighed nothing until they fell on you.

Freya went back to the table.

The dispatch required acknowledgment. That was the procedure, and procedure was the last clean thing she owned: read twice, acknowledge, distribute, destroy. The acknowledgment page lay beneath the folder, a single sheet bearing the letterhead of the Provisional Directorate and a line for signature: *Received and understood, orders to be executed within the coastal zone by the bearer.*

By the bearer. By her. Not by some garrison captain she would never meet—by the courier who carried the orders, because the Republic was short of everything now except signatures, and a courier's hand was as good as a magistrate's when the alternative was waiting.

She picked up the pen. It was a cheap pen, harbor-issue, the nib slightly split so that it caught on the downstrokes, and she had signed a dozen acknowledgments with it over the years without once feeling the catch.

Her hand would not move.

This was the truth she would carry afterward, the detail she would return to at night in whatever years remained: not that she hesitated—hesitation was human, hesitation was forgivable—but that her hand simply refused, the way a horse refuses a bridge, some animal intelligence in the tendons knowing what the mind had not yet admitted. She stood over the page and the rain ticked and the stove ticked and somewhere below a door banged, and her hand hovered above the line like a bird that has forgotten what air is for.

*Sign it,* said the professional voice in her head, the voice that had kept her alive through checkpoints and shelled roads and one long terrible week in Torvane when the city burned. *You are a courier. You carry. What the orders contain is not yours. If you don't sign, another will, and the list does not care whose hand fills the line.*

And then, quieter, in a voice that sounded terribly like her mother among the blossoming trees: *And if you sign it, whose hand is it, after that?*

She thought of the ledger in the customs house—she had thought of it often since the census taker's visit, since Aldous Renn had stood in her doorway with his careful questions and his careful eyes, and asked the one question that wasn't standard: whether anyone had ever claimed kinship with her. He had known. Of course he had known; his mother's handwriting sat beside her mother's in a customs ledger from twenty years ago, two women trading fruit and trust across a property line, and the paper remembered what people were allowed to forget. Paper always remembered. That was the whole terror and whole holiness of paper.

And now she was the paper. She was the sheet on the table, and Ilse's name was the ink, and if she signed, she would be the instrument that carried the name to the men who would act on it.

*Detain on location.*

But if she refused—if she hesitated visibly, if the acknowledgment came back unsigned or late—the suspicion would fall not on the Directorate but on her. A courier who balks is a courier who is examined. A courier who is examined has her papers opened, her safehouse searched, her associations traced, and somewhere in that tracing, sooner or later, a customs ledger from twenty years ago would surface with its two mothers' handwriting side by side, and then it would not be Ilse Renn's name alone on a list in a distant office. It would be Freya Fairweather's, and the orchard deed hidden in her papers would become not a relic but evidence, and the promise made in May would be buried under the rubble of both their lives.

She saw it all with terrible clarity, standing in the cold room with the pen in her hand. There was no clean refusal available. The Republic had designed it that way, or rather the Republic had rotted into that shape, every exit bricked over except the ones that led deeper in.

So Freya Fairweather did the thing that couriers do. She bent her head, and she signed her name.

The split nib caught on the downstroke of the F. She felt it snag, felt the tiny resistance, felt the blot of ink well up where the nib tore the fiber—a small dark bloom at the start of her own name, like a bruise. She finished the signature anyway. *Fairweather.* The word looked foreign to her, the way any word looks when you have stared at it too long: *fair weather*, a joke of a name for these years, a name meant for orchards and harbors and honest wind.

She straightened. Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of clinical surprise—they were steady hands, famous-steady hands, hands that had carried sealed orders through artillery fire without a tremor—and she folded them into her sleeves to make them stop, and they did not stop, and finally she simply laid them flat on the table, palms down, as if steadying the wood itself.

*It's done,* she told herself. *A signature is not a bullet. Eleven pages of names and half of them are dead already or moved or misrecorded; clerks write faster than soldiers march; perhaps no one will ever reach District Nine; perhaps—*

But she had read the second dispatch. She knew the evacuation timetables. She knew that the garrison would pull out of the coastal districts in nineteen days, and that before they went, they would sweep. *Protective detention pending further directive* meant trucks, and trucks meant the processing camps inland, and the camps inland were the place where the Republic sent the people it could not afford to feed and could not afford to free. Everyone in the harbor towns knew what the camps were called, in the low voices people used near children. Everyone knew.

Nineteen days. Somewhere in District Nine, or District Seven, or in a ditch, or in a cellar full of stored apples, a woman named Ilse Renn had nineteen days.

Freya gathered the dispatches together with hands that had finally gone quiet, because quiet had come the way it always comes after shock—not as peace but as numbness, the body conserving itself. She squared the edges. She refolded the thin folder along its original creases. She placed everything back in the dispatch case, and her fingers lingered a moment on the clasp, and she thought: *I am the last person between these orders and the world. For nineteen days, the paper sleeps in this case, and while it sleeps, nothing has happened yet. Nothing is real until it is delivered.*

That was a lie and she knew it was a lie. The orders were real the moment the undersecretary wrote them. Her signature had merely made her part of them.

She closed the case. Outside the window the rain had thickened at last into something like commitment, drumming on the flagstones, running in bright threads down the panes. The fishing boats knocked and knocked against the moorings, patient, going nowhere. In nineteen days the garrison would leave and the coast would belong to whoever could hold it, and the orchards along the ridge road—her family's orchard among them, three hundred trees gone wild and unpruned for six years—would stand inside the detention zone with their roots in ground that was about to change masters.

She should deliver the orders tomorrow. Procedure demanded delivery within one day of receipt, and the garrison commander at the ridge fort was expecting them, and every hour of delay was another thread of risk wound around her own neck.

Freya stood in the doorway a long moment, looking back at the table where the dispatch case sat, small and brown and harmless as a lunch box. The lamp had burned low. Her shadow lay across the floor toward the door, long and wavering, as if she were already leaving herself behind in the room.

Then she thought of the one thing she had not let herself think of, the thing that had been waiting at the bottom of her mind since the census taker's visit, since the question about kinship, since the ledger: *Aldous Renn knows who I am. He has known since he stood in my doorway. And his sister's name is on a list that I have just signed into motion.*

He had chosen not to report her. She had seen the choice happen—had watched it cross his face like weather, the hunter deciding, for reasons he probably couldn't have named, not to be one. And now the debt ran the other direction, or perhaps it had run both directions all along, two people holding each other's most dangerous secret across a table strewn with forms.

Which meant that before she delivered anything, before the orders woke up, there was a man in the census office in District Seven whom she needed to see.

Freya lifted the dispatch case. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

She did not go straight to District Seven. Caution had been bred into her bone by four years of survival, and caution said: *sleep, eat, walk the long way, be a woman buying lamp oil and bread, be nobody.* So she slept badly in the safehouse above the chandler's shop, listening to the rain and to the chandler coughing in the room below, and in the morning she bought bread and lamp oil and a twist of salt, and she walked the long way along the harbor road with the gray water on her left and the ridge rising green and black on her right, where the orchard stood, where the wild trees stood, and she did not look up at it more than twice.

District Seven was the clerks' quarter, narrow streets and tall thin houses leaning together like conspirators, gutters running with rainwater, a cat on every third windowsill watching the world with bureaucratic indifference. The census office occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a music school; she could see the old painted notes fading on the lintel above the new stenciled number. Through the fogged window she could make out desks, lamps, the stooped shapes of men doing the Republic's counting.

She stood across the street in the doorway of a shuttered bakery, holding her parcel of bread like any errand-running woman, and she watched the window, and she waited, and the rain dripped off the bakery's broken gutter onto her shoulder, cold as a finger.

Because here was the arithmetic that the night had forced on her, the cruel sum that would not balance: Aldous Renn had shown her mercy once, unprompted, unasked. But mercy given in a doorway was one thing; mercy asked for outright, across a desk, in an office full of colleagues and files, was another. And she was about to ask him for something far larger than silence. She did not yet know what shape it would take—she only knew that his sister's name was on a list her own hand had acknowledged, and that the nineteen days had begun, and that the first step of any road was always the same: find the person who might help you, and stand in front of them, and speak.

A door opened across the street. A junior clerk emerged, collared against the rain, hurrying east with a document case under his arm. And behind him, framed for a moment in the lit rectangle of the doorway, stood a taller figure in a gray coat, adjusting his collar, preparing to step out into the wet—

Aldous Renn, going home to whatever supper the Republic allowed him, unaware—or was he aware, had he heard, did the lists circulate through his own office—that the name of the person he loved best in the world had just been entered into the machinery of detention by a hand that trembled.

Freya stepped out of the bakery doorway, into the rain, and started across the street.
