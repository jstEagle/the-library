## 5. The Man Who Looked Away

The census office smelled of cold ink and wet wool, the way all government buildings in Vessany had come to smell that winter—like something trying very hard to be permanent while the walls sweated. Aldous Renn arrived before the lamps were lit, as he always did, and stood a moment in the doorway of the records room watching the frost bloom on the inside of the windows. Beyond the glass, the capital lay gray and quiet under its shroud of smoke. Somewhere to the east, guns he could not hear were deciding the shape of the world.

He hung his coat on the peg by the stove, which had not yet been fed, and took down his ledger from the shelf. Volume eleven. Coastal districts, households four hundred through six hundred and twelve. His own neat hand filled the columns: names, ages, trades, kinships declared or undeclared, the little marginal codes that told the Ministry who was loyal, who was suspect, who was merely tired.

Fairweather, F. Household 547. The entry stared up at him from beneath the lamplight he had not yet turned on, legible anyway, the way certain words become legible in the dark.

He had written it nine days ago, sitting in her parlor with his satchel open on his knee while she answered his questions with the smooth courtesy of a woman who had answered such questions before, in other towns, under other names perhaps, though he had no proof of that and wanted none. *Any kinship claims against this household?* he had asked—the standard question, the one that meant nothing in nine hundred cases out of a thousand—and she had said *no* half a beat too late, and in that half beat he had seen her eyes go, briefly and involuntarily, to the writing desk by the window. To the papers weighted there beneath a brass dish of dried lavender.

He should have risen then. He should have asked to examine the desk, and she would have refused, or she would have allowed it, and either way the machinery would have taken over: the report filed, the investigators sent, the household sealed, the woman taken somewhere with a number instead of a name. He knew how it went. He had set it in motion often enough, always telling himself that the census taker only counted; it was the Ministry that decided what the counting meant.

But he had looked at her hands instead—capable hands, ink-stained at the cuticle, a courier's hands or a clerk's—and he had thought of Ilse.

That was the truth of it, and he made himself hold the truth up to the light now, in the freezing records room, because a lie you refuse to examine is the most dangerous kind. He had looked away from Freya Fairweather's desk for one reason only: that somewhere south, past the closed border, past the occupied zones where letters stopped arriving and names stopped being spoken aloud, his sister might be alive. And if Ilse was alive, she was alive because someone, sometime, had chosen not to see her.

He opened the drawer where the blank forms were kept and drew out a household amendment sheet. The paper was thin, cheap, the Republic printing less on more every month. At the top: DISTRICT, HOUSEHOLD, NATURE OF CORRECTION. He dipped his pen. The nib scratched like a small animal in the silence.

Household 547. Correction: occupation amended from *courier, licensed (provisional)* to *clerical assistant, harbor provisioning*. Kinship claims: none outstanding. Residency duration: extended to seven years, continuous.

Seven years. It was almost true. She had been in the capital five, by his best reconstruction of the customs ledgers he'd spent three sleepless nights cross-referencing, and five could be made to look like seven by anyone who wasn't looking too hard. That was the whole art of the falsification—not invention but blur. A lie so close to the truth that the difference dissolved into the general fog of a nation's paperwork. The Ministry was drowning in paper. Everyone knew it. Half the districts hadn't been reconciled since the spring offensives. A blurred line in volume eleven would never be found unless someone went looking for it specifically, with Fairweather's name already in mind.

And why would anyone look? Unless—

Unless the woman herself did something. Unless a dispatch went astray. Unless a name surfaced somewhere it shouldn't. He could blur her entry, but he could not blur her life. She was still carrying, whatever she carried; he had seen the callus on her right forefinger where a courier's case strap wore, the same callus Ilse used to have from her satchel, back when Ilse carried nothing more dangerous than seed catalogues between the orchard villages.

Aldous set down the pen and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

Ilse. Say the name once, in an empty room, the way a man tests ice before putting his weight on it. She had been gone fourteen months. Fourteen months since the last letter, postmarked from a town whose name he had checked twice against the maps and found straddling the new border like a man straddling a fence with dogs on both sides. Fourteen months since her handwriting—slanted left, impatient, the descenders of her g's like hooks—had appeared at the bottom of their mother's page saying *don't worry about me, I've found good people, I'll wait out the war where the trees are.* Where the trees are. He had thought it sentiment. Now he thought it might have been an address.

Because there was the deed. He hadn't touched it—he would swear before any tribunal that he hadn't touched it—but he had seen it, or the corner of it, when Freya Fairweather shifted the lavender dish to answer some question about water rights, and the heavy rag paper beneath had carried the watermark of the Harbor Provincial Land Office, and the visible fragment of text had read *…orchard situate upon the north ridge, together with rows and rootstock thereon…*

An orchard. Where the trees are. He was not a romantic man; the census does not reward romance; it teaches you that every household is a column of numbers wearing clothes. But he felt the two facts click together like the tumblers of a lock, and what they unlocked frightened him more than anything the Ministry could do to him.

If Ilse had gone to ground anywhere in this broken country, she had gone to ground among people who had promised, in peacetime, to shelter her. People like the woman in household 547, whose hesitation lasted half a second and would cost him, if he let it cost him, everything.

He picked up the pen and finished the amendment in a steady hand. That was the terrible thing, he thought as he blotted it—that his hand was steady. Treason, apparently, required no trembling. It required only the small daily cowardices of a careful man, performed neatly and filed on time.

* * *

Supervisor Grell arrived at nine, shaking snow off a hat that had been fashionable during the annexation and had outlived three fashions since, the way Grell himself had outlived three ministries. He was sixty, pink-faced, fond of herring and of the phrase *"the form is the form,"* and he had survived the purges of two separate administrations by possessing, in equal measure, no ambition and no imagination. Aldous had always considered him the safest man in Vessany.

"Aldous." Grell dropped into the chair across the desk without asking, which was his privilege. "You've got a discrepancy."

The words landed in Aldous's chest like a swallowed stone. He kept his face in the mild, faintly bored expression that ten years of census work had worn onto him the way water wears limestone. "Which district?"

"Coastal. Your volume." Grell flipped open his own summary folio and ran a blunt finger down a column. "Household 547. Your original field entry, nine days ago, lists residency at five years. This morning an amendment comes up from your own queue changing it to seven. And amending the occupation. You don't usually amend, Aldous. You're the one who tells the juniors that amendments are how errors breed."

"The field entry was wrong," Aldous said. "I misread the prior registry. The household was entered in the provisional rolls of the evacuation year—it happens along the coast, whole streets were re-rolled twice. When I reconciled against the customs ledgers—" he paused, precisely, letting the pause do its work, a pause suggesting long hours and honest labor— "the discrepancy was obvious. Five years understated her tenure. She'd been provisionally registered during the evacuation and never struck off. Seven years continuous. I corrected it."

Grell looked at him over the folio. Outside, the snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, in the yard, a soldier was shouting at a cart horse, and the sound came up muffled, like grief heard through a wall.

"Customs ledgers," Grell said. "Those are sealed since the harbor directive."

"I have standing access. Census reconciliation clause."

"I know the clause. I wrote half the clause." Grell closed the folio and laced his fingers over his stomach, and for a moment his small piggy eyes lost their bureaucratic glaze and became simply the eyes of an old man looking at a younger one. "It's a tidy story, Aldous. Tidy stories make me nervous. Not because they're lies—most tidy stories are true, that's what makes them tidy. They make me nervous because tidiness is expensive. It costs effort. And I ask myself, what makes a careful man spend effort on household 547?"

"There's nothing in 547," Aldous said. "A single woman. Provisional license, clerical work. No kinship claims, no enemy ties flagged, no arrears. If there were anything there, Grell, you'd have found it before I did—you read the flag summaries every morning over your herring."

It was flattery, barely disguised, and it worked the way flattery works on men who are lonely inside their own competence. Grell grunted, half pleased, unconvinced, and reached for the amendment sheet itself. He held it up to the lamp as though the light might reveal forgery the way it reveals a watermark.

"Your hand?" he asked, unnecessarily.

"My hand."

"You corrected toward *more* tenure. You understand that makes the household *more* established, *less* subject to the coastal detention review? Anyone auditing your corrections this season is going to notice you've softened three entries toward establishment and none the other way."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts. *Three,* Aldous thought. There had been others—he had blurred them too, small mercies scattered through volume eleven like coins thrown behind him, the Kessler boy who'd married a border-zone girl, the old ferryman with the foreign-born wife—and he had believed each blur invisible, each coin sunk in deep water. But Grell read summaries the way other men read scripture.

"I softened them," Aldous said, "because the review criteria changed in November and nobody sent us the memorandum until January. Under the old criteria those households fail. Under the current ones they pass. I corrected forward, not backward. Check the dates."

He had spent part of the night constructing this. Not the lie itself—he had built the lie in the first hour, quickly, almost mechanically—but the scaffolding around it, the dates and clauses and memoranda that made the lie load-bearing. He watched Grell check the dates now, watched him flip back through the bulletin file with his cracked thumbnail, and hated himself with a slow, thorough hatred that had nothing to do with fear. Because the scaffolding would hold. That was the obscenity of it. He was good at this. Ten years of counting souls had taught him exactly how souls could be made to disappear on paper, and tonight he had used that knowledge not to build the Ministry's machine but to jam one small gear in it, and the gear would stay jammed, and no one would ever know except him, at night, in the dark behind his eyes.

"Mm," said Grell. "Dates bear you out." He set the folio down. "For what it's worth, I think the coastal review is madness. Detaining half the harbor population because of where their grandmothers were born—we'll fill the prisons with fishermen and call it victory." He rose, gathered his hat, and paused at the door with the air of a man adding a postscript. "There's talk of an emergency recount. Coastal districts again, full sweep. If it comes down to us, I'll want you on it. You know the ground."

"I know the ground," Aldous said.

When the door closed he sat for a long time without moving. The stove had warmed the room at last, and the frost on the windows had melted into runnels, and through them the city appeared sliced into strips: gray sky, gray roofline, a chimney's thread of smoke rising perfectly straight in the windless cold, like a plumb line dropped from heaven to measure how far everything had fallen.

He thought: *this is the step that cannot be taken back.* Not the amendment—that was ink, and ink can be burned, though he would not burn it. This. Sitting here, in warmth, having lied smoothly to the safest man in Vessany and gotten away with it, and feeling not relief but a kind of terrible settling, the way a house settles onto its foundation. He had crossed. However quietly, however tidily, however much he told himself he had only delayed, only bought time, only kept options open—he had chosen. There was a side now, and he was on it, and it was not the Ministry's side, and if the Ministry ever learned it, the charge would be treason and the penalty would be the wall behind the prison yard, where the snow drifted against the stones and no one swept it because no one was assigned to.

And underneath the fear, quiet as groundwater, another thing moved. He examined it reluctantly, the way one examines a wound. It was hope. Not hope that he would escape detection—he was too clear-eyed for that, and the recount was coming whether he liked it or not, and he had just volunteered himself, in effect, to walk the ground where the truth was buried. The hope was smaller and stranger than that: hope that Ilse was alive, that the trees existed, that a promise made in peacetime among orchard rows had somehow held against fourteen months of war the way a deed holds against weather—faded, watermarked, but signed.

He opened the drawer and looked one last time at the amendment sheet before locking it away. Household 547. Seven years continuous. None outstanding.

*None outstanding,* he had written. He almost laughed. Every promise he had ever cared about was outstanding; that was what promises were.

Then the door opened again and Grell put his head back in, snow melting on his shoulders, his face arranged into the particular blankness he reserved for news from above.

"That recount," Grell said. "It's confirmed. Orders came down an hour ago. Full coastal sweep, starting with the harbor districts—and Aldous, there's a list attached. Names to verify residence on. Priority households." He held out a single typed page. "Yours has forty-one entries on it. You'll want to start today."

Aldous took the page. The paper was warm from Grell's hand, and his own hands, as he took it, were steady.

He scanned down the column of typed names—forty-one households, forty-one lives reduced to a verification checkbox—and stopped at the thirty-seventh line, where the type spelled out, in the Ministry's flat and patient voice:

*Fairweather, F. — Harbor District, North Ridge vicinity. Confirm presence. Report immediately.*

Report immediately. He folded the page into quarters, and into eighths, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where it sat like a coal.

Outside, the snow had begun again, falling straight and silent on the capital of a republic that had perhaps six weeks left to live, and Aldous Renn buttoned his coat and went out into it, carrying the order that would send him—to the orchard, to the ridge, to whatever waited where the trees were.
