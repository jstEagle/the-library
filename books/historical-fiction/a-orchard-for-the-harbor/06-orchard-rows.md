## 6. Orchard Rows

The road to the orchard had been closed for eleven days, which meant the soldiers manning the barricade were bored, cold, and eager to be told something that let them stop looking.

Freya gave them something better than papers. She gave them tedium.

She arrived in a requisitioned produce cart, wrapped in two blankets and a borrowed coat that smelled of another woman's woodsmoke, and she complained—loudly, in the flat accent of the river districts—about the price of feed grain, the state of the roads, her brother-in-law's gout, and the Republic itself, which she blamed with cheerful vulgarity for all three. The corporal at the checkpoint asked to see her manifest. She produced it from a canvas satchel along with an orange he had probably not seen since autumn, and by the time he had finished reading the manifest—which listed forty crates of dormant apple rootstock bound for the coast depots, all of it true, all of it traceable—he was holding the orange and asking no further questions.

"You shouldn't be on this road," he said, without conviction.

"Nobody should," Freya agreed. "But my trees don't know there's a war."

He waved the cart through, and she did not look back at him, because looking back was a habit of guilty people and she had spent six years training it out of herself. She kept her eyes on the road ahead, on the ruts frozen into iron ridges, on the bare poplars standing along the field edges like witnesses waiting to be called.

The detention zone began where the poplars ended. There was no fence—that would have required lumber the army needed elsewhere—but there were notices nailed to every gatepost and barn door, printed in the heavy black type the Ministry reserved for pronouncements it hoped people would not read closely: *By order of Harbor Command, movement within this district is restricted. All persons bearing enemy kinship or correspondence must present themselves for registration within five days.*

*Enemy kinship.* The phrase had been drafted in a warm office by a man who had never had a sister, or if he had, had never promised her anything.

Freya drove past the notices. The cart horse, a placid mare named Bess who belonged to a widow paid handsomely not to ask questions, walked at the pace of an animal that had outlived several governments and expected to outlive this one too.

The orchard appeared first as a darkening in the sky—a smudge above the hedgerow where the trees rose taller than anything the fields could offer. Then the hedge itself, untrimmed now for years, grown into a ragged wall of blackthorn and briar. Then the gap in it where the old gate had hung, the gate her father had painted green every spring until the spring he died, after which the paint had weathered to a color with no name, grey-green-silver, the color of things that have stopped pretending to be cared for.

She left the cart in the ruined lane behind the hedge, where it could not be seen from the road, and walked the last quarter mile on foot. Her boots crunched through frozen mud printed everywhere with the tracks of birds and once, faintly, half-obliterated by rain, with the tracks of a person. A boot print. Narrow heel. She stood over it for a long moment, breath steaming, heart doing something unprofessional.

*Don't hope,* she told herself. *Hope is how they catch you. Hope makes you walk toward things instead of away.*

She walked toward them anyway.

* * *

The orchard in winter was a cathedral with the roof burned off.

Two hundred trees, maybe fewer now; she counted gaps the way other people counted their dead. The Fairweather apples had been famous once—not famous the way generals are famous, but the quiet fame of a thing done well for three generations: the Fairweather Russet, small, brown-gold, sweet as a secret, keeping until Easter in a good cellar. Ships had carried them down the coast. Ilse Renn had eaten her first one here, standing under the third tree in the third row, juice running down her wrist, laughing at how seriously Freya took the whole enterprise.

That had been the last summer of peace. Six years ago. It felt like a life that had happened to someone else, someone gentler, who had died of the war along with everything else—or so Freya had believed, walking through the rows now with her collar up and her eyes moving the way a courier's eyes move, reading ground, reading light, reading absence.

Absence told her things.

There was no snow inside the orchard's center, though snow lay everywhere else, because someone had swept the paths—and not recently swept, but *kept* swept, day after day, the packed earth beneath polished by feet. Near the old well house, the brambles had been cut back, the canes stacked with a neatness no soldier or scavenger would bother with. And against the south-facing wall of the packing shed, someone had stretched a line between two nails, and on the line hung washing: a shift, a pair of stockings, a shirt gone grey with many boilings. Frozen stiff as boards, rimmed white with frost, but washed. Cared for. Someone in this dead place was still performing the small liturgies of being alive.

Freya stood very still among the trees and listened the way her mother had taught her to listen—for the sound a house makes when it is not empty. Wind in bare branches. A crow somewhere, complaining. Water moving under ice in the irrigation channel. And beneath all of it, almost below hearing, from the direction of the cellar doors set into the hillside behind the shed: the small percussion of a lid set down on a pot.

Her whole body knew before her mind consented to know it. Six years is a long time, but the body keeps its own ledger, and hers recorded the weight of that particular silence, the particular care with which that particular person moved, even before her mind formed the word *Ilse* and the word broke something open in her chest that she had soldered shut a long time ago.

She made herself walk slowly. She made herself scuff her boots, make noise, be a sound a hiding person could identify as human and singular and not a patrol. She came around the corner of the shed and stood at the top of the four stone steps that led down to the cellar doors, and she said, to the frost-bright air, to the trees, to whoever was listening below:

"The russets kept until Easter. I told you they would."

The silence went on so long she thought she had been wrong. Then the cellar door opened inward, slowly, on a hinge someone had oiled, and Ilse Renn stood in the dark of the doorway holding a knife.

* * *

It was not a dramatic knife. It was a kitchen knife, the kind used for paring apples, held low and competently, and Ilse held it the way a woman holds a knife who has learned in six hard years exactly what it is for. She was thinner than memory. Her hair, once the pale gold of cider foam, was shot through with grey at the temples and cut short and unevenly, the haircut of someone who does it herself with shears and a hand mirror. She wore three layers of clothing and a man's coat, and her eyes—her eyes had not changed at all, and that was the worst of it, because they were the same clear winter-grey eyes that had looked at Freya across a table covered with apple blossoms, and they were looking at her now with an expression Freya had never seen in them before.

Suspicion. Calculation. The arithmetic of survival performed in real time.

"Say something else," Ilse said. Her voice was rough from disuse, or from smoke, or from weeping, or from all three. "Say something only you'd know."

Freya's mind, trained for codes and ciphers, offered up the obvious key. "You said the russets tasted like forgiveness tastes. You said—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "You said you'd never had anything worth keeping until you ate one off the ground, and I said that was blasphemy, and you said God grew them, so it couldn't be."

The knife stayed up for three more heartbeats. Then Ilse's arm dropped to her side, and her face did something complicated—the suspicion collapsing inward like a structure whose load-bearing walls have finally been permitted to fail—and she sat down abruptly on the cellar step, the knife clattering onto the stone, and put both hands over her mouth.

"I thought—" she said through her fingers. "I thought when you didn't come the first winter. I thought the second. I thought—"

"I know."

"—that either you were dead, or you'd forgotten, or it had all been a game to you, some rich girl's charity, and I couldn't decide which was worse, so I decided not to think about it, and then I'd wake up at night thinking about nothing else—"

"Ilse." Freya came down the steps and knelt, awkwardly, on the frozen stone, and took her hands, and the hands were cold as the step itself. "I wrote. Every month for two years. The letters came back. Then the border closed and I couldn't write at all, and then I was doing work I can't describe, and I looked for you—I spent money I didn't have paying people to look for you—and everyone said the Renns went east over the mountains and everyone was wrong."

"We went east." Ilse laughed, a terrible small sound. "Halfway. Then the fighting caught us and Papa turned us around and we came back the long way, south, and Mama died on the road outside Vellmark, and Papa got us to the river and then his heart—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I crossed the border zone in October. With a family of charcoal burners. In a coal cart. I've been here since the first week of November."

"Six weeks."

"Nine. Your well still works. The cellar's dry. There were jars in it—" She stopped again, and this time what surfaced in her face was embarrassment, fierce and human, at the confession of having lived on another family's stores in another family's cellar. "I meant to leave. I told myself every week, when the weather breaks, when the patrols change, I'll go on to the harbor, find a boat. But the notices went up on the posts, and my name—" She looked at Freya then with those unchanged eyes, and finished simply: "My name is on a list somewhere. Isn't it."

It was not a question. Freya did not insult her by pretending otherwise.

"Yes."

"And you came anyway. Into the zone. Where they shoot couriers on sight and ask questions about the body later." Ilse pulled one hand free and touched Freya's face—tentatively at first, then not tentatively at all, her cold fingers mapping cheekbone and jaw as though verifying a document against its description. "You came anyway. You stupid, stubborn—"

"Fairweather," Freya supplied. "Yes. It's a family condition."

And then Ilse was crying, silently, the way people cry who have spent years learning that noise attracts attention, her forehead pressed against Freya's shoulder, her whole thin frame shaking, and Freya held her in the doorway of a dead orchard's cellar with the frost climbing the stone steps beside them and understood that she had been holding this exact posture in her imagination for six years and that the reality was colder, thinner, more frightened, and infinitely better than anything she had dared to construct.

* * *

They went down into the cellar because cellars are where you go when the world above belongs to other people. Ilse relit the spirit stove—hoarded fuel, hoarded matches, everything arranged with the pitiful orderliness of scarcity—and made something hot from dried apples and barley that tasted, Freya thought, better than anything served in any capital restaurant in the last year of the Republic. They sat on crates in the lamplight, knees almost touching, surrounded by the ghosts of harvest: empty racks, the smell of old wood and older fruit, dust sifting down whenever the wind shook the doors.

Ilse talked. It came out of her in a flood at first—the road, the coal cart, the charcoal burners' daughter who had taught her which mushrooms killed and which merely sickened—and then slowed, and deepened, and became the other account, the one underneath: the account of what it does to a person to spend nine weeks alone underground listening for boots.

"I talk to the trees," she admitted, staring into the stove flame. "When I go up for water. I tell them things. I've become one of those madwomen from stories." A pause. "They're good listeners. Your father's trees. They have his patience."

"They have his stubbornness. He lost arguments to them for thirty years."

"I remember." Ilse smiled, and for a moment the lamplight found the girl from the last summer of peace, sitting cross-legged in the grass with juice on her wrist. "You argued with him about the third row. Whether to graft the new stock. You were nineteen and certain."

"I was right."

"You were right," Ilse agreed gravely, "and insufferable, and I loved watching it." The word *loved* passed between them and neither pretended it hadn't, and the silence that followed was not uncomfortable but full, the way a jar is full, sealed against a worse season.

Then Ilse set down her cup, and her face changed, and Freya saw the survivor come forward again, the woman who had crossed a border in a coal cart.

"Tell me why you're really here," Ilse said. "Not sentiment. Sentiment doesn't get a courier past a checkpoint with a cartload of rootstock in winter. You came here because you already knew, or guessed, or hoped, and hoping isn't like you. What do you know, Freya?"

And Freya, who had rehearsed evasions the whole drive out, who had planned to soften it, to ration the truth in merciful doses, heard herself say none of them—because Ilse had earned the truth the way she'd earned everything, the hard way, on roads Freya hadn't walked.

"On Thursday," she said, "Harbor Command begins detaining every person in this district with enemy ties. The lists are already typed. Yours is on them—I've seen it. I signed—" Her throat closed. She opened it by force. "My name is on the order, Ilse. My commission. If I do nothing, soldiers come up those steps within the week, and they will not be bored corporals with oranges. So I need to move you, quietly, soon, and I have an idea of how, and it is dangerous and probably insane and it requires me to betray everything I've sworn to, which I find I am entirely prepared to do."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment across the little flame.

"What do you need?" she said.

Which was, Freya thought, the most Ilse answer imaginable—no weeping, no gratitude, no argument. Just the question of a woman who had survived by being useful to herself, now offering to be useful to someone else.

"I need a customs stamp," Freya said. "And there is only one man in this district who can give it to me without reading what he's stamping." She paused, and the name felt strange in her mouth, a word from a language she was still learning. "His name is Aldous Renn."

The stove flame jumped in a draft. Ilse went very still, and in the stillness her face did something Freya had never seen it do—something old and buried rising fast through the water, breaking the surface—

"You know that name," Freya said.

"Ilse," Freya said. "What aren't you telling me?"
