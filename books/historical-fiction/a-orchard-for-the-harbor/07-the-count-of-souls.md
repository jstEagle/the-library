## 7. The Count of Souls

The summons came at dawn, folded inside the usual gray envelope that smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, and Aldous Renn knew before he broke the seal that it would be about the coast.

He had learned to read the ministry's moods the way a sailor reads weather—not from any single sign but from an accumulation of small wrongnesses. The ink this morning was too fresh. The clerk who delivered it did not meet his eyes. And beneath his door, where no envelope should have been, the draft from the corridor moved with the particular hush of someone having just walked away from it.

He stood in his narrow room above the counting house and let the paper sit unopened on his palm for the length of three heartbeats. Outside, Vessany's capital was coughing itself awake: the rattle of the milk carts, the groan of the tram wires, somewhere eastward the flat percussion of artillery that the newspapers still called "seasonal exercises." Three years of war had taught the city to sleep through its own dying.

He opened the envelope.

*By order of the Directorate of Population Registers,* the letter began, in the bureaucratic liturgy he knew by heart, *a supplementary enumeration of all coastal districts shall be conducted forthwith, owing to irregularities detected in the autumn census returns.* Irregularities. He read the word twice and felt it like a splinter working under the skin. There had been no irregularities in the autumn returns. He had made certain of that himself, line by line, in long evenings of falsification so careful they had begun to feel like prayer—each forged household adjusted by fractions, a widow here recorded as present when she'd fled south in September, a fisherman's son struck from one parish and entered into another, small mercies distributed across the ledger like coins dropped into different pockets so no single pocket would clink.

And now the Directorate wanted a recount of the coast. Of the districts where those mercies were buried.

*Districts Seven through Eleven,* the order continued, *to include the harbor parishes, the salt marsh settlements, and the orchard lands above Tarnwater.* The orchard lands above Tarnwater. His eyes snagged there as though the words were barbed. He set the letter down on the sill, went to the window, and looked out at nothing—the gray roofscape, a gull hanging motionless over the chimney pots—and let himself think the thing he had been refusing to think since the night Freya Fairweather had stood in her safehouse doorway with the orchard deed showing at the edge of her papers like a wound through a sleeve.

Ilse was up there. Somewhere in those hills, in some collapsed farmhouse or root cellar or abandoned bothy, his sister was hiding from a Republic that had put her name on a detention list—a list Aldous had seen with his own hands, had even carried, because carrying lists was what census men did, and because refusing to carry it would have ended everything far sooner than discovery would.

The recount was not suspicion aimed at him. That was almost worse. It was simply the machine grinding forward, indifferent, thorough, hungry for numbers. The war was going badly; the coast was going to be given up or fought over; and before either happened, the Directorate wanted to know exactly how many souls remained to be evacuated, conscripted, detained, or lost. A count of souls. As if souls could be counted. As if he had not spent eleven years learning precisely which souls slipped between the fingers of any count, and how to make more of them slip.

He dressed in the dark. Census men dressed like clerks, on purpose—gray coat, soft collar, nothing memorable—and he buttoned the gray coat with hands that were steady enough, he noted with detached approval, and wondered when steadiness under fear had become simply the condition of being alive rather than anything worth approving of.

* * *

The Bureau of Enumeration occupied four floors of what had once been a merchant's palace, back when the harbor had merchants instead of ministries. The marble stair still bore a ship's figurehead carved into the newel post, a woman with blank eyes and weathered breasts, and every morning Aldous touched the banister below her without thinking, the way other men crossed themselves.

His supervisor, Magister Corvane, received him in a room that had been a ballroom and was now a landscape of filing cabinets arranged in ranks like artillery. Corvane was sixty, dry as old rope, with the gift—which Aldous had always envied and lately begun to fear—of appearing to hold no opinions whatsoever on any subject except arithmetic.

"Renn." Corvane did not look up from the route schedule spread before him. "You know the coastal parishes."

"I walked them last autumn."

"You'll walk them again. Emergency enumeration, effective tomorrow. Seven through eleven, door to door, every soul physically sighted and entered. No reliance on prior returns." Now he looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient as river stones. "The Directorate believes the autumn figures were inflated. Ghosts on the rolls. Deserters drawing ration cards under their mothers' names, that sort of thing. They want the dead and the departed struck out and the living counted twice."

"And if the living don't wish to be counted?"

Corvane's mouth made a movement that in another man might have been a smile. "Then they're ghosts themselves, aren't they? Strike them out." He slid the route schedule across the desk. "You have nine days. The standard forms, the standard oath. Any anomalies go directly to me, not into your field book. Directly to me, Renn. The Directorate has been very clear about the chain of custody on this one."

Anomalies. Aldous took the schedule and understood, with the clarity that comes in the moment after a blow, exactly what an anomaly would look like: a woman's name on no register anywhere, sheltered in a district she had no right to, in a house that appeared on no tax roll. An anomaly with his sister's face.

"The roads," he heard himself say.

Corvane raised an eyebrow.

"District Nine and Ten are joined by the coast road past Tarnwater," Aldous said. "It flooded in the autumn storms. I walked it in October—it was barely passable then, and we've had two months of freeze and thaw since. If I'm to cover seven through eleven in nine days, I'll lose a full day bogging down on that stretch. Better to come inland, take the high road through the valley, and drop down to the harbor parishes from the north. Adds perhaps half a day to Nine and Ten but saves two on the whole circuit."

He said it evenly, with the mild pedantry of a man concerned only with logistics, and watched Corvane consider it. This was the trick of lying well, Aldous had learned: never lie about the thing you care about. Care leaks. Lie only about roads.

"The high road through the valley," Corvane repeated. "That runs behind the orchard lands?"

"It skirts them. The orchard district is covered separately—District Eight, door to door in the villages. But the farmsteads up in the hills—" he shrugged, a small economical gesture, "—most of them are empty. The fruit trade died with the blockade. Half those houses haven't had a soul in them since before the war."

Which was true, and false, and both halves of it true enough to pass.

Corvane drew the schedule back, made a notation with his fountain pen—a scratchy, decisive sound, like a rat moving in a wall—and initialled it. "Approved. Take the valley route. Report daily by courier pouch." He glanced up again, and for a moment something moved behind the river-stone eyes, something Aldous could not name and did not dare examine. "You've done good work, Renn. The Directorate notices such things. In times like these, accuracy is a form of loyalty."

"Yes, Magister."

"In times like these," Corvane said again, softly, returning to his papers, "everything is a form of loyalty. Or its absence. Dismissed."

Aldous walked out past the ranks of cabinets, past the figurehead with her blank eyes, and found that his palms were damp against the route schedule. Borrowed time, he thought. All of it, borrowed. Every day of Ilse's safety, every day of his own freedom, drawn against an account he could not see and could not replenish, and now the creditor had called for an audit.

* * *

There was a junior clerk named Tam Wexler who worked the dispatch room on the second floor, a young man of perhaps twenty-two with quick hands, quicker eyes, and the terrible earnestness of the recently promoted. It was Wexler who prepared the courier pouches, Wexler who logged the route schedules, Wexler who had once, months ago, asked Aldous with open admiration how he managed to find so many hidden households in the hill parishes—as if the finding of frightened people were a skill to be admired.

Aldous stopped at the dispatch window to confirm his first pouch pickup, and Wexler looked up from his stamps with that bright, guileless attention that Aldous had come to dread more than any interrogation.

"Coastal recount," Wexler said, reading the schedule upside down with the shameless fluency of youth. "Seven through eleven. That's a big circuit for one man in nine days."

"They asked for me."

"They always ask for you." Wexler grinned. "You're the best they've got. My uncle lives up past Tarnwater—says you're the first census man who ever climbed the hill to his door instead of shouting up from the road. Says you counted his bees."

"Bees affect the honey levy."

"Bees," Wexler agreed solemnly, and stamped a form. Then, without any change of tone at all, without looking up: "You changed your route. Autumn circuit you took the coast road past Tarnwater. This one you're going inland. Road trouble?"

For a moment—one heartbeat, perhaps two—the dispatch room was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a door slammed. Aldous felt the familiar sensation, which had visited him twice before in his life and each time had changed him permanently, of standing at the edge of something and feeling it give slightly under his weight.

"Flooding," he said. "Freeze-thaw since October. The culvert at Miller's Bend went out, according to the road survey. I filed a query about it in November." He had, in fact, filed no such query; but queries were logged in triplicate, and the third copy went to the archive in the basement, and Aldous had spent part of the previous evening in that archive with a candle and a steady hand. The record existed now. It bore a date in November. It was, in the eyes of the Republic, older than the lie it supported.

Wexler nodded, satisfied, and reached for the next stamp. "Shame about the roads. Whole country's coming apart, my mother says. Roads, railways, government—" he laughed at his own boldness, the laugh of a boy whistling past a graveyard, "—don't tell anyone I said the last one."

"No one will hear it from me."

But as Aldous descended the stairs, past the blind carved woman, out into the gray morning where the artillery had fallen silent and the silence was somehow worse than the noise, he replayed the exchange and found the splinter of it working deeper. Wexler had known his autumn route. Had remembered it. Had noticed the change and mentioned it casually, the way one mentions weather—casually being precisely how a careful man would test a hypothesis he wasn't yet ready to voice.

Or perhaps the boy was nothing. Perhaps he was exactly what he seemed: twenty-two, eager, fond of stamps and gossip, loyal to the Directorate with the uncomplicated loyalty of someone who had never yet been asked to choose between it and anything else. The capital was full of such boys. They were the Republic's most durable product.

But Corvane had said *anomalies go directly to me*, and had said it twice, and had let his gaze rest one beat too long. And the Directorate had ordered a recount not of one suspect district but of five, sweeping wide, the way a man sweeps a floor when he has lost a coin and isn't sure which room he lost it in. Somewhere in the machinery, a gear had caught on something. Perhaps only a ghost on a ration roll in District Seven. Perhaps a discrepancy in a customs ledger, an old entry in a dead woman's handwriting beside the name Renn, a thread that, pulled gently, ran from a burned warehouse file through a safehouse door in the capital to a hillside above Tarnwater where a woman who officially did not exist was waiting, trusting, for a promise kept.

Aldous walked home through streets where the snow lay gray as ash, and made his preparations the way he made all of them now: methodically, thoroughly, and with the constant low awareness that method and thoroughness were merely elegant ways of arranging furniture in a burning house. He packed the census forms, the oath book, the indelible pencil. He packed, beneath them, in a false bottom of his satchel, the things a census man was not supposed to carry: a second identity card, blank but signed; a fold of banknotes; and a letter, unsent, undeliverable, addressed in careful script to *I.R., above Tarnwater*, containing nothing but instructions for what she should do if a gray-coated man came up the hill asking questions—run south, avoid the coast road, trust no uniform, wait for the orchard woman.

Nine days. Five districts. Every soul sighted and entered, and any anomaly reported upward along a chain of custody that ended, if the threads held together much longer, around his own neck.

On the eve of departure, he sat at his desk by lamplight and wrote his first field report in advance—District Seven, preliminary observations, weather, road conditions, general morale—all of it invented, all of it plausible, dated three days hence. A man who wrote reports for days that hadn't happened yet was either a prophet or a forger, and the Republic had no use for prophets. He sealed the report, marked it for the courier pouch, and set it beside his packed satchel.

Through the window, far to the west, the sky above the coast held a faint bruised glow where the harbor lights burned, and beyond them, invisible at this distance and this hour, the dark line of the hills where his sister hid among dead apple trees, counting the same numbered days he counted, trusting the same borrowed time.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark he sat a while longer, listening to the city breathe, and thought: *One of us will be late. One of us will be early. The count always finds somebody.*

In the morning, a junior clerk named Tam Wexler would log the route change in the master ledger, initial it, and pause—with the stamp hovering, with that bright, guileless attention turned inward at last—over a single line in the November archive: a road survey query, filed by Renn, concerning a culvert that Wexler's own cousin, a road engineer, had inspected in December and found entirely sound.

The stamp hovered. Outside, the snow began again, soft and thick, falling on the capital, on the coast, on the orchards, erasing roads and footprints and boundaries alike, as if the world itself were trying to help everyone hide—and failing, as the world always failed, to hide anyone for long.
