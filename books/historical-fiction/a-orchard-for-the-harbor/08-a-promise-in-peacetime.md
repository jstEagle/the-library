## 8. A Promise in Peacetime

The cellar smelled of apples that had been dead for years.

Ilse had learned every corner of it in the six weeks since she'd come down the broken steps with her shoes in her hands and her heart going like a trapped bird. The Fairweather orchard cellar was a long low room cut into the hillside above the harbor road, walled in fieldstone gone soft with damp, and it still held the ghost of the fruit that had once been packed here by the thousandweight — a sweetness like something remembered rather than present, sunk into the stone itself, into the wooden racks and the rotted slats of the sorting tables. Her grandmother's generation had called such places *kellerhüs*, house-cellers, and said a good one kept the summer alive all winter.

This one kept a dead summer alive. Ilse lived inside its memory like a tenant.

She knew the sound of Freya's step on the stairs now — the particular hesitation at the third step from the bottom, where the tread had cracked and gave underfoot if you trusted it too much. Freya never trusted it. She came down the way she did everything, carefully, completely, as though the world were an order to be delivered intact.

"You brought light," Ilse said, without turning from the wall where she'd pinned her map.

"I brought light." Freya set the storm lantern on the sorting table. Its flame stood up straight and yellow behind the glass, and the shadows of the empty racks went reeling across the ceiling like the ribs of some enormous fish. "And bread. And news you're not going to like."

"Then give me the bread first."

It was an old joke between them, older than the war: *bad news keeps better on a full stomach.* Freya almost smiled. Almost. Ilse watched the almost happen and felt the fear she'd been carrying all week put out another root.

She broke the loaf and handed half back across the table. They ate standing, the way soldiers eat, the way fugitives eat, and the lantern threw their two shadows together against the far wall so that they seemed joined at the shoulder, one creature with four hands and two hungers.

"Ilse." Freya set down her crust. She brushed the crumbs from her fingers with that same terrible care. "I have to tell you what I've done."

*What I've done.* Not *what has happened.* Ilse set her own crust down too, though her stomach clenched around it and begged her not to waste it.

"Before you do," she said, "I want to tell you something. Because I've been rehearsing it for six weeks, in this cellar, talking to the dark like a madwoman, and if you go first I'll never say it, and I think—" her voice caught; she pushed through it the way you push through a drift of snow, committing to the step before you know if there's ground beneath— "I think you need to hear it more than I need to say it. But I need to say it anyway."

Freya waited. That was the thing about her that strangers mistook for coldness: she could wait like the earth waits, without impatience, without judgment. Ilse had loved it about her even at fourteen, when waiting had seemed like nothing, when time had seemed like a well that would never run dry.

"Sit down," Ilse said. "Both of us. This is a story for sitting."

There was only one chair left in the cellar, an old orchard ladder-chair with a woven seat, and they shared it the way they had shared everything as girls, hip to hip, Freya's shoulder warm against hers. The lantern hissed softly. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the bare trees with a sound like the sea rehearsing.

* * *

"You remember the summer we made the promise," Ilse began. "You don't have to nod. I know you remember. You remember everything — it's your curse and your trade."

The orchard had been at its heavy height then, the last true peacetime summer, the branches so laden with Fairweather Pippins that props held them up like crutches under old men's arms. Ilse had come to stay with her aunt in the harbor town, and every day she had walked up the cart track to the orchard where Freya worked among the trees with her sleeves rolled and her forearms streaked green to the elbow, and every day Freya taught her something new: how to tell a ripe apple by lifting it and turning it a quarter-turn — *if it lets go, it's ready; if it fights, let it be* — how to graft, how to read weather in the look of the light off the water below.

"We were lying in the grass between the third and fourth rows," Ilse said. "I remember because you'd propped your boots up on a fallen branch and your hat over your face, and you said, right through the hat, *if there's ever a war, you come here.*"

"I remember saying it," Freya murmured.

"You said it like you were reading a deed. Like it was already law. And I laughed, because there wasn't going to be a war, everyone said so, and you took the hat off your face and looked at me, and I stopped laughing." Ilse's hands had gone still in her lap. "You said, *I'm not joking, Ilschen. If there is ever a war, whatever happens, whoever comes asking, you walk up this track and you knock on my mother's door and you are family. You are Renn-Fairweather. I will write it in the ledger myself.*"

"And then I wrote it," Freya said quietly. "In the ledger. My mother's hand beside yours. *Renn, harbor household, kinship claimed.*"

"That page is why I'm alive."

The words came out of Ilse flat and hard, like stones dropped on stone, and she heard in them all the weight of what came after, and she made herself keep going, because she had promised herself she would say all of it, every mile of it, and let Freya carry her share.

"When the border closed, I was on the wrong side of it. You know that part. What you don't know—" and here her voice did break, and she let it, because some things cannot be carried smoothly— "is what the crossing cost."

She told it plainly, because ornament would have been unbearable. She told about leaving Vessany's eastern districts in the second winter, walking at night along the frozen drainage canals because the roads had checkpoints and the fields had dogs. About the woman who had guided them — eleven of them, then nine — and who turned back at the river for her fee and no goodbye. About the farmer's barn where they'd hidden three days in the hay, listening to soldiers laugh in the yard, close enough to smell the tobacco through the boards. About the boy, seventeen, whose name she still didn't know, who coughed the whole way and grew quieter and quieter until the morning they found him cold in the ditch where he'd slept, and they buried him shallow because the ground was iron, and she had put a stone at his head and promised him a better marker someday, and did not know if she would live to keep even that.

"The ninth day," Ilse said, "we crossed the occupied zone in a root wagon. Under sacking, between crates of dormant stock. It was February. The driver was paid and drunk and brave, which is a bad combination but the only kind that does that work." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "I lay against those bare-root trees for hours with the cold coming up through the wagon bed like water, and I thought: this is what it is to be an apple tree in winter. Dug up, roots bare, heeled in somewhere that isn't yours, waiting to see if you take or die. And I thought of you, and I thought of the ledger, and I thought: *she wrote it down. Ink doesn't freeze.*"

Freya had gone very still beside her. Ilse felt the stillness the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm.

"I never doubted," Ilse said. "That's what I need you to understand. Six weeks in this cellar, eating raw potatoes and listening to the patrols on the coast road, counting the cracks in the ceiling, and I never doubted, not once, not in the worst hour. People asked me afterward — the guide-woman, the others on the road — *who takes you in? Who claims you?* And I said, *the Fairweather girl,* and they said, *there's a war on, girl, promises rot faster than apples,* and I said—" her throat closed; she opened it— "*not hers.*"

The lantern hissed. Above them the wind worried the bare branches.

"So that's my half," Ilse finished. "Now you said you have something to tell me. Tell me."

* * *

Freya did not speak at once. Ilse watched her gather herself, and understood, with a cold sliding certainty, that whatever was coming had been carried a long way and had worn grooves into the shoulders that bore it.

"I signed an order," Freya said.

The words dropped into the cellar like a coin into a well, turning as they fell, and Ilse listened for them to land.

"Harbor Command received the capital's dispatches through me. The Republic is abandoning the coast. And before the withdrawal, all persons with documented enemy ties in the coastal districts are to be detained — taken inland, to the holding camps at Merrow Fen." Freya's voice was level, courier-level, the voice she used for orders, and Ilse hated that voice suddenly, hated its professional calm the way you hate a locked door. "The list came with the dispatches. Compiled from customs ledgers, census rolls, harbor manifests."

"Ilse Renn," Ilse said. Not a question.

"Ilse Renn," Freya confirmed. "Third name on the second page."

"And the order needed signing."

"The district officer was dead — fever, two days before. The dispatches required a commissioned signature to activate the detention directive. Mine was the only commission in the room." Freya's hands were flat on her knees, pressing down as though her knees might otherwise rise without her. "So I signed it."

The silence after was very long. Ilse made herself sit inside it instead of fleeing it. She looked at the lantern, at the racks, at the map she'd drawn on the back of a seed catalog — the coast road, the patrol times, the gap in the wire at the north fence — all the careful architecture of survival she'd built on the foundation of one sentence written in a ledger in a summer that no longer existed anywhere but here.

"You signed the order that says I should be taken to Merrow Fen," she said.

"Yes."

"With your own hand."

"With my own hand." Freya turned her head at last, and in the lantern light Ilse saw that her eyes were dry and wrecked, both at once, the way only certain kinds of grief can manage. "I have spent every hour since trying to find the version of that moment where I don't sign. There isn't one. If I refuse, they send another courier, they activate it anyway, and they open an inquiry into why the loyal courier hesitated — and an inquiry means your name moves from page two to page one. Signing it was the only way to keep any control over what happened next. I told myself that. I've been telling myself that. Some nights I believe it."

"And other nights?"

"Other nights I know I inked my name beside a list of human beings like varieties in a nursery catalog, and that the difference between me and the people who built the camps is only a matter of degree, and degrees are exactly what wars are made of." She drew a breath that shuddered at its edges despite everything she'd done to steady it. "I'm not telling you this to be forgiven. I'm telling you because you nearly died on the road to reach a promise, and you deserve to know the promise was kept by someone who also did this. Both things are true. I need you to know both things are true."

Ilse looked at her hands. They were chapped, nail-bitten, the hands of a woman who had crawled under fences. Somewhere in the walls above them a beam ticked as the temperature fell, the old house settling into its bones.

When she had imagined this reunion — in barns, in ditches, in the root wagon with the cold rising through her spine — she had imagined weeping, and running, and the two of them laughing at the impossibility of having made it. She had not imagined this: the promise-keeper confessing, the promise-keeper's signature on the paper that would have shipped her inland like confiscated goods.

And yet.

"I'm angry," Ilse said slowly, testing each word before trusting it. "Don't mistake me. I'm angry in my teeth. You signed a paper with my name on it, Freya. Whatever the arithmetic — whatever they'd have done if you hadn't — my name was under your pen, and you pressed down."

"Yes."

"But." She turned fully toward her, hip to hip on the old chair, and took Freya's face in both chapped hands, forcing the grey eyes to meet hers. "But you burned the capital's letter. Aldous told me — the census man, the brother, don't look like that, of course he tells me things, he's been feeding us information like a nurse spooning broth. You were ordered to destroy everything and run alone, and instead you memorized a promise you made in peacetime, and you walked into a detention zone to find me. You signed the order, and then you spent everything you had un-signing it. Every favor, every risk, every piece of yourself." Her thumbs traced the hollows under Freya's eyes, the new lines the war had carved there. "That's not nothing. That isn't nothing, and I won't pretend it is."

"Forgiveness shouldn't be arithmetic either," Freya whispered.

"No," Ilse agreed. "Which is why I'm not giving it to you tonight."

She felt Freya flinch — actually flinch, the small animal motion beneath the courier's calm — and she gentled her hands.

"Listen to me. Forgiveness that arrives whole, the same night, is just mercy, and mercy is cheap and I am not feeling merciful. What I feel is this:" she laid Freya's hand over her own heart, over the pulse going quick and stubborn beneath the rough knit of her sweater. "This beat here walked nine days and buried a boy and lay under sacking in a root wagon because a girl in an orchard took off her hat and made a promise like a law. You are that girl. You are also the woman who signed the order. I am choosing, tonight, to live in the orchard and not in the office — but I get to choose again tomorrow, and the day after, and you don't get to assume which way I'll choose. That's my terms. That's what I offer: not absolution. Attendance. I will stay while you earn it back, row by row, the way an orchard comes back after a bad winter. Slowly. With pruning. With years."

For a moment Freya said nothing. Then a breath went out of her that seemed to have been held since the day of the signing, weeks ago, a held breath Ilse realized she herself must somehow have been breathing around, the two of them sharing one lung across the miles without knowing it.

"Row by row," Freya repeated.

"It's how we do things in orchards," Ilse said. "Nothing ripens all at once. Everything worth keeping takes seasons."

They stayed like that, forehead against forehead, while the lantern burned low and the wind rose outside, and Ilse let herself have thirty seconds — thirty, no more — of simply being found.

Then she pulled back, because the map was on the wall and the patrols kept their schedule whether or not promises were being mended, and because Freya had said *news you're not going to like* and had not yet given it.

"All right," Ilse said. "Tell me the rest. You said there was news. And Freya—" she caught her friend's wrist as she reached for the lantern— "whatever it is, however bad it is — remember we plant in winter. Dormant stock. It looks like death, a whip of wood with bare roots. Everyone who doesn't know better thinks it's finished. But it's the *only* thing that survives the transplanting. Anything you move in leaf dies. It's the things that look most dead that take."

Freya looked at her a long moment, and Ilse watched something shift behind her eyes — a plan, half-formed, taking root.

"Then it's fitting," Freya said, "that what I have to tell you involves a shipment of rootstock."
