## 9. Manifest of Winter Fruit

The idea came to her the way cold comes through a window seam—not all at once, but in a thin, persistent line that she could not stop tracing with her mind.

Freya sat in the cellar of the orchard house with a single candle guttering between them, and Ilse slept above, or pretended to, wrapped in the wool blanket that still smelled faintly of cedar and of summers neither of them would see again. The cellar walls were stone older than the Republic itself, older than any flag that had ever flown over Vessany's harbors, and they held the cold the way a cupped hand holds water—generously, without judgment. Freya had come down here to think, because thinking upstairs meant listening for hoofbeats on the frost-hardened lane, and she could not plan while her ears were full of fear.

She had brought down the one thing she had not burned: her mother's trading ledger, salvaged from the customs house before the coastal districts closed. It lay open on her knees now, its pages soft as cloth from years of handling, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. Her mother had kept it in a beautiful hand—each entry ruled true, each figure set down like a small act of faith in an orderly world. *Twelve barrels cider apples, to Port Calloway. Forty young trees, whips, to the nursery at Sennet. One crate quinces, gift, no charge.*

And there, in the column of names, over and over across twenty years: *Renn. Renn. Renn.* Seed stock bought from old Tomas Renn. Grafting wax. Scions of the Winesap that had made the Fairweather orchard famous in three counties. Two families braided together in ink long before their daughters had braided themselves together in anything at all.

It was the ledger that gave her the thought, but it was the smell of the cellar that made it live—the deep, sweet rot of last autumn's fallen fruit, the mineral chill of the root cellar, and beneath it all the particular green scent of dormant wood, the scent of trees asleep and waiting, holding their spring inside themselves like a secret.

Dormant rootstock.

In winter, the nurseries shipped trees by the thousand. Bare-rooted, bundled in damp straw, their branches bare as broomsticks, their buds sealed tight as fists. No one inspected dormant wood closely. A tree out of leaf was a tree without a face; forty crates of them were a forest of anonymity. They traveled in unheated holds, stacked like cordwood, and no customs officer in a hurry wanted to pry into straw that might carry blight spores onto his gloves. They checked manifests. They checked stamps. They checked, if they checked at all, the first crate and the last, and between those two points a human being could lie curled in the dark among the roots, breathing the green breath of sleeping trees, for as long as the crossing took.

Freya sat very still and let the plan assemble itself, piece by piece, the way ice forms on a pond—from the edges inward, silently, until one morning the whole surface bears weight.

* * *

Her mother's trading license was the second piece, and it frightened her more than the first, because using it felt like waking the dead.

The license lived sewn into the lining of the coat Freya had worn out of the capital—a document heavy with seals, issued in the last year of real peace, bearing the name Maren Fairweather and the right to ship fruit, stock, and nursery goods from Harbor Quay to any port not under embargo. Her mother had carried it for thirty years. She had died believing, perhaps, that the paper would outlive the world that honored it. Licenses did not expire so much as their countries did, and the Republic of Vessany was expiring now, week by week, district by district—but it had not yet finished dying, and while a thing dies slowly it can still be used.

There was a clerk's maxim Freya had learned in her courier training: *a document is only as dead as the stamp beneath it.* Her mother's license bore the harbor stamp, the agricultural board countersignature, the tax seal—all of them struck before the war, all of them still technically valid, because the machinery that would have invalidated them had been requisitioned for the war effort and stood idle, rusting, its clerks conscripted. The paper trail of peacetime still ran through the harbor like a ghost through a house, unseen but not yet exorcised.

If the manifest was clean—if the license number matched the registry, if the fees were paid, if the stamp at the bottom was genuine—then the shipment would pass. Not because the system was kind. Because the system was drowning, and drowning men do not count the straws they clutch.

But the stamp. That was the hinge on which everything turned, and Freya turned it over in her mind until it hurt.

A trading license authorized a shipment; it did not certify one. For that, the manifest needed the customs examiner's stamp—the blue wax, the double ring, the initials of the officer who swore he had seen the goods and found them what they claimed to be. In the old days, any of a dozen examiners might have done. Now the harbor offices had been consolidated, thinned by conscription, and the examination of outbound cargo in the winter quarter fell to a skeleton staff working double tides.

Which meant, when Freya finally let herself think it, that the stamp she needed belonged to Aldous Renn.

She put the candle out and sat in the dark and hated how quickly her mind had gone to him, how inevitably, the way water goes to a low place. Because it was not only that he held the office. It was that he had already looked at her once across a table with the census form between them, and had seen what he saw, and had said nothing. He had already chosen, once, in a small way, with a small thing—a line left blank, a hesitation unreported. She would be asking him to choose again, in a large way, with a large thing, and the difference between those two choices was the difference between a fine and a firing squad.

And she would be asking him to do it for his sister. For Ilse, who was asleep above her head in the house their mothers' friendship had built, whose name was written on a detention list with Freya's own signature beneath it.

*Hope requires betrayal,* her handler had told her once, in another life, teaching her the grammar of the work. *Every plan worth making asks someone to give up something they cannot get back. If your plan costs no one anything, it is not a plan; it is a wish.* He had meant it cynically, a warning against sentiment. But lying in the dark cellar with the ledger against her chest, Freya understood it differently. Hope required betrayal because hope required motion, and every motion in a occupied country stepped on someone. The only question—the only question that would ever matter again—was whether you chose whom you betrayed, or let the war choose for you.

* * *

In the morning she went up to the kitchen and found Ilse already awake, standing at the window with her sleeves pushed back, looking out at the orchard rows where the snow lay blue in the shadows of the trees.

"They need pruning," Ilse said, without turning. "Look at the water sprouts on the Gravensteins. Your mother would have wept."

"Your mother pruned them last," Freya said. "Eleven years ago. Before the border closed."

"I know." Ilse touched the glass, and the cold of it must have bitten her fingertip, but she didn't pull away. "I counted the winters. Eleven. I used to stand at my window in Calloway and try to imagine these trees growing without me. I couldn't do it. I imagined them frozen instead. Stuck at the size they were the summer I left." She turned then, and her face in the gray light was thinner than Freya remembered, carved down to its essentials, but the eyes were the same—dark, level, unhurried, eyes that had watched things and refused to look away. "They grew anyway. Didn't they?"

"They grew anyway."

Ilse nodded, satisfied, as if this confirmed some private theory about the persistence of living things, and went to put the kettle on the iron stove. Watching her move through her mother's kitchen—opening drawers and knowing what was in them, finding the good knife by touch, setting out two cups without asking—Freya felt the plan rise in her throat like something that needed to be said before it choked her.

"Ilse. I've thought of a way to get you out."

The kettle did not stop its low gathering note. Ilse filled it anyway, set it on the ring, adjusted the damper with a small precise movement, and only then turned around. "Tell me."

"A shipment of rootstock. Dormant trees, bound for Sennet—it's neutral water, they're still taking Vessany freight. You'd travel in a crate. With the trees. It's—" She heard her own voice go flat and professional, the courier's voice, the voice that stripped the horror out of things so they could be planned. She stopped, and started again, plainer. "It will be cold, and dark, and the crossing takes four days in winter weather. You'd have to lie still among the bundles with straw packed around you, and if anyone opened the crate, you'd have maybe ten seconds to decide whether to run or to pretend to be cargo. I won't dress it up. It's a terrible way to cross a sea."

"It's better than the way I came," Ilse said.

Freya had heard fragments of that way—fragments only, offered in the cellar at night in a voice kept deliberately light, the way one describes a fall by describing the ground. The mountain passes closed behind her. The river guide who took payment in rings. Three days in a hay barn with soldiers billeted below, listening to them laugh. She did not ask for more. There would be time, or there would not.

"The manifest has to be perfect," Freya went on. "My mother's license covers the shipment. The registry still honors the number—I checked the gazette lists before I left the capital; the agricultural board hasn't formally dissolved, it just hasn't met since autumn. Fees can be paid at the quay. Everything can be true except one thing." She made herself say it. "The examiner's stamp. And the examiner for the winter quarter is Aldous."

The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. Then she crossed the kitchen, took down the tin of black tea from the high shelf where it had waited eleven years, and measured it out with the same care her mother had once measured grafting wax. When she spoke, her back was still to Freya.

"You're afraid to ask him."

"Yes."

"Because of what it costs him."

"Yes."

Ilse set the tin down. She turned around, and there was something in her face that Freya had seen only once before—on the last morning of that peacetime summer, standing between the apple rows with juice staining her fingers, when she had said, *if it ever comes to it, you'll know where to find me, and I'll know where to find you,* and both of them had laughed at the absurdity of needing such a promise, and both of them had meant it completely.

"My brother filed the census that should have sent me to the detention rolls," Ilse said. "He wrote *household: none* beside my name, or wrote nothing at all—I don't know which, and I don't want to know, because either way he lied with a government pen in his hand. Do you understand what that means for a man like Aldous? He irons his shirts. He keeps his receipts. He has never in his life been late for anything, and he committed treason in ink, quietly, on an ordinary Tuesday, because a woman he hadn't seen in eleven years was hiding in our mother's orchard." She picked up the kettle and poured, and the steam rose between them like a small white flag. "You are afraid to ask him for a stamp. He has already given me his whole life. Ask him."

* * *

Freya spent two days building the manifest, because if it was going to save Ilse's life it deserved to be built the way her mother had built orchards—slowly, honestly, with attention to what each thing actually needed.

She copied figures in her best hand, a hand trained in the capital to be unremarkable, the handwriting of a hundred thousand clerks. Forty crates, mixed dormant stock: apple, pear, quince. Weight, gross and net. Origin: Fairweather Orchard, Harbor District. Destination: Sennet Nursery, free port. Consignee: the nursery's name, verified against a two-year-old trade directory she had carried in her dispatch case through three checkpoints without once being asked to open it. License number: her mother's, entered in the space provided, the ink drying brown-black in the cold room.

She paid the harbor fees herself, in coin counted out at the customs wicket by a boy of perhaps sixteen who stamped the receipt without looking up, who had the shell-shocked efficiency of someone doing a dead man's job. The quay smelled of tar and frozen fish and coal smoke, and the cranes stood idle against a sky the color of dishwater, and everywhere the Republic's paperwork continued to move through the system like blood through a body that had already decided to die.

On the second evening she walked past the examination office on the north side of the customs house. Its windows were lit—one lamp, one desk, one man. Through the frosted glass she could see him bent over his work, and even distorted by the old glass she knew the posture: the careful shoulders, the head inclined toward the page as though the paper might say something worth hearing. A man who believed in documents. Who had staked everything, already, on the belief that a line written truthfully mattered more than a line written safely.

Freya stood in the falling snow outside the customs house until her feet went numb, rehearsing what she would say. Every version ended the same way: *I am asking you to betray your commission, and I cannot promise you anything except that your sister is alive, and that I am sorry.* There was no clever formulation. There was no courier's trick. Some doors could only be opened by knocking.

Inside, the lamp burned. The pen scratched. Somewhere beyond the harbor bar, the tide was turning, indifferent and exact, keeping its own ledger.

She climbed the steps, and knocked.
