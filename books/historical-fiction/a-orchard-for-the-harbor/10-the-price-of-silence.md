## 10. The Price of Silence

The Customs House on Vane Street had been built in the confident years, when the Republic believed it would outlast its architecture. Its ceilings were high enough to hold echoes, its windows tall enough to admit a gray and generous light, and its stone steps had been worn into shallow bowls by a century of clerks climbing toward their desks with ledgers under their arms. Aldous Renn had climbed those steps for eleven years. He knew which of them sang underfoot and which had gone mute, and on the last morning of February he climbed them slowly, the way a man climbs when he is no longer certain the room at the top of the stairs wants him in it.

The building was colder than it should have been. The coal ration had been cut twice since midwinter, and the great iron stoves in the counting hall stood cold as monuments, their chimneys breathing nothing. Clerks worked in their coats, fingers stiff around pens, breath faintly visible in the upper corners of the rooms where the warmth gave up. The war had entered the building the way cold enters a house—through the gaps, the seams, the places no one had thought to caulk—and now it sat in every office like a tenant who paid no rent.

Aldous's own office was a narrow room on the third floor with a window that looked out on the customs yard, where crates were stacked in the rain and the gulls argued over the fish market refuse two streets away. He had a desk, a filing cabinet with a broken lock he had never repaired because no one stole from the Republic anymore except the Republic itself, and a rubber stamp that had outlived three directors. The stamp was the whole of his authority: *Renn, A. — Census & Trade Verification, District IV.* When it came down on a manifest, goods moved. When it withheld itself, goods rotted on the quay. It was, he had often thought, a small and stupid kind of power, the power of a gatekeeper in a wall no one wanted to enter anymore.

He had not slept. The falsified census entries from the week before sat in the district archive like a debt he could feel accruing interest, and the junior clerk, Pell, had asked him twice more about the coastal routes—once in the corridor, once at the door of the records room, with a politeness that had begun to feel like surveillance. Aldous had answered both times with the same mild smile, the same story about washed-out roads and a surveyor's report he had, that morning, actually drafted and backdated so that it existed. He was learning, at fifty-one, how easily the hands perform treason when the mind is elsewhere. It frightened him how little it frightened him.

He was at his desk with the window open a hand's width—the cold kept him awake—when the knock came.

Two knocks. Even, unhurried. Not the knock of a supervisor, which was always three and always impatient, and not the knock of Pell, which was always four and always apologetic. This was the knock of someone who had knocked on many doors in many towns and had learned to make the sound unremarkable.

"Come in."

She closed the door behind her without being asked to, which was itself an answer to a question he had been carrying for weeks. Freya Fairweather stood in the gray light of his office wearing a plain traveling coat the color of wet slate, her dark hair pinned back with no ornament, and she looked, as she had looked at his own kitchen table in the autumn, like a woman who had made an inventory of every exit in the room before sitting down. She did not remove her gloves.

"Master Renn," she said.

"Madam Fairweather." He rose, because his mother had raised him to rise, and the habit held even now, even here. "This is a customs office. If you've come about a shipment, the trade desk is downstairs."

"I haven't come about a shipment." She remained standing. Her eyes went briefly to the window, the yard, the door—completing the inventory—and returned to him. "I've come because I need a stamp. Yours."

The word *stamp* sat in the cold air between them. Aldous looked at her for a long moment, and then he sat back down, slowly, because his legs had decided the matter for him.

"You should know," he said, "that a man in my position cannot simply—"

"I know exactly what a man in your position can and cannot do." Her voice was low and level. It was the voice, he thought, of someone reading a dispatch aloud in a room where the walls listened. "I know that your stamp means nothing without a counter-signature from the harbor office. I know that shipments bound for neutral ports are inspected twice, once at the yard gate and once at the crane. I know that the manifest has to match the cargo to the ounce, because the Republic has stopped trusting everyone and started weighing everything. I've done my homework, Master Renn. I've had nothing to do for weeks but homework."

"Then you also know," he said quietly, "that if I stamp a false manifest, and it is discovered—and it will be discovered, because everything is discovered now—then I am not fined. I am not dismissed. I am shot against the north wall of the yard, and my name goes on a list that my sister's name is already on."

He had not meant to say that last part. It came out of him the way blood comes from a cut you haven't noticed—the pressure had been building for weeks, since the kitchen table, since the deed with the orchard on it, since the night he had sat up with a lamp and a pen over the census forms and falsified the first entry in eleven years of honest service. Ilse. He had not spoken his sister's name aloud in this building, perhaps not in this city, since the border closed.

Freya did not flinch. But something moved behind her eyes, some gate swinging on its hinge, and when she spoke again her voice had lost a degree of its armor.

"I know," she said. "I know what it costs. I'm not asking you to do it for nothing."

"I'm not asking for anything."

"That's what I came to argue about."

* * *

They stood on either side of his desk like two parties to a negotiation neither wanted, and the negotiation, when it finally came, was stranger and more terrible than either of them had prepared for.

She laid the papers on the blotter. A manifest, drawn up in a fair commercial hand, for a shipment of dormant rootstock and scionwood—apple, pear, quince—two hundred and forty crates, consigned from the Fairweather orchard holding in the coastal district, bound for Callowmere, a free port that had so far declined to take sides in the war because taking sides was bad for business. The trading license attached to it was yellowed at the edges, the ink faded, the signature of its holder a small, upright, disciplined hand.

Aldous knew the hand. He had seen it in the customs ledger, in the old volumes, beside the entries for fruit shipments that had come down from the orchard to the harbor every autumn of his childhood. He had seen it beside his own family's name, in the years when the Renns had bought cider and pears from the Fairweathers and the two households had been, if not friends, then something warmer than accounts.

"Your mother's license," he said.

"It's still valid. Licenses don't expire because their holders do. I checked the statute." Freya's gloved finger rested on the manifest. "Two hundred and forty crates of rootstock, packed in damp straw, sealed against the cold. Dormant wood. Nobody inspects dormant wood closely in February—it looks like kindling and it smells like a cellar. The crates are deep. The straw is deep. There's room in the middle of the shipment for—" She stopped. She did not say the name. She looked at him instead, and made him say it, or not say it, with his eyes.

"My sister," Aldous said. The word came out of him like a tooth.

"Is alive," Freya said. "Yes."

He had known. Of course he had known—she would not be here otherwise, would not have built this whole edifice of manifests and statutes and straw. But knowing a thing in the marrow and hearing it in the air are different countries, and Aldous Renn sat very still in his cold office with his hands flat on the desk and let the words arrive in his body one at a time. *Is alive.* Present tense. The tense he had not dared to use in his own head for two years, because the border had closed with her on the wrong side of it and the lists had begun and the lists, everyone knew, did not distinguish between the detained and the dead for very long.

"Where?" he asked. It was a foolish question. He knew it was a foolish question the moment it left him, and Freya's silence confirmed it—she would not say, could not say, and his asking put her in the position of refusing him, which was a small cruelty neither of them had budgeted for.

"No," he said, before she could answer. "Don't. Don't tell me. If I don't know, I can't—" He passed a hand over his eyes. "Eleven years, Madam Fairweather. I have filed eleven years of true returns. I have counted households the way a priest counts souls, and I have never once written down a lie. Last week I wrote down three. Do you understand what that is like? It's like—" He searched for it. "It's like finding that your own handwriting has been forging your name."

"I understand," she said. "Better than you think."

"Then you understand that I can't take payment for it." He said it firmly, because he had decided this in the night, lying awake under his coat with the lamp out. "Whatever you've brought to offer—money, favors, a place on the ship, passage—I won't take it. If I do this, I do it the way a man pays a debt. Not the way a man earns one."

"There is no debt."

"There is." He looked at the license, at his mother's handwriting beside the Fairweather name in his memory, at the summers his family had eaten fruit that had ripened in that orchard, at Ilse at fourteen standing under someone else's apple trees with the juice of someone else's pears on her chin, laughing at something he could no longer hear across the distance of twenty years and a war. "There is a debt. My family ate from your family's trees. And when the lists came, when they started taking people with enemy ties off the coastal districts—my mother wrote to the harbor office, she wrote to the ministry, she wrote to everyone, and do you know how many of those letters were answered? None. Not one. The Republic took my sister's name and put it in a ledger and closed the book, and the whole apparatus of the state that I have served for eleven years could not be bothered to answer a letter from a widow in Vane Street." His voice had risen, and he brought it down with an effort, the way you lower a heavy thing without letting it fall. "So yes. There is a debt. And I am going to pay it. And I am not going to be paid for paying it."

Freya stood very still. Then she did something he did not expect. She reached up and pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and laid her bare hand on the desk between them—an old courtesy, the gesture of a person showing an empty hand, and he understood it as such. Whatever she had brought to offer, she withdrew it.

"Then I'll ask you for something that isn't payment," she said. "Something you can give without cost to yourself. And it isn't for me."

The gulls cried in the distance. Somewhere below, a crane groaned and swung its load out over the yard, and the sound came up through the floor like the moaning of the building itself.

"Ask," Aldous said.

"Say it first. Before I ask. I need you to say it, so I know you understand what's in those crates. Not cargo. Not contraband. Say the word."

Aldous looked at the manifest, at the two hundred and forty crates of dormant wood that would go down to the quay in three days' time, and he said, quietly, in the cold office with the gray light falling on the blotter:

"Ilse."

"My God," Freya said, and her voice cracked on it, the first crack he had ever seen in her, the first evidence that the armor had a person inside it. "Yes. Ilse. She's been living in the orchard cellar since the border closed. She walked out of the occupied zone with nothing, in winter, and she made it to the one place in the world she thought might still hold her, and she's been there ever since, in the dark, waiting. And in four days the Republic's detention order reaches the coastal district, and every name on that list goes into a camp, and hers is on it, Master Renn. I signed the routing for that order myself. I carried it myself. I am the reason it will arrive on time." She stopped, and he watched her gather herself the way you watch someone gather spilled grain, handful by handful. "So I'm going to get her out. And the only stamp in this city that can put her on that ship is yours."

The room was silent. The cold had settled into Aldous's knees and the backs of his hands, and he thought, absurdly, of the stove downstairs, cold as a monument, and of all the warmth the Republic had rationed away from the people it claimed to keep.

"And your request," he said. "The thing that isn't payment. What is it?"

Freya took a breath.

"Say she's alive," Freya said. "Out loud. I know you know it. I know you've known it since the kitchen table, since the deed. But I need to hear you say it, because I've spent three weeks not saying it myself—planning, routing, measuring crates, and never once saying it aloud, because saying it makes it real and real things can be lost. So say it. Say my sister's name and say she's alive, and that's all I'll take from you. Nothing else. No payment. No passage. Just the words."

The gulls cried. The crane groaned. Somewhere in the building a door slammed, and the echo of it went up through the high ceilings and died.

Aldous Renn sat in his cold office with the manifest in front of him and his sister's name in the air between them, and he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes to a man only once or twice in a life, that he had already decided. He had decided it in the autumn, at the kitchen table, when he had looked at the orchard deed and asked his one question too many and then let her go. He had decided it last week, with the falsified entries. Every step since the autumn had been a step down this road, and the road had only ever led here, to this desk, to this stamp, to this moment.

He looked at Freya Fairweather, and he said:

"Ilse is alive."

The words came out of him steady and clear, and they were the truest words he had spoken in this building in eleven years, and they cost him nothing and everything, and he knew, as he said them, that he would say them again, under whatever lights they eventually put him under, and that he would not add a single word to them, not a name, not a route, not a date.

Freya closed her eyes. For a moment—only a moment—she looked like a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long she had forgotten it was heavy. Then she opened her eyes, and they were wet, and she did not wipe them, and she did not look away, and Aldous understood that this, too, was a courtesy, the same courtesy as the bare hand on the desk: she was letting him see what his words had done, because he had earned the sight.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me." He drew the manifest toward him. "Help me load the ship. That's my thanks. That's the only thanks I want."

He read the manifest. He read every line of it, because he was not a man who could do otherwise, and because the Republic had taught him that the difference between a true document and a false one was only ever the difference between the truth and the truth told at the right moment. Two hundred and forty crates of dormant rootstock and scionwood, apple, pear, quince, consigned Fairweather, bound Callowmere, license valid, weights correct, seals intact. It was, he thought, the most honest lie he had ever held in his hands.

He inked the stamp. He pressed it to the manifest, and the sound it made was small and dull, the sound of a rubber die meeting paper, and it was the loudest sound Aldous Renn had ever heard.

"There," he said. "It's done."

Freya took the manifest. She folded it once, with the care of a woman folding a letter she intends to keep, and she put it inside her coat, against her body, where the warmth of her would keep the ink from cracking in the cold. Then she pulled her glove back on, finger by finger, and she looked at him one last time.

"Three days," she said. "The *Meridian* sails on the morning tide. If you want to help load—"

"I'll be there," Aldous said. "I'll be there."

She went to the door. She stopped with her hand on the handle, and she turned, and for a moment she looked at him with an expression he could not read—something between gratitude and grief and a warning—and then she said, very quietly:

"Master Renn. When this is over, whatever this is, when it's over—there's a hillside above a harbor in Callowmere where the soil is good and the wind is kind. If you ever get out. If any of us ever get out. There's a place there with room in it for a man who knows how to count."

She opened the door and went out, and her footsteps went down the corridor and down the stairs and out into the cold, and Aldous Renn sat alone in his office with the gray light falling on the blotter where the manifest had been, and he listened to the gulls and the crane and the sound of the building breathing around him, and he understood that he had just spent the last coin of his old life.

He looked at the stamp in his hand. He cleaned it carefully, the way he cleaned it every evening, and he set it in its tray, and he drew the next file toward him, and he began, in his small, upright, disciplined hand, to work.

Outside, the rain had turned to sleet, and the sleet ticked against the window like a clock counting down, and three streets away, in the records office of the harbor district, a junior clerk named Pell was reading a route schedule with a frown on his face, and the frown was beginning to look like a question.
