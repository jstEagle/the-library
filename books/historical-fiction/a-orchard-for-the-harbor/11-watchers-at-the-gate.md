## 11. Watchers at the Gate

Pell had been a clerk for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned that the truth of a district lived not in its grand declarations but in its small arithmetic. Populations rose and fell the way tides did, and most of it meant nothing. But some numbers itched. Some numbers were a splinter under the skin of the ledger, and Pell's whole life was the slow, patient work of drawing splinters out.

The itch was this: Census Taker Renn, Second District, had requested a route change on the fourteenth, citing road damage on the coastal stretch between the old tollhouse and the harbor road. The request was routine. The approval was routine. But Pell had walked that stretch himself in October, before the frost closed the season, and he remembered the roadbed—new gravel, laid in the summer, graded by convict labor, dry as bone even in the rains. There was no damage to cite. And men like Aldous Renn did not invent reasons; that was what made them good census takers. They counted what was there.

So why had he asked not to count what was there?

Pell pulled the file again from the archive drawer, though he knew it by heart now. Renn's entries for the coastal holdings were clean—too clean, maybe, though cleanliness proved nothing; half the district's takers were too clean because they copied from the tax rolls instead of walking their routes. But there was one more thing, small as a seed: the household at Fairweather Orchard, listed as *vacant, structure unsound*, with a notation in Renn's hand dated the ninth of the month. *No occupants observed.* Yet the provisioning ledgers—the ones nobody cross-checked because they were boring, because they were his, Pell's, domain—showed fuel oil drawn against that same holding twice since the first snow. Fuel oil for an unsound vacant structure, in a winter when every honest family in the district was burning driftwood and furniture.

He sat with that a long time, the lamp guttering, the sleet ticking against the records office window like a clock counting down. He thought about reporting it up the chain and being laughed at. He thought about not reporting it and having it found anyway, later, by someone who would ask why Pell had held a splinter in his own hand all winter and said nothing.

In the end it wasn't courage that moved him. It was the same thing that moved him every day: the belief that the numbers wanted to be true, and someone had to make them so.

He wrote the memo himself. Four sentences. He kept it dry, factual, unimpeachable—*discrepancy between census notation and provisioning draws, holdings 44 through 49, coastal sector; recommend verification*—and he signed it, and he carried it through the freezing corridors to the office of Sector Security before the lamps were trimmed for the night, because he had learned long ago that memos delivered after dark were read before dawn, and memos read before dawn were acted on by noon.

By noon, the harbor would not be the harbor he knew.

* * *

Freya felt the change before she understood it, the way one feels a storm coming in the teeth.

She had come down to the customs yard at first light to confirm the loading berth for the rootstock shipment—*Fairweather Estate, dormant stock, forty crates, consigned neutral port*—and she stopped at the gate because the gate had changed. Two sentries where there had been one. A new barrier of timber and sandbags. And beyond it, on the quay where the crates stood in their patient rows under sacking, soldiers.

Not harbor police. Soldiers—coastal garrison men in gray wool gone greenish with salt fog, with measuring tapes coiled at their belts like whips waiting to be used. One of them knelt beside the third crate in the second row with a slate in his hand, and another stood over him calling out figures, and Freya watched the tape come out, watched it crawl along the length of the crate, and felt her heartbeat change key.

Forty crates. She had chosen the number herself, chosen the dimensions, chosen the packing straw and the ventilation gaps and the water skin tucked into crate nineteen behind the false bulkhead, and every choice had been made against the known tolerances of customs inspection—a bored examiner, a probe rod, a glance at a manifest stamped by a man who owed her nothing except the truth that her friend was alive. It had seemed, three days ago, like a fortress of small details.

Now the fortress was being measured inch by inch by men who had all morning and no reason to hurry.

She made herself walk. That was the discipline her mother had taught her without meaning to: *when you are afraid, be more ordinary than you have ever been.* She crossed the yard at the pace of a woman with dull business, showed her papers to the sentry—he barely looked; the stamp on her trading license did the looking for her—and went to the harbormaster's shed, where the shipping clerk Voss-no-relation, a fat, kind, frightened man named Dremmel, was already sweating in the cold.

"They came at dawn," he said, low, without greeting. "Garrison order. Every outbound consignment gets full dimensional inspection and manifest audit until further notice. Full." He said the word twice, as if once couldn't hold its weight. "Not sampling. Not probing. Measuring."

"Since when do they measure rootstock?"

"Since somebody upstairs started wondering what else leaves this coast in the bottom of a boat." Dremmel wiped his neck with a cloth that had given up long ago. "There's a war ending, Frau Fairweather. Wars ending are worse than wars beginning. Beginning, everyone's busy. Ending, everyone's settling scores and checking lists." He leaned closer, and his breath smelled of chicory and fear. "Your shipment is scheduled for inspection tomorrow, ninth bell. I could not move it. I tried."

"I know," Freya said, though she hadn't known until that moment that it was true—that Dremmel had tried, that there was a shape of kindness in the world still working on her behalf. "Thank you for trying."

"Frau." He hesitated, and she saw him decide something, saw the decision cost him. "Crate nineteen. It's the one they measured longest this morning."

She thanked him again, and her voice did not shake, and she walked back across the yard past the soldiers with the steady, dull, ordinary stride of a woman going home, and only when the gate was behind her did she let herself stop in the lee of a fish warehouse and press her hand flat against the wet stone and breathe.

Recalculate everything. Her mother's voice, or her own, or the voice of the commission that owned her body if not her heart: recalculate everything.

Tomorrow, ninth bell. Which gave her one night and part of a morning. Which gave her a crate built to pass a probe and a glance, facing men with tapes and slates and time. Which gave her Ilse, in the cellar under the orchard, waiting for a knock that might never come in the form it was promised.

There were only three moves left, and two of them were surrender wearing different coats: abandon the shipment, or empty crate nineteen and let Ilse take her chances in the detention zone on foot. The third move required a miracle with a rubber stamp, and the man who owned the stamp had already spent more for her than she had any right to spend.

She turned toward the records office, and the wind off the harbor pushed at her back like a hand, hurrying her or barring her—it hadn't decided which.

* * *

Aldous heard the news from Pell himself, which was either honesty or cruelty or the particular efficiency of a system that no longer bothered to distinguish them.

"I filed a memo," Pell said, standing in the doorway of Aldous's cubicle with his cap in his hands and the light of a man justified behind his eyes. "Coastal sector. Provisioning discrepancies against your notations. Security doubled the harbor inspections this morning—you'll have seen it. Full dimensional audits, effective immediately."

For a moment Aldous could not speak. He looked at the young man's earnest face—the face of diligence itself, the face the Republic printed on its recruitment posters when it still bothered printing posters—and felt something between hatred and pity, and knew it was neither. It was recognition. He had been Pell once. He had believed the numbers wanted to be true.

"You cited my notations," Aldous said carefully. "Which discrepancy?"

"The Fairweather holding. Vacant per your survey of the ninth. Oil drawn twice since." Pell shifted his weight. "I'm sure there's an explanation. Squatters, probably. But squatters get detained too, these days, and better it comes out now than—" He stopped, hearing himself, hearing what the *now rather than later* implied, and flushed. "It's procedure," he finished, quieter.

"It's procedure," Aldous agreed. "You did right."

And the terrible thing, the thing he would carry afterward like a stone sewn into his coat, was that he meant it. Pell had done exactly right, by every rule Aldous had served for twenty years, and the doing of it would put a woman in a cellar into chains unless the next thirty hours undid what twenty years of correctness had built.

His supervisor appeared at noon with the new inspection schedule and a question shaped like a courtesy. "Renn. Your coastal route—the Fairweather parcel. You noted it vacant on the ninth. You want to amend anything before security walks it? Better an amended entry than a contradicted one. They're pulling files for the audit."

Every exit was a door into fire. Amend the entry—claim he'd seen someone, claim occupancy—and he invited a search of the orchard with soldiers instead of clerks. Leave it—and tomorrow, ninth bell, when they measured crate nineteen and found the false bulkhead, they would trace the consignment to the Fairweather license, and the license to the orchard, and the orchard to his notation, and his notation would become evidence that he had looked at a hiding place and written *empty*.

"No amendment," Aldous said. "I walked it. It was vacant. If someone's drawn oil since, that's a security matter, not a census matter." He met his supervisor's eyes with the flat calm of twenty years. "Unless you'd like me to go out there myself and re-survey."

"No need. Garrison will handle it." His supervisor gathered his papers, then paused. "This Pell business. You vouch for your own route?"

"I vouch for my own work. Always have."

When the man had gone, Aldous sat very still. Outside, the sleet had turned to proper snow, fat and silent, erasing the yard, the walls, the barbed wire along the quay—all of it softening, whitening, as if the world were being prepared for burial rather than inspection. Tomorrow, ninth bell. He calculated the hours the way he calculated everything, precisely, uselessly, and then he took out the fair copy of the coastal survey, the one with his original field notes, and fed the pages one by one into the stove, and watched the ink lift off the paper in brown ghosts before the flame took it.

If they searched, let them search. Let them find a clerk whose notes were ash and whose word was all that remained. A word was a small thing to bet a life on. But it was the only coin he had ever traded in, and he had decided, somewhere in the last weeks without announcing it to himself, that he would spend all of it.

* * *

That evening, snow still falling, Freya climbed to the orchard through lanes gone blue with dusk, and told Ilse everything: the doubled inspections, the measurement, the ninth bell, the memo, the name Pell.

Ilse listened the way she had always listened, completely, her hands quiet in her lap, and when Freya finished she was silent a while, and then she said, "Then we don't wait for the ship."

"We don't have another way."

"We have tonight." Ilse's eyes caught the last of the light from the storm lantern. "One night, one cellar, and a woman who has crossed worse than a harbor yard to stay alive. You get me to the crates, Freya. What happens after ninth bell is mine to survive, not yours to solve." She reached across the dark and took Freya's hand, and her grip was cold and certain. "You promised me shelter. You never promised me certainty. I didn't expect it then, and I don't now."

Freya held on and said nothing, because everything she might say was a lie or a prayer, and she had burned her allowance of both years ago.

Below them, far off, the harbor lights burned in double rows where single rows had been, and the snow came down on the crates, on the soldiers' slates, on the whole measured, numbered, narrowing world, and covered it, briefly, mercifully, the way snow covers everything—without asking what lies underneath, and without promising it will still be there in the morning.
