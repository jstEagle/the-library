## 12. Burning the Correspondence

The order came at dusk, folded inside a courier's satchel that smelled of lamp oil and other people's fear.

*All field operatives to destroy remaining correspondence, codes, and identifying documents by 0600 hours. Acknowledge by signal. No exceptions.*

No exceptions. Freya read it twice, standing in the doorway of the safehouse while the last grey light drained out of the capital's rooftops. Across the street a chimney had taken a shell two nights ago and still smoked, a thin black thread rising into the cold like a question no one would answer. Somewhere to the east the guns had gone quiet again—not because the war was ending, but because both sides were too exhausted to spend more than they had.

She carried the paper inside and set it on the table beside her mother's brass lamp. The wick needed trimming. It was the kind of small domestic failure that would have made her mother click her tongue—*a Fairweather keeps her light in order, Freya, or the dark gets ideas*—and Freya found herself smiling at the ghost of it before the smile collapsed into something harder.

They wanted everything burned. Everything.

She looked at the room as if seeing it for an inventory she'd never filed: the floorboard under the north window where the deed lay wrapped in oilcloth; the false-bottomed drawer with its bundle of cipher sheets; the packet of unsent letters from three years of running messages through a country that was eating itself. Each item had a weight she knew by hand. She had carried them across checkpoints in flour sacks and funeral wreaths, sewn into coat linings, pressed beneath crates of winter pears. Paper was supposed to be lighter than iron. But paper decided who lived, and so it weighed more.

Freya filled the basin at the corner sink, set the lamp close, and began.

* * *

She burned the ciphers first. That was discipline; that was the trade speaking in her voice even now. Sheet by sheet she fed them into the basin, watching the ink curl and lift off the page in little ghosts of grey before the flame took it entirely. The characters she had memorized until they lived behind her eyelids—*harbor, tide, detain, abandon*—went up in a smell like scorched sugar.

Then the letters. These were harder, not because they held secrets anymore but because they held people. A woman in Vessany Row asking after her son's regiment. A customs clerk confessing, in careful code, that he had falsified a manifest to feed his sister's children. A whole city's worth of small treacheries committed in the name of staying human. She read each one once, quickly, the way you look at a face in a coffin, and then gave it to the fire.

The ash rose and settled on her sleeves. She thought about how ash was honest. Ash never pretended it had been anything but something else first.

Outside, a patrol went by, boots keeping time on frozen cobbles. The safehouse had been chosen for its silence, and the silence held, and when the boots faded Freya found her hands were steady and her breath was not.

Last of all, almost as an afterthought that was not an afterthought at all, she reached into the drawer and took out the final packet: her commission papers. The Republic's seal, cracked with age now, the oath she had signed in a marble hall when the war was still a thing other people did. *To carry, to protect, to destroy when ordered.* She had recited those words believing they made her a servant of something larger than herself. Holding them over the basin, she understood at last that they had only ever made her a servant of paper—and paper could be burned by anyone.

She let it go. The seal bubbled, went white, went to nothing.

And then there was one document left in the room besides the order itself, and Freya sat down on the cold floor with her back against the wall and did not move toward it for a long time.

* * *

The deed.

Her mother's name on the top line in that upright, unhurried hand: *Maren Fairweather, of Harbor Town, holds in freehold the orchard lands north of the salt road, twelve acres and the trees standing thereon.* Below it, the surveyor's marks. Below that, the Republic's stamp—the same Republic whose last orders sat on her table demanding she burn every scrap of what she was.

Twelve acres. Forty rows of pear and quince, two of old apple nobody planted anymore. She could walk them blindfolded. Row seven had the tree her father grafted the year Freya was born, half bitter crab, half butter pear, and every autumn it bore fruit of two minds on a single branch. Her mother used to say the tree couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Freya hadn't understood until this winter that the tree had decided long ago—it simply refused to choose between its natures, and grew anyway.

The order said *no exceptions*. The order did not know what it was asking. The deed was not correspondence. It was not a code. It named no agent, revealed no route. And yet it was the most dangerous paper she owned, because it proved that somewhere under all the oaths and aliases there was still a woman from an orchard, a daughter of Harbor Town, someone with roots the Republic had never managed to classify.

If they found it, it would hang her. Not as a courier—a courier was a function, replaceable—but as a person with loyalties that predated the war and would survive it. The detention lists already carried names like hers. Names like *Renn*.

Ilse was asleep in the cellar. Freya could hear nothing through the floor, which meant Ilse was sleeping the way hiding people slept—absolutely still, ears open, breathing like someone practicing not to exist. Three days ago Freya had signed the order that put her name on the lists. Tomorrow, God willing and Aldous Renn willing, she would fold her into a crate of dormant rootstock and ship her out of the world's grief entirely.

Between those two facts lay this deed, and Freya held it and understood that burning it would be the last act of obedience she had left in her.

She thought of her handler, Corvane, who had taught her the trade with a patience that was almost tenderness: *A courier is a vessel, Fairweather. What you carry must never be yours.* She thought of the marble hall, the oath, the eleven years. She thought of row seven, and of a summer afternoon when two girls had lain in the grass between the trees and promised each other the future as if futures were things you could hand back and forth like fruit.

*I'll come for you,* Ilse had said. Or Freya had. It didn't matter anymore which mouth had said it. The promise belonged to both of them now, the way the tree's fruit belonged to both its halves.

Freya stood. She crossed to the hearth instead of the basin, knelt, and fed the fire a length of split oak until it roared. Then she did not burn the deed. She wrapped it back in its oilcloth, knelt on the north floorboard, and set it in its hollow beneath the window where frost ferned the glass like white lichen.

It was such a small motion. Thirty seconds of work. She had destroyed state secrets weighing less. But her hands shook as though she had lifted something enormous, and when she pressed the board flat and scattered ash over the seam, she knew exactly what she had done. Not hidden. Chosen. The commission was dead in the basin, gone up in scorched-sugar smoke, and what remained lying under the floorboards was the only authority she recognized now: a dead woman's handwriting, and a promise older than the war.

Let the order say *no exceptions*. Exceptions were how you stayed human. Somebody had to keep them, or the word *no* would win forever.

She wrote her acknowledgment signal—three short flashes from the shuttered lamp, the code for *compliance confirmed*—and watched the dark take it, and felt the lie settle into her chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Corvane came at midnight.

He always knocked twice and waited, though he had keys to every door in the district, and Freya had never learned whether the courtesy was manners or method. Tonight he stamped snow off his boots in the entryway and came into the lamplight looking ten years older than he had in autumn, his coat collar up, his eyes red-rimmed from wind or something worse.

"The basin," he said, without greeting. He crossed to it and looked down into the wet grey slurry of her night's work, stirring it once with a pencil as if reading tea leaves. "Thorough."

"I follow instructions."

"So I'm told." He turned. He was a tall man gone stooped by decades of leaning over other people's secrets, and in the lamplight his shadow climbed the wall behind him like a second, truer figure. "You signaled compliance at nineteen-forty. That's fast, even for you."

"There wasn't much left worth slow burning."

"No." He said it mildly. He took off his gloves finger by finger, laid them on the table, and stood regarding her with the expression he wore when he was about to ask a question whose answer he already possessed. Freya had seen that expression end careers. "Eleven years, Fairweather. You were the best vessel I ever trained. Do you know why I chose you?"

"Because I could memorize a page in ninety seconds."

"Because you never kept anything." He walked slowly around the table, around the basin, around the small burnt kingdom of her obedience. "A courier's virtue isn't loyalty. Loyalty can be bought, bent, redirected. A courier's virtue is emptiness. You carried what we gave you and held nothing back, and I trusted you the way one trusts a river—not because it's good, but because it goes where the banks tell it." He stopped. "Tonight I want to believe you're still a river."

"Ask me what you came to ask."

He looked at the north window. At the floor beneath the north window. Freya kept her breathing even and her hands loose at her sides and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that the next ten seconds would decide whether she died in this room or merely began to.

"When you received your commission," Corvane said, "you surrendered your personal holdings for cataloguing. Standard procedure. Among them was listed a property instrument—orchard land, Harbor Town district. Freehold deed, Maren Fairweather. It was to be returned to your possession only upon decommissioning." His voice stayed level, unhurried, almost gentle. "I have the catalogue here. I do not have the deed."

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere below the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth beneath forty years of frost, her mother's handwriting lay quiet as a seed.

"Paperwork mislaid in the relocation," Freya said. "You know how the archives are this winter. Half the ministry is being burned faster than we can file it."

"Mislaid." He tasted the word. "In eleven years you have never lost so much as a train ticket."

"People change."

"Yes," Corvane said quietly. "That is precisely what terrifies me."

He picked up his gloves. For a long moment he stood with them in his hands, and Freya watched him weigh something—duty against whatever worn and buried thing he still kept under his own floorboards—and she saw the moment the scale tipped, though not which way.

"The harbor inspections doubled today," he said instead of anything else. "A junior clerk in the customs office has been filing reports about anomalies in the coastal districts. Manifests, census counts, shipments of agricultural stock." He settled his coat onto his shoulders. "Whatever rivers run through this city, Fairweather, some of them are going to be dragged into the light before the ice breaks. If you have anything under any floorboard anywhere—" he met her eyes, and for one unguarded instant he looked not like her handler but like a tired man standing at the edge of a country's grave, "—decide tonight what it is you serve. Because come morning, the Republic will no longer be able to protect you from itself. Or itself from you."

He let himself out. The door closed with the softness of a man who had chosen, for one more night, to look away.

Freya stood alone in the lamplight with the ash settling around her like grey snow. Under the floorboard, the deed. In the cellar, Ilse. At the harbor, a crate of rootstock and a customs stamp she still had to beg, steal, or trust out of Aldous Renn's hands. And above it all, closing like weather, the net Corvane had just described—a suspicious clerk, doubled inspections, hours draining away with the tide.

She pinched out the lamp. In the dark she knelt one last time at the north window, laid her palm flat against the cold board, and felt through the wood the faintest solidity of what she'd chosen.

"Tomorrow," she whispered—to the deed, to the dark, to whoever was still listening. "We go tomorrow."
