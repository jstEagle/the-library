## 13. Night Loading

The cellar smelled of earth and apples long dead, and Ilse had learned to love it the way one loves a thing that is keeping you alive—gratefully, and without illusion.

She had counted the hours by the drip of water somewhere behind the far wall. Four days since Freya had knelt in this cellar and told her about the rootstock, the manifest, her mother's trading license, the customs stamp that only Aldous Renn could provide. Four days of waiting with nothing to do but listen to the war move through the town above like weather: boots on cobbles at odd hours, a truck's engine failing to start three times before catching, once—the worst—a woman screaming two streets over until the sound simply stopped, which was worse than if it had continued.

Now it was night, and Freya had come for her.

"Can you climb?" Freya had asked, holding the lantern low so its light pooled between them and did not leak up the stairs. "It's only a short walk. There's a cart waiting past the tannery wall."

"I can crawl if I have to," Ilse said.

Freya's face did something complicated at that—not quite a smile, not quite grief—and she reached out and touched Ilse's hair, just once, the way she had done under the trees all those summers ago, when the Republic was a word that meant markets and ferries and not this.

* * *

They moved through streets that had been emptied of everything but fear. Ilse kept her head down inside the hood of Freya's borrowed coat, watching her own feet make their small progress across stones slick with sea damp. The harbor announced itself before she saw it: the smell first, tar and brine and cold iron; then the sound, hawsers groaning against bollards, water knocking patiently at hulls; and last the light, lantern after lantern strung along the quay like a string of low orange stars that had fallen and given up on the sky.

The cart was there, as promised. So were the crates.

Ilse had imagined them in the abstract—wooden boxes, straw padding, a shipment of dormant apple rootstock bound for neutral ports—but seeing them was different. They were real. They were *her*. Seven crates stacked in the cart bed, each stenciled in faded paint with the Fairweather orchard mark: a tree inside a circle, and beneath it, in her mother-in-law's careful hand from years before anyone knew it would matter, the words WINTER STOCK — KEEP FROM FROST.

"You'll be in the third one," Freya murmured. "It's the largest. We've cut air holes along the bottom slats where they won't show. Straw beneath you, straw above. It will smell like the orchard."

It will smell like the orchard. As if that were comfort. As if the smell of that place were not itself a kind of wound, sweet and unbearable, the whole lost summer compressed into wood and bark.

"Ilse." Freya took her shoulders. Her hands were steady; her eyes were not. "Once you're in, I can't let you out again until we're past the mole. No matter what you hear. No matter what happens. Can you do that?"

Ilse thought of the occupied zone she had crossed to get here—the checkpoints, the ditch she had lain in for six hours while soldiers argued over cigarettes twenty paces away, the farmer's wife who had hidden her under flour sacks and never asked her name because asking would have made them both guilty. She had already done this once. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: she was practiced.

"I can do that," she said.

* * *

Climbing into the crate was like being buried by people who loved her. That was the thought that came, unbidden, as Freya and the carter—silent, broad, a man who had taken no names and offered none—lifted her over the rim and lowered her into the straw. She lay on her back with her knees drawn up, and the lid came down above her face, and the last thing she saw of the open world was a slice of black sky and the orange halo of a lantern held too close.

Then nails, driven home. The sound went through her chest like hammer blows to her own coffin, and she had to press both hands flat against the crate walls to keep from crying out—not from pain, but from the animal certainty that she was being entombed.

*Breathe.* The straw smelled of dust and old sap and, faintly, impossibly, of apples—the ghost of the orchard living in the wood of these very crates, cut from trees her own family had known. *Breathe slowly. Count the drips.*

There was no dripping water here. She counted heartbeats instead.

The cart lurched forward. Through the air holes along the bottom slats—slits no wider than two fingers, angled so light could not easily enter—she saw fragments of the world slide by: cobblestones, a gutter running silver with frost melt, the legs of a sentry standing at some corner with his rifle planted like a fencepost. The wheels spoke a language of their own, iron-shod and rhythmic, and Ilse let the rhythm carry her the way a swimmer lets a current carry her, neither fighting nor trusting, merely moving within it.

At the harbor gate the cart stopped, and voices rose around her like birds startled from a hedge.

"Papers." A voice bored flat with repetition.

"Manifest of winter fruit, seven crates, rootstock, consigned to Veldt & Sons, neutral registry." Freya's voice—level, unhurried, the courier's voice Ilse had heard her describe once, years ago, as *the voice I wear so my hands don't have to shake.*

A long silence. Paper rustled. Somewhere above her, a man walked the length of the cart, and his boots passed directly over her head, and Ilse stopped breathing entirely.

"This license," the guard said. "Fairweather. The Fairweather orchards were seized in the autumn requisition."

"The license wasn't. Trade instruments survive requisitions—that's half the point of them. Check the seal if you like. It's countersigned at the customs house."

Another silence, longer than the first. Ilse lay in the dark listening to her blood move and thinking, with strange clarity: *this is how it ends or this is how it doesn't, and either way it happens in the next sixty seconds and I will know nothing about it except whether I am still lying here afterward.*

"Move along," the guard said at last. "Quay four. Don't stop between here and the ship."

The wheels resumed. Ilse breathed again, shallowly, through her mouth, tasting straw.

* * *

Loading was a violence of noise. Crates hoisted and swung, chains rattling through pulley blocks, men shouting numbers, the deep complaint of the ship herself as cargo shifted her balance degree by degree. Each time the crane took a crate from the cart, Ilse felt the absence like a tooth pulled—*one fewer between me and discovery*—until she understood, with a lurch, that hers was the last. That the whole operation now narrowed down to the box she lay in, the straw she lay in, the breath she was trying so hard not to need.

Hands took hold of the crate. It swung free of the cart bed, tilted, hung. Through the bottom slats the quay wheeled below her: lanterns, dark water, the white scar of foam where the tide worked against the pilings. For three seconds—she counted them; counting was all she had—she swung above open space, a woman in a wooden box suspended between the country that wanted her detained and the sea that might yet refuse her.

Then the deck came up to meet her, and she was aboard.

They set her down among the other crates in the hold, snug against the second one, and the work went on around her—more cargo coming aboard, dunnage being laid, a bosun's whistle shrieking orders she couldn't parse. She arranged herself against the cramp in her legs and waited for the next voice, the next boot, the next arbitrary mercy or catastrophe. Waiting, she had learned, was not the absence of danger. Waiting was the danger, stretched thin enough to live inside.

It came sooner than she expected.

"Hold it." A new voice, younger than the gate guard's, harder. "This lot hasn't been inspected. Rootstock's a listed commodity—soil attachments, pest vectors. Open them. All of them."

Her heart did not speed up; it stopped, the way a clock stops, all its motion gathered into one suspended instant. Through the slat she saw boots arrive in front of her crate. Two pairs. One set planted wide, authoritative. One shifting, reluctant.

"Sir, the manifest's stamped—"

"Stamped by whom? Half the customs office has run off and the other half will stamp anything that salutes. Open it."

The carter began, "These are agricultural goods, they're sealed for—"

"I said *open them*."

And then a third pair of boots came down the gangway, unhurried, deliberate, and a voice said:

"That won't be necessary."

Ilse knew the voice.

Not from life—from paper. From the ledger she had found in her mother's things after the border closed, the old customs book with two names entered side by side season after season, the Fairweather hand and the Renn hand alternating down the columns like two people taking turns at a well. *Renn,* written in a small, precise script, the double n closed tight as a gate. She had read that name a hundred times in the cellar, tracing it with her finger in the dark, because it was the only relic she owned of the family her mother had trusted with everything, back when trust was something a person could still afford.

The new boots stopped beside the guard's. "Harbor census authority," the voice said. "Emergency recount order, effective tonight. All outbound manifests are subject to verification at the customs table, not the quay. If your man wants to inspect cargo, he can walk his inspection down to the office and do it where the seals are logged properly." A pause, papers shifting. "Or he can stand here and explain to the harbormaster why he delayed a licensed neutral shipment past its tide window. The tide window is in forty minutes. I'd choose quickly."

Silence. The kind of silence in which a career is weighed and found lighter than a piece of paper.

"The manifest's stamped," the guard said finally, sullen, retreating into procedure. "I'll note it as cleared pending office verification."

"Do note it," the other voice agreed pleasantly. "Accurately."

The hard boots withdrew up the gangway. The third pair remained a moment longer, standing beside her crate in the lantern light that leaked down through the hatch, and Ilse pressed her eye to the slat and looked.

She saw a man perhaps forty, narrow through the shoulders, in a census clerk's grey coat with the collar turned up against the harbor wind. He was looking down at the crate—at *her*, though he could not know it, though he must have known it, and the contradiction of that was its own kind of vertigo. His face was ordinary and exhausted and carefully composed, the way faces are composed by people who have decided something irrevocable and are carrying the decision around like a stone they cannot put down. And then, in the space of a breath, the composure failed—not into weakness but into something older and rawer, a grief without an object, and he said, so quietly that only the crate could have heard it:

"Hold on, Ilse."

He turned and walked away up the gangway, and his footsteps diminished into the general noise of the ship, and Ilse lay in the straw with her hand pressed over her mouth and shook.

He knew her name. Not from the ledger—the ledger held only surnames, columns, seasons. He knew her name the way one knows a name that has been spoken in a household, worried at, kept. *Freya told him,* she thought, and then: *no. He knew before she asked him for anything. He stamped the manifest without reading it—she said he would—and he came down here anyway, unbidden, off his own route, risking whatever a forged authority risks, to stand over a box and say my name.*

Brother. He was the brother. The Renn boy grown into a tired man in a grey coat, the one whose handwriting sat beside her mother's in a ledger neither family had opened in years, the one whose sister had made Ilse promise, under the apple trees, in another country's summer: *if it ever comes, you'll take us in.* And Ilse had promised, laughing, certain that *it* would never come, the way the young are certain of everything.

*It* had come. And the promise had traveled, somehow, through ledgers and censuses and burning borders, and arrived here, in a hold that smelled of tar, wearing the face of a stranger who called her by name.

Terror and gratitude rose in her together and could not be separated, the way salt cannot be separated from the sea. Because she understood—he must understand, and she understood for him—that the forged authority would be discovered. Documents like that left trails. Men who redirected guards with invented recounts got audited, questioned, remembered. He had spent something tonight that could not be earned back, and he had spent it on a woman he had never met, for the sake of a promise his sister had made among trees that were no longer theirs.

The hatch covers began to go on overhead, plank by plank, shutting the sky away. In the narrowing dark Ilse curled around her own heartbeat and made herself a vow to match the old one: whatever it cost him, whatever it cost any of them, she would not let this be a debt that died unacknowledged in a hold full of winter fruit.

Above her, muffled by planks and distance, the bosun's whistle shrilled, and the great moaning voice of the ship answered—lines cast off, engines turning over, the first shudder of departure running down through the frames into the straw where she lay.

Through the slat, in the last band of lantern light before the hold went fully dark, Ilse watched the quay begin, slowly, terribly, to slide away.

And standing at the edge of it, apart from the dockhands, his collar up, his face lifted toward the ship he had no place aboard—she saw Aldous Renn raise one hand. Not a wave. Just the fingers, briefly, the smallest possible farewell, as if even goodbye were contraband.

Then the harbor swallowed the light, and the ship leaned into the dark water, and Ilse Fairweather-Renn—no, she corrected herself, pressing her palm flat against the crate wall, *Ilse Renn, still, always, that promise ran both ways*—began to count the sounds of her escape: the engine, the swell, the gulls outraged at the wake.

She was still counting when she heard, far above and behind, thin across the water, the sound she would spend the rest of the voyage trying to unhear: a whistle, repeated, urgent—the harbor police, calling something back toward the customs house where a tired man in a grey coat stood alone with his forgery and his family name.

The ship did not slow. Ships never do. Ilse lay in the dark among the dormant trees and understood that she was sailing out of the reach of one promise and straight into the shadow of another—and that the man who had bought her passage was, even now, standing on the quay to pay for it.
