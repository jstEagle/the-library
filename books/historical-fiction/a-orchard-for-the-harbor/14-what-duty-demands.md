## 14. What Duty Demands

The summons came at dusk, folded into the ordinary machinery of the day: a slip of grey paper delivered by a boy who would not meet his eyes, bearing a room number and a time and nothing else. *Third floor, Records Annex. After the last bell.* Aldous Renn read it twice, set it beneath the brass weight on his desk, and finished the entry he was making — household of seven, fisher street, two children under ten — in his careful census hand, because whatever waited for him on the third floor, the work was still the work, and the ink did not know enough to tremble.

He had known this day would come. Not with certainty — no man can be certain of his own ruin; hope is a tenant that never fully vacates — but with the settled dread of a farmer who has watched the frost come down the valley night after night and knows which orchards will not survive it. He had filed the false entries eleven weeks ago. He had lied to Supervisor Kessler's face nine weeks ago, smoothly, terribly well, and had gone home afterward and been sick into the canal like a boy after his first drink. Since then he had lived in the space between the lie and its discovery, and he had learned that such a space has a texture all its own: every corridor slightly too long, every clerk's laughter a half-tone too bright, every bell that rang through the annex sounding, for one suspended instant, like his name.

Now the bell had rung.

* * *

The Records Annex occupied a former granary near the customs house, and in the Republic's fat years it had smelled of grain and lamp oil and the patient industry of a state counting itself. Now it smelled of mildew and cold iron and the particular staleness of paper that no one expects to keep much longer. The clerks had gone home. Their desks stood bare as winter fields, and Aldous's footsteps on the stair came back to him from the high dark rafters like the footsteps of another man, someone he was following at a distance, someone whose fate he could observe with a kind of clinical pity.

The door marked with the room number stood half open. Lamplight lay across the floorboards in a long gold band.

Supervisor Kessler was not there. Instead, a woman sat behind the supervisor's desk as though she had owned it for years — though Aldous knew she had arrived from the capital only days ago, one of the new inspectors the Ministry had begun seeding through the coastal districts as the war turned inward, quiet women and quiet men with quiet powers, sent to find out why the Republic's ledgers no longer matched its people.

She was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, dressed in the undistinguished wool of a person who wished to be remembered by nothing but her questions. A single lamp burned before her. Beside it, squared to the desk's edge with geometric precision, lay a folder. Aldous knew the color of that folder. Everyone in the annex knew it. It was the color reserved for discrepancies — for souls who appeared in one column and vanished from another, for births unrecorded and deaths unreported, for the small evasions of a frightened population.

And for census takers who falsified their own returns.

"Master Renn." She did not rise. Her voice was neither warm nor cold; it was the voice of a scale, designed to hold whatever was placed upon it without tipping. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please."

There was only one chair, placed opposite her, and he understood that even the furniture had been arranged as an instrument. He sat. The chair was lower than the visitor's chair in any office he had ever entered, and he felt the difference in his spine, in the angle at which he had to lift his chin to meet her eyes. He refused to lift it. He looked instead at the lamp, at the folder, at the grain of the desk between them, worn pale by decades of clerks' elbows.

"You've served the Bureau eleven years," she said. "Districts four through nine, coastal. Your accuracy rating is among the highest in the province. Your supervisors describe you as meticulous. Conscientious." She let the words sit a moment, like coins laid out one by one. "It is precisely because your record is so clean that what I am about to show you interests me."

She opened the folder and turned it to face him.

He had prepared himself for many things. For shouting. For chains. For the flat recitation of charges in a voice drained of all human inflection. He had not prepared himself for the gentleness of it — for the way she turned each page toward him slowly, almost courteously, giving him time to read what he already knew by heart.

Household returns, coastal districts. His own handwriting — unmistakable, the careful verticals he had practiced since his apprenticeship — recording families that did not exist, or recording existing families at addresses where they no longer lived, or recording, in the case of one abandoned orchard house above the cliff road, a structure listed as *uninhabited, roof failed, no occupancy possible*, when he knew better than any man alive that the roof held, that there was smoke some mornings, that a woman was living there who did not appear in any ledger of the Republic.

"The recount you requested," the inspector said. "The route change. Road damage, you claimed." She drew out another sheet. "I sent a survey team along that road three days ago. The road is damaged, it's true. One culvert washed out in the autumn storms. But it is passable on foot, and it was passable in the week you cited, and a census taker of your reputation does not request reroutes over a culvert." She paused. "You requested it so that your recount would skip exactly six households. Six, Master Renn. Not five, not seven. Six."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below them, in the customs house, a crane groaned against its moorings, loading or unloading he knew not what, and the sound came up through the floorboards like the breathing of some vast animal turning in its sleep.

"Do you know what I thought at first?" the inspector went on, and now something almost conversational entered her voice, the tone of a woman thinking aloud in front of a prisoner she has already decided about. "I thought: smuggling. They always smuggle — tobacco, brandy, ration cards. A census taker is perfectly placed for it; he knows which houses are empty, which families are away, which roads are watched. So I looked for the money." She tapped the folder. "There is no money. Eleven years, and your accounts are those of a modestly paid man who spends what he earns and saves a little against winter. No new coat. No debts paid off. Nothing." She looked at him then, directly, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "So I asked myself: if not money, then what? And there is only one answer left, isn't there? People. You have been hiding people."

Aldous said nothing. He had decided, on the stairs, that he would say nothing, and the decision held him now the way a riverbed holds a river — not by force, but by shape.

The inspector studied his silence with the patience of her profession. Then she closed the folder, aligned its edges with the desk once more, and folded her hands upon it.

"I am going to offer you something," she said, "and I want you to understand how unusual it is, so that you weigh it properly. The Ministry is stretched thin. The capital is falling — you know it, I know it, the dockworkers sing about it in the taverns. We do not have the resources to investigate every thread, and so we prioritize. A census taker who falsified entries for money is worth prosecuting. A census taker who falsified entries out of — sentiment, conviction, some private loyalty — is worth *using*. Because such a man has accomplices. And accomplices are currency."

She slid a second sheet from the folder. It was blank except for a heading and a line of instructions, and beside it lay a pen, uncapped, angled toward him as if it had walked to the edge of the desk on its own.

"Name them," she said. "Every person you sheltered, every document you altered for another's benefit, every soul you moved from one column to another. Name them, sign it, and you walk out of here tonight with a reprimand in your file and your pension intact. Clemency, Master Renn. Full clemency. In three months none of this will matter anyway — you must see that. The Republic is finishing. Why drown for a ship that is already going down?"

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's bell struck the hour. Eight bells. Aldous counted them without meaning to, the way he counted everything now — crates, households, hours, the distance between the annex and the quay, the minutes until a certain shipment of dormant rootstock was scheduled to move from the warehouse to the water. Two hours. Perhaps less. The tide would turn near midnight.

He thought of Ilse's face through the slat of the crate — no. She had not seen him; he had made certain of that. But he had seen *her*, one glimpse through the lantern light, her face pale above the straw, and he had thought: *so that is what my sister's promise looks like when it walks.* Freya Fairweather had come to his office with her grief folded up small inside her coat and asked him for nothing but a word spoken aloud — *say that Ilse is alive* — and he had stamped the manifest unread, and in that moment he had passed, quietly and forever, from the country of the law into the country of the promise. There was no border post between them. There was no going back. He had understood that even then, standing at his window watching her cross the yard, understanding it the way one understands rain: not as information but as fact.

"Master Renn." The inspector's voice brought him back. "You are not being asked to betray anyone. You are being asked to *describe* them. There is a difference, and men in your position comfort themselves with it. But consider: if you do not name them, I will find them myself. I am good at finding things; it is the whole of my trade. And when I find them, they will not be offered clemency, because you will not be here to make yourself useful. Do you see the arithmetic? Your silence does not protect them. Your silence only removes the one person in this room who is willing to trade."

She was right. He saw the arithmetic perfectly. It was the same cruel arithmetic he imagined Freya weighing on the quay — sail or stay, one life or two — and he felt a strange kinship with her across the dark town, two people doing sums with human lives, arriving at different totals.

But she was wrong about one thing, and he found, to his surprise, that he wanted to say so. Not to save himself. Not even to save them. But because a man should not go silently into the machine, and because somewhere in the wreck of his career there remained one small sovereign territory: the truth, spoken plainly, cost what it cost.

"You think I hid them out of sentiment," he said. His voice sounded rusty, a door long unopened. "Conviction. Some private loyalty. You wrote it down as one of the possibilities, there in your folder, and you were nearly right. But it wasn't private. That's what you've missed, sitting in the capital, counting columns." He nodded at the folder. "My sister married into a family in the hill country, and when the border closed she was trapped on the wrong side of it, and no one in any ministry lifted a finger, because she was one soul, and one soul is a rounding error. Freya Fairweather promised her — years ago, in peacetime, under the apple trees on that farm above the cliff road — that if war ever came, she would have a place. And when war came, everyone forgot. The Republic forgot. The ledgers forgot. Every column in every book in this building forgot." He laid his hand flat on the desk, on the pale worn wood, as if taking an oath upon it. "She didn't forget. One woman, carrying papers through a besieged city, remembered a promise made under apple trees, and kept it. That is not sentiment, madam. That is the only honest accounting left in this country. I merely corrected the ledger to match it."

The inspector listened without moving. When he finished, the silence lasted long enough that he heard the crane again, and the wind worrying at the granary's high windows, and his own heart, steady, which astonished him.

"That was very fine," she said at last, and there was no mockery in it. "It will sound very fine at your hearing too, if there is a hearing. It will not change the outcome." She turned the blank sheet toward him and set the pen beside it. "Last time, Master Renn. Names."

"No," Aldous said.

Just that. He had expected the word to cost more — to require force, ceremony, some inner convulsion — but it came out of him the way water comes out of a cup tipped over: simply, completely, without remainder. *No.*

The inspector regarded him for a long moment. Then she did something he had not anticipated. She took up the pen herself, capped it, and returned it to the drawer, and she gathered the folder, and she stood — the first time she had stood since he entered — and she said, in the voice of a clerk closing out a day's work:

"Aldous Renn, census taker, first class. By authority of the Ministry of Interior, acting under emergency provisions of the State Security Act, you are detained on suspicion of falsification of official records, obstruction of a lawful count, and aiding persons designated under enemy-association statutes. You will be conveyed to the harbor detention facility pending inquiry." She paused at the door, her hand on the latch, and looked back at him — and for one unguarded instant he saw not an inspector but a tired woman in a falling country, doing her work in the dark as best she could. "For what it is worth," she said, "in twenty years I have heard a hundred men explain themselves, and most of them lied even when the truth would have served them better. You are the first who told the truth when it could serve him nothing at all." She opened the door. "It will not help you. But I will remember it."

Two soldiers waited in the corridor. They took his arms with the impersonal competence of men who had done this many times, and he did not resist, because resistance was a coin he needed elsewhere — spent later, perhaps, in a different currency, at a different hour. They walked him down the stairs of the old granary, past the bare desks, past the tall windows where the last grey light was failing over the rooftops, and out into the street, where the cold came off the harbor like a living thing.

* * *

They did not hurry. That was the strange mercy of it. The escort moved at a walk, and the route to the detention facility ran along the quay, and so Aldous Renn, under arrest, guilty of everything, was given — whether by chance or by some oversight of the collapsing state — a procession through the very scene of his crime.

The harbor at night was a country of lanterns. Oil lamps swung from the cranes; torches guttered in brackets along the customs wall; and out on the black water the riding lights of the ships burned in long uneven rows, each one a small stubborn star that refused to acknowledge the fog rolling in from the sea. Dockworkers moved cargo in silence — real silence, the silence of men who had stopped singing, who loaded and unloaded with their eyes down and their thoughts elsewhere, because everyone on that quay understood that the cargoes were changing meaning: no longer goods leaving a country, but things being rescued from one.

And there, at the third berth, under a gantry strung with lanterns: the rootstock crates. He knew them instantly, the way a father knows his children's coats in a crowd — the stenciled orchard mark, the straw packed visible at the seams, the manifest he had stamped with his own hand lying clipped to the lead crate in its oiled sleeve. Soldiers stood around them. Of course they did; the suspicious clerk had done his work, inspections had doubled, and Freya's careful plan hung now by threads finer than the straw in the crates. But the crates were still there. Still waiting. Still bound for neutral water, if the night allowed it.

Aldous slowed — he could not help it — and the soldier on his left tightened his grip, misreading the slowing as flight. It was not flight. It was the opposite. It was a man taking leave of the thing he had bought with himself, checking with his eyes that the purchase stood.

As they passed the foot of the gangway, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the customs wall and fell into step alongside the little procession — a woman in a courier's greatcoat, hood back, face bare to the lantern light, walking openly where any officer might see her, which was itself a kind of madness. Freya Fairweather did not look at the soldiers. She did not look at the crates. She looked only at Aldous, and she walked beside him for perhaps thirty paces, matching his steps, saying nothing, because there was nothing that could be said aloud on a watched quay, and both of them knew every word that could not be said.

At the mouth of the lane that led up to the detention facility, she stopped. He stopped too — the soldiers permitted it, puzzled, sensing some current they could not name — and she reached out and straightened his collar, a small domestic gesture, absurd and enormous, the gesture of a sister or a wife or a friend of forty years, performed in full view of the Republic's last armed men.

"They will ask you for names," she said quietly. "Give them the orchard. Tell them everything happened at the orchard. Let them dig there. It will buy the night."

Then she stepped back into the lantern light's edge, and the soldiers walked him on, up the lane, toward the gate, the lamps of the harbor burning behind him like a fleet of small promises.

At the gate, the sergeant of the guard took his papers, glanced at them, and looked up.

"Renn. The census man." Something flickered in the sergeant's face — recognition, or rumor, or simple curiosity. "Ship sails on the midnight tide. You picked a poor night for it."

"No," Aldous said. He turned once, to look back down at the water, at the third berth, at the lanterns swinging over the crates of dormant trees. "I picked the only night there was."

The gate closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting, and out on the dark harbor, unseen, unheard, the tide began to turn.
