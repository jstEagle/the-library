## 15. The Harbor Holds Its Breath

The message reached her in the shape of a boy.

He came down the fish-market stairs just after dusk, weaving between the gutting tables with the practiced indifference of someone who had been told to look casual and had interpreted this as *walk quickly while staring at nothing*. Freya was counting herring for a woman whose name she had learned that afternoon, because a courier in hiding learns the names of fishwives, learns the price of salt, learns to hold coins so the wear on them tells her which district she is standing in. She had been in the harbor town three days. She knew the herring were running thin, that the salt tax had doubled twice since autumn, and that the woman's son was stationed at the north battery and wrote letters full of weather.

The boy did not stop. He set a folded paper beneath the herring barrel as he passed, the way a man sets down a stone he no longer wishes to carry, and was gone into the smoke of the frying stalls before Freya had finished paying.

She waited until the fishwife turned to another customer. Then she took the paper, walked to the end of the pier where the wind off the water could be blamed for her expression, and opened it.

*The census man is taken. Gate arrest, midnight yesterday. Held at the customs house. No charges posted.*

She read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

Around her the harbor went on with its evening — gulls arguing over offal, a winch groaning somewhere behind the net sheds, the great slow breathing of water against pilings. A lamp-lighter moved along the quay with his pole, touching flame to wick after wick, and Freya watched him do it and thought: *this is what I have done.* Touched light to light and left them burning over an empty thing.

Aldous Renn had been arrested at the gate. Not discovered — *taken*, the message said, which meant someone had come for him by name, which meant the false entries had finally been traced, or the junior clerk had finally spoken, or some ledger in some ministry had at last refused to lie quietly any longer. It did not matter which thread had been pulled. What mattered was that the man who had stamped her mother's license onto a manifest of winter fruit without reading it was sitting now in a room above the customs house, and midnight was fewer than six hours away, and the *Merrow's Daughter* would sail with or without him on the turning tide.

Freya folded the paper small, held its corner to the coals of a braziers' leavings near the market wall, and let it become ash between her fingers.

Then she stood on the pier and did the arithmetic.

It was not difficult arithmetic. That was the cruelty of it. She had expected, in the long sleepless rehearsals of the past week, to face some knotted problem worthy of the codes she carried — some cipher where every solution cost something hidden, something she could tell herself she hadn't chosen. Instead the numbers sat in plain rows, patient as gravestones.

*Sail,* said the first row. Sail on the midnight tide with Ilse hidden among the rootstock crates, and Ilse lives. Ilse crosses to neutral water, plants her feet on soil that has never heard of Vessany, and keeps breathing. And Aldous Renn sits in the customs house through the fall of everything, and when the Republic goes down — and it would go down; even the harbor town could feel it coming now, in the emptied granaries and the soldiers selling their boots — when it goes down, men like Aldous are not pardoned. They are examples. He had looked away once, gently, for love of a sister he believed dead, and the state would answer his gentleness with rope or ice or whatever instrument the last desperate ministries preferred that winter. She would sail, and she would live with the sound of a gate closing like a book shutting.

*Stay,* said the second row. Stay, surrender herself, offer the codes she still carried sewn into her coat lining — the Republic's own dispatch ciphers, the ones Harbor Command would trade anything to keep from enemy hands. Bargain: two lives for one secret. And perhaps it worked, and perhaps it didn't, but either way the ship sailed without her, and if the bargain failed, Ilse sailed into a port where no one waited, where the manifest bore a dead woman's license and the crates held a living woman's breath, and if anything went wrong at the neutral dock there would be no Freya Fairweather standing on that quay to lie for her, to claim kinship, to say *she is mine, she is with me.* Ilse would land alone with nothing but cuttings and a promise.

Two columns. One life weighed against two. And underneath both columns, written so faintly it might have been watermark: *you promised her first. You promised her in peacetime, under the trees, when promises were cheap.*

Freya stood at the end of the pier until her hands were cold enough to hurt, and then she went to see the ship.

* * *

The third berth lay in the shadow of the customs house itself, which struck her now as a joke of very poor taste. Lanterns swung from the loading boom, throwing moving circles of orange across the stacked crates — *DORMANT STOCK. ORCHARD ROOTSTOCK. KEEP FROM FROST* stenciled in her mother's old trading style, the letters slightly wrong because Freya had painted them herself and grief does not stencil evenly. The *Merrow's Daughter* sat low in the water, honest-looking, dull-sailed, the kind of coastal trader no examiner looks at twice. That had been the whole design. Dullness as armor.

She found the mate, a broad silent man called Torv who had been paid enough not to be curious, and asked him the question she already knew the answer to.

"Tide?"

"Turns at twelve-forty." He chewed something slowly. "Captain says we wait for no one past one."

"Anyone asking about the cargo?"

"One fellow yesterday. Measured a crate." Torv spat thoughtfully over the rail. "Didn't buy anything."

She left him and walked the length of the berth, and at the third crate from the stern she stopped, as she stopped every evening, and rested her palm against the wood. Inside, packed in damp straw among the bundled roots of her mother's orchard, was a woman who had crossed an occupied country to reach her.

"Ilse," she said, very quietly, to the crate. "Something has happened."

Of course there was no answer. There was only the small discipline of breath — she had taught Ilse the rhythm herself, two nights ago in the cellar of the orchard, *slow in, slower out, they listen for hurry* — and beneath her palm the wood gave back nothing but cold and the faintest settling of straw.

She told the crate anyway. She told it about the gate and the sergeant and the arrest, in the flat courier's voice she used for casualty lists, because Ilse deserved the truth delivered cleanly and Freya could not yet bear to deliver it any other way. When she finished she kept her hand where it was, and imagined she felt warmth through the planking, though she knew it was her own blood lying about itself.

"Whatever I choose," she whispered, "know that I chose it with you in front of me. Not behind. In front."

Somewhere inside the crate, softly, the breathing changed rhythm. *Slow in, slower out.* Then, deliberately, three short exhalations — their cellar signal, *understood, go on* — muffled almost to nothing by straw and wood and forty feet of night air. Freya closed her eyes.

Ilse knew. Or Ilse guessed, which came to nearly the same thing, because Ilse had always been able to read the shape of silence around her sister-of-the-orchard better than most people read print. Three short breaths. *Understood.* And no fourth breath, no fifth, no plea to stay, no plea to sail — because Ilse Renn did not make other people's choices for them. She never had. That was the whole foundation the promise had been built on, back when the trees were bearing: *I will shelter you if war comes, and you will not owe me your obedience, only your survival.*

Freya took her hand off the crate. It was the hardest physical act of her life, harder than the first dispatch she'd ever carried through shellfire, harder than the burning of the letter she had memorized. She made herself walk away up the quay without looking back, because looking back was a luxury belonging to people with time.

* * *

The customs house glowed from every window.

That was the thing she hadn't anticipated — how *lit* it would be. In a harbor town conserving oil, in a republic rationing everything down to hope, the customs house burned lamps in all nine windows like a celebration. She understood why, walking closer along the dark side of the street: they were working late because they were afraid, and fear in bureaucracies expresses itself as paperwork. Clerks' shadows crossed and recrossed the glass. Somewhere upstairs a door slammed. Two soldiers stood at the main entrance stamping their feet against the cold, rifles slung with the loose carelessness of men who had not slept properly in weeks.

Freya stood in the lee of a chandler's doorway across the street and studied the building the way she had once studied bridges she intended to cross under fire.

Nine windows. Two sentries, visible. A yard gate at the rear, chained — she had watched it chained for two days, part of her cover as a fruit-factor's agent checking warehouse access. The cells, according to the fishwife's son's letters and the general knowledge of anyone who had grown up in this town, ran along the seaward side, low rooms that had once held smugglers and now held whoever the collapsing state needed them to hold. There would be an interrogating officer. There would be papers on a desk. There might, if the ministries were still trading, be a man with authority to bargain.

And there would be Aldous Renn, in one of those lit windows' worth of building, having spent his silence the way he'd spent everything else — completely, without drama, expecting nothing back.

She thought of him demanding nothing in return but one sentence: *say aloud that Ilse is alive.* She had said it. He had stamped the manifest unread. She thought of the way he had looked away from her across his desk, the way men look away when looking directly would cost more than they can pay.

"You picked the only night there was," she murmured, quoting the wind, quoting rumor, quoting whatever fragment of that final exchange had drifted to her through the harbor's endless gossip. "So did I. I just don't know yet which night I'm picking."

A bell somewhere in the town began striking ten. The sound rolled across the water and came back off the headland, thinned, and died. Two hours and forty minutes until the tide. She did the arithmetic again, standing in the chandler's doorway, and got the same answer both ways, which was the nature of cruel arithmetic: it always balances, and it never comes out even.

Sail, and one survives. Stay, and maybe two survive, and maybe none. And beneath the columns, faint as watermark, the trees of her childhood swaying in a summer that no longer existed anywhere except in a deed she had refused to burn and a cellar full of bundled roots that were, even now, asleep and waiting, trusting the season to turn.

Freya pulled her collar up, stepped out of the doorway, and began to walk toward the lit windows of the customs house — then stopped, three paces out of the shadow, because across the street a door opened and a clerk came out into the lamplight, young, red-eyed, carrying a file under his arm with the grip of a man carrying something that burns.

He looked up. Their eyes met for half a second — the junior clerk, the one whose suspicion had threaded through the whole design like a hairline crack — and Freya saw recognition begin to assemble itself behind his exhaustion.

She smiled at him, blandly, the smile of a fruit-factor's agent out walking after supper, and turned away down the lane toward the water, her heart going like a drum, the tide pulling at the world behind her.
