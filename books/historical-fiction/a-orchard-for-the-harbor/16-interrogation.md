## 16. Interrogation

The room they gave him had once been a counting house. Aldous could tell by the brass fittings still bolted to the walls, the ghost-rings in the wood where ledgers had been chained down, the high narrow window set too deep in the stone to admit anything but a blade of grey light. Someone had dragged a table to the center of the floor and set a single lamp upon it, and the lamp's shade was crooked, throwing its light sideways across the table like something spilled.

He sat with his hands flat on his knees and counted, because counting was the only prayer he knew. Forty-one knots in the floorboards he could see from where he sat. Three cracks in the plaster shaped, if he let his eyes soften, like river deltas. One door, iron-banded, with a viewing slot that had not been opened since they brought him in.

They had taken his coat, his boots, his satchel, his pencil. They had left him his shirt and his dignity, or what passed for dignity in a man who had spent two days in a cell learning the exact temperature of stone through wool trousers.

*Errors*, he reminded himself. *You are a man who made errors.*

The story was simple enough that it might hold. Census takers made mistakes; it was practically doctrine. A name entered twice, a household miscounted, a route walked out of order so that two districts bled into one another on paper. He had been tired. The winter was long. The roads were bad — that much was even true; he had requested the route change himself, citing washouts, and the request existed in an official file with an official stamp, which was more than most lies could claim. Errors multiplied under pressure. The Republic itself was an error multiplying under pressure. Perhaps they would appreciate the symmetry.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The man who entered was broad through the shoulders and narrow through the patience, with a jaw that looked as though it had been quarried rather than grown, and Aldous felt the bottom drop out of his stomach before the door had even closed behind him.

*Marek Vohl.*

Marek Vohl, who had shared a desk with him for three years at the provincial archive in Kesselmark, back when the census was a census and not a net. Marek, who had taught him the trick of reading watermarks by candlelight. Marek, whose sister had married into the very household Aldous had once miscounted on purpose, though Marek didn't know that, would never know that, because knowing it would put him in this room on the wrong side of the table. Marek, who used to bring salted plums to work wrapped in wax paper and split them down the middle with his thumbnail, giving Aldous always the larger half, saying, *You think better when you're chewing.*

Marek set a folder on the table. He did not sit at once. He stood looking at Aldous for a long moment, and in that look Aldous watched a man try to be two people at once — the interrogator and the friend — and fail at both, landing somewhere in between, in the terrible country between.

"Aldous Renn," Marek said, as if confirming it for a record. Then, quieter, almost against his will: "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping on a shelf."

"They gave you a shelf?"

"It has a blanket. The blanket has ambitions."

Something moved at the corner of Marek's mouth and died there. He pulled out the chair across the table and sat, and the lamp light cut across his face and turned him briefly into a stranger — all shadow above, all hard line below — before he shifted, unconsciously, into the glow again, and became Marek once more.

"You understand how this works," Marek said. "I ask. You answer. I write down what you answer. What I write down matters more than what you say, so choose your words as if your life depends on their weight, because it does."

"I understand."

"Good." He opened the folder. "State your name, position, and district assignment."

"Aldous Renn. Census taker, second class. Coastal districts, western reach."

"When did you first falsify a household record?"

And there it was, no approach, no circling, the question laid on the table like a severed hand. Aldous kept his breathing even. He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it lying awake on his shelf with the ambitious blanket, walking through it the way one walks through a dark orchard in memory, knowing every tree.

"Falsify is a strong word," he said.

"It is the legal word."

"The legal word assumes intent." He spread his hands, palms up, the gesture of a man showing he carried nothing. "Marek. You've done field work. You know what winter census-taking is. Half the coastal houses don't have numbers on them anymore — the storms took the plates, or the families took them down when the requisition officers started coming. You knock on a door, a woman tells you she lives alone, her brother sleeps in the hay barn and hasn't slept under a roof since autumn. Where do you enter him? There is no entry for the hay barn. There is no column for men who have become weather."

Marek's jaw worked. "You're telling me it was sloppiness."

"I'm telling you it was a system built for peacetime being run in wartime by tired men with bad pencils." He leaned forward slightly. "You want a confession of conspiracy. Look at my file. Look at my whole life. I have never conspired with anyone in my life. I once failed to conspire with a woman who was openly conspiring with me over tea. I am constitutionally incapable of it. Ask anyone."

It was true, and truth has a texture that trained listeners can feel through paper. Marek felt it. Aldous saw him feel it — saw the small settling of the shoulders, the fractional loosening around the eyes. But then Marek turned a page, and the loosening stopped.

"There were eleven discrepancies," Marek said. "Eleven households along the western coast where persons appear in the pre-war registers but vanish from yours. Eleven. That isn't fatigue, Aldous. Fatigue loses one. Maybe two. Fatigue doesn't lose eleven in a pattern running precisely north to south along the old smugglers' road."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, faintly, a ship's horn sounded off the harbor — long, low, patient, the sound of a place that did not care what happened in counting houses.

Aldous looked at his hands. He thought about the eleven. He thought about Ilse Renn, who was not on any list he'd ever filed, who existed in the records of no nation at all now except as ink dried and gone. He thought about the fact that some of those eleven had simply fled, or drowned, or starved, and some of them — perhaps — had been found by other hands than his, other silences, other tired men with bad pencils who also chose to see weather instead of people. The coast was full of vanishing acts. He had only ever been one of them.

"People leave," he said. "That's what war is. It's a machine for making people leave."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have."

Marek closed the folder. For a moment he just sat there, both hands flat on it, as if holding down something alive. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into another register entirely, low and rough, meant for no record.

"Do you remember Kesselmark? The flood year? When the archive cellar filled and we spent nine days hauling registers up the stairs two at a time, and you ruined your back and complained about it for a decade?"

"Eight years," Aldous said. "I complained for eight years. It was a good complaint. I got mileage out of it."

"And the night we finished, we drank that awful plum brandy your mother sent, sitting on the steps of the archive with our feet in the floodwater, and you said—" Marek stopped. His throat moved. "You said the worst thing a keeper of records could do was let the record matter more than what it recorded. You said a ledger that forgets people is just a list of lies wearing a stamp."

Aldous said nothing. The words hung in the lamplight between them, and he understood suddenly that Marek had not come to break him. Marek had come to find out whether the man on the steps still existed somewhere inside the prisoner, and whether whatever he found could be survived.

"Marek," Aldous said carefully, "what happens to me—"

"Don't talk yet." Marek rubbed his face with one hand. Outside, the ship's horn sounded again, nearer, insistent, a thing counting tides while men counted souls. "There's a hearing tomorrow. There are people above me who believe the eleven households are a cell. A network. They want names, Aldous. They want accomplices, routes, a harbor contact. Give me something and I can shape it. Misdirection, dead ends, a name that checks out and leads nowhere — I can build you a wall out of it. But silence, real silence—" he tapped the folder "—they read silence as architecture. Silence means there's a building underneath, and buildings have rooms, and rooms have people."

"And if I give them a name that goes nowhere, and someone follows it, and finds a family hiding in a root cellar—"

"Then you've traded yourself for strangers."

"Yes," Aldous said. "That was always the price. I did the arithmetic months ago, in an office, in daylight, with a cup of tea going cold. I'm not doing it again in here."

Marek stared at him. Something in his face was coming apart slowly, quietly, like ice going out of a river — nothing dramatic, just a softening at the edges, a loosening of the whole structure. He looked older than he had walking in. He looked like a man carrying something heavy up stairs, two at a time.

"You always were the stubborn one," he said finally. "I got the larger half of the plums because I thought feeding you was charity. It wasn't. It was fear. I've been afraid of your conscience since Kesselmark."

"Your conscience isn't smaller, Marek. It's just better housed."

Marek laughed — one short, broken sound, quickly strangled — and then he stood, and the interrogation resumed for the benefit of the slot in the door, which Aldous realized with cold clarity must have ears behind it, or a man, or both. For forty minutes they went through it properly: the questions crisp, the answers smooth, the whole performance of a system interrogating a man. Aldous gave him errors. Errors in abundance, elaborately dull, each one documented, each one explicable. The washout on the coast road that had forced the route change. The missing house-plates. The woman in District Four who had given the same name for herself and her sister because, she insisted, they had always been called by each other's. Marek wrote it all down in his blunt clerk's hand, and if there was disbelief anywhere in the transcript, it was buried so deep beneath procedure that no spade would find it.

At the end, Marek capped his pen.

"One last item," he said, in his official voice. "Supporting documentation. The original register pages for the eleven households were requested from your office for comparison." He paused, and the pause stretched just past natural, just into meaning. "They cannot be located."

Aldous kept his face still, but his heart had begun, absurdly, to hope.

"The file transfer log shows them checked out four days ago," Marek continued, tonelessly, reading from a memo he did not look at. "By an unidentified clerk. Given the current disruption of government services, the loss is regretted but not considered actionable. The hearing will proceed on testimony alone." He closed the folder. "Testimony alone," he repeated, softer, and looked at Aldous, and for one unguarded second his eyes said everything the transcript could never hold: *I lost them on purpose. I walked them out of the archive under my coat. I don't know why. Yes I do. Don't thank me. Thank me and I'll have to arrest you twice.*

"Testimony alone," Aldous echoed.

"Errors," Marek agreed. "This whole season of the world is full of them."

He gathered the folder, straightened his collar with two sharp tugs — armor going back on, buckle by buckle — and knocked on the door. As the lock turned he paused, half in the doorway, his back to Aldous, and said, quietly enough that only a man listening for it would hear:

"There were plums in my desk drawer at Kesselmark. If the archive floods again, I hope someone thinks to save the drawer."

Then he was gone, and the lamp burned on alone with its crooked shade, and Aldous sat in the spilled light with his heart hammering out a rhythm that was not quite fear and not quite gratitude but some third thing for which no census had ever created a column.

They came for him an hour later and marched him back toward the cells, and it was as they crossed the corridor that he heard it — boots, many, and voices raised, and a bell beginning to ring somewhere in the upper floors of the building, ragged and urgent, the kind of bell that does not ring for hearings.

A guard ran past them without saluting, without slowing. Another was shouting something about the waterfront. And through a tall window at the corridor's end, Aldous saw smoke rising over the harbor — not the thin domestic smoke of chimneys, but a black, billowing column of it, climbing into the grey sky from the direction of the quays, from the customs house, from the offices where manifests lived and died.

The Republic was burning its own paperwork now.

He pressed his forehead briefly against the cold glass, thinking of Marek walking out of an archive with stolen pages under his coat, thinking of a woman somewhere on the water or the quay or already beyond it, thinking: *the ledger is coming loose, page by page, and tonight anything written may still be unwritten.*

Down in the harbor, unseen from the window, a ship with dormant rootstock in its hold was preparing, tide by tide, to make for neutral water — and Aldous Renn, prisoner, stood smiling at the glass like a madman, because for the first time since his arrest, the arithmetic had changed in favor of the living.
