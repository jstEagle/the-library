## 17. The Turning Tide

The tide turned at half past four, and with it the whole harbor seemed to exhale.

Freya stood on the quay beneath the customs shed's leaking eave and watched the water begin its slow reversal—the scum of ice and coal dust that had drifted seaward all morning now curling back toward the pier pilings, dragging with it a drowned crate lid, a gull feather, the small debris of a dying republic. The *Meridian Lark* sat low in the channel, her holds full of winter rootstock and one hidden passenger, her papers stamped in Aldous Renn's careful hand. A tug was already nosing against her hull, impatient as a dog at a gate.

Ilse was aboard. Ilse, who had crossed an occupied zone on foot with frostbitten feet and had still found it in herself to forgive—or to offer forgiveness, which might be harder—was behind those crates, breathing through a gap in the slats, alive because Freya had betrayed every oath she'd ever sworn and would betray them all again without hesitation.

So why did her boots feel nailed to the stones?

Because she knew what the tide was taking with it. She had learned it two hours ago, in the smoke-stink anteroom of Harbor Command, from a junior clerk who spoke too quickly and wouldn't meet her eyes: *Renn, Aldous — census division — detained this morning, charges pending.* Four words that had gone through her like a fish-gutting knife: *charges pending*. As if a man could be pending. As if his silence, which had already bought Ilse's passage, were not itself a sentence being written somewhere by someone with a stamp.

* * *

She had tried, in those first minutes after hearing, to do the arithmetic that duty demanded. It was the same arithmetic the Republic had taught her across nine years of courier work: weigh the cargo, weigh the cost, choose the lighter grief. One man against one woman already safe at sea. One man against the mission entire. The Republic did not trade hostages; the Republic did not even acknowledge it took them.

But arithmetic was a cold room, and Freya had spent too many winters in cold rooms to believe they kept anything alive.

She thought of Aldous's office—the green-shaded lamp, the ledgers squared to the desk's edge with a precision that must have been the only order left in his life. She thought of how he had looked when she said it aloud, the words he'd asked for: *Ilse is alive.* Not *safe*, not *well*, just *alive*, as if that single fact were a coin he had been holding under his tongue for months, waiting for someone to confirm its weight. He had stamped her manifest without reading it. Without reading it. Any clerk who stamped unread paper in Vessany that winter was either corrupt or already hanged, and Aldous Renn was neither. He had simply decided, in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes, that some debts outranked the state.

And now the state had him.

The tug gave two short blasts. Down the gangway, the *Lark*'s mate called for last hands ashore. Somewhere inland—too far to see, close enough to imagine—prison bells were ringing the hour, and Aldous was sitting in a room with a lamp and a man asking questions, giving them nothing but a story about clerical errors, spending himself the way Freya had once seen soldiers spend themselves at Vellmark: quietly, completely, without expecting the debt to be honored.

Her mother had kept orchards through two wars and a blight, and she used to say that a tree never mourns the fruit it drops—it lets go so the branch can bear again. Freya had always thought it a farmer's comfort, a pretty lie about loss. Standing on the quay with the tide pulling the world seaward, she understood it at last. Her mother hadn't been talking about acceptance. She'd been talking about choice. The tree chose what to release. That was the whole secret. Grief came whether you chose or not; agency lived only in the letting go.

"Last call for shore party!" the mate bellowed, and then, squinting up at her through the gray: "You there—are you off or on?"

*Off,* said everything she had promised her commission. *On,* said everything else.

"I'm staying," Freya said, and heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her, since inside she felt like a building coming down floor by floor. "Tell your passenger—" She stopped. Careful. Even now. Even here. "Tell whoever's below that the promise is kept. All of it. Tell her to plant deep."

The mate frowned—he was a harbor man, harbor men knew better than to understand things—but something in her face made him touch his cap and turn back up the gangway without another question.

She watched the mooring lines come off. She watched the *Meridian Lark* swing her blunt bow toward the channel, the tug worrying at her like a sheepdog, the gulls rising off her rigging in a white scatter. She watched until the headland took her, and then she watched the empty water where the ship had been, because looking away felt like abandoning Ilse twice, and she would not do it twice.

Then the tide had turned, the ship was gone, and there was nothing left on the quay but Freya Fairweather, alone, with a war on one side of her and a prison on the other, and one thing in the world she could still spend.

* * *

She walked toward the Commandant's compound the way she had once walked into burning villages: fast, because hesitation killed, and with her hood down, because a courier who hid her face was a courier already convicted.

The city had passed beyond siege into something worse—siege at least implied someone expected you to hold. Shopfronts stood open to the snow, their goods long looted, their doors swinging like mouths mid-sentence. A municipal noticeboard held three layers of proclamations, the newest pasted crooked over the oldest, and the newest read, in type that had clearly been reset in haste: *BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COUNCIL, ALL PERSONS HOLDING ENEMY CORRESPONDENCE MUST SURRENDER SAME TO NEAREST COMMAND POST WITHIN FORTY-EIGHT HOURS.* Below it, smaller: *Collaboration is treason. Treason is death.*

Someone had thrown mud at the board. Someone else had scraped the mud into a crude star. The city was writing its own epitaph in filth, and Freya read it as easily as any dispatch.

At the checkpoint before the Commandant's gate, a boy soldier barred her way with a rifle that was taller than his fear. "State your business."

"I'm here to surrender myself." She let that land. Let his eyes go wide. "And to make an offer to whoever has authority to accept it. Tell them a Republic courier is standing in the snow with the cipher keys in her head, and she'll give up every code the Service still trusts—for a price."

"You can't just—"

"Boy." She kept her voice level, almost kind. "In forty-eight hours there will be no Republic and no Service, and these codes will be worth nothing to anyone. In forty-eight hours I will be worth nothing to anyone. Whoever talks to me today is the only person in this command who will ever have made a trade like this one. Do you want to be the sentry who turned that away?"

He stared at her. Snow collected on the muzzle of his rifle. Somewhere behind the wall, a bell rang the wrong hour, or the right hour wrongly—she couldn't tell anymore, and suspected no one in Vessany could.

He went and got an officer. The officer looked at her warrant card, went pale in the particular way of men watching their world's furniture rearrange itself, and marched her through the gate himself, faster than dignity required.

* * *

They put her in a room that had once been a dining hall—a long table, chairs pushed askew, maps pinned to the walls with the pins already rusting—and left her alone for forty minutes, which was standard procedure, meant to soften prisoners with silence. Freya had invented half of the Service's interrogation protocols. She used the time to take inventory.

Her body: tired, cold at the edges, functional. Her assets: the coastal ciphers, the harbor rotation codes, the emergency routing table for the northern wire—every key the collapsing government still believed private, memorized across nine years of carrying other people's words. Her liabilities: desertion of post, destruction of official correspondence—no, incomplete destruction, which was worse—the smuggling of a listed detainee out of a detention zone on falsified manifest, and complicity, through Aldous, in fraud against the census. By the book, any one of these was enough. Together they were a gallows with her name carved on the beam.

Against all of it she could set one man: Aldous Renn, arrested this morning, charged with heaven knew what, guilty of nothing but mercy.

When the door finally opened, it was not the Commandant. It was a woman she didn't know—colonel's tabs, ink-stained fingers, the hollowed look of someone who had stopped sleeping sometime around the fall of the southern front—and behind her, two guards and a stenographer whose pencil was already moving before anyone spoke.

"Freya Fairweather," the colonel said. Not a question.

"In person," Freya said. "Which is more than most people in this building can claim."

The stenographer's pencil paused. The colonel didn't smile, but something behind her eyes shifted, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to hold or break.

"You walked in here," the colonel said. "Voluntarily. Through my gate. With your face bare. Either you're the bravest traitor in Vessany or the stupidest loyalist, and I've met both, and neither survives me."

"I'm neither. I'm a courier. I'm delivering a final dispatch, and the recipient needs authority to act on it. Do you have that authority, Colonel, or should I wait while you fetch someone who does?"

A long silence. Outside, artillery muttered—not the enemy's guns yet, just the Republic shelling its own abandoned rail junction, denying it forward. Even the sounds of the war had become self-inflicted.

"Sit down, Fairweather."

"I'll stand. This won't take long." She laid her hands flat on the scarred table, and saw the colonel's gaze drop to them—steady hands, a courier's hands, hands that had carried the life of the coast in their palm for nine years—and understood that she was already winning, or losing, whichever this was. "You know what I am. You know what I carried. Which means you know that when the Provisional Council flees north—and they will flee, everyone in this harbor knows they will—every cipher in my head goes with them into oblivion, unless it goes into yours first."

"And your price."

"Two pardons. Full, signed, dated today. One for me." Freya made herself say the next name slowly, so the stenographer caught every letter. "One for Aldous Renn, census division, arrested this morning on charges I will personally demonstrate are false. He is innocent. I am not. That distinction matters to me. Make it matter to you."

The colonel pulled out a chair and sat, unhurried, and Freya recognized the gesture for what it was: the mirror of her own refusal, a woman planting herself. "Codes rot, Fairweather. By the time anyone acts on them, half will be dead letters. You're selling me melting ice."

"Some of it won't melt. The harbor rotation table is good through spring. The northern wire key hasn't changed in fourteen months—your signals people have complained about it in writing, which means the enemy knows it, which means changing it requires knowing what to change it *to*, and I am the only person in this building who carries the replacement." She leaned forward. "You want the truth? You don't need me. You need the version of me that existed before this morning. She's gone. What's left is a woman trading the only valuable thing she owns, and she knows exactly what it's worth, and she will burn it rather than sell cheap."

"Or we hang you and find the codes in your effects."

"There are no effects. There never were. That was always the design of me." She smiled, and hated the smile, and kept it. "Hang me and you get ash. Trade with me and you get the coast's last working month. Your move, Colonel."

The stenographer had stopped writing. Everyone in the room understood that whatever happened next would happen outside the record, in that borderless country where desperate governments made their real decisions.

The colonel studied her for a long moment. Snow ticked against the high window. Down in the harbor, faintly, a ship's horn sounded—some other vessel, some other departure, the sea indifferent to all of it.

"The Renn man," the colonel said at last, slowly, "is charged with falsifying census entries. The evidence against him is substantial."

"The evidence against him is that he counted honestly where it mattered and mercifully where it didn't. If your tribunal can't tell the difference, then this Republic deserves to die, and I use *deserves* advisedly." Freya heard her own voice crack on the last word and let it crack. Let them see it. There was no longer any advantage in being granite. "Colonel—I buried a sister to this war already. I'm not burying a brother to its paperwork."

Something moved through the colonel's face then—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice—and Freya realized, with a strange vertigo, that the woman across the table had lost people too, had spent this whole ruinous winter signing things she hated, and that for one unguarded second they had recognized each other across the table like two survivors of the same wreck.

"Pardons require countersignature above my rank," the colonel said. "Above several ranks. And the Council is—" she glanced at the rusted map pins, at the muttering artillery, at the century collapsing outside the window— "the Council is occupied."

"Then we negotiate with whoever remains when they finish being occupied. I'll wait. I've waited out sieges before." Freya straightened. "But hear this part plainly, because it's the whole dispatch: I gave you my terms before I gave you my codes, and I keep that order of operations even now. Codes follow pardons. Pardon first. Both names. Then I will sit with your signals officers until dawn if they want, and I will empty my head for them like a purse, and they will leave this room with more working intelligence than the Council itself possesses."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then in a week you'll be trading with the occupation authorities for bread, and they drive harder bargains than I do."

The colonel looked at her for a long, long moment. Then she took the pencil from the stenographer's hand—a small theft, almost gentle—and wrote two names on the corner of a map of a coastline that would soon belong to someone else. *Fairweather. Renn.* She did not sign beneath them. Not yet. But she folded the map corner inward, protecting the names from smudging, the way a woman protects something she intends to keep.

"No promises tonight," the colonel said. "Come back at dawn. If the building's still ours, we talk terms. If it isn't—" she almost smiled, "—then none of this ever mattered, and you'll have wasted your last good day on paperwork."

"It wouldn't be the first thing I've done for paper," Freya said. "Or the last."

They escorted her out through corridors full of men carrying boxes—evacuation dressed up as reorganization—and set her loose at the gate into the blue hour of evening, where the snow had stopped and the lamps along the quay were being lit one by one by lamplighters who had not yet been told that lamplighting was a dead profession.

Freya stood at the top of the harbor steps and breathed. The air tasted of coal smoke and salt and the iron smell of ships. Out past the mole, the water lay black and patient, keeping Ilse's wake somewhere in its memory, bearing her toward neutral water and orchard soil and a life that owed its existence to a forged stamp and a man who had asked only to hear a name spoken aloud.

*I'm coming,* Freya told the black water, though she could not yet say whether she meant it as a promise or a farewell. *One way or another, I'm coming.*

Then she turned back toward the city, toward the cells, toward the long night between now and dawn—toward the hardest negotiation of her life, where she would learn that republics, like drowning men, will take whatever is offered and promise only what they mean to keep.

The tide had turned. Now everything else had to.
