## 19. News from Neutral Water

The harbor of Vellsmark was the color of pewter that morning, and it smelled of nothing at war.

That was the thing Ilse could not stop noticing—the absence. No smoke haze lying over the rooftops like a hand pressed flat. No queues outside the baker's, no chalked ration numbers on doors, no boys in oversized coats checking papers at the quay gates with eyes too old for their faces. Just gulls, and fish guts on the cobbles, and women arguing cheerfully about the price of herring, and a church bell that rang because it wanted to, not because it was counting the dead.

She stood at the rail of the *Maren Lisbet* with her hands folded on the salt-swollen wood and let the strangeness of it move through her like weather. Behind her, in the hold, forty crates of dormant rootstock sat stacked in the dark, most of them holding exactly what their manifest claimed: apple and quince cuttings packed in damp straw, asleep through winter, waiting to be woken in foreign soil. One crate had held her.

For eleven days she had been cargo. She had learned the rhythms of the hold—the groan of the timbers, the way the straw breathed warmth around her body when she curled small enough, the particular silence of sailors who knew what they were carrying and had decided, for wages and conscience both, not to know it too precisely. Old Brandt, the first mate, had brought her water twice a day and never once met her eyes, which she understood as a kindness. Looking at her would have made her a person. Not looking kept them all safe.

Now she was a person again, standing in neutral water under a flag that belonged to no one she had ever sworn anything to, and the safety Freya Fairweather had bought with treason sat around her shoulders like a coat two sizes too large.

"You'll want to come ashore," the harbormaster's clerk said behind her, in accented Vessene. He was young, pink-cheeked, officious in the way of men whose wars were other people's news. "Manifest inspection. Standard procedure. Nothing to fear—your goods are agricultural, yes? Rootstock? We're always glad of trees."

*We're always glad of trees.* Ilse almost laughed. She followed him down the gangway onto stones that did not shake with artillery, past soldiers who glanced at her travel paper—Freya's mother's trading license, bearing the name Renn now, amended in careful ink—and looked away with the mild incuriosity of men who had no reason to look closer.

That was the other absence. Here, her name was just a name.

* * *

The news came to her three days later, in a boarding house above a chandlery that smelled of tar and dried cod, from a newspaper she bought with coins Freya had sewn into the lining of Ilse's coat.

She had been reading the shipping pages first—that habit was new, born of eleven days listening to Brandt and the captain argue about tonnage and tide—but the front page caught her because of a photograph. A harbor she knew. Cranes she had walked beneath. The caption was in the local language but the names needed no translation: *Vessa Harbor Command. Last garrison of the coast.*

And below it, in smaller type, an item the editor had clearly found too strange to leave out: the arrest of a census official, name withheld, charged with falsification of records and conspiracy to subvert lawful detention orders. The item was four sentences long. It said the man had refused, under questioning, to name his accomplices. It said his case was pending before the military tribunal at Vessa.

Aldous.

Ilse read it four times. The words did not change. The coffee went cold in her cup and she did not drink it, because her hands had gone still around the newspaper the way hands go still around something living, something that must not be startled.

Her brother. In a cell. Because he had stamped a manifest without reading it, because he had looked away once too often, because he had spent whatever he had so that a stranger's sister could stand in a warm room drinking bad coffee in a country where the bells rang for no one.

She set the paper down. Then she picked it up again and read the four sentences a fifth time, as if repetition might open a seam in them, some clause that said *he will be fine, they released him, it was a clerical matter.* There was no such clause. There never was.

* * *

It took her another day to learn about Freya, because Freya had left nothing behind except silence, and silence had to be dismantled piece by piece.

The captain of the *Maren Lisbet* was a careful man named Ostrow who spoke in the flat voice of someone who had decided long ago that the sea forgave more than the land ever would. When she came to him on the second morning and asked—not casually, though she made her voice casual—whether anyone had come asking after the shipment, whether any message had arrived from Vessa, he studied her for a long moment across his chart table.

"There was a woman," he said finally. "On the quay, the night we sailed. Dark coat. She watched us clear the breakwater and then she turned around and walked back into the city." He tapped one blunt finger on the table. "I assumed she was your friend. The one who arranged everything." A pause. "You didn't know?"

"She was supposed to be on this ship."

Ostrow's face did something complicated and then settled into something very simple, which was pity, and Ilse hated him for it with a completeness that surprised her.

"Then I'm sorry," he said. "I've carried refugees long enough to know what that means. Sometimes it means she couldn't get away. And sometimes—" he hesitated, choosing, "—sometimes it means she chose not to."

Chose not to. Ilse walked out of the captain's cabin and up onto the deck and stood in the cold until her fingers ached, and the phrase turned over and over in her like a stone in a current. Chose not to. Of course. Of course it was that. Freya Fairweather had never in her life taken a door that opened away from someone else's danger. She had signed a detention order with shaking hands and then spent a month un-signing it with her whole self; she had burned her commission's letters but kept the orchard deed like a held breath; she had put Ilse in a crate of sleeping trees and sealed the lid herself, and Ilse had heard her voice through the slats, low and steady, saying *eleven days, count them, I'll see you in Vellsmark,* and Ilse had believed her because Freya did not lie about small things and this had not been a small thing—

Except it had been a lie. Or half of one. *I'll see you in Vellsmark,* spoken by a woman who had already decided, somewhere in the arithmetic of her heart, that seeing her meant staying behind.

Ilse gripped the rail and the harbor wind came off the water smelling of salt and fish and freedom, and she thought: *you absolute fool. You brave, impossible, self-annihilating fool.*

Then she thought: *I am not cargo you may deliver without my consent.*

* * *

She went back to the boarding house and laid everything out on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The trading license—her mother's, Freya's mother's, the ink amendments still sharp. The manifest, stamped by Aldous's hand, listing forty crates of rootstock bound from Vessa to Vellsmark, consigned to the Fairweather orchard interest. Eleven of those crates were empty now, their cuttings delivered or sold or simply abandoned, and eleven crates were a shape, a size, a story already told and believed once. The remaining coins. The coat with its sewn seams. Her own face, which had passed inspection once in wartime Vessa and would pass again, because faces that pass once are practiced.

Against all of it: the newspaper. Four sentences. A tribunal at Vessa. And underneath the newspaper, unspoken but heavier than all the rest, the knowledge of what Freya had done—what she was surely doing right now, in some interrogation room or office or corridor, trading the Republic's codes, trading herself, buying pardons with the only currency she had left. Buying freedom for Aldous. Believing Ilse safe at sea.

Believing wrong.

Ilse sat on the edge of the bed for a long time. Below the window, the chandlery boy sang while he coiled rope, tunelessly, happily. Somewhere a dog barked at gulls. The world here was so ordinary it felt almost obscene, a room in a house where the war was furniture in another wing.

She thought about the promise. Not the one Freya had made her—she thought about the one she had made, standing among the trees in the last summer of peace, when the apples hung heavy and the light came down through the leaves in coins. *If it comes, you come to me,* Ilse had said, or Freya had said, or both had said it to each other, promises are like that, they don't have a single author. And promises, Ilse thought now, are not transactions. They do not conclude. You cannot pay one off and walk away owing nothing. A promise is a direction, not a destination. It points, and keeps pointing, and the pointing does not stop because you have reached some shore you were aiming for.

Freya had pointed Ilse at safety. Fine. But Freya was still in the war zone, and Aldous was in a cell, and the promise had never once said *save yourself.*

"I refuse," Ilse said aloud, to the empty room, and the sound of her own voice settled it the way a signature settles a document.

* * *

Ostrow refused her first, as she knew he would.

"Absolutely not." He stood behind his chart table like a man behind a barricade. "I ran you as cargo once under a stamped manifest with a customs man spending his neck on your behalf. That man is in a tribunal now. You want me to sail back into Vessa with you standing on my deck in broad daylight? They'd board us inside the hour. They'd take the ship. They'd take me."

"They won't board us," Ilse said. "They'll inspect us. There's a difference, and you know it better than I do—you've been inspected a hundred times. We're carrying agricultural stock inbound. Neutral-flag trader returning for a second consignment—the Fairweather license requests delivery of additional cuttings for spring planting. It's even true; there are crates here that never sold. Nobody questions a merchant going back for more goods. Merchants are greedy. Greed is believable."

"And you? You'll be standing on deck when they inspect."

"I'll be standing on deck," Ilse said, "as the licensed agent of the consignment, holding papers that have already cleared their own customs once, signed by the man they arrested—which means the stamps are genuine, which means the paperwork is flawless, which means the only dangerous thing about me is my face, and my face has already fooled them once."

"It fooled them once," Ostrow repeated. "Once is luck. Twice is arithmetic."

"Captain." She came closer to the chart table, close enough to see the faded tattoo of an anchor dissolving into the hair on his wrist. "I know what you risk. I'm not asking you to forget it. I'm asking you to weigh it against what you already did. You carried a woman in a crate because a dead man's mate decided not to look too hard at the manifest. You've already chosen which side of that line you stand on. I'm only asking you not to pretend otherwise now."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside the cabin door, the ship creaked and settled against the pier, restless, wanting the sea.

"The tribunal man," Ostrow said at last, not looking up. "The census taker. He's why you're doing this."

"He's her brother in every way that matters. And she's—" Ilse stopped. Found the word and held it. "She's mine. Whatever else she is—courier, traitor, fool—she is mine, and she stayed behind believing I wouldn't come back for her, and I will not let her be right about that. Love that only runs toward safety isn't love. It's accounting."

Ostrow looked at her then, really looked, the way he had carefully not looked at her through eleven days in the hold. Whatever he saw made him exhale slowly through his nose.

"Tide turns at dawn," he said. "Be aboard by first light. And if they take us at Vessa, you never saw me, you never sailed with me, and you stowed away without my knowledge. Understood?"

"Understood."

"One more thing." He pulled a canvas sack from under the chart table and set it on top with a thump. "Eleven crates of unsold cuttings, as requested by the Fairweather interest. Straw, packing, the lot. If anyone asks, you're a merchant restocking for spring." His mouth twitched, dry as old rope. "Planting trees in a war zone. It's the maddest cargo I've ever carried, and I once shipped a circus."

"Not mad," Ilse said, taking the sack's weight into her arms. "Dormant. Like everything else worth saving. It waits, and then it grows."

* * *

She loaded the crates herself that evening, working beside Brandt in the lamplit hold, folding the straw down over the roots with hands that remembered how—hands that had done this work as a girl in another country, before borders closed and names became dangerous. The cuttings smelled of wet wood and cold earth, and the smell filled the hold like a memory filling a room.

Brandt worked in silence for a while. Then, not meeting her eyes, in the manner that was his particular kindness: "Going back, then."

"Yes."

"Hmph." He tucked a last handful of straw around a quince root with enormous delicacy, as if tucking in a child. "My wife's family had an orchard. Burned in the second year. She still talks about it. Talks about the trees like they were people." He straightened, wiped his palms on his thighs. "Maybe that's because they were. Maybe trees are just people who can't run."

Ilse looked at him. "Then somebody has to go back for the ones who can't run."

"Aye." He nodded once, slow, and picked up the next cutting. "Aye. That's the truth of it, isn't it."

They finished near midnight. Ilse climbed to the deck and stood at the rail one last time, looking back at Vellsmark—the honest lamps, the bell that rang for no one, the whole soft ordinary weight of the safety she was walking away from. It would be easy, a small voice said, to stay. To plant the cuttings on some rented hillside. To live. Freya had paid enormously for exactly that, for her; to refuse it was to spend Freya's sacrifice like loose change, to throw it back in her face—

But that wasn't what Freya had paid for. Ilse knew it the way she knew the weight of good soil in her hands. Freya hadn't bought Ilse's safety. Freya had bought what she believed was the only version of safety available: distance. And distance was not the same thing. Safety that required leaving Freya to the tribunal and Aldous to the cell wasn't safety at all. It was just a bigger crate, with nicer air.

The tide turned in the grey hour before dawn. The *Maren Lisbet* warped off the pier, her engine muttering, and Vellsmark slid away astern in the mist, lamp by lamp, like a dream being politely returned.

Ilse stayed at the rail until the harbor lights were gone. Then she went below, to the hold, and sat down among her crates of sleeping trees, and began counting the days.

Not eleven this time. However many it took.

Through the port above her, the sky was turning the color of pewter, and somewhere ahead of her a city waited with a tribunal in it, and a prison, and a woman who thought she had finished paying—and Ilse Renn, licensed agent of the Fairweather orchard interest, carrier of dormant things, keeper of a promise that refused to conclude, was sailing straight back into the war with a hold full of spring.
