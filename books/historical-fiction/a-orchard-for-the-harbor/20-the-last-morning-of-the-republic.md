## 20. The Last Morning of the Republic

### Aldous

The cell had no window, but Aldous Renn had learned to read the light anyway. It came under the door in a thin blade, and on the last morning of the Republic of Vessany, that blade arrived late and stayed pale, and he knew before anyone told him that it was snowing.

He had been in the cell for eleven days. Long enough for his interrogator—Corben, who had once shared a desk with him in the census bureau, who had taught him the shorthand for grain yields and the longerhand for lying to superiors—to stop asking questions and start bringing him tea. Long enough for the charges against him to blur from *treason* to *irregularities* to nothing at all, not because anyone had forgiven him, but because the machinery that processed forgiveness was grinding to a halt.

He heard the machinery stopping now. Doors banging open along the corridor, one after another, like a row of teeth being pulled. Boots. Shouting that had no discipline left in it. The particular sound of men abandoning a post they no longer believed in—that shuffling, apologetic hurry.

His door opened without ceremony. Corben stood there, coat over his arm, keys jangling loose in his fist like a handful of dropped coins.

"Renn," he said. "Get up."

"Is this an execution?"

"This is a Tuesday." Corben almost smiled; it didn't take. "The Assembly dissolved itself at dawn. The Marshal's staff burned their files in the courtyard—I could smell it from my quarters. There's no one left to hold you, and no one left to free you either. So I'm doing neither. I'm just opening a door." He stepped aside. "Walk out or stay. I won't record either choice."

Aldous stood. His legs were unsteady after eleven days of stone floors, and he hated that Corben saw it. He took his coat—the same gray coat, still carrying the crease of his arrest—and put it on like a man dressing for work, because habit was all the dignity left to him.

"The file," Aldous said at the threshold. "My file."

Corben looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere down the corridor another door crashed open, and voices spilled through like water finding cracks.

"There was a fire," Corben said. "In the records office. Very careless of somebody." He turned away. "Go home, Aldous. If you still have one."

The corridor smelled of smoke and wet wool and the iron tang of a radiator dying somewhere. Aldous walked past cells whose doors hung open, past a man weeping quietly into his hands with relief, past a guard sitting on an overturned bucket eating bread with both hands like a starving child, his rifle leaned against the wall where anyone could take it. No one looked at Aldous. In the collapse of a state, everyone becomes anonymous at once—the jailed and the jailer alike—and there was a terrible freedom in it that he did not trust.

Outside, snow was falling on the capital the way it always fell: sideways, indifferent, erasing. The square before the prison was empty except for a toppled statue of some minister Aldous couldn't name, its bronze head buried face-down in a drift. Paper everywhere. Orders, warrants, census rolls—years of the Republic's careful accounting of its citizens, scattered and soaking, ink bleeding into gray mush. He knelt and picked up one sheet without meaning to. A detention list. Names he might have written himself, in another life, with a steadier hand.

He let it go into the wind.

*Ilse,* he thought, as he had thought every morning for eleven days, and every night. *Live. Be somewhere warm.*

He walked toward the harbor, because it was the only direction he knew anymore.

### Freya

They had moved Freya Fairweather three times in six days, and each move had been quieter than the last. Fewer guards. Longer silences between the questions she no longer answered. Her cell in the Ministry annex had a window—a narrow one, barred, facing east—and she had spent her nights watching the city's lights go out district by district, like a body cooling.

She had given them the codes. That was the bargain, and the Republic had honored half of it: Aldous's pardon, signed and stamped, delivered to the prison across town. Hers they had kept, the way institutions keep everything—promises, people, guilt—indefinitely, pending review by authorities who ceased, one by one, to exist.

Her handler, Maren Kolve, had come to the bars on the fifth day and said, without apology: "You should have run. I told you to run."

"You told me to sail."

"I told you to save yourself. It's the only order anyone ever gives that makes sense." Maren had pressed something flat against the bars—a folded paper, oilskin-wrapped. Freya hadn't opened it until now. She didn't need to. She had known what it was: the orchard deed, the one document she'd refused to burn, the one her commission had demanded she destroy, smuggled back to her by the one woman in the bureaucracy who remembered why paper mattered.

On the last morning, the guards didn't come at all.

Freya heard it first as absence—the missing rattle of the tea trolley, the missing boots. Then as sound: running in the stairwell, a shot somewhere that no one investigated, laughter, actual laughter, high and unhinged, from a floor above. She stood at her door and put her ear to the cold iron and listened to the Republic die, and it sounded less like thunder than like a house settling.

When the shooting started again, closer, someone's key ring hit the floor outside her cell and skidded under the door. Whether it was dropped in flight or thrown in mercy, she never learned. She picked up the keys with fingers gone clumsy from disuse, tried four of them in the lock, and the fifth turned.

The corridor was empty. Papers drifted across the tiles like leaves. On a desk near the stairs lay a duty roster for the following week—guards assigned to shifts at a ministry that would not exist tomorrow—and beside it a cup of coffee, still faintly warm. Someone had planned to come back. Someone had believed, right up until the moment they didn't, that the world would continue requiring them.

Freya took the oilskin packet from inside her shirt, checked it, and went down the stairs two at a time.

The streets received her without comment. Snow, refugees, soldiers walking out of uniform and into the crowd mid-stride, shedding coats and rifles like snakeskin. A woman was sweeping the steps of the customs house, which was absurd, which was holy. A boy sold matches from a tray and charged double, because commerce, unlike governments, does not pause for history.

Freya walked toward the harbor with the deed against her heart and the codes already spent, and felt lighter than she had since the war began—not happy, nothing so clean as happiness, but *unburdened*, the way a courier feels when the satchel is finally empty. Every order she had ever carried was ash or archive. Every promise she had made was either broken or kept or at sea.

Only one remained outstanding, and it had a name, and the name was Aldous Renn.

### Ilse

Ilse Renn stood at the rail of the *Meridian Star* as the neutral coast slid past—gray hills, green fields, a church spire, ordinary country—and felt the specific grief of the rescued.

Six days at sea. Six days of salt wind and hardtack and the crew's rough kindness, of sleeping in the rootstock hold among the dormant cuttings wrapped in damp burlap, breathing the smell of orchard earth in the middle of the ocean like a hallucination. Six days of telling herself: *this is what she wanted for me. This is the promise, kept.* And underneath it, growing louder each day like water rising in a hull: *she is still there.*

At Vessany Harbor they had told her the truth in fragments. The ship's purser, kind and embarrassed. A dockworker who'd recognized the name Fairweather from the old trading ledgers. The story assembled itself piece by piece: the courier who surrendered herself at the quay. The bargaining. The codes traded for pardons—one granted, one withheld. The ship sailing with one Fairweather promise aboard instead of two.

Ilse had stood on the neutral dock with her bundle in her arms and understood that she was holding her own life like an object someone else had paid for, and she could not bear the weight of it.

So she had done the arithmetic Freya never could. The rootstock crates were still in the bonded warehouse—dormant cuttings, honest cargo, a manifest with a dead woman's license number that no collapsing customs house would ever audit. The *Meridian Star* ran the coastal route twice more before spring, if there was still a route and a spring. And a woman traveling alone with fruit trees was the least suspicious creature in a world coming apart.

She bought passage back the same afternoon.

Now, watching the enemy coast resolve out of the sea mist—smoke columns where the capital should be, the harbor mouth opening like a wound—Ilse pulled her mother's shawl tighter and thought about the summer in the orchard, the year the war was a rumor. Freya had been nineteen, standing on a ladder in the Gravenstein rows with her sleeves rolled, laughing at something Ilse couldn't remember, sun through the leaves making green coins of light on her skin. They had been so certain then that promises were easy things, that keeping them was just a matter of showing up.

*I'm showing up,* Ilse told her across the water. *You showed up for me. Now we're even, and now we start over, and this time you don't get to decide alone.*

The harbor bells were ringing wrong—too fast, unscheduled, the sound of a city announcing its own ending. Ilse gripped the rail and watched the flag above the customs tower, small in the haze, and did not yet know that she was watching it come down.

### Both

The harbor gates of Vessany stood where the customs quarter met the quay, twin iron posts crowned with lamps that hadn't been lit in weeks. Beyond them, cranes leaned over the water like herons; beyond those, gray sea, and freedom, and everything else.

Aldous reached the gates first, because he had nowhere else to be and so walked slowly.

The crowd there was strange and quiet—not rioting, not celebrating, just *gathered*, the way animals gather before weather changes. Sailors. Refugees with bundles. Off-duty soldiers without duties anymore, standing in loose groups, some weeping, some grinning the stunned grins of men released from a spell. On the customs tower, two figures worked at the flag halyards, and the crowd watched them the way crowds watch executions, because that is what it was: the execution of an idea, carried out with rope and pulley in front of witnesses.

Aldous stopped at the gatepost and put his hand on the cold iron. Eleven days in a cell, and before that years of small falsifications, small mercies, small treasons, all of it spent for a woman he'd met through a ledger line and a lie of omission. If Ilse was at sea—if the ship had made the crossing—then every entry he'd faked, every question he'd failed to ask, had been worth the price he was paying. He touched the pardon folded in his pocket, the one Freya had bought with the Republic's secrets, and found he could not feel it as his own. It was hers. Everything good in his last months had been hers.

The flag began to come down.

It descended slowly, catching the snow-wind, snapping once, twice, then hanging slack as the halyard gave it to gravity. The crowd made a sound—not a cheer, not a groan, something older than either, the sound people make when a long illness ends. Somewhere a church bell rang the hour, wrong-headed and faithful.

And through the parting crowd, moving against its current, came a woman in a gray coat with her collar up, walking with a courier's economy, scanning faces the way you scan a horizon.

Aldous did not call out. He simply stopped moving, the way a fixed point stops, and waited for her eyes to find him—which they did, in perhaps four seconds, because couriers are trained to find things.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The flag settled onto the tower roof behind them like a shed skin. Snow came down between them, fine and slow.

"They released me," Aldous said finally. "Or rather, nobody remembered to keep me."

"I know. I—" Freya's voice caught, and he watched her, this woman who had memorized letters by heart and burned them and signed death orders and bargained away a nation's codes, fail to finish a sentence. "I bought your pardon with everything I had. I thought I'd never know if it reached you."

"It reached me." He touched his pocket. "I've been walking around holding it like a ticket. I kept thinking I should give it back to you. It seems like it should be yours."

"It's yours." She stepped closer. "Aldous. Is she—did the ship—"

"She sailed. I don't know more than that." He hesitated, then gave her the truth whole, because she had earned truth whole. "If she's alive, she's on neutral ground, and she's angry with us both for how we saved her. That's my guess. She has your stubbornness and none of your patience for being protected."

Something moved through Freya's face—relief and fear in equal measure, braided tight. "Then she'll come back."

"That's my fear too."

Around them the crowd was thinning, dispersing into the fallen city like water into cracked ground. The lamplighter's ladder leaned abandoned against the gatepost. On the quay, a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and neither of them turned to look.

"Where will you go?" Aldous asked.

Freya reached into her coat and drew out the oilskin packet, worn soft now at the folds. She opened it just enough to show the edge of the deed, the old ink, her mother's handwriting beside the word *orchard*. "There's a hillside," she said. "Above a harbor. Neutral soil, if any soil is neutral anymore. I have cuttings. Dormant ones, wrapped in burlap, if they survived the winter." She looked up at him, and for the first time since he had known her, Gideon's celebrated courier looked simply tired, and young, and human. "I owe you a debt I can't pay, and you owe me nothing, and Ilse will need someone who can forge a document when the new government starts asking questions. Come with me. Plant trees with us. Grow something instead of counting it."

Aldous Renn looked at the harbor, at the falling snow, at the flag lying crumpled on the tower like a dead bird. He thought of ledgers and censuses, of souls counted and miscounted, of the long winter of the Republic that had swallowed millions and, by some miracle of error and mercy, had coughed the three of them back up.

"Yes," he said.

Freya smiled—small, cracked, real. Then her gaze lifted past his shoulder to the gangway of a coastal steamer just docking, where passengers were stumbling down into the snow, and she went very still.

Aldous turned. At the foot of the gangway, wrapped in a dark shawl, a woman stood searching the crowd—and found them.

"Ilse," Aldous breathed.

But Ilse wasn't looking at him. She was looking at Freya, and her expression held fury and love in exactly equal measure, and she was already pushing through the crowd toward them with her jaw set like a woman arriving to settle accounts.
