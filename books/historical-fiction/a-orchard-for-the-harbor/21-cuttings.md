## 21. Cuttings

The hillside above the harbor was terraced in the old way, stone walls holding back the earth in patient steps, and when Freya first saw it she understood that she had been walking toward this place her whole life without knowing it. The soil was darker than Vessany's—wetter, fed by mists that came off the water each morning like slow-breathing animals—and it smelled of loam and salt and something green just beneath the surface of everything, waiting.

She stood at the top terrace with the bundle of cuttings against her chest, wrapped in sacking and waxed cloth the way her mother had wrapped them once, years ago, when the orchard was still theirs and winter shipments were an ordinary business and not a miracle smuggled past soldiers with lanterns and clipboards. The cuttings had come through everything: through the burning of correspondence, through the harbor inspections, through the long gray passage on a ship whose name Freya had never learned, through the weeks of waiting in neutral ports while the Republic behind them finished its dying. They were dormant, leafless, looking more like kindling than like trees. That was the whole secret of them, her mother used to say. Dormant wood forgives you. It holds its life folded up inside like a letter no one has opened yet.

"Are you going to look at it all day," Ilse called from below, "or are you going to help me carry the tools?"

Freya laughed—a sound that still surprised her when it came out of her own body, unbidden, unbudgeted, belonging to no commission and no code. She came down the slope carefully, the bundle held close, and found Ilse standing among the terraces with two spades over her shoulder and dirt already on her hands, because Ilse Renn had never in her life waited for permission to begin.

Aldous was at the bottom of the hill by the wall, where the ground met the road, doing what Aldous always did: taking inventory. He had laid out their small wealth on a flat stone—twelve cuttings bundled in damp cloth, a tin of grafting wax, a knife with a broken tip, three spades they had bought that morning from a man who asked no questions, which in itself felt like a luxury beyond price. He counted things twice. He had counted souls for a living once; he could not seem to stop counting now, even though the ledger he kept was only for himself, and even that he would burn someday, probably, when the habit finally loosened its grip.

"There are eleven bundles," he said as Freya reached him. "You said twelve."

"One is for planting where nobody sees it," Freya said. "The north side of the hill, below the frost line. If everything else fails, that one might live."

He looked at her for a moment, and then he nodded, and wrote nothing down, which was his own kind of prayer.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the work was slower than any of them wanted, because none of them had ever planted grief before and did not know the technique.

Ilse dug the holes. She dug the way she crossed occupied territory, apparently—which was to say with a ferocity that made Freya ache to watch—with the spade driven deep and turned hard, stones hauled out and stacked neatly at the edge of each terrace because waste offended her even now, especially now. Her hair had grown back in since the winter, thick and dark again, but there was still a way she flinched at sudden noises, still a way her eyes went to the harbor road whenever a cart rattled past, cataloguing, measuring, deciding whether to run. Freya had learned not to touch her shoulder from behind. She had learned a great many small things like that, in the months since the ship, and she expected to keep learning them for years, and she had made her peace with it. Some debts you do not pay off. You simply keep paying, gladly, forever.

Freya set the cuttings. This part she knew from childhood, from springs spent trailing her mother between the rows while her mother talked the whole time—to Freya, to the trees, to God, interchangeably—about how a cutting wants to be buried deeper than seems sensible, how the bud unions must face away from the prevailing wind, how you do not coddle them because a tree that is coddled grows shallow roots and dies of the first honest drought. *Treat them like people,* her mother said once, kneeling in the old orchard with dirt to her elbows and the whole valley golden around her. *Give them hardship early and they'll stand when it matters.*

Freya had not understood it then. She understood it now. She set each cutting into the dark hole Ilse had made, firmed the soil around it with her palms the way she remembered, and said nothing aloud, but inside her chest, with each one, she spoke a name.

Her mother. Her father. The orchard keeper, Marta, who had taught her grafting and died believing the Republic would protect the coast. Captain Ostrer of Harbor Command, who had signed her orders and wept once, privately, where he thought no one saw. The clerk whose name she never learned, who had doubled the harbor inspections out of diligence and nearly ended everything. The interrogator who lost the file. Every person the winter had taken or bent or scattered, every soul she had carried paper for and could carry nothing for now except the memory of them pressed into wet earth with a stick of dormant wood. It was not enough. It was also, she understood suddenly, kneeling there with her hands black to the wrist, the only currency anyone had ever really possessed.

Aldous tamped the soil and watered. They had carried water up from the harbor spring in two dented pails, and he made trip after trip without complaint, his coat hanging looser on him than it had in the capital, his face thinner, a white line of scar at his temple where a prison door had met him once. His hands were steady. That was the thing about Aldous that still undid her a little: after everything—the falsified census, the forged authority, the arrest, the interrogation, the months of exile papers and transit camps—he had come out of it with his hands steady and his voice quiet, as if the ordeal had burned away everything in him that trembled and left only the part that chose.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up from the watering can.

"I'm allowed," Freya said. "I bargained my life for you. I intend to get full value."

"You got a census taker with bad knees and a talent for lying to supervisors." He tipped the last of the water around the ninth cutting. "The exchange rate was terrible."

"It was the best trade I ever made."

He didn't answer, but he smiled down at the soil, and Freya saw that his eyes were bright, and she let him have the privacy of turning away to fetch another pail.

* * *

At midday they sat on the low stone wall and ate bread and hard cheese and the small bitter oranges that grew wild along the coast road, and below them the harbor did its eternal work.

It was a foreign harbor, and it took some getting used to. The boats were wrong—the rigging arranged differently, the hulls painted colors Vessany's fishermen would have considered indecent—and the language on the customs house was a language Freya could read only slowly, syllable by syllable, like a child. But the shape of it was right: the curve of the quay, the cranes leaning out over the water, the smell of tar and fish and diesel smoke, the gulls conducting their endless corrupt negotiations above it all. A harbor was a harbor. People came ashore carrying everything they owned; people went out to sea carrying less; and somewhere in between, every day, someone stamped a document that decided whether the cargo lived or died.

Freya watched a customs officer on the quay below open a crate of fish, poke through it with a bored expression, wave the boat through, and move on to the next. Her chest tightened and released. Six months ago that gesture had been the axis on which the world turned. Now it was just a tired man doing a tired job in a country that was not at war, and the ordinariness of it was so beautiful she almost couldn't bear it.

"The last of them," Ilse said.

Freya turned. Ilse was holding the twelfth bundle—the one for the hidden place—turning it in her hands, peeling back the damp cloth to look at the pale wood, the small dormant buds like closed fists.

"Which tree?" Ilse asked.

"The Reinette. The one by the well. You used to climb it."

"I fell out of it four times."

"And climbed back up five." Freya touched the cutting, the smooth cold bark. "Mother said it was the stubbornest tree in the orchard. She tried to take it out twice and it grew back both times from the root."

"Then it's the right one to hide," Ilse said. "The ones that grow back are the ones worth keeping."

They sat with that a moment. Below them a ferry came in, and the crowd on the quay shifted and reformed around it, and somewhere a bell rang the hour in a dialect of bells Freya was only beginning to learn.

"Can I tell you something I haven't told either of you," Aldous said.

Ilse glanced at him sideways. "You've been building up to it all morning. You count twice when you're nervous."

He almost laughed. Then he didn't, and Freya saw the seriousness settle onto him like weather coming in off the water.

"When they arrested me," he said, "in the office, with the evidence on the desk—my handler offered me the deal, and I refused, and I want to be honest that refusing wasn't brave. Or it wasn't only brave. There was a calculation in it too. I thought: if I name them, Ilse dies, and Freya dies, and then I will spend whatever years they leave me knowing I am the reason, and that is a sentence worse than anything they can give. So I refused partly because it was right and partly because I could not survive being wrong." He turned the empty watering can slowly in his hands. "And then in the interrogation room, when they asked me about the census entries, I gave them the story about clerical error, and I watched my old friend Brenner across the table, and I realized something. He knew. He knew the errors weren't errors. He'd known for weeks. And he lost the file anyway." Aldous looked out at the harbor. "There were more of us than I ever knew. That's what I've been carrying. Not guilt—I've made my peace with the guilt, mostly—but this strange grief for all the people who looked away and I never learned their names. The whole machinery of that state ran on people doing exactly what they were told, and it still couldn't stop a handful of liars with a shipment of rootstock. Because underneath the obedience there was always somebody willing to misfile a document. There always is. That's the whole hope of the species, as far as I can tell. Misfiling."

Ilse put her hand over his on the wall. She didn't say anything. Neither did Freya. Some confessions don't want absolution; they just want witnesses, and they had become, the three of them, expert witnesses to one another.

* * *

In the afternoon they planted the eleventh visible cutting, and then Freya and Ilse went down together to the hidden place, below the frost line on the north slope, where bracken grew tall and the harbor couldn't see them and they couldn't see the harbor.

The soil here was colder, untouched, full of the fine white roots of grasses and the smell of things decomposing into other things. Freya dug while Ilse stood guard out of pure habit, scanning the treeline, and halfway down Freya hit a stone the size of a loaf and had to lever it out with both hands, gasping, and when it came free the hole beneath was dark and perfect and smelled like the inside of the earth, like the cellar under the old orchard where Ilse had hidden through the worst weeks of the war, where forgiveness had been offered and not yet accepted, where everything had begun to turn.

"Do you remember," Ilse said quietly, "when you told me. About the order you signed."

"I remember."

"I lay awake half that night being furious with you. Properly furious. You signed a paper that would have put me in a detention camp, and then you drove through a checkpoint with me packed in a crate like winter fruit, and I could not make those two women be the same woman." Ilse knelt beside the hole. "And then somewhere toward morning I understood that they were the same woman, and that was the point. You weren't good despite the order. You were good through it—you carried it, and it broke you, and you paid for it, and you kept going anyway. Anyone can refuse an evil order from the safety of another country. It costs nothing. You refused it from inside, where refusal costs everything." She took the twelfth cutting from Freya's hands. "I accepted your forgiveness a long time ago, Freya. I think maybe you never accepted mine."

Freya knelt in the cold grass and found that her eyes were wet, and did nothing to stop it.

"I keep waiting for the next order," she said. "That's the truth. Six months of freedom and every morning I wake up and reach for the dispatch case that isn't there. I don't know how to be a person with nothing to deliver. Everything I was, was the route. Capital to harbor, harbor to capital, the codes, the seals, the burning. Take that away and I'm—" She gestured at herself, at the hillside, at the wide indifferent sky. "I don't know what I'm."

Ilse pressed the cutting into the dark hole and began firming the soil around it with both hands, working it down the way she had watched Freya work the others, learning her mother's technique secondhand.

"You're the person who promised a frightened girl shelter if war ever came," Ilse said. "And then, twenty years and one apocalypse later, kept the promise. That's not a job. That doesn't end. There's no final delivery." She looked up, dirt on her cheekbone, harbor light in her eyes. "You're not between orders, my love. You're between plantings. It's different. One of them has fruit at the end."

Freya laughed and cried at once, kneeling in the bracken, and helped her firm the soil down, and they covered the little hidden grave-that-was-not-a-grave together, the stubborn Reinette from the tree by the well, buried deep where no census would ever find it, facing away from the wind.

* * *

They walked back up as the light went long and gold across the water, and found Aldous on the top terrace, standing very still among the row of eleven planted cuttings, counting them yet again—or perhaps not counting. Perhaps, for the first time in his professional life, simply looking at them.

"Eleven," he said. "Plus the hidden one. Twelve."

"All accounted for," Freya said.

He nodded. Then he took something from his inside pocket, and Freya stiffened—old reflex, paper in a man's hand meaning trouble—until she saw what it was. A single sheet, soft with handling, creased into quarters. She knew it before he unfolded it. Everyone who had ever been in that cellar knew it.

The orchard deed. Her mother's handwriting on the margins, the customs ledger entries copied out in her careful merchant's hand, the Renn name beside the Fairweather name going back three generations. The document Freya should have burned with the rest of her correspondence and had instead committed treason twice over to keep.

"I've been thinking about what to do with this," Aldous said. "It's evidence. Of everything. Of us. If anyone ever—" He stopped. He looked at the paper for a long time. "But it's also the only proof that any of it happened. The promise. The ledger. That our families ever trusted each other enough to write each other's names down."

"So you couldn't burn it," Freya said.

"I couldn't burn it." He held it out. "But I don't think it should stay hidden either. Hidden things get found by the wrong people. Maybe—" and here he hesitated, this careful man, this counter of souls, reaching past the edge of his own caution—"maybe it belongs in the ground. With the trees. Where it can turn into something else."

Ilse was shaking her head slowly, but her eyes had gone wide and bright. "Bury the deed."

"Bury the deed," Aldous said. "Plant the orchard on top of it. Let the roots read it. In fifty years nobody will know why these trees grew so well here, and it won't matter, because the reason will be in the wood. In the fruit. Anyone who eats one will be eating the promise, whether they know it or not." He smiled, small and crooked. "That's the best kind of record-keeping. The kind you don't have to guard."

So they dug a small grave at the head of the new row, in the best soil of the top terrace, where the morning sun would reach it first, and Freya knelt and unfolded the deed one last time and read it—not the legal text but the margins, her mother's hand, the lists of varieties, the prices per bushel from years when prices were the largest cruelty anyone could imagine, and beside them, in a different ink, a different hand, the Renn accounts, the shared harvests, the two families' lives interleaved like roots.

*Here is the proof,* she thought. *Not in archives. Here.*

She folded it once more, kissed it—it seemed necessary; she could not have said why—and laid it in the earth, and Ilse and Aldous knelt on either side of her, and together they pushed the soil over it and pressed it down with their palms, all six hands flat on the dark ground, the way you press a seal into wax.

Above them the evening came on soft. Below them the harbor lights came up one by one, a constellation assembling itself over the water, and somewhere down there a young customs officer was stamping documents without reading them closely, and somewhere a ship was loading, and somewhere a child was asking a question an adult wouldn't answer, and the great ordinary machinery of the living world turned on, indifferent and merciful, requiring nothing of them tonight.

Freya stayed kneeling after the others rose. The soil was cold under her hands and the cuttings stood in their row like a sentence waiting to be finished, twelve bare sticks that would not speak until spring, and she found she was not grieving, exactly, and not at peace either, but somewhere between the two, in the same handful of earth, which was, she supposed, the only country she would ever hold citizenship in now.

"Come inside before the dew," Ilse said from the path, holding out her hand. "Tomorrow we start the walls."

Freya took her hand and stood, and looked back once at the buried deed, the hidden Reinette, the eleven sentries in a row against the darkening sky.

She did not know it yet—would not know it for years, until a stranger with a clipboard came asking questions on this same hillside and Ilse answered honestly, freely, choosing memory over invisibility—but something in her already suspected: nothing planted in love stays buried forever.

The dew came down off the sea, gentle as snowfall, and watered everything.
