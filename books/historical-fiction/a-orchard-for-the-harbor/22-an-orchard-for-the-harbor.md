## 22. An Orchard for the Harbor

The first pear of the new orchard came ripe on a morning that smelled of salt and cut grass, and Ilse Renn stood beneath the tree with her hand cupped around it as though it were an egg she was afraid to set down.

It was smaller than she had expected. The pears of the old orchard—the one above the harbor at Vessany, the one her mother's ledger had made famous in three counties before the war ate the ledgers—had been heavy things, golden-green, with a blush like a fever breaking. This one was modest, a little lopsided, its skin still carrying a fuzz that would smooth away by full ripeness. But it hung from a branch that Ilse herself had grafted, seven years ago, from a cutting carried out of a burning republic in the false bottom of a crate of dormant rootstock.

She turned it once in her palm. She did not pick it.

Below the hillside, the harbor of Merensund went about its morning. Fishing boats came back through the gap in the breakwater with their lamps still lit against the early fog. The customs house opened its shutters—she could hear the wooden clatter from here, a sound that had taken years to stop frightening her. Somewhere down there a bell rang the hour, and the fog took the sound and softened it into something almost kind.

"Freya," she called, not loudly. The hillside carried voices well; it always had. "Freya, come see."

Freya Fairweather came up the path from the water cistern with two buckets swinging from the yoke across her shoulders, gray at the temples now, her courier's lean gone soft at last into something a person could live inside. She looked up at the branch, then at Ilse's face, and set the buckets down with the deliberate care of a woman who had learned, late and expensively, that some moments deserve to be entered slowly.

"Well," Freya said.

"Well," Ilse agreed.

They stood together under the tree for a while without touching, which was its own kind of touch after all these years. Above them the fog was burning off the ridge in long rags, and the light came through in shafts and lay across the rows of young trees—forty-one of them now, apples and pears and a single stubborn quince that Aldous swore would never fruit and which fruited every year out of what Ilse could only assume was spite.

"It's the Vessany Gold," Ilse said. "Or whatever we're calling it now. It doesn't have a name here yet."

"Then we should give it one," Freya said. "Names matter. I've seen what happens when people go unnamed."

Ilse smiled despite herself. "You would say that."

"I've earned the right to say it." Freya reached up—not for the fruit but past it, laying her palm flat against the bark the way a physician lays a hand against a chest to feel the heart underneath. "Seven years. My mother waited nine for her first crop, and hers wasn't even war-delayed."

* * *

Aldous Renn came up the hill an hour later, as he did most mornings, walking from the room he kept in town because he had never quite been able to make himself sleep under a roof he hadn't built or paid for with his own hands. He carried bread wrapped in cloth and a newspaper folded under his arm, and when he saw the two women standing under the third row, he stopped where he was on the path and simply looked, the way he looked at everything: thoroughly, quietly, like a man counting souls.

"You're smiling," he said to his sister. "I don't trust it."

"The trees are bearing," Ilse said.

Something moved through Aldous's face then, quick and deep, like a fish turning under ice. He had been a census taker in another life, in another country—a man whose whole trade was knowing how many, how many, how many—and Ilse knew that somewhere behind his eyes he was doing the arithmetic he could never stop doing: seven years since the ship, five since the amnesty, four since they'd bought this hillside with money Freya's handlers had paid for codes and never asked how it was spent. Four years since Aldous had come off the prison transport at Merensund thin as a fence rail and stood at the bottom of this same hill and refused, absolutely refused, to climb it until someone told him he was welcome.

He climbed it now. He always climbed it now.

He broke the bread into three pieces without being asked and handed them out, and the three of them ate standing among the trees like hired hands taking their midday, which in a way is exactly what they were.

"The paper says they're reopening the coastal archives," Aldous said at last. He said it lightly, the way he said everything that mattered, watching them over the top of the bread. "Vessany's old records. Customs ledgers, shipping manifests. A historian from the university wants to write about the harbor trade in the last years."

Freya went very still. Ilse felt it happen beside her, the way you feel weather change.

"They'll find your mother's handwriting," Freya said to Ilse. "In the ledger. Her name beside yours."

"They already found it once," Ilse said. "And look what came of it."

But she said it gently, because she remembered—she had never once forgotten—the winter night in the orchard cellar at home, the smell of stored apples rotting sweet into the straw, when Freya had confessed in a voice like something breaking that she had signed the detention order, that Ilse's name had passed through her own hands on its way to the lists. Forgiveness had been offered that night. Acceptance had come later, the way frost comes later than cold. Some debts between people who love each other are not forgiven so much as converted—turned over years at a time into small daily payments, a bucket carried, a graft bound, a piece of bread broken three ways.

* * *

The census taker came on a Thursday.

Ilse saw him first from the kitchen window, coming up the cart road with a leather satchel and a government-issue hat that sat on him slightly too large, the way such hats always do on young men not yet grown into their offices. Behind him the valley lay gold and green in the autumn light, the harbor below with its honest smoke, and for one unreasoning moment Ilse's chest went tight the way it still sometimes went tight—at door knocks, at uniformed shoulders, at the word *registration* spoken in any accent.

She made herself breathe. She put the kettle on. Fear, she had learned, was a fire to be tended, not extinguished; left untended it spread, and smothered entirely it went underground and burned the roots.

He knocked. Freya answered, because Freya always answered doors—it was the courier in her, the old reflex of putting herself between the world and whatever the world wanted. Ilse stayed in the kitchen with her hands in flour, listening.

"Good morning. Municipal census, district four. Routine enumeration." The voice was young, careful, rehearsed. "Name of the household head?"

"Fairweather," Freya said. "Freya Fairweather."

A pause, the scratch of a pen. "Occupation?"

"Orchardist. We keep the hillside."

"And the other members of the household?"

"Two. My—" and here Freya paused, and Ilse in the kitchen heard the pause the way you hear a floorboard settle, "—my partner. And her brother. They're both Renn."

Another scratch. Then the young man's voice again, and Ilse heard the change in it, the slight drop from recitation into genuine curiosity, the tone of a man going off his printed questions:

"The Renns. There were Renns in the harbor registries out of Vessany, weren't there? Before the annexation. Trading house, dried fruit and rootstock. My instructor used their ledger as a teaching example—their bookkeeping was famous. A woman's hand did most of it. Maren Renn."

Flour on her hands, Ilse walked out of the kitchen.

She did it deliberately. Later she would try to explain to herself why—whether it was the mention of her mother's name in a stranger's mouth, or the sight of the young man's open, earnest face, or simply the fact that she was fifty-eight years old and tired of being a name omitted. Perhaps it was simpler than any of that. Perhaps it was only that the pear had come ripe that week, and a thing so long awaited makes a person honest.

"That was my mother," she said.

The young man turned. Up close he was very young indeed, twenty-three perhaps, with ink stains on his fingers and the particular hunger of someone who had chosen paperwork because he believed papers could hold a country together. His eyes went to her flour-white hands, to her face, and Ilse watched him do the arithmetic—Renn, Vessany, the harbor, the famous ledger—and arrive at a number he had not expected.

"You're—" He stopped himself. She could see the training catch up with him, the caution, the instinct toward discretion that governments beat into their clerks. "Madam, I'm only here for the count of persons. Occupations, ages, that's all."

"I know what you're here for," Ilse said. "I've been counted before. In Vessany, the last winter. A man named Aldous Renn counted my household—he didn't know yet that I was hiding in it. He asked whether anyone had claimed kinship with me." She glanced at Freya, who had gone quiet in the doorway, watchful in the old way. "That question saved my life. Or the answer to it did. It depends how you look at it."

The young man's pen had stopped moving.

"Will you write it down?" Ilse asked him. "What I'm about to tell you?"

"If—if it's relevant to the enumeration—"

"It isn't. Not at all. Write it anyway, if you want. Or don't." She wiped her hands on her apron, leaving white ghosts on the cloth. "My mother kept the books for a trading house in a country that no longer exists. When the war came, I hid in an orchard cellar for eleven months. A woman who had promised me shelter in peacetime smuggled me out in a crate of dormant rootstock, and her handler burned the correspondence, and a census taker falsified his own entries to buy us time, and he was arrested for it, and he survived because the state fell apart faster than it could punish him. Every person in this household owes their life to a document that was either falsified or destroyed. That is the true census of this house. Count it however you like."

The silence afterward was enormous. Below the hill the harbor went on ringing its bells, mending its nets, living its unremarkable life. A wind came up the valley through the rows of trees and stirred the leaves, and Ilse heard it move through forty-one young crowns and thought: *listen, they're all talking at once.*

The young man looked down at his form. Then he did something Ilse had not expected. He turned the page over, to the blank back, and uncapped his pen.

"Would you say that again?" he asked. "Slower? My instructor—the one who taught us from your mother's ledger—he'd want to know this. He's writing the history. He says the official record has holes in it the size of the war itself." He looked up at her, and his face had lost its office and become simply a young man's face, eager and a little afraid. "Nobody writes down the ones who looked away. The ones who lied on their own forms to save somebody. How would anyone ever learn that it's possible, unless somebody admits to it?"

*Some inheritances are meant to be found.* Ilse thought of her mother's handwriting marching down the columns of the customs ledger, patient and exact, recording weights and prices and port fees for a world that believed itself permanent. Her mother had never hidden anything. It had been Ilse's generation that learned invisibility—learned to fold themselves into cellars and crates and blank spaces on forms. And here was this boy with his too-large hat asking her, in effect, to write herself back into the record.

Freya was looking at her from the doorway. Freya, who had memorized a letter by heart before feeding it to a stove; who had signed an order and then spent the rest of her life unsigning it; who understood better than anyone alive what it cost to put a true thing on paper.

Ilse nodded once, and Freya nodded back.

"Sit down," Ilse said to the young man, pulling out a chair. "It's a long story, and it starts with a promise made among the trees, in peacetime, by two fools who thought peace would last. I'll tell you all of it. The names, the dates, the crate, the stamp, the arrest—all of it. You can check my figures against the archives when they reopen." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "We were always accurate. It ran in the family. My mother's books balanced to the last coin."

The pen began to move.

Outside, the light shifted through the rows, and the first pear of the new orchard hung golden-green on its branch, unnamed still, waiting for someone to say its name aloud—as if saying it could make the tree, and the ground under it, and the three old conspirators eating bread beneath it, permanently, finally, real.
