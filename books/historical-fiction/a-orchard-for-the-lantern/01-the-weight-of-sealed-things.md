## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The dispatch case arrived at dusk, as the important ones always did, carried by a boy too young to know what he was carrying.

Isandro Voss watched him come up the courtyard of the Courier's House with the black lacquered box held against his chest like something he'd been told might bite. He was maybe fourteen, still growing into his uniform coat, and he walked with the careful, exaggerated steadiness of someone who had practiced not dropping it on the way over.

She set down her pen mid-sentence — she had been drafting a report about road conditions in the eastern passes that no one would ever read — and waited.

"You're Voss?" the boy asked, though the courier's seal worked into the leather of her satchel, the grey-and-gold cord at her shoulder, and the eleven years of reputation that preceded her through every gate in the Republic of Vellamare should have answered that question before he opened his mouth.

"I'm Voss."

"From the Chancellery. For your eyes only." He held out the case with both hands, the way they taught them now, since the war had taught everyone else to shoot at couriers first and ask credentials after. "There's a receipt."

"There's always a receipt."

He blinked. She signed where he indicated, took the case, and felt its weight settle into her palms — and there it was, the old habit, the one she'd never managed to break and had long ago stopped wanting to: *weighing it.*

Every courier did it. They'd deny it if you asked, the way soldiers deny checking their blades or priests deny counting the collection plate twice. But Isandro had carried sealed things for the Republic since she was nineteen years old, and in those eleven years she had learned to read an order the way a physician reads a face — before it ever speaks.

Weight first. This case was heavier than a diplomatic pouch had any right to be. She'd once carried a peace treaty to the Sarnish coast that weighed less than this, and that document had ended a seven-year war. Treaties were ink and vellum. This was ink, vellum, and something else — a second layer of wrapping, she judged, by the density, perhaps a metal folio inside the wax-sealed envelope. Documents that got metal folios were documents intended to survive fires. Or documents whose authors worried about who might burn them.

Then the wax. Through the case's viewing window she could see the chancellery seal pressed deep and clean — the twin lanterns of the Republic, unbroken, flawless. A hurried seal smeared. An ashamed seal was thin, as though the hand pressing it had wanted to use less wax than protocol demanded. This seal was neither. It was generous, deliberate, pressed by a steady hand that had taken its time. Whoever had closed this had wanted it to look important.

*Important,* Isandro thought, turning the case over, *is not the same as good.*

And last — urgency. The boy was already shuffling his feet. The case bore three courier's marks along its spine: the single dash for routine, the double dash for priority, and then the third mark, the one they called the widow's stroke, a long diagonal slash that meant *ride through the night, change horses without resting, deliver before all else.* She had seen the widow's stroke maybe six times in her career. Twice it had carried mobilization orders. Once, a death warrant for a governor who had sold a province. Once, word of the plague reaching Corveth Harbor, which had saved more lives than any treaty she ever delivered.

She had never stopped to wonder, until this moment, why they called it the widow's stroke.

"Will there be an answer?" the boy asked.

"Not tonight."

"They said—"

"They said a lot of things," Isandro said, and softened it with a smile, because the boy hadn't chosen the war any more than she had. "Tell them the dispatch is received and under guard. That's answer enough for tonight."

He went, relieved to be released from her presence, and she stood a moment longer in the courtyard watching the light fail over the rooftops of Corveth, the capital going amber and quiet around her, lamplighters working their rounds below. Somewhere a cart groaned past loaded with cabbages. Somewhere a woman was singing to a child, badly, off-key, with great love.

*Eleven years,* she thought. *And I have never once opened a case early.*

It was true. It was the thing she was known for, the thing Consul Beretha Hale would say of her with that dry half-smile of hers when other officials wondered aloud whether Voss could be trusted with sensitive material: *Voss carries what she is given and delivers what she carries.* Eleven years, two wars' worth of roads, three assassins (one competent), and not a single broken seal. Not because she wasn't curious — curiosity was the engine of the whole profession; a incurious courier was just luggage with legs — but because the seal was the whole architecture of the Republic's trust. You didn't peek behind the wall that kept you standing.

She climbed to her rooms with the case under her arm and told herself the heaviness in her chest was supper arriving late.

* * *

Her rooms were small and hers, three floors up in the Courier's House, with a window that looked east toward the mountains and a stove that looked, philosophically speaking, toward nothing in particular and heated even less. She lit the lamp, fed the stove the day's ration of coal, and set the case on the table between her cold supper and her unfinished report.

Then she sat across from it and studied it, the way you'd study a stranger who claimed to be family.

The wrapping was chancellery black, correct in every particular. The address panel bore the routing marks in the registrar's neat hand: *To the bearer, under seal, destination per addendum. Lantern's Reach.* Lantern's Reach. Of course it was. The whole capital had been talking of little else for a month — the border city holding its breath behind its old walls while the Kessarin army sat outside it like winter itself, patient and certain. There were rumors of negotiations. There were always rumors of negotiations. There were rumors the Republic would trade the city, rumors it would reinforce it, rumors it would do the dignified thing and simply look away while it starved.

And now, on the eve of what everyone called the final mission, the Republic's best courier was handed a case heavy enough to hold a fire-proofed folio, sealed generously by a steady hand, marked with the widow's stroke.

*Destination per addendum.* That was unusual. Normally the routing was written plain: to the consulate, to the general, to the magistrate. *Per addendum* meant the exact delivery point was specified on a separate slip — sealed separately — so that not even the registrar who addressed the case knew where it truly terminated. She had carried per-addendum orders exactly once before, five years ago, to a garrison commander whose name she still couldn't say aloud without tasting ash. Those orders had ended a village. Not destroyed it — nothing so honest as destruction. They had ended it: withdrawn its protection, struck it from the provisioning rolls, left it to be solved by weather and hunger and whatever came down from the hills. She had delivered the orders, saluted, ridden away, and told herself for five years that she had done her duty.

She had gotten very good at telling herself things.

Isandro poured herself the last finger of plum brandy, drank half of it, and made herself stop looking at the case.

*You don't know anything yet,* she told it silently. *You know weight, wax, and urgency. You know the war is dying the way old animals die — slowly, then all at once. You know Lantern's Reach has forty thousand people in it and ovens that are somehow still lit. You know nothing except what frightened people always suspect: that somewhere in the dark, the Republic is deciding something about somebody, and the somebody is never consulted.*

She drank the rest of the brandy.

Outside, the lamplighter passed beneath her window, and for a moment his shadow stretched up the wall beside her — tall, thin, carrying fire.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of the river.

She almost never dreamed of the river anymore; it had been nine years and the cold had long since worked its way out of her bones, or so she'd believed. But in the dream she was back in it — the Aldwater, in the high country near the border, during the peace talks, the year everything had briefly been possible. She had gone walking alone against every rule, because she was twenty-two and invincible and the water meadows had looked soft in the autumn light. The ice had looked solid too.

What she remembered waking — what she always remembered, the dream giving it back to her in perfect detail — was not the cold. It was the face above her when the current finally let her go. A man kneeling on the bank, breathing hard, having run — actually run — along the ice shouting something she'd been too far gone to hear. He'd pulled her out with a horse blanket and furious hands, and when she'd stopped shaking enough to speak, he'd said, "Next time fall closer to the road," and she had laughed, which hurt her ribs, which made her laugh again.

Aldric Fen. Then a colonel of engineers attached to the Kessarin delegation, a quiet man who drew orchards in the margins of his maps and argued about bridge design with anyone who'd stand still. They'd spent that whole autumn being, impossibly, friends — two people on opposite sides discovering that the sides were farther apart than the people. He'd taught her a card game she still played badly. She'd taught him a Vellamari drinking song he still, presumably, sang worse.

That was peacetime. Peacetime was a country that no longer existed, with borders that had dissolved into the war like sugar into tea.

Fen was a traitor now. Everyone knew it — the general who had turned his coat, or refused to turn it, depending on which broadsheet you believed, and vanished into the mountains with a price on his head large enough to buy the street Isandro lived on. The Republic's papers used his name the way you'd spit.

In the dream, the river said nothing. Rivers never do. That's the trouble with rivers.

* * *

Morning came grey and businesslike. Isandro dressed in her riding leathers, checked her satchel's hidden seams out of habit, and went down to the stables where Sorrel — a mare with opinions and the legs to enforce them — greeted her with the equine equivalent of *you're late.*

Sef Andrel was waiting by the mounting block, already saddled, already polished, already insufferable. Twenty-six years old, deputy courier, ambitious the way ivy is ambitious — quietly, constantly, and in every direction at once. He had a talent for being present when superiors needed something remembered and absent when they needed something forgotten.

"Voss." He nodded at the case strapped now to her saddle frame. "So it's true. You've got the widow's stroke."

"You read my mail?"

"I read the stable ledger." He swung up onto his own horse. "They're giving me the southern route — supply manifests to the Second Army, if you can believe it. Manifests." He said the word the way other men said *exile.* "Meanwhile the golden courier gets the history-making run. Lantern's Reach. Terms of surrender, is it? Terms of relief? Come on, Voss, give an old friend something."

"You're twenty-six. I'm not old."

"Give a young friend something."

"I carry what I'm given," Isandro said, "and deliver what I carry. You know the creed."

"The creed's for novices." He grinned, and there was nothing cruel in it, which somehow made it worse — Sef genuinely believed what he said, the way only the truly ambitious ever believe anything completely. "We're past creeds. This is the end of the war, Isandro. Whatever's in that case decides whether forty thousand people go home or go into the histories. Doesn't it itch? Doesn't it just drive you mad, sitting next to it all those miles?"

"It weighs four pounds, eleven ounces," she said. "I weighed it."

He stared at her. "Of course you did."

"And it's none of my business what's in it until the seal breaks, and the seal doesn't break for me. Ride safe, Sef. Mind the ford at Brast — the thaw's coming early this year."

She mounted and rode out through the gates of the Courier's House into the grey morning, and if her heart was hammering harder than the pace warranted, well. Horses feel these things. Sorrel tossed her head and blew, and Isandro leaned forward and spoke nonsense to her ears, and the city fell away behind them mile by mile — the capital, the chancellery with its generous, deliberate seal, the whole anxious machinery of a republic that had begun eating its own furniture to feed the war.

She rode north and east, toward the mountains and the border and Lantern's Reach, and by the second day she had named the thing in her saddlebag honestly, because couriers are trained to name their cargo:

Dread. Four pounds, eleven ounces, plus wrapping.

On the third evening she crested the pass and saw it below her: the city in its valley, walled and lamplit, stubborn as a candle in wind. Smoke rose from a thousand chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Even at this distance she could swear she smelled bread.

Somewhere down there was a consulate expecting her. Somewhere down there was a consul who would ask no questions, because Voss carries what she is given. And somewhere in the saddlebag, wrapped in chancellery black and sealed with twin lanterns, was whatever the Republic had decided about all those chimneys, and all that bread, and every child learning to walk behind walls that had stood for six hundred years.

Isandro touched her heels to Sorrel's flanks and began the descent, and the case rode behind her silent as a stone, heavier every mile, though it had not gained a single ounce.
