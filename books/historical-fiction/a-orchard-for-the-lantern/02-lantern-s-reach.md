## 2. Lantern's Reach

The city announced itself before Isandro could see it, the way a sick relative announces themselves by the smell of medicine in the hall. Woodsmoke, tallow, wet ash — and under all of it, faint but stubborn, bread.

She had been riding for eleven days on roads that grew worse the closer she came to the border, and her back had filed a formal complaint somewhere around day seven. The dispatch case rode against her ribs where it always rode, warm from her own body, and she had long ago stopped thinking of it as paper and wax and thought of it instead as what it was: a weight with a destination. Two pounds, four ounces, if you cared to weigh it. She did care to weigh it. That was the whole of her profession, in the end — knowing precisely how heavy a thing was before anyone asked you to carry it.

At the last ridge before the valley, she stopped and let the mare blow, and looked down at Lantern's Reach for the first time.

She had expected a city already half-dead. Siege towns in the dispatches she'd carried these past two years came in two varieties: those burning, and those pretending not to be. What lay below her was the second kind, and it was better at pretending than most. The walls stood whole. Smoke rose from a thousand chimneys in orderly columns, like a choir standing up to sing. Along the river gate she could see carts queuing, small and patient as beetles. And rising above the eastern quarter, from some street she couldn't name yet, that smell of bread — which meant ovens lit, which meant flour, which meant someone down there still believed tomorrow was worth preparing for.

"Well," Isandro said to the mare, whose name was Turnip because she had named her at nineteen and lacked the imagination or the cruelty to change it. "Let's go see how long they've got."

Turnip flicked an ear, unimpressed. She had heard this tone of voice before, usually before river crossings.

* * *

The outer checkpoint was manned by four soldiers and one dog, and it was immediately clear who was in charge. The dog, a brindled thing with the build of a barrel and the eyes of a disappointed schoolmaster, put his paws on Turnip's shoulder and conducted a thorough customs inspection of her saddlebags while his handlers pretended to have opinions about paperwork.

"Courier's seal," said the youngest soldier, holding out his hand with the desperate firmness of a man who has been told, repeatedly, not to be fooled again.

Isandro drew off her glove and pressed her ring into the wax he provided. She watched him compare it against his book of impressions, tongue between his teeth, and felt the old small pride she always felt — the seal was her face, her office, her whole self compressed into a thumbprint of hot wax. Somewhere behind that pride, quieter, was the thought she'd been refusing to look at since Vellamare: *two pounds, four ounces.* She set it down the way you set down a pack at the top of a hill, telling yourself you'll pick it right back up.

"Voss," the soldier read. "Isandro Voss." He looked up at her with sudden interest, the way people do when a name arrives attached to a rumor. "You're the one who carried the armistice papers through Marrow Pass. In the flood year."

"I carried papers through Marrow Pass," Isandro said. "Whether they were armistice papers depended rather heavily on which week you asked me."

That earned her a grin from the dog's actual owner, a sergeant built like a door with a beard attempting to escape his chin. "Marrows flooded twice that year," he said. "Once with water, once with refugees. You come through both?"

"The water was better organized."

The sergeant laughed, then caught himself, glancing east — toward the lines nobody at this checkpoint would say out loud, though every one of them kept their eyes calibrated to that horizon like instruments. The laughter died in the air between them, and Isandro understood that here was a town where humor had learned to duck.

"You'll want the causeway road," the sergeant said, waving her through. "River gate's backed up with grain wagons. Causeway's slower but it puts you out by the bakeries, and—" he hesitated, and then, because he seemed like a man who paid his debts even when no one was collecting, added quietly: "Smell something good while you still can, courier. Rumor is the flour runs out next month."

"Rumor is usually early," Isandro said.

"Not lately," said the sergeant, and turned back to his ledger, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

Lantern's Reach smelled, in order of intensity, of woodsmoke, wet stone, horse, tallow, and — threading through everything, persistent as conscience — bread.

The causeway road ran between low houses whose shutters were freshly mended, which struck Isandro as either optimism or defiance, and she suspected the town made no great distinction between the two. A woman was re-thatching a roof with the brisk fury of someone daring the sky to try something. Two doors down, an old man sat on a step sharpening a kitchen knife on a whetstone with the unhurried rhythm of a man who had decided that if the world intended to end, it could wait until he finished this knife, and the next one after that.

Isandro had delivered orders in eleven cities during the war's slow unraveling, and she knew the taxonomy well. There were cities that had gone gray — where the color drained out of the walls and the faces together, and people moved like figures in another person's dream. There were cities that had gone loud — markets shouting, hoarders hoarding, priests of three competing faiths all preaching doom within earshot of each other and taking collections for it. And then, rarest, there were cities like this one, which had gone *domestic*, and which frightened her more than either of the others.

Because domestic meant hope, and hope meant something to lose, and she was carrying two pounds and four ounces of whatever-it-was into the middle of it.

A queue had formed outside the bakery on Cordwainer Row — she knew it was called Cordwainer Row because a boy had painted the street name on the wall himself, in careful letters, adding a decorative boot beneath it that looked less like a cobbler's product and more like a boot that had lost an argument with a bear. The queue was orderly. It was, in fact, suspiciously orderly: soldiers and grandmothers interspersed, a system clearly negotiated long ago and enforced now by nothing but mutual agreement and the presence of one enormous grandmother positioned near the front like a fortification.

Isandro counted forty-one people. Then she counted the loaves cooling on the window rack. Eleven.

The arithmetic of siege, she thought. Forty-one souls, eleven loaves, and everyone in line politely pretending not to have done the same sum.

Halfway down the queue, a girl of perhaps seven was playing hopscotch in the gutter with a piece of chalk, hopping her numbered squares with total commitment, tongue out, arms flailing at each turn. Nobody told her to stop. Nobody told her to get away from the street. An old woman ahead of her simply widened the gap when the girl hopped backward, without looking, the way water makes room for a stone.

Isandro slowed Turnip to watch, and hated herself a little for slowing, and watched anyway.

The girl reached the end of her squares, executed a pirouette that owed more to enthusiasm than geometry, and bowed to the queue at large. Three people applauded. The enormous grandmother said, "Again, but this time land it," in the tone of a woman coaching a general.

*They're playing,* Isandro thought. *There are artillery positions on the ridgeline east of here — I passed their picket smoke myself, twelve miles back — and this child is playing hopscotch in a ration line.*

It should have been pitiable. It wasn't. It was the most stubborn thing she'd seen in two years of stubborn things, and she found her throat doing something inconvenient about it, and so she did what couriers do with feelings that arrive unscheduled: she marked them *received* and rode on.

* * *

The consulate stood on Consulate Square, which was less a square than a triangle that had given up on its ambitions, and it was smaller than she remembered from the briefing maps. Three stories of pale stone, shutters closed against the morning, the republic's lantern-and-wave sigil above the door freshly repainted — the paint still bright enough to look embarrassed among the grime of everything around it.

A clerk met her in the entry hall, a narrow young man with ink on three fingers and, she suspected, the rest of his personality hiding underneath it.

"Courier Voss? Consul Hale is at the granary inspection until midday. She asks that you rest, eat, and present yourself at dinner." He paused, and added, with the delicacy of a man delivering a message he hadn't written: "*Dinner,* specifically. Not a report. Dinner."

"How terrifying," Isandro said. "I once dined with a admiral. I still have nightmares about the fish course."

The clerk almost smiled. Almost. It was clearly against regulations, and he retreated behind his desk to recover, and Isandro surrendered her travel papers, declined help with Turnip — "she bites clerks, it's nothing personal, it's practically a vocation" — and went out into the square to do what she always did first in any city she'd been ordered to treat as a route rather than a place.

She walked it.

This was technically against procedure. Procedure held that a courier arriving with sealed dispatches should minimize exposure, vary her patterns, and cultivate the social footprint of a piece of furniture. But Isandro had learned, over fifteen years and too many cities, that you could not carry a thing safely through a place you refused to know. A route was a map. A city was a hundred thousand decisions being made daily about whether to keep going, and if your errand touched any of those decisions — and hers always did, eventually, in ways the seals never admitted on the envelope — then you owed the city at least one honest walk.

So she walked.

She walked past the chandler's, where candles were being rationed two to a household and yet someone had bought, that very morning, an entire case of them, and the chandler's wife was wrapping the case with the particular tenderness of a woman who knows exactly what the candles are for and approves of it. A funeral, probably. Or a wedding. In a siege town, Isandro reflected, the candle arithmetic came out the same: someone deciding the dark should not win a round.

She walked past the fountain at the triangle's blunt end, dry now, its basin full of sand and children — four of them, conducting a war with wooden swords and elaborate rules that changed whenever losing threatened. A small boy with a mop of white-blond hair declared himself "the Republic" and immediately began losing. His sister, playing "the East," showed him no mercy whatsoever, which Isandro found bracingly honest.

"You can surrender," the sister advised, magnanimous, sword at his throat. "Surrendering is allowed. My teacher says even generals do it."

"They *retreat strategically*," the boy said, with the precise intonation of a phrase overheard from adults and deployed like a shield.

"That means losing but longer," his sister said, and knocked the wooden sword from his hand, and the Republic of Vellamare, represented here by a boy with a cowlick, sat down in the sand and began negotiating terms with impressive speed.

Isandro stood there a moment too long.

*Two pounds, four ounces.* The thought arrived uninvited, wearing its usual expression. She looked at the children in the dry fountain, at the candle shop, at the mended shutters and the defiant thatch and the bread smell rolling down Cordwainer Row like a promise the town kept making to itself each dawn. Whatever the consul asked of her at dinner — and consuls always asked, the asking just wore nicer clothes — the answer was supposed to be yes, sealed and stamped and carried without question. Fifteen years of yes. Her whole spine had been built out of it.

But she had done the arithmetic on the ridge, and she'd done it again here in the square, and the numbers kept coming out wrong. Not the loaf arithmetic — that was simple grief, and grief she knew how to carry. This other sum. The one where she took the weight against her ribs and tried to imagine it landing anywhere in this town, in any form, and could not make the equation balance.

"You're staring," said a voice at her elbow.

She turned. An elderly woman had come up beside her with a market basket and the unerring radar of the elderly for anyone standing still in public. Up close, Isandro saw the woman's basket held turnip greens, a heel of black bread, and a single egg, cradled in straw like an heirloom.

"My apologies," Isandro said. "I'm new to the city."

"Mm. Courier, are you? We get them through sometimes." The woman peered at her with frank appraisal. "You've got the look. Thin, tired, and guilty about something you haven't done yet."

Isandro blinked. "That's remarkably accurate."

"It's not a gift, dear, it's experience. Every courier who comes through looks like that eventually." The woman shifted her basket to her hip. "The ones who don't are the ones you worry about." She nodded toward the fountain, where the Republic had negotiated a favorable ceasefire and was demanding a rematch. "My granddaughter. The one with no mercy."

"She fights well."

"She fights *first*. Judgment comes later, if we're lucky." The woman looked at Isandro a moment longer, and her eyes — quick, dark, entirely unsentimental — did something that felt less like looking and more like counting. "You'll be at the consulate, then. Having dinner with the Consul."

"News travels fast."

"In a town this size, dear, news travels at the speed of boredom." She adjusted her basket, turned to go, then paused. "Eat what they give you," she said, over her shoulder. "And when they ask you questions at that table — and they will, they're very fond of questions there — remember that some answers cost more than others. Some cost the whole city."

She walked off down Cordwainer Row before Isandro could decide whether she'd been warned, threatened, or merely offered the local brand of hospitality.

* * *

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of weak cider, and Isandro stood at her window in the consulate guest room, having failed, for two hours, to nap.

Below, the square was emptying. Lamplighters were making their rounds — actual lamplighters, with poles, an occupation she'd thought extinct everywhere but the capital's oldest quarters. One worked his way along the triangle's edge, and where his light touched, the shuttered windows seemed to lean toward it, the whole square tilting gently toward warmth like plants toward sun.

Turnip, stabled below, was complaining to someone about dinner portions. From Cordwainer Row, faintly, the ovens were being banked for the night, and the smell of tomorrow's first bread was already settling into the stones, patient, certain, absurd.

*Two pounds, four ounces.*

Isandro laid the dispatch case on the table. She did not touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a sleeping animal you have known all its life and have recently begun, uneasily, to wonder about.

Tomorrow she would dine with Consul Beretha Hale. Tomorrow there would be questions dressed as courtesy, and courtesy dressed as pressure, and at the end of it her orders would pass from her keeping into the machinery of this town, and whatever they were — terms, timetables, names — they would stop being hers to carry and become everyone's to live with.

Unless.

She didn't finish the thought. Fifteen years of discipline closed on it like a hand. But it sat there anyway, unfinished thoughts being the only cargo that never needed sealing, and she slept badly, and dreamed of bread.

She woke once, deep in the night, to a sound below — the consulate door, opening and closing softly, and footsteps in the entry hall that were too light for a soldier and too deliberate for a cat.

Isandro lay in the dark, listening, one hand already finding the edge of the blanket, and told herself it was the clerk, the watch, the wind.

The footsteps stopped on the stairs.

They did not continue up. They stopped, exactly between floors, as though whoever climbed them was waiting — for courage, or for permission, or simply for the building to decide whether it meant to let them in.

Isandro sat up slowly in the dark, heart tapping out its old courier's rhythm — *weight, wax, urgency* — and waited to see what the night had chosen to deliver to her door.
