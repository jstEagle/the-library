## 3. A Child at the Door

The knock came at the worst possible hour, which Isandro would later understand was the only hour it could have come.

She had been awake anyway. The consulate's guest room had a bed that creaked like a ship in weather and a window that overlooked the inner courtyard, where the sentries changed watch every two hours with a ritual of coughs and lantern-shading that no one had thought to make quiet. Beyond the walls, Lantern's Reach lay under its curfew, and beyond the city, somewhere in the dark hills, an army she was not permitted to think about too precisely was doing whatever armies did at night — sharpening, counting, waiting.

So she was awake when she heard it: not a knock, really. More of a tapping. Small, arrhythmic, apologetic. The sound a bird makes against glass, if birds could hesitate.

Isandro sat up in the dark and listened the way couriers listen — with her whole body, cataloguing. Wind through the courtyard fig. A sentry's boots on gravel, moving away from her wing, toward the gate. And then the tapping again, three soft knocks at the service door below, followed by a silence so deliberate it had to be intentional. Whoever it was knocked, then waited as though they'd been told exactly how long to wait and what would happen next.

Nobody knocked on a consulate door at midnight by accident. Especially not this consulate, in this city, on this particular week.

She dressed quickly, pulling on her courier's grey over the shift she'd slept in, and took the stairs down past the sealed records room, past the map room with its shuttered lamp, her bare feet silent on stone worn smooth by forty years of nervous diplomats. The housekeeper, Marta, kept a cudgel behind the kitchen door, and Isandro considered borrowing it. Then she remembered that anyone dangerous enough to threaten a Vellamarine consulate would hardly announce themselves with the gentleness of a moth.

The service door had a viewing slot, iron-shuttered. She slid it open.

A child stood on the step.

* * *

She was very small — six years old, perhaps seven, though Isandro's experience with children began and ended with a cousin's twins she'd met twice — and she stood the way soldiers stand when they've been told to stand somewhere, feet together, chin up, as if the instruction might be rescinded at any moment and she wanted to be ready. She wore a coat far too big for her, wool, good quality once, now stained with mud to the elbows. Her hair had been braided with great care and then rained upon with less care. In one mittened hand she clutched a folded piece of paper as though it were the last warm thing in Vellamare.

Her eyes went straight to the slot. Straight to Isandro. Children always found faces in dark windows faster than adults did; adults looked for doors.

"Are you the lady?" the child asked. Her voice carried the flat vowels of the northern provinces, softened slightly, the way accents soften in children who have been moved around.

"I'm a lady," Isandro said carefully. "There are several in this building."

"The lady who carries things." The girl held up the paper. "He said you'd be the one who opens things."

The wind came off the hills then, cutting through the slot and carrying with it the smell of woodsmoke and cold stone and, faintly, from the direction of the siege lines, something scorched. Isandro looked at the child — at the mud on the coat, at the careful braids, at the paper held out with a steadiness that had been drilled into her or beaten into her, Isandro couldn't yet tell which — and felt the first cold drop of recognition slide down her spine.

Because the coat fit someone else. Because the braids were the work of a father's hands, patient and unpracticed. Because nobody abandoned a child at a consulate door at midnight unless every other door had been tried, burned, or watched.

"Who is *he*?" Isandro asked, though some traitor part of her already knew.

The girl considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate. "Papa said I shouldn't say his name out loud anymore. He said names are like matches right now." She frowned. "That's a funny thing to say. Matches don't have names."

"No," Isandro agreed faintly. "They don't."

"He said to give this to the courier lady and say—" The girl closed her eyes briefly, reciting. "—say *forget the face*. That's all. Forget the face." She opened her eyes again. "I don't know what face."

Through the iron slot, Isandro took the note. The girl released it reluctantly, fingers lingering, and Isandro understood that the paper wasn't the treasure; the handing-over was. The mission. The thing she'd been told to do, performed correctly, in the mud, at midnight, six years old.

"Wait there," Isandro said. "Don't move. Don't let anyone see you. Can you hide yourself? Behind the rain barrel?"

The girl nodded once, crisp as a salute, and stepped back into the shadows beside the barrel with the ease of someone who had recently become very good at small spaces. Something about the economy of the movement made Isandro's chest hurt.

She unfolded the note by the light of the passage lamp.

Three words. *Forget the face.*

And beneath them, nothing — no signature, no seal — but the handwriting itself, and that was signature enough. Isandro knew handwriting the way musicians know voices. It was her trade. She had spent twenty years reading the character of strangers through their penmanship: the consul's cramped ambition, Sef's showy flourishes, the Republic Chancellery's anonymous, machine-even script that meant a hundred clerks had touched a document before it reached her hands.

This hand she would have known blindfolded, upside down, in ash.

*Forget the face,* written in the strong, slanted, slightly impatient script of Aldric Fen.

The world tilted gently, the way it does in the moment before a fall you haven't admitted to yet.

* * *

She made herself move. That was the discipline, the one thing her training had actually given her that mattered: the body obeys while the mind catches up. She unbolted the service door, drew the child inside — a quick look left and right along the empty courtyard first, sentry still at the far wall — and shut the world out behind them.

In the kitchen's banked firelight, she got her first real look at the girl. Thin. Too thin for a general's daughter, which meant rationing, which meant flight, which meant weeks of it. There was a scrape healing on her cheekbone. Her boots were two sizes wrong. But she stood straight, and she watched Isandro with those steady eyes, and she did not fidget, and Isandro thought: *she has been practicing being brave, and she has gotten frighteningly good at it.*

"What's your name?" Isandro asked, kneeling to bring herself level. Kneeling was in the manuals, oddly enough. *Meet sources at their height.* She had never imagined applying it here.

"Maren," the girl said. Then, after a visible internal struggle: "Maren Fen."

She said the name defiantly, chin up, daring Isandro to flinch. Which meant she knew exactly what the name meant now. Which meant she'd heard the whispers, the circulars, the word *traitor* spoken in kitchens and doorways by adults who forgot children collected words like coins.

"You're not scared of me?" Isandro asked.

"Should I be?"

"Most people are, a little. I carry sealed orders. Sealed things frighten people."

Maren considered this. "Papa says seals are just wax pretending to be important."

Despite everything — despite the hour, the siege, the note burning a hole in her pocket — Isandro laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, a short, startled sound, and Maren smiled with such sudden, total delight that Isandro understood the smile was rare currency, spent only when earned.

"Papa," Isandro said quietly. "Your papa told you to find me."

"To find the courier lady at the stone house with the lanterns by the gate. He drew it." She rummaged in her enormous coat and produced a scrap of paper, a charcoal sketch of the consulate's facade, accurate down to the cracked third step. "He said you'd open the door even if it was late. He said you're the kind of person who opens doors."

Isandro looked at the drawing. Looked at the note in her hand, the three words in a dead man's living handwriting. *Forget the face.* Aldric Fen, declared traitor four months ago, his name struck from the rolls, his estates seized, his portrait — she'd seen it, everyone had seen it — nailed to garrison walls across three provinces with a bounty printed beneath it large enough to buy a farm.

The man who had pulled her out of the frozen Arvelin River eleven years ago, during the peace talks at Coldmere, when she'd taken a shortcut across ice that a courier's confidence had no business taking, and gone through it into water so cold it stopped being water and became a fist. He'd been there — why had he been there? walking, he'd said later, because he liked rivers better than conference rooms — and he had gone in after her without hesitating, without even putting down his pipe, and hauled her out, and wrapped her in his own coat, and walked her back to the talks steaming like a kettle, laughing at her fury, saying, *"Well, courier, that's the fastest anyone's ever delivered themselves to me."*

They had talked for three nights afterward, beside the inn's fire, about nothing — maps, poetry, whether rivers or roads told the truth about a country. On the last morning he'd shaken her hand and said something she had filed away for eleven years in the drawer where she kept things too heavy to carry daily:

*"One day your republic will ask you to carry something you can't put down again. When that day comes, remember the river. Remember that some deliveries are worth drowning for, and some are worth refusing."*

She had smiled and called him a philosopher and a bad influence, and she had never forgotten a word.

Now his daughter stood in a consulate kitchen smelling of yesterday's bread, and his handwriting sat in her palm like a debt come due, and outside, beyond the walls, his name was worth more money than Isandro would earn in ten honest years.

* * *

Practicalities first. Training again — body obeying, mind catching up. Food: there was half a loaf and hard cheese in the cold box, and Maren ate with the terrible politeness of a hungry child pretending not to be hungry, and Isandro cut more cheese than any child needed and set it within reach without comment. Milk, warmed. Questions, none yet. Marta would rise at four; the household staff at five; Consul Hale rose before dawn as a point of pride and principle. Isandro had perhaps five hours to decide what this child was, officially, and where she had come from, and whose problem she became at sunrise.

Unofficially, of course, the decision had already been made. It had been made the moment she opened the slot and saw the small soldier standing at attention in the cold. Some decisions are just paperwork.

"Where's your papa now?" Isandro asked gently.

Maren's jaw tightened. For a moment Isandro saw the whole architecture of the child's recent history pass behind her eyes — and how much of it she was choosing not to say. "He said he'd find me. He always finds me. We have a game." Her voice wobbled once, precisely once, and then steadied. "He said if I ever got lost, I should go to the person who pulled me out of the river."

The kitchen went very quiet. The banked fire ticked.

"He told you about that?" Isandro managed.

"He tells that story all the time." Maren rolled her eyes with devastating accuracy. "*And then the courier lady came up out of the hole in the river swearing in four languages—*"

"In three languages," Isandro said automatically. "The fourth doesn't count, it's mostly gestures."

Maren grinned, and for a heartbeat the kitchen was warm in a way that had nothing to do with fire, and Isandro thought: *he sent me his daughter. Not to a friend — he has no friends left, that's rather the definition of traitor — but to me. To a stranger he met once, eleven years ago, because of a hole in a river.* 

And underneath that thought, colder, sharper: *which means he trusts no one else alive. Which means he believes I can keep her safe when armies cannot. Which means he knows what's coming to this city.*

Which meant he knew what was in the dispatch tube strapped beneath her coat, the sealed orders she had carried across four hundred miles without knowing their contents, weighing them each night by habit — weight, wax, urgency — like a midwife assessing a pregnancy.

The wax on those orders had been black. She had told herself it meant nothing. Black wax was used for matters of state. Black wax was used for war.

Black wax was also used, she now recalled, for condemnations.

"Forget the face," she murmured, turning the note toward the firelight. Whose face? A guard who'd waved them through? A servant who'd helped? Or — the thought arrived slowly, dreadfully — her own? *Forget the face of the woman you met at the river, forget our friendship, forget that this favor means anything, so that when they question you, you'll be clean.*

Or worse, simpler: the face on the circulars. *Forget my face. Don't go looking for a traitor. Just take the child.*

Above them, a floorboard creaked. Both of them froze — Isandro reaching instinctively for a courier's calm she no longer possessed, Maren reaching for the shadow behind the flour barrels with a speed that broke Isandro's heart cleanly in half.

The board settled. Only the old house settling, groaning at the cold, as houses do. But Maren stayed hidden a moment longer, small and silent among the sacks, and Isandro looked at her crouched there in the dark — looking, despite everything, entirely at home in hiding places — and understood with perfect clarity that the past had not merely arrived tonight.

It had arrived demanding payment. And it had sent a six-year-old collector, because the past knew perfectly well that Isandro Voss could refuse anything in the world except a promise made beside a frozen river to a man holding out his coat.

She crossed the kitchen, knelt by the barrels, and held out her hand.

"It's all right," she said. "You can come out. From tonight on, this kitchen is yours."

Maren took her hand. Her grip was small and fierce and certain, and Isandro thought, with the strange floating clarity of a woman stepping off a ledge she's been circling for days: *Well, courier. Here is the delivery you cannot put down.*

Somewhere above them, in the direction of the consul's rooms, a candle flared to life behind a shuttered window. Consul Beretha Hale was awake early — or had never slept — and dawn, with all its questions, was coming up over the hills like a bill collector.

Isandro had until sunrise to invent a lie worthy of the truth.

It would be the first lie of her career. Looking down at Maren's trusting face, she suspected — correctly, as it would turn out — that it would not be the last.
