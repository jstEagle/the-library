## 4. Forget the Face

The girl did not cry.

Isandro had braced for crying — had, in the space between reading the note and lifting the child over the threshold, rehearsed an entire strategy for crying, which was to say she had planned to stand very still and look helpless until a neighbor's sympathy drove the spectacle indoors. But Maren Voss-Fen — no, just Fen, surely, though the surname on her tongue felt like a confession — simply walked into the consulate's back corridor as if she had been expected, set down her small bundle with both hands, careful as a courier setting down a dispatch, and looked up at Isandro with eyes that were already taking inventory of exits.

"Are you the lady from Papa's letters?" she asked.

"I don't know what letters."

"The ones he reads out loud sometimes. When he thinks I'm asleep." She considered this, then added, with the merciless precision of six-year-olds everywhere, "He does voices. Yours has a limp in it."

"A limp," said Isandro faintly.

"He makes your voice go *slowly, General, slowly*, like it's walking uphill." Maren demonstrated. It was uncanny. It was also, Isandro reflected, exactly how she spoke when cornered by generals, which meant Aldric Fen had been listening to her argue for years, or imagining it, or — worse — remembering it.

The corridor was cold. The consulate's heating had been rationed down to a grumble, and the oil lamps burned low out of the same arithmetic. Somewhere above them, Consul Hale's clerks scratched at their ledgers, keeping the republic's dying accounts. Isandro looked at the child, at the note crushed in her own fist, and understood that she was standing at the exact center of the largest decision of her life, and that the decision would not announce itself as one. It would only feel like a sequence of small practical things.

Hide the child. First small thing.

* * *

She put Maren in the linen room off the kitchen — not because it was comfortable but because it was the one room in the consulate nobody entered after sundown unless something was on fire. She brought bread, cheese gone waxy at the edges, a cup of watered wine that Maren drank with the gravity of a woman sampling a suspect vintage.

"It's not very good," Maren reported.

"No. It isn't."

"Papa says wine is like weather. You don't complain about it, you just describe it." A pause. "He complains about weather all the time, though. So."

So. Isandro sat down on a crate of table linens and studied the girl while pretending to study the door. Pale hair, cut short and unevenly — someone had done it in haste, with a knife, probably by firelight. A scab on one knee in the shape of a small country. Boots two sizes too big, stuffed at the toes with what appeared to be moss. Someone had loved this child competently and recently and in a great hurry.

"Maren. Do you know where your papa is?"

"Gone." The word arrived without drama, the way children deliver enormous facts. "He said *run to the consulate, find the courier, forget the face.*"

"Forget the face," Isandro repeated. "Whose face?"

Maren looked at her with mild pity. "Yours, silly. He drew it for me. On the boat." She dug into her bundle — past a folded nightshirt, past a wooden horse missing an ear — and produced a scrap of paper creased into eighths. On it, in Fen's unmistakable hand, was a sketch: a woman with sharp cheekbones and a mouth set like a closed ledger, and beneath it, in letters pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *SHE WILL ARGUE. LET HER. THEN DO WHAT SHE SAYS.*

Isandro laughed. It came out wrong, half a breath, nothing anyone could call a laugh.

Because she knew that hand. Of course she knew that hand. She knew the way the *g* descended like a man stepping off a ledge, the way the crossbars slanted slightly downhill, left to right, as if even his handwriting were walking toward something. For three years of fragile, improbable peace, she had watched that hand annotate treaty margins, sketch caricatures of her superiors, and once — memorably — draw her asleep in a chair at the Kellin talks, drooling slightly, captioned *THE REPUBLIC'S FINEST.*

Aldric Fen. General. Hero of the Vellamare border wars. And now, per every circular nailed to every post between here and the capital, traitor.

"You know my papa," Maren said. Not a question. Children could smell recognition the way dogs smelled fear.

"I knew him," Isandro said carefully. "Before the war came back."

"Did you like him?"

"Yes."

"Then why'd you make the slow-voice face?"

Isandro made the slow-voice face again before she could stop herself. Maren nodded, satisfied, as if a hypothesis had been confirmed, and ate her cheese.

* * *

That night, in her narrow room under the eaves, Isandro did not sleep. She sat by the window with the sealed orders on the desk in front of her — heavy paper, black wax, the republic's sunburst stamped deep — and she interrogated herself with the thoroughness she applied to suspicious cargo.

Question the first: Why send the child here?

Answer: Because no soldier searches a courier's baggage for anything but paper. Because the consulate was the one building in Lantern's Reach still flying a neutral-ish flag. Because Fen knew — he had always known — the geography of her habits better than she did herself. During the Kellin talks he'd once told her, apropos of her complaining about her route assignments: "You think of yourself as a straight line between points, Voss. But I've watched you walk, and you always take the long way past water. Remember that about yourself. It'll matter someday."

She had laughed then. Water. What nonsense. And now here she was, stationed in a city built around a river, holding a child who had crossed one in the dark to reach her.

Question the second: What does he want?

Not rescue — if Fen wanted extraction for himself he would not have spent his daughter like a coin, however lovingly minted. Not asylum — a wanted general's daughter was a liability any consulate would hand back within the day, and Fen knew that too. No. He wanted custody. He wanted the girl beyond reach of whatever was coming, and he had chosen Isandro not despite the risk but because of it: because a courier's oath made her incorruptible, and because — this was the part that sat in Isandro's chest like swallowed ice — because he trusted her more than he trusted her own government, and had bet the child on it.

*Forget the face.* She turned the phrase over. At first read: erase the girl's identity, keep her anonymous, keep her safe. But there was a second reading, and it was aimed at her. Forget the face — the face at Kellin, laughing at his terrible jokes across a negotiating table. Forget the man who pulled you out of the river. Be the courier, not the friend. He is asking me to bury him alive.

Or he was asking her to remember so hard she couldn't obey.

Fen had never been able to leave a sentence with only one door in it. It was why he'd been a good negotiator and, she supposed, why someone had eventually decided he was a traitor.

* * *

The river, four years ago.

Kellin, late autumn, the talks going badly in the way talks go badly when everyone at the table has buried someone on the other side of it. Isandro had gone out at dawn to walk the frozen millpond behind the guesthouse — her long way past water — testing the ice with her heel like an idiot, like a person who had grown up in a southern city and believed ice was a rumor. It held for three steps and then, with a sound like a door closing, didn't.

The cold was total. It wasn't pain; pain would come later. It was a white certainty that the world above the surface had been a dream and this was the true condition of things. She remembered thinking, absurdly, *the dispatches will be late,* and then a shadow broke the sky, and then hands.

Fen had gone through the ice deliberately, flat on his belly, distributing his weight like a man who had grown up on northern ponds, and had caught her collar and hauled her toward the broken edge, talking the entire time in a low, steady voice, nonsense mostly — "up we come, there's a good courier, mind the edge, kick, yes, *kick*" — as though she were a horse being gentled. He dragged her out, stripped his own coat onto her shaking body, and marched her back to the guesthouse where he bullied the kitchen staff into a bathtub of hot water and stood guard outside the door so no superior officer could fuss at her before she stopped shivering.

"You nearly died for a walk," he said through the door.

"I take the long way past water," she said, teeth chattering. "You told me to remember that."

"That was before I knew you'd test the ice with your *heel.*"

Later, dried and wrapped in blankets and deeply humiliated, she had tried to thank him properly and he'd waved it off. "You'll repay it. Everyone repays everything eventually; it's the only law I believe in." Then, quieter, in the tone he used when he was about to say the true thing: "Listen. You serve a republic that's running out of itself. I've read its supply ledgers; I sign the other side of some of them. Men like us are kept because we're useful, and used up because we're honest. One day it will ask you for something unforgivable, and it will ask in your own handwriting — your forms, your routes, your seal — so it won't feel like a request. It'll feel like a Tuesday. When that day comes—"

"When that day comes, what?"

He'd smiled at her, that crooked smile that made the other delegation's aides distrust him on principle. "I haven't decided whether to tell you to obey or refuse. I'll decide when I see what they're asking. But I'll tell you this for free: the unforgivable thing always arrives sealed, and it always arrives on schedule."

She had told him he was being dramatic. He had agreed cheerfully. They had drunk bad wine and described it instead of complaining.

On schedule, she thought now, in the dark of the consulate, looking at the black wax. On schedule.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin to Lantern's Reach. Isandro went down early, before the clerks, and found Maren awake, sitting cross-legged on the linen crate, having apparently organized the entire room's contents by some private taxonomy known only to six-year-olds and archangels.

"Good morning," said Maren. "I hid your hairbrush. You have to find it."

"Why?"

"Practice. Papa says if you can hide a thing once you can hide it twice, and if you can find a thing once—" She shrugged, a small, complete philosophy. "I'm practicing being hidden."

Something in Isandro's chest cracked — not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks, a long white line running out from under a heel. She crouched down to the girl's level.

"Maren. Listen to me. From today, if anyone asks, you are my niece. Your name is Mara. You came from Corvess, your mother is dead, and you're waiting on papers. Can you hold all that?"

Maren's face went serious, and in that seriousness Isandro saw the general's daughter whole — the child who had run from a burning farm and crossed a river and walked up to a stranger's door because a note told her to. "Niece," Maren repeated. "Like in the letters? The ones with the limp voice?"

"...Yes."

"Then you have to do the voice right." And Maren pointed a small finger at Isandro and commanded, with terrifying gentleness: "*Slowly, General, slowly.*"

Down the corridor, a bell rang for the morning meal, and footsteps approached — brisk ones, familiar ones, the consul's secretary with her clipboard and her questions. Isandro straightened, smoothed her coat, and felt the crack widen by one degree.

The first lie of her career was scheduled for dinner. But the second one started now.

"There you are," said the secretary, appearing in the doorway, eyes flicking from Isandro to the child and back. "Consul Hale would like a word before the morning post. She's asking after your orders."

Behind her, small and silent, Maren slid off the crate and vanished among the hanging linens — hidden once, therefore hidden twice — leaving Isandro alone in the doorway with the truth in her pocket and the whole long day coming down the hall.
