## 5. The Consul's Table

The consulate of Lantern's Reach occupied what had once been a merchant's pleasure house, back in the decades when the border had been less a wound and more a seam. Its dining room still bore the marks of that gentler ambition: a ceiling painted with clouds gone gray as dishwater, tall windows fitted now with shutters that could be barred in four seconds, and a long table of dark walnut that had seen better centuries.

Isandro arrived at the seventh bell, washed and changed into her formal courier grays, and found Consul Beretha Hale already seated at the table's head, reading dispatches by candlelight and eating bread with the efficiency of a woman who considered chewing an administrative task.

"Courier Voss." Hale did not look up. "You found the city intact."

"I found it loud, madam. Intact is a generous word."

"Loud means alive." The consul set down her papers at last and studied Isandro across the candle flames — a study Isandro felt like a customs inspection. Hale was perhaps fifty-five, built like a door, with iron-gray hair cropped short and hands that looked as though they had personally strangled at least one treaty. "Sit. The soup is terrible. Eat it anyway."

Isandro sat. The soup was indeed terrible — some root vegetable boiled past all dignity — but it was hot, and hot food in a besieged city was a kind of luxury she had learned never to insult.

"You delivered the sealed orders to my office this afternoon," Hale said. "Unopened."

"That is the custom."

"The custom," Hale repeated, as though tasting the word for spoilage. She tore a piece of bread in half with unnecessary violence. "You'll forgive me, Courier Voss, but I have been served by couriers for twenty-two years, and I can tell you that the good ones are always curious. Curiosity is how they stay alive on bad roads. So when one arrives who shows no curiosity at all—" she gestured with the bread, "—I begin to wonder whether she is very disciplined or very frightened."

"Perhaps I am simply tired, madam."

"Tired people eat their soup. You've eaten exactly three spoonfuls and arranged the rest into a small landscape." Hale's mouth did something that on a kinder face might have been called a smile. "I am not your enemy, Courier. I am asking you to dine with me because in eleven days, if the republic's arithmetic holds, we may all be dead together, and I prefer to know whom I'm dying beside."

It was, Isandro had to admit, a disarming way to conduct an interrogation: confess to it openly and call it companionship. She ate another spoonful of soup, mostly to buy time.

"The orders are not mine to discuss," she said carefully. "You will receive them under seal, at the appointed hour, per protocol."

"Protocol." Hale set down her spoon. "Do you know what I was, before the war made me a consul?"

"No, madam."

"A schoolteacher. In Marrowfield, which you will not have heard of because it no longer exists. I taught arithmetic to children who could count on one hand the number of winters they'd survive." She said it without drama, the way one reports weather. "So forgive me if protocol sounds to me like a lullaby sung to keep the children quiet while the adults decide something dreadful."

*While the adults decide something dreadful.* Isandro kept her face pleasant and her pulse steady, and thought: *she knows. Or she suspects. Or she is fishing.* All three were possible; only one was survivable to confirm.

"I am a courier, madam," Isandro said. "I carry. I do not decide."

"Everyone decides," said Hale. "They just don't all sign their decisions."

A servant came then with the second course — salted fish, which in Lantern's Reach passed for optimism — and for a few minutes there was nothing between them but cutlery and the tick of the case clock in the hall. Isandro used the interval to take inventory of herself, as she would inventory a satchel before a hard crossing. What did Hale know? What could she see? Had Sef Andrel already whispered something over his wine?

Sef. She had left him an hour ago in the map room, being charming and useful, volunteering for extra rounds, offering to copy route schedules in his beautiful clerk's hand. He had watched her with those pale, patient eyes of his, the eyes of a man who collected other people's hesitations the way some men collected coins.

"He eats quickly, your deputy," Hale remarked, as if following her thoughts down the same corridor. "Andrel. Quick at everything, from what I observe. Quick to volunteer. Quick with flattery. Quick to agree."

"He is ambitious, madam. Ambition makes people quick."

"Ambition makes people *useful*, until the day it doesn't." Hale wiped her mouth. "You, though. You're slow. Deliberate. You weigh things." Her eyes came up sharp. "What are you weighing, Courier Voss?"

*Careful,* said the old voice of training in her head. *Careful, careful.*

"The roads," Isandro said. "The eastern pass is watched. If I'm to run messages out during negotiations, I'll want to know which checkpoints change hands at night. That's what I weigh. Weight, wax, and roads. It's the whole of my religion."

"Hm." Hale turned her wineglass slowly by its stem. "Then let me trouble your religion with a rumor. You've heard the name Fen."

Not a question. A stone dropped into still water, and Isandro watched the ripples spread through her own chest while her face, thank every god who still took calls, remained a wall.

"Fen," she repeated. The name sat in her mouth like a coin she was trying not to bite.

"General Aldric Fen. Formerly the Republic's finest argument for itself. Now, according to the bulletins from the capital, its most famous traitor." Hale's tone was flat, official, recitation-shaped — but her eyes stayed on Isandro the whole time, the way a card player watches the other player's hands rather than the cards. "He commanded the Third Border Legion until last spring. Then he burned his own supply trains, walked two thousand men off the rolls, and vanished into the hill country. They say he's been selling our positions to the Karvathi ever since. They say he has a family somewhere that he abandoned to do it. They say a great many things."

"People usually do, about traitors," Isandro said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Madam, may I ask why you tell me this?"

"Because you crossed the eastern pass alone, three days ago, through country where General Fen's partisans are known to operate. And you arrived without a scratch, without a stolen seal, and without — so far as I can tell — having been robbed of so much as a button." Hale leaned back. "Either you are extraordinarily lucky, Courier Voss, or somebody on those roads decided you were not to be touched. And I find myself wondering which."

*The checkpoint. The captain who had waved her through too easily, who had looked at her travel writ and then at her face with something like recognition and said, "Safe roads, courier," in a tone that hadn't sounded like a wish at all.* She had filed it away as fatigue. Now it reassembled itself into something else entirely, and cold walked up her spine in stockinged feet.

"I was fortunate," she said. "Or unremarkable. Bandits rarely rob poor men, and couriers carry paper, not gold."

"Mm." Hale let the silence stretch, testing its weight like a rope bridge. Then, abruptly, she smiled — a real one, tired and crooked, and it transformed her whole face into something almost gentle. "You lie well, Courier Voss. Better than Andrel, who lies badly and knows it. But you breathe wrong when you do it. Just slightly. A little pause before the sentence, like a woman stepping over a threshold in the dark."

Isandro's stomach dropped through the floorboards of her composure, down past the cellar, into whatever lay beneath cellars.

"Madam—"

"Don't." Hale raised one hand, and the gentleness in her face folded away again, put back in its drawer. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I am telling you that I was a schoolteacher for nineteen years, and children lie to teachers constantly, and the ones who frighten me are never the bad liars. The bad liars I can catch. It's the good liars who worry me, because they've usually got a reason worth lying well for." She refilled both their glasses, hers half, Isandro's nearly to the brim — a small courtesy, or a small test. "Drink. You've gone pale."

Isandro drank. The wine was rough and dark and tasted faintly of smoke, like everything else in Lantern's Reach.

"Now," Hale went on, conversational again, as though they had been discussing fish, "let me tell you what I know, since you've been so scrupulous about telling me nothing. In nine days, a delegation arrives from the capital to negotiate terms with the Karvathi envoys. My job is to keep this city quiet, fed, and standing until they do. To that end I have received — today, via your excellent self — sealed instructions whose contents I am apparently not to know until the delegation arrives, because the Ministry of Ways and Means believes that knowledge is a leakable substance." Her lip curled. "In twenty-two years of service, I have never once received orders I wasn't permitted to read. Have you?"

"No," Isandro said. And it was true, and the truth of it rang in the room like a struck bell.

"No," Hale agreed. "Neither have I. Which means either the capital has developed a sudden passion for mystery, or these orders contain something the capital does not trust its own consul to see." She looked at Isandro over the rim of her glass. "You carried them. You held them against your body for six days. Did they feel like terms of peace to you, Courier? Because I have held peace treaties before, in duplicate, and they have a certain heft. These feel thin. Thin like a list."

*A list.* Isandro kept her breathing even through sheer professional spite. "Paper is paper, madam. I couldn't say."

"No. Of course not." Hale sighed, and for a moment — only a moment — she looked less like a door and more like what she had been underneath all along: a schoolteacher in a burning schoolhouse, doing sums with children's lives. "Well. We shall all learn together, won't we. Nine days." She stood, gathering her dispatches. "You'll dine with me each evening while you're stationed here. Consider it an order. Consider it, if you like, a courtesy. I find that at the end of things, one wants to know the faces around the table."

She paused at the doorway, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"One more thing, Courier Voss. The circulars went up this morning. There's a child missing in the hills — a girl of six, daughter of the traitor Fen. The Karvathi want her. The republic wants her more, as leverage, presumably, should the general prove difficult." Hale's voice stayed perfectly level. "If such a child were to appear anywhere near this consulate, protocol would require me to report her within the hour. Do you understand me?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a shutter banged in the wind off the walls.

"Perfectly, madam," Isandro said.

"Good." Hale glanced back, just far enough to show one iron eye. "Sleep well, Courier."

* *

She did not sleep well.

She lay on the narrow cot in the courier's quarters above the stables and listened to the city breathe: the watch bells, the wind, the distant argument of dogs. Above her, on a shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, the sealed orders sat in her satchel like a sleeping animal she was afraid to check on.

*Thin like a list.*

Hale knew. Not everything — maybe not even much — but enough. Enough that every hour Maren spent hidden in the linen room behind the third-floor pantry was an hour borrowed against a debt that compounded. *If such a child were to appear anywhere near this consulate, protocol would require me to report her within the hour.*

Within the hour. Not immediately. Not *at once*. Within the hour.

Isandro stared at the ceiling beams and turned the phrase over like a coin of doubtful minting, looking for the mark that would tell her whether it was true currency or a forgery. Was it a warning shaped as a threat? A threat shaped as a warning? Or simply a bureaucrat stating regulations to a subordinate, meaning nothing by it except the sound of her own correctness?

And beneath that question, quieter and colder, swam the other one — the one she had been refusing to look at directly since the seventh bell:

*You lied to her.*

Not a small lie. Not a courier's necessary evasions about routes and seals. She had sat across a candlelit table from a woman who had taught arithmetic in a town that no longer existed, a woman feeding her terrible soup out of her own short rations, and Isandro had lied to her face and done it *well*, and Hale had told her so, complimented her on it, the way one compliments a fencer on form.

Her hands, she noticed, were shaking. Not much. Just enough to be honest.

All her life the work had been clean: carry, deliver, do not open, do not bend, do not read. The seal was the seal. Trust was a chain of wax impressions running from the capital to wherever the road ended, and she had been a link in it, and links don't have to know what the chain is hauling. That was the mercy of the work, the thing that had let her sleep in a hundred bad beds in a hundred worse towns: *it isn't yours to decide.*

But the chain, she understood now, lying in the dark with her heart going too fast, had never been neutral. Chains haul something, always, in one direction or another. And she had felt the weight of this consignment against her ribs for six days and called it discipline, and it had not been discipline. It had been dread, wearing discipline's uniform.

A floorboard creaked in the stable below. She went still, listening. Hooves — the night rider coming in from the east gate, mud and frost and the usual small terror of the walls. Ordinary. She exhaled.

Ordinary, except that tomorrow she would sit across from Beretha Hale again and lie again, and lie better, because practice improves everyone. And the evening after that. And the evening after that. Until the lying stopped being a thing she did and became a thing she was, and some morning she would look in the cracked mirror over the washbasin and be unable to find the seam.

*Curiosity is how they stay alive on bad roads,* Hale had said.

*Yes, madam,* Isandro thought at the ceiling. *And it's curiosity that's going to kill me here.*

Because she wanted to know. That was the rot at the center of her, exposed now, gleaming: she wanted to open the satchel. She wanted it the way the thirsty want water — not as treason, not yet, but as thirst. Six days of carrying a secret addressed to someone else, and her fingers had begun to dream of wax.

She rolled onto her side. Through the shutter slats she could see a slice of the city: rooftops, the glow of a baker's early ovens — they baked at midnight now, before the shelling hours — and beyond it all, black against the stars, the bare ridge where the old orchards ran up the hillside toward the ruined farmhouses.

Somewhere out there, if the circulars were honest, a general played traitor, and a six-year-old girl slept behind a wall of linens three floors above a woman sworn to report her within the hour.

*Forget the face,* the note had said.

Isandro closed her eyes and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only at the worst possible hour, that forgetting was no longer among her options. The face was upstairs. The name was in her mouth. And in nine days, whatever thin, list-shaped horror rode in her satchel would come due, and Beretha Hale would open it at her own table, and Isandro would either be the link in the chain—

—or the place where it broke.

Below, the night rider's horse stamped twice and settled. The baker's ovens glowed on. Isandro Voss, courier of the Republic of Vellamare, liar of one evening and counting, lay awake until the fourth bell, rehearsing tomorrow's lies — and hating, more than she could say, how good at them she was becoming.
