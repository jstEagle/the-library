## 6. What Maren Carries

Maren had counted the doors in the consulate eleven times and gotten a different answer every time, which meant either the house was growing doors or she was bad at counting. She suspected both.

The lady with the grey eyes — Isandro — had told her to stay in this room. *The blue room,* Isandro had called it, though Maren thought it was more of a green room pretending to be blue, like a cat pretending not to want to be petted. There was a bed too big for any person, with a blanket that smelled of cedar and someone else's dreams. There was a window that looked out on a courtyard where men in uniforms walked back and forth, back and forth, like ducks who had somewhere very important to be.

Papa had said *run*. That was the last thing. Not goodbye. Papa didn't say goodbye; he said things like *I'll see you at supper* even when there wasn't going to be supper, because he believed words were seeds and you should only plant good ones. So he had crouched down behind the woodpile while the sky turned orange, and he had held her face in both hands, and he had said, *Run, little wren. Run until running is done.*

She had run. Running was mostly done now, but her legs still remembered it, all tight and buzzy, like bees had moved into her calves and were making honey out of fear.

* * *

The soldiers at the farm had come at dusk, when the light was the color of weak tea. She remembered that part in pieces, the way you remember a dropped plate: not the falling, just the smash.

There had been shouting. There had been Herron the dog barking, and then not barking, and she did not let herself think about why not-barking was worse than barking. Papa had come through the kitchen door with his face doing something she'd never seen it do, and he'd shoved bread into a sack — bread, of all things, as if bread were what mattered — and then he'd knelt by the loose board under the stairs where they hid from thunderstorms, except this time he hid her instead of a storm coming.

*Don't make a sound,* he'd whispered. *Not even a brave sound.*

She hadn't made a single sound. She had been so quiet she'd surprised herself. She'd heard boots on the floorboards above, and a voice asking Papa questions in a flat way, and Papa answering in his talking-to-officials voice, the polite one with edges hidden inside it like knives wrapped in napkins.

Then later — how much later? A night? Two? Time went strange when you were folded up small — Papa had lifted the board away and his face was smudged with smoke and he'd smelled like the bad kind of fire, the kind that eats houses, and he'd given her the note and the name of the city and said *run*.

And she had run, and run, and run, and now here she was in a house full of strangers where the soup came in bowls with gold rims and nobody had told her the rules yet.

That was the worst part, actually. Not the soldiers. Not the fire. The rules. Every house had rules. At home the rules were: shoes off at the door, don't feed Herron from the table no matter how he looked at you, and if Papa started whistling, pretend you hadn't heard anything important. She didn't know the rules here. You could get sent away for breaking rules in a strange house. She knew that from the neighbor's boy, Tomas, who broke a rule once at an aunt's house and got sent home in the rain.

She decided the first rule would be: be small. Be so small nobody remembers you're here. It was a rule she was already good at.

* * *

Isandro came in at some point carrying a tray, and Maren performed being asleep, which she was quite skilled at, having practiced on many mornings. But Isandro sat down on the edge of the enormous bed anyway, and after a while Maren gave up the performance because lying still was boring and also Isandro's coat smelled like road dust and ink, and ink was a smell from home, because Papa wrote letters every evening.

"You're awake," Isandro said.

"No," Maren said.

"Mm." Isandro set the tray down. On it was bread and butter and a cup of milk, and also a small honey cake, the kind sold at fairs. "Well, the sleeping girl can have the honey cake."

"The sleeping girl wants two."

"Greedy sleeper." But Isandro broke the cake in half and put both halves on the plate, and Maren decided, in the serious way children decide things, that this was a good person. It wasn't the cake. Well. It was partly the cake. But it was mostly that Isandro hadn't asked her any questions yet, even though Maren could tell — the way you can tell weather coming — that Isandro had a whole sackful of questions and was holding the sack closed very carefully.

"Where am I?" Maren asked, around honey cake.

"A consulate. In Lantern's Reach. It's a building where my government keeps its paperwork and its worries."

"Do you live here?"

"I do for now."

"Are you married?"

Isandro made a sound like a laugh that had tripped over something. "No."

"Why not?"

"That's a very large question for such a small girl."

"I'm six," Maren said, as if that explained everything, because it did. "Aunt Vessa got married at twenty and she says twenty is nearly dead."

"Your aunt sounds delightful."

"She's not. She smells like vinegar." Maren chewed. The milk was cold and good. Outside the window the duck-men kept marching. "Are those the ones chasing me?"

Isandro went still. Not scared-still. Careful-still, like a cat deciding whether the thing moving in the grass is dinner or danger. "What makes you think anyone's chasing you?"

Maren shrugged, which was a lie, because nothing about it was a shrug-shaped truth. "Because Papa doesn't send people away unless someone's chasing them. He said forget the face. He wrote it on the note." She said it lightly, the way you repeat a spell without understanding it, and watched Isandro's face do something complicated, like a door opening onto a room full of furniture being rearranged by invisible hands.

"Forget the face," Isandro repeated quietly.

"That's what I said."

"And do you know whose face?"

"Mine, obviously." Maren rolled her eyes, because grown-ups sometimes needed help keeping up. "It's the only face I've got. Papa says I look like Mama." She stopped, because saying Mama made her chest do the thing, the hollow thing, the thing that felt like biting into bread and finding no bread inside. She took a very long drink of milk instead.

Isandro didn't push. That was another point in her favor. Grown-ups always pushed, the way you push a door that sticks, and it never helped; you had to wait for the door to decide. Maren knew about stuck doors. She had been one for three days now.

* * *

In the morning — or what she guessed was morning, since the window went from black to grey — Maren explored.

Exploring was against the first rule, technically. But rules had a hierarchy, and the highest rule of all was *don't get caught*, which meant exploring was fine as long as you were excellent at it, and Maren was excellent at most things she decided to be excellent at.

The consulate was a maze built by someone who hated mazes. Stairs went up to corridors that went nowhere. Doors opened onto closets full of other doors' worth of coats. There was a hallway where the wallpaper showed birds flying toward a window that was painted on, which Maren found personally insulting. She catalogued everything the way Papa had taught her to catalogue the orchard trees: *left at the crooked one, right past the one with the broken branch, straight on till you smell the river.*

The kitchen was the best place. Kitchens always were. The consulate cook was a round woman named Bess who talked to her pots the way other people talked to horses ("Now don't you scorch on me, I've had a day"), and who pretended not to notice Maren in the corner and then, without ever looking at her directly, produced a heel of warm bread with butter already melting into it.

"You again," Bess said to a pot.

"Yes," Maren said to the floor.

"Children who answer pots get extra jam."

Maren got extra jam.

It was in the kitchen, sitting on an overturned crate with her legs swinging, that she heard the men's voices through the door — Sef, the sharp one who smelled like hair oil and hurry, and another man — talking low and fast about schedules and seals and something called *the dispatch*, and the way Sef said *dispatch* made it sound less like a word and more like a knife being unwrapped.

Maren ate her jam and memorized the voices. This was another Papa-lesson, though he'd taught it as a game: *close your eyes, little wren, tell me who's walking across the yard.* Herron's clack-clack on the stones. Old Piet's uneven shuffle. The postman's whistle. She'd been very good at the game. She was getting very good at it again, here, where the game was not a game.

* * *

In the afternoon Isandro found her in the linen cupboard on the second floor, which Maren had claimed as a fort on account of its excellent sightlines and defensible door.

"There you are," Isandro said, not sounding surprised, which ruined everything. "Bess said a small ghost had been stealing jam."

"Ghosts don't steal jam. Ghosts can't hold things. Everyone knows that."

"Then we have a burglar."

"A squatter," Maren corrected. "Squatters stay. Burglars leave." She hugged her knees and considered Isandro from the shadow of the towels. Then, because the question had been sitting in her chest since the night before, pressing like a stone in a boot: "Are you going to give me back?"

The cupboard went very quiet. Somewhere below, Bess banged a pot at a disobedient pot.

"Give you back to whom?" Isandro asked carefully.

"To whoever's chasing me. To the government. To the men with the circulars." Maren had seen the circulars. Papa had burned theirs before the soldiers came, but she'd caught a glimpse once, at the market in Correnvale, before everything: a drawing of a man with a hard jaw, and smaller drawings beside him, and one of the smaller drawings had been her. *Associates and dependents,* it said underneath. She hadn't known what *dependent* meant and had made Papa tell her, and Papa had said, *it means someone who depends on you, and I depend on you entirely, little wren,* which had made her feel enormous and important for a whole week.

Isandro crouched down so their eyes were level. Up close, her grey eyes had flecks in them, like a bird's egg. "Listen to me, Maren. I'm going to answer honestly, because I think you've had enough of people not doing that."

Maren nodded solemnly. Honesty was rare, like honey cake.

"I don't know yet what I'm going to do," Isandro said. "But I know I'm not giving you to anyone who would hurt you. That much I know the way I know my own hands. Can you live with an answer that's only half a roof?"

Maren thought about it with the full weight of six years of experience in half-roofs — the lean-to by the well, the wagon awning in the rain, all the shelters that worked until they didn't. "Half a roof keeps the rain off your head," she said finally. "You just have to sit in the right spot."

Something moved across Isandro's face, quick as a fish under ice. "Yes," she said. "You just have to sit in the right spot." She held out her hand, palm up, an offer rather than a demand. "Come on, squatter. Bess is making dumplings and she'll count heads."

Maren took the hand. She couldn't have said why — Papa always said trust was earned like money, slowly and suspiciously — but the hand was steady and ink-stained and it had broken the honey cake fairly, and besides, deep in the animal part of her that had carried her three days through ditches and hedgerows, some old instinct lay down and stopped growling. She trusted Isandro without knowing why, the way you trust a bridge without checking its beams, simply because you are already halfway across.

They walked down together, hand in hand, past the painted birds flying toward their painted window.

At the stair landing Isandro paused. Through the tall windows of the hall, far beyond the walls of Lantern's Reach, the winter fields lay flat and pale, and on the road that crossed them — so far away it might have been imagination — something small was moving. A rider, or a cart, or a man on foot, coming toward the city with the particular patience of someone who has a long way to go and all of it urgent.

Isandro looked at it for three heartbeats. Then she squeezed Maren's hand, a little tighter than before, and said, "Dumplings," in a voice that was thinking about something else entirely.
