## 7. Orders Unread

The dispatch case sat on the consulate desk like a guest who had outstayed its welcome.

Isandro Voss had been looking at it for the better part of an hour. She had made tea. She had drunk the tea. She had washed the cup and dried it and set it back on its hook at precisely the angle the consulate's housekeeper preferred, which was a thing she had never once cared about in eleven years of service, and which she now found herself doing because her hands wanted occupation and her eyes refused to leave the leather case with its brass corners and its single wax seal.

*You could open it.*

The thought arrived with the same polite confidence as a tax collector.

She sat down across from it, the way one sits across from an adversary at cards. The lamp between them threw the seal's shadow long across the desk — the Republic of Vellamare's triple-tower crest, pressed into dark red wax by some clerk in the Chancellery who would never see Lantern's Reach, never smell the smoke of its ovens or hear the children in the ration lines arguing over whose turn it was to carry the bread home. A clerk who had pressed his thumb into warm wax on an ordinary afternoon and gone to lunch.

Eleven years, and Isandro had opened exactly nothing.

It was not law, precisely, that couriers did not read what they carried. It was something older than law — a covenant, almost. *We trust you with the weight of things; do not presume to know them.* A courier who read was a courier who might judge, and a courier who judged was a courier who might hesitate, and hesitation in the delivery of state papers had toppled ministries, lost wars, gotten better people than Isandro Voss hanged from bridge railings with their seals stuffed unceremoniously into their mouths.

So: eleven years, nothing opened. Not the armistice terms at Corvel Bay. Not the succession dispute at Ambrun. Not even the infamous "personal" packet for the Duke of Sarelle that everyone in the service swore contained love letters and that Isandro had delivered, sealed and smugly unread, only to learn later it had been arrest warrants.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake.

*Because you already know,* said the thought again, quieter now, less like a tax collector and more like Maren's voice asking *why is the door locked?*

She didn't know. She suspected. Suspicion was not knowledge, and knowledge was not proof, and proof — well. Proof was what happened when you broke a seal that could not be re-broken.

Unless.

Isandro leaned back in the chair until it creaked its complaint, and let herself consider the shape of the problem the way Fen had once taught her to consider a river before crossing it: don't fight the current, read it.

* * *

He had been reading a river when they met, actually. Or pretending to. It was the third day of the Corvel Bay talks, the winter the Republic still believed itself immortal, and the negotiators had retired to argue about grain tariffs over brandy while their aides stood around the frozen mill pond making conversation about weather. Isandro had been twenty-four and bored past endurance, watching a boy skate too far toward the weir where the ice went thin and grey as old bone.

She remembered shouting. She remembered the crack sounding wrong — not a snap but a sigh, the ice simply giving up — and then she remembered cold so total it stopped being cold and became a kind of white silence, and Aldric Fen's hand closing around her wrist like something forged rather than grown.

"You went in after him," he'd said later, wrapping her in his own coat in the millhouse while her teeth chattered hard enough to loosen fillings. "That was either very brave or very stupid."

"The boy first," she'd managed. "Did you get the boy?"

"He's fine. He'll be telling this story badly for the rest of his life." Fen had crouched in front of her, rubbing warmth back into her hands, and she'd noticed he was shaking worse than she was. "I'm Fen. I negotiate things. Badly, according to everyone."

"Isandro Voss. I carry things."

"What sort of things?"

"Sealed things."

Something had moved through his face then — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Ah," he'd said. "Then we're colleagues of a kind. I open sealed things. You carry them. Between us we make one complete bureaucrat." And he'd laughed, and she'd laughed, and the laugh had hurt her ribs in a way she'd decided not to examine.

Later — much later, the last night of the talks, both of them too tired and too honest — he'd held his glass up to the firelight and said, without any joke in it at all: "One day your republic will ask you to carry something you can't unknow. You won't be able to open it. But you'll know anyway. Couriers always know. The trick is deciding, before that day comes, whether you serve the paper or the people it lands on."

"I serve the Republic," she'd said, young and certain.

"Mm," said Fen, and looked into his wine, and said nothing else.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Isandro came back to the present with the particular jolt of a person who has been somewhere else entirely and finds the room unchanged, waiting, patient as a debt.

The dispatch case. The seal. Eleven years.

There was a way, and she had known there was a way since her second year in the service, when old Courier Demane — three months from retirement, fond of plum brandy and unsolicited wisdom — had shown her over a bottle how routing marks worked.

"Not reading," Demane had said, waggling a finger at her. "Reading is a crime. This is *bookkeeping.* Every dispatch bears its routing marks — origin codes, transfer stations, priority class, terminal designation. They're stamped on the case, plain as day, because the whole apparatus of the post depends on them. Any fool can look at them. Only a fool looks at them *carefully.*"

"And what does careful get you?" she'd asked.

Demane had smiled with all the serenity of a man who intended to die in bed. "It gets you knowing where you're going. Which, in my experience, is more than the Chancellery knows half the time."

She rose and fetched the case to the lamp.

The routing marks ran along the spine of the case in a vertical column of stamped characters, small as grains of rice, each one a compressed syllable of bureaucratic language. Origin: VC-1, the First Chancellery, Vellemare City proper. Priority: *Aurel-class* — highest diplomatic urgency, the class reserved for treaties, declarations of war, and other documents that could not afford to wait for breakfast. Transfer: none. Direct carriage. That alone raised the hair on her arms; Aurel-class direct meant no intermediate officer ever touched it, meaning no one along the route would know its contents except sender and receiver.

And receiver —

Isandro took out her lens. It was a courier's tool, technically for verifying fine-print authentication marks against forgery, and technically she was using it for bookkeeping, and technically she was going to keep telling herself that right up until the moment she couldn't.

The terminal designation was six characters. She knew the code tables the way she knew her own name; they were drilled into every courier until recitation replaced thought. She read them once. Then she read them again, slowly, the way one reads a sentence that has rearranged itself between glances.

*LR-GD-7.*

Lantern's Reach. Granary District. Sub-station seven.

Sub-station seven. She knew it. Everyone in the city knew it — it wasn't a negotiation hall, wasn't a consulate annex, wasn't anything diplomatic at all. Sub-station seven was the fortified relay depot at the edge of the granary quarter, the place where military dispatches terminated, where field commanders' liaison officers collected operational traffic. It had a loading yard. It had a garrison detachment. It had, as of two weeks ago, a newly constructed signal tower visible from half the city, flying the pennant of the Third Artillery Group.

Third Artillery Group.

Isandro set the lens down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing one wants very much to throw through a window.

Negotiating terms went to the negotiating table. That table sat in the consulate — in this building, behind her, forty paces away, where Consul Beretha Hale kept her good silver polished for the peace she still spoke about in public. Terms terminating anywhere else would have been strange. Terms terminating at a fortified artillery relay depot in the granary district were not strange.

They were coordinates.

No — not quite. The orders themselves weren't coordinates. The orders were probably something mundane-sounding: authorization for provisioning, instructions for the reception of "special matériel," perhaps a timetable dressed in administrative language. But they would terminate where the artillery lived, and the artillery did not live near negotiating tables, and the granary district — she closed her eyes and saw the map of the city printed on the inside of her own skull, drawn there by a decade of carrying its mail — the granary district was the densest civilian quarter inside the walls. Bakeries and storehouses and tenement rows and the market square where the children played their endless, vicious games of lantern-tag among the stalls.

If the Republic were sending peace terms, they would land on a table with chairs around it.

These orders were landing where guns are fed.

"Cold clarity" — she'd heard the phrase once, from a philosophy tutor she'd briefly and disastrously been involved with, who claimed that real understanding felt not like revelation but like frost: precise, silent, spreading. She understood him now. There was no thunderclap. No dramatic gasping. Just the frost moving through her, methodical, taking inventory: *the ovens will go out. The ration lines will become burial lines. The children playing lantern-tag —*

She made herself stop. Frost was useful. Feeling could wait its turn; feeling had been waiting its turn all week, standing politely at the back of her mind like Maren waited outside the pantry door each morning, trusting that someone inside would eventually hand her breakfast.

Maren.

The thought arrived connected to everything else, the way thoughts do when the frost has finished mapping the territory. *Forget the face.* Fen's handwriting — she had known it instantly, that upright, deliberate script, each letter built like something meant to survive rough handling. The man who had pulled her from white silence and laughed with cracked ribs and told her to decide, *before the day comes*, whom she served.

He hadn't sent her a plea. He'd sent her a child and four words, because a child was the one dispatch no checkpoint would dare turn away, and four words were the one message no cipher clerk could misread.

*The trick is deciding, before that day comes.*

The day had come. It had come wearing a six-year-old's boots and sleeping in the consulate's linen closet, and Isandro Voss had not decided anything yet, and every hour she didn't decide was an hour the frost spread further toward whatever she would have to be afterward.

She looked at the seal.

Here was the arithmetic of it, laid bare: if she opened the case and found peace terms — actual terms, table-bound terms — then she had spent this night suspecting her own republic of murder on the strength of a routing code, and she could reseal nothing, unsee nothing, and her career would end in disgrace at best. If she opened it and found what the frost said she'd find — targeting authorizations, timetables, the paperwork of erasure — then she would hold in her hands the death warrant of everyone who had ever sold her bread, and she would have to choose, tonight, with no seal left to hide behind, between the oath and the orchard.

Either way, the woman who went to sleep tonight was not the woman who woke tomorrow.

*Not yet,* she told the case. *Not while there's another road. Bookkeeping first. Reading is a crime.*

She returned the case to the desk — spine out, marks facing the wall, a small pointless decency — and stood at the window instead, where Lantern's Reach lay under its curfew lamplight, bakeries banked and dark but ready, chimneys holding their breath, the whole city dreaming its stubborn dream of morning. Somewhere out there beyond the walls, the Third Artillery Group was also preparing for morning. One of them was going to be disappointed.

A floorboard shifted in the corridor.

Isandro turned, hand already flat on the desk — over the case, covering it, an instinct so fast it bypassed thought entirely and told her something about herself she wasn't ready to hear.

Sef Andrel stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, candle in hand, wearing the pleasant half-smile he wore like a second uniform. Younger than her by six years, quicker than her by a mile, ambitious in the way ivy is ambitious — patient, green, and quietly everywhere.

"Burning the midnight oil, Senior Courier?" His eyes did the corridor-and-desk sweep that all couriers learned at the academy, the one that pretended to be casual and never was.

"Admiring the view," Isandro said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"At this hour the view is mostly siege lines." He drifted a step into the room, candle tilting. "Funny thing — I passed your door earlier and heard you moving about. Thought you might want company. Or tea." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "Or help with the dispatch. Aurel-class direct carriage, isn't it? Heavy responsibility. I remember my first one. Couldn't sleep a wink."

*Couldn't sleep a wink.* Was that a confession or a probe?

"No help needed," Isandro said, and smiled, and discovered that lying — real lying, load-bearing lying, the kind that holds up roofs — sat strangely in her mouth, like a coin of unfamiliar mint. "But thank you, Sef. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we deliver."

"Tomorrow we deliver," he agreed warmly, raising his candle in a little salute, and withdrew, and his footsteps went soft down the corridor, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be and all night to be nowhere in particular.

Isandro stood listening until the consulate's silence resettled. Then she looked down at her own hand, still resting flat over the leather case, guarding it — or hiding it — and understood that the frost had reached a new address.

Sef Andrel had heard her moving about. Sef Andrel had come looking. And Sef Andrel, who believed doubt was the first treason, would be counting hours now, same as her.

The race, such as it was, had begun — and she was running it carrying the one cargo she could neither read nor put down.
