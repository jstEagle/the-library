## 8. The Loyalist's Argument

Sef Andrel had a theory about doors.

A courier learned to read them the way other men read faces. A door that stood open said *we have nothing worth hiding*. A door that was locked said *we have something worth hiding*. But a door that had been locked and then carefully, deliberately left ajar — that door said *someone wants you to think they've forgotten it exists*, and in Sef's experience those were always the most interesting doors in any building.

The consulate's records room door was currently ajar by exactly two fingers' width.

He stood in the corridor of Lantern's Reach's consular residence, a folder of routine dispatch confirmations under his arm he didn't actually need to deliver, and considered the gap. The lamp inside had been trimmed low. Someone had been in there recently — recently enough that they'd wanted the room to look untouched, which was itself a kind of touching.

Isandro Voss had gone down that corridor an hour ago. He'd counted her footsteps. He was very good at counting things; it was one of the qualities that had gotten him his deputy's sash at twenty-six, along with a memory like a customs ledger and the good fortune to look harmless, which was a currency all its own in rooms full of suspicious people.

He pushed the door open with one knuckle.

The records room smelled of dust and cold tallow. Shelves of municipal correspondence, trade ledgers, three years of garrison reports nobody had read since the siege made reading them feel like an insult. And on the table by the window, squared neatly with the edge of the wood — because Isandro Voss squared everything, even her griefs, even her lies — lay the diplomatic pouch containing the republic's final sealed orders for Lantern's Reach.

Sef looked at it for a long moment.

Then he looked at the window, which was latched. Then at the floor, where the dust showed no footprints but did show, near the table leg, the faint crescent where someone had set down a boot heel and thought better of whatever they'd been about to do.

He didn't touch the pouch. Touching it would be a crime. He simply stood there in the cold lamplight and let the facts arrange themselves in his head the way he liked them: numbered, cross-referenced, damning.

*She hesitated at the checkpoint on the road in,* he thought. *She asked the granary quartermaster twice about the eastern district — twice, when once is courtesy and twice is interest. She has not slept. She has not eaten more than half of anything put in front of her. And now she has come to this room alone and stood over these orders like a woman standing over a well she's thinking of drowning something in.*

He straightened his sash. It was a small vanity, the sash — deep courier blue, silver thread at the hem — but vanities were load-bearing structures. A man who kept his sash straight kept his principles straight, or so his father had said, and his father had been a toll-keeper for forty years without ever once letting a cart through unpaid, which in the Andrel family constituted sainthood.

He left the door exactly as he'd found it. Two fingers' width. Let her wonder if he'd seen.

* * *

Dinner at the consulate was served at seven, in a room that had once hosted trade delegations from four nations and now hosted three people and a pot of barley soup stretched with turnip. Consul Beretha Hale presided from the head of the table with the weary majesty of a woman who had stopped believing in the republic sometime around the fall of the southern fleet but saw no reason to let the china know it.

"Deputy Andrel." Hale nodded him to his place. "You look pleased with yourself. That's either good news or bad news wearing its good coat."

"Just confirmation receipts, Consul. The northern relay acknowledged our last transmission."

"The northern relay," Hale said, ladling soup, "is a tower full of frightened boys sending up smoke signals and hoping the smoke means something. But yes. Good news, then. Or its coat."

Isandro sat across from him. Sef watched her the way he watched everything — sideways, patiently, without appetite for the soup but with a full plate anyway, because a man who ate nothing drew attention, and attention was a debt you paid back with interest.

"You were quiet today," he said to her, pleasantly, over the turnips.

"I'm often quiet."

"You're efficient-quiet. Today you were thinking-quiet. There's a difference. Efficient-quiet sounds like this—" he tapped the table twice, briskly— "and thinking-quiet sounds like this." He tapped nothing at all.

Isandro smiled at him. It was a good smile; it always had been. Warm at the edges, wry in the middle, and completely opaque past the first layer, like frosted glass over a lit room. He'd known her four years. He still couldn't tell you what she dreamed about.

"And what does ambitious-quiet sound like, Sef?" she asked.

"Louder than people expect," he said, and got a short laugh out of Hale, which was the point. You fed the consul a joke the way you fed a stove — small pieces, regularly, so the heat never spiked enough to scorch anyone.

Hale set down her spoon. "There's a matter I want raised at this table, since we're all pretending to relax. Rumors came in with the last refugee column. A general named Fen — Aldric Fen, formerly of the Third Army, formerly decorated, formerly a great many things — has turned traitor. Defected east, they say. Carrying troop dispositions, they say." She said *they say* the way other people spat. "If any of that reaches your ears in the city, I want it brought here. Not interpreted. Not softened. Brought."

Sef felt rather than saw Isandro go still. It was a small thing — the pause between spoonfuls stretching half a second too long — but he collected small things the way some men collected debts.

"Fen," Isandro said. "The name's familiar. Peacetime talks, wasn't it? Years ago."

"You were there?" Hale's eyebrows rose.

"For part of it. River delegation. He pulled a courier out of the ice, as I recall." A beat. "Me, as it happens."

"Well." Hale considered her soup. "The republic has a long memory for its heroes and a short one for its traitors, and lately it can't keep the files straight. If Fen's turned, then anyone he ever saved should be asking themselves what that says about their judgment." She said it lightly. She meant it entirely seriously. Sef admired that in a superior officer the way one admires a well-maintained gallows.

"I'll ask myself," Isandro said, "at a more convenient hour."

* * *

After dinner, Sef found her on the consulate's back steps, wrapped in her courier's cloak, watching the city. From the steps you could see a slice of Lantern's Reach between the garden wall and the cypress: lamplight in crooked streets, the bakery chimney still breathing its sweet smudge of woodsmoke into the dark, somewhere a fiddle being played badly by someone determined to keep playing it anyway.

Besieged cities, Sef had learned, developed a specific stubbornness. They baked bread out of spite. They played fiddles at the artillery. It was sentimental, impractical, and — he suspected — the actual reason republics survived as long as they did, though he'd have denied saying so under oath.

"You're watching me," Isandro said, without turning.

"I'm standing near you. Watching is your department. You've been doing it to me since the road."

"Mm." She shifted to make room on the step, which was either hospitality or a trap, and with Isandro it was genuinely difficult to say which. He sat.

For a while neither spoke. The fiddle stumbled through something that might have been a harvest song. A dog barked twice at nothing, thought better of it, and stopped.

"Do you remember Vellamare City in festival week?" Isandro said suddenly. "Two years ago. The lantern boats on the canal."

"I remember the crowds. I remember losing you in the crowds and finding you again because you were the only person in Vellamare standing perfectly still in the middle of it."

"I was counting the boats."

"Of course you were." He smiled. "You count everything. It's why I trust you, incidentally. A person who counts things can be audited."

Something crossed her face then — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. He pretended not to see it, which was its own kind of skill, and possibly the most useful one he had.

"Sef," she said. "Do you ever wonder what's in the orders?"

The question landed in the space between them and sat there, warm as a coal.

"No," he said.

"Not even a little? Not at three in the morning?"

"Especially not at three in the morning. Three in the morning is when wondering does its best work." He turned to look at her properly. "You want me to say yes. You want me to sit here and speculate with you — artillery, terms, evacuation, treachery, pick your poison — and after an hour of it we'll both know things we can't unknow, and neither of us will have delivered anything faster or safer for the knowing." He shook his head. "I don't wonder, Isandro. I decline to. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole job."

"That's not an answer. That's a locked door painted to look like an answer."

"I'm a deputy courier. My entire profession is locked doors painted convincingly." He kept his voice gentle. That mattered. What he had to say next needed to arrive soft, the way a verdict arrives soft before it arrives at all. "But since you've opened yours — here's what I believe, and you can laugh at it later. Loyalty isn't agreeing with the republic. Half the time I suspect the republic couldn't find its own agreement with both hands and a map. Loyalty is carrying the thing anyway. Sealed. Unread. Delivered. Because the moment a courier decides which orders deserve delivering, we're not couriers anymore. We're forty thousand private governments, each with our own foreign policy, and the republic dies right there — not in the war, in us. Doubt is the first treason, Isandro. Everything else is just paperwork."

The fiddle below finished its song. In the silence after, Isandro looked out at the lamplit streets for a long time.

"And if the orders are wrong?" she said quietly.

"Then wrongness travels at courier speed, same as everything else, and gets corrected by people whose job it is to correct it. Not ours."

"And if there's no one left to correct it?"

"Then we'll have been loyal to the end of something." He shrugged. "I'd rather be loyal to the end of something than clever to the end of myself."

She laughed — a real one, brief and startled out of her — and for a moment it was two years ago and festival week and the lantern boats, and he almost believed she'd heard him.

Almost.

Because he'd watched her hands while he talked. Couriers' hands told the truth; faces were for amateurs. Her hands had gone still in her lap, thumbs pressed together, the way hands go when they're holding something invisible shut.

"There's a child," she said abruptly. "Consul's staff took in a refugee girl yesterday. Six or so. You may see her about. Nobody important."

"A child," Sef repeated.

"Maren. Dark hair, doesn't talk much. If anyone asks, she's a dead colleague's daughter. Consular protection."

"Does anyone usually ask you to pre-explain children, Isandro?"

"No," she said, rising, pulling her cloak close. "That's rather the point of explaining them first." She paused at the top of the steps, silhouetted against the city glow, and for one unguarded instant she looked less like the most reliable courier in the service and more like a woman standing at the edge of a frozen river deciding whether the sound under her feet was ice or something else.

"Goodnight, Sef."

"Goodnight."

He watched her go. Then he sat alone on the cold steps with his hands folded, doing what he did best: counting.

One — she'd lied about the child, or at least rehearsed the lie, which amounted to the same thing in a court of inquiry. Two — she'd asked about the orders directly, which Isandro Voss, who never asked anything directly, would only do if the indirect asking had already failed. Three — she'd mentioned Fen's name at dinner unprompted, laying her history with him on the table like a card face-up, which was either honesty or camouflage, and Isandro did not do honesty with her cards.

Four — tomorrow morning, per the schedule, she would make her final routing preparations. And tomorrow evening, per the schedule, the orders would leave for the granary district under her seal.

Unless.

Sef stayed on the steps until the cold drove him in, and then he went to his room and took from beneath his spare shirt a flat square of wax no bigger than a coin — an impression he'd taken weeks ago, in another city, for reasons he'd told himself were administrative diligence and had known, even then, were ambition wearing a uniform two sizes too big.

He turned it over in his fingers. Once, twice.

He was not a traitor. He wanted that understood, by history, by himself, by whichever clerk eventually wrote the file. He was a man who believed in the republic the way his father had believed in the toll-gate: imperfectly administered, occasionally unjust, but the only structure standing between the carts and the ravine. If Isandro Voss was merely tired, merely grieving, merely human — then his seal impression would stay buried under a shirt forever, and he would carry his small shame privately, like everyone else.

And if she wasn't.

He blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the distant, patient thud of the siege guns, and made himself a promise shaped exactly like a threat:

Tomorrow, he would watch her hands.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, with frost ferning the consulate windows and the smell of the bakery reaching even the residence, defiant and absurd. Sef was in the corridor by six, ostensibly checking the dispatch schedule, actually positioned where the records room door was visible from three angles.

At half past six, Isandro passed him in the hall. Dressed. Composed. Courier-blue and silver thread, sash straight.

"Early start," she said pleasantly.

"Big day."

"The biggest." And she smiled at him — warm at the edges, wry in the middle, frosted glass over a lit room — and walked on toward the stables with a stride he knew by heart, steady as a metronome, unhurried as certainty.

It was only after she'd turned the corner that Sef realized what was wrong with the picture, and the realization arrived in his stomach before his head, cold and complete:

Her saddlebags were packed for a long ride.

And slung across her shoulder, wrapped in oilcloth, riding high against her hip where a courier carried the thing she could least afford to lose —

was not the diplomatic pouch.

He stood in the empty corridor a long moment, frost melting off the windows behind him, the republic ticking quietly toward noon somewhere inside his chest.

Then he went to get his coat.
