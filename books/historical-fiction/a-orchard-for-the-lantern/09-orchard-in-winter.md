## 9. Orchard in Winter

The advertisement ran in the *Lantern's Reach Herald* on the third morning after Maren arrived, tucked between a notice about a lost goat and an announcement that the tanner's guild was temporarily suspending the sale of boot wax.

Isandro read it four times over her cold breakfast before she allowed herself to believe it.

**WANTED: QUIET HANDS FOR WINTER PRUNING. ORCHARD ON THE NORTH ROAD, THE ONE EVERYONE REMEMBERS WRONG. ASK FOR THE MAN WHO OWES A DEBT TO WATER. PAY IN KINDNESS. NO EXPERIENCE NECESSARY. NO EXPERIENCE PREFERRED.**

The lost goat, she suspected, was real. The boot wax announcement had the desperate sincerity of a man who had genuinely run out of things to sell. But the orchard advertisement — no one wrote like that. No one *paid in kindness*, and certainly no one advertised for workers who had *no experience preferred* unless what they actually wanted was someone who would understand that the work being offered was not pruning at all.

*The one everyone remembers wrong.* There had been an orchard on the north road — everyone in Lantern's Reach knew it, or thought they did. It had belonged to a family named Corvel, and it had burned in the first border war, and children were told it was haunted, which was a lie invented by adults who wanted the apples for themselves before anyone could stop them. That much was true and commonly remembered.

What everyone remembered wrong was where it stood. The burned orchard people pointed to was half a mile past the river bend. The real one — the Corvels' actual ground, the one Aldric Fen had once walked her through during a lull in peace talks six years ago, pointing out which trees might still be coaxed back to life if the war ever ended — sat closer to the water, behind a collapsed wall, deliberately unremarkable.

*Ask for the man who owes a debt to water.*

Isandro set down her cup. Her hand was steady, which surprised her. Inside her chest, something was doing the opposite of staying steady; something was running in circles, barking like the consulate's ancient dog had done whenever the post rider came up the drive.

He was alive. He was close. He was asking for her — not with a name, not with a plea, but in the only language two people who had spent their lives being watched could safely speak.

"You're smiling," said Maren, who was sitting across from her building a fortress out of bread crusts. "You don't smile at breakfast."

"I'm reading an interesting advertisement."

"About what?"

"A job."

"Do we need money?" Maren asked, with the grave practicality of a child who had recently learned what happened when households fell apart. "Papa says we're not poor, we're just between houses."

"We are not poor," Isandro agreed, and then, because lying to the consul had turned out to be disturbingly easy and lying to this child made her feel as though she'd swallowed a stone: "We are just between several things."

Maren considered this, decided it was acceptable, and ate her fortress.

* * *

Getting out of Lantern's Reach alone was a small bureaucratic miracle, and Isandro achieved it through the courier's oldest trick: looking bored.

The north gate guard — a boy of perhaps nineteen with a helmet two sizes too large and a mustache he was clearly still negotiating with — glanced at her papers, glanced at her face, and visibly decided that whatever she was, she wasn't his problem today.

"Courier business?" he asked, because the form required him to ask.

"Inspection of the relay stations." She let her eyes drift toward the horizon with the specific weariness of a woman who had seen too many relay stations and expected nothing better from these. "I'd tell you it's thrilling, but I've been sworn to honesty by my mother's ghost."

"Relay stations," he said sympathetically. "My cousin does that for the salt office. He says the highlight of his month is a ladder."

"There's always a ladder," Isandro agreed gravely, and rode out into the gray morning before he could remember to be suspicious of anything.

The north road was rutted and quiet. The siege had thinned traffic to a trickle of carts hauling firewood and one determined woman transporting what appeared to be an entire beehive balanced across a mule's back, both parties — woman and hive — radiating mutual grievance. Snow lay thin and dirty along the verges. Somewhere behind her, Lantern's Reach went about its morning: ovens lit, ration lines formed, bells rung by whoever rang them, stubborn as weeds growing through cobblestone.

She did not look back at the city. Looking back felt like admitting she might not return, and she had found, in recent days, that there were certain thoughts she could not afford to think all the way through. She carried Maren's safety in one pocket of her mind and the sealed orders in another, and the two pockets pressed against each other constantly, like strangers forced to share a narrow bench.

The orders rode in her saddlebag. They had ridden everywhere with her for eleven days now, through checkpoints and rain and one memorable incident involving a fallen mule, and they had never once grown lighter. Sealed things didn't. That was rather the point of sealing them: weight without content, authority without answer. She had spent her whole career respecting that weight. Lately she had begun to wonder whether the respect was wisdom or simply habit wearing wisdom's coat.

The river bend came up faster than she'd expected. She recognized it the way you recognize a house you dreamed about once — the leaning willow, the flat stone like a table, the water moving dark and patient under its skin of ice at the edges.

And beyond it, behind the collapsed wall she remembered, the trees.

* * *

The orchard in winter was not beautiful, exactly. It was beautiful the way old soldiers are beautiful — scarred, bare, upright anyway. Two dozen apple trees stood in crooked rows, their branches black against the snow, some broken by storms and never pruned, some still bearing the pale fuzz of mistletoe like medals pinned on carelessly. The Corvel farmhouse had been a roofless shell even six years ago. Now its stones wore moss like a uniform they'd finally been issued.

Isandro dismounted at the wall and led the horse through the gap, walking slowly, letting her boots announce her. If Fen was watching — and he was watching, she would have bet her seal on it, except she'd recently begun betting her seal on other things — then he deserved the courtesy of knowing she came openly.

"You know," she said to the empty rows, "most people who advertise for help at least have the decency to be home when applicants arrive."

Snow slid off a branch somewhere to her left.

She stopped. Breathed. The cold bit down through her gloves, and the silence stretched, and she became aware of her own heartbeat with an intimacy she usually reserved for long rides and bad news.

"The debt to water," she said. "That's a strange way to put it. Most men would say I owed them. You pulled me out. All I did afterward was sit wrapped in blankets drinking terrible tea while you fished my dispatch case out of the river and pretended it hadn't taken you three dives to find it."

Another branch. Closer.

"You pretended it was very heavy," she continued, warming to it, talking to the trees because talking to the trees was easier than talking to the fear. "You said it must have been full of bricks. I said couriers don't carry bricks. You said *this republic puts bricks in everything eventually*. I laughed. I shouldn't have laughed. You weren't joking, were you?"

"No," said the orchard.

He stepped out from behind the third tree in the row — the crooked one, the one he'd called *the philosopher* because it grew sideways toward the light and refused, year after year, to apologize for it. Six years had silvered him at the temples and carved new lines around his mouth, and there was a healing cut above one eyebrow, and he moved like a man who had spent recent years practicing how to arrive places quietly.

But his eyes were the same. That was the thing that undid her, standing in the snow with her breath hanging in front of her: the eyes were exactly the same, warm and tired and unbearably direct, the eyes of a man who looked at people instead of through them.

"Hello, Isandro," said Aldric Fen.

"Hello, traitor," said Isandro Voss, and immediately wished she'd chosen any other word in the language, because his face did something complicated and then settled into acceptance, and acceptance was worse than anger.

"That's fair," he said. "It's the official title. I should probably have it engraved somewhere."

"They'd charge you for the engraving. The republic's short on funds. It's putting bricks in everything."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — the ghost of the man who had joked with her on a frozen riverbank while her teeth chattered hard enough to keep time. Then it faded, and he looked past her shoulder, up the road, the way a hunted animal checks the wind, and the flicker was gone.

"You came alone?"

"I told the gate I was inspecting ladders."

"Ladders."

"Relay stations have ladders. It's a whole world of vertical access. You wouldn't know; you've been off the government payroll."

"I've been off more than the payroll." He came closer, stopping an arm's length away — close enough to speak softly, far enough that neither of them had to decide about anything else. His gaze moved over her face with a thoroughness that made her want to turn around and present her other side for inspection. "You got my message."

"It took me three readings. *Pay in kindness.*"

"I was proud of that one."

"It's terrible. No one pays in kindness. Kindness isn't currency; that's the entire tragedy of kindness."

"And yet," Fen said quietly, "here you are. On your own time, outside the walls, in winter, answering an advertisement for winter pruning." He glanced at her hands. "No gloves suitable for pruning, I notice. No saw. No experience, in fact."

"The ad said experience wasn't necessary."

"The ad also said the orchard was the one everyone remembers wrong, and here you are, having remembered it right." He exhaled, a long white plume that the wind took apart. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to hear the whole question before you answer any part of it."

"Fen—"

"Hear it first." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about him; she remembered that from the river, from the peace talks, from every conversation they'd ever had. He never raised his voice, and so everything he said arrived already serious. "There is a girl. Six years old. Dark hair, a scar on her left knee from falling off a fence she was specifically instructed not to climb. Her name is Maren."

The cold seemed to move inside Isandro's coat, settling against her ribs.

"She arrived at the consulate four nights ago," Fen said, "with a note in my handwriting. I wrote it myself. I sealed it myself. I sent her with a woman I trusted, who got her within sight of the city and died doing it, and I will carry that as long as I carry anything." His jaw worked once. "I couldn't come myself. There are circulars with my face on them in every garrison from here to the capital. But a child — no soldier looks twice at a child going the wrong direction. Everyone assumes someone else has claimed her. Children are invisible in wartime. It's one of the few mercies we've managed to invent, and it's a mercy built entirely on negligence, but I'll take it. I took it. I used my daughter as a letter."

"You used your daughter as a letter," Isandro repeated. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"Yes."

"To me."

"To the one person I knew would understand the note. *Forget the face* — I knew you'd remember it. I knew you'd know it was mine. We talked about it once, years ago, by that river. I told you—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "I told you that someday the republic would ask you to do something unforgivable, and that you'd know it was unforgivable because you'd suddenly find yourself very busy not thinking about it."

The snow creaked under Isandro's boot as her weight shifted. She hadn't decided to shift it. Her body was conducting its own negotiations.

"What do you want, Fen?"

"I want to know she's alive."

"She's alive."

"I want to know she's safe."

"She's hiding in the consulate's linen closet and eating bread crusts shaped like fortresses, and yesterday she informed me that the chamberlain snores in B minor, so yes, I'd say she feels safe enough to offer musical criticism."

Fen closed his eyes. For a moment — just a moment — the general, the traitor, the man on the circulars, all of it fell away, and what remained was a father standing in a dead orchard receiving the only intelligence report in the world that mattered to him. His shoulders dropped half an inch. When he opened his eyes again, they were wet, and he didn't bother pretending otherwise, and somehow that pretense-not-bothered was the most honest thing anyone had shown Isandro in weeks.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet. I haven't agreed to anything, and you haven't asked for anything, and we're both pretending this meeting has a shape. What do you want, Aldric? Say it plainly. I've spent my career carrying plain things wrapped in seals. I can bear plain speech."

He looked at her for a long moment. Wind moved through the bare rows, and somewhere a crow remarked on the situation and flew off, unimpressed.

"The orders you're carrying," he said. "The ones in your saddlebag right now. Do you know where they terminate?"

"I know they're diplomatic terms for the city's surrender negotiations—"

"Do you know where they *terminate*, Isandro?"

And because he asked plainly, and because some traitorous honest part of her had been waiting eleven days for permission, she heard herself say: "The granary district. I decoded the routing marks three nights ago. They don't end at any negotiating table. They end at coordinates."

The wind moved again. Fen nodded slowly, without surprise, without triumph — and that absence of triumph was its own confirmation, worse than any speech.

"Artillery grids," he said. "For Lantern's Reach. The republic isn't sending terms. It's sending targets, dressed as terms, so that the courier who carries them believes she's carrying peace. So that if she's caught, the enemy finds surrender documents and lets their guard down while the guns finish their arithmetic." He paused. "So that a good woman spends her last days as a loyal servant of a lie with excellent calligraphy."

Isandro discovered she was gripping her horse's reins hard enough to whiten her knuckles inside her unsuitable gloves. She made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why bring me out here? You could have kept your distance. Kept your daughter's location secret from the woman carrying the death warrant. I could ride back and deliver those orders tomorrow and you'd never know whether I did it because I believed the lie or because I chose the republic over your girl."

"I know," he said simply. "That was the risk. Every choice I've made since the defection has been a risk, and most of them have cost more than I budgeted for." He stepped closer, and now there was nothing of the advertisement in him, nothing clever or coded — just a tired man making the last request he had. "I brought you here because I promised myself, years ago on that frozen river, that if I ever needed you, I would ask you to your face and give you the chance to refuse. That's the difference between me and the people who sealed those orders, Isandro. They spend people like coin and never show their faces. I'm showing mine. I'm asking. And you are free — do you hear me? — *free* to walk back through that gate and forget the orchard and forget the face and deliver what you were told to deliver. I will not think less of you. I will think about you every day until the shells fall, but I will not think less of you."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Isandro looked at him — at the philosopher tree behind him, still growing sideways toward the light, still refusing to apologize — and thought about a frozen river six years ago, and strong hands closing on her collar, and a voice saying *hold on, hold on, I've got you,* and how she had believed, with her whole thawing heart, that she had been saved.

She understood now, standing in the ruins of the orchard, that she hadn't been saved that day. She'd been *invested in*. And investments, the republic had taught her, always came due.

"Maren has a blanket," Isandro said at last. Her voice was rougher than she intended. "Wool, blue-grey, with little woven stars that she insists are fish. She sleeps holding one corner of it and won't explain why."

"She's held that blanket since she was two. It was her mother's weaving." Fen's voice frayed on *mother* and repaired itself. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm going to tear it in half."

His brow furrowed. "You're going to—"

"As a signal. Half stays with her, half comes back to this orchard, and when you see it, you'll know she's real, and known, and kept." Isandro was already unwinding the blanket from her saddlebag, the wool stiff with cold. "And because I can't promise you anything else yet, Aldric. Not out loud. Not while Sef Andrel is sniffing around my saddlebags and Consul Hale is watching me across dinner tables like I'm a sum she's checking twice. But I can leave you this."

She braced the wool against her knee and tore it. The sound was small and enormous, a soft ripping that seemed to go on much longer than the fabric did — the sound, she thought wildly, of an oath coming apart so that a truer one could be tied in its place. Stars-that-were-fish scattered across the snow when the halves came free.

She folded one half and pressed it into his hands. His fingers closed around it slowly, carefully, the way men handle things they're afraid to hope for.

"Half stays with me," Isandro said. "When this is over — however it's over — the halves get sewn back together. Those are my terms. I don't prune trees and I don't pay in kindness; I pay in promises, and I keep them."

Fen looked down at the torn blanket, and then at her, and for the first time since she'd arrived he smiled — a small, cracked, entirely un-general-like smile.

"You always were a terrible negotiator," he said. "You're offering me everything and asking for nothing."

"I'm asking for everything. I'm asking you to stay alive to sew."

From the road behind them, faint but distinct, came the sound of hoofbeats — more than one horse, moving at the trot, coming north from the city.

They both went still. Fen's smile vanished into the professional blankness of a hunted man, and his hand found her arm, steering her toward the wall with quiet urgency.

"Back gate, past the well," he murmured. "Ride easy. Couriers inspect ladders; fugitives gallop." A beat. "Isandro. Whatever you decide — decide fast. And if anyone asks—"

"Forget the face," she said.

"No." He almost laughed, pressing the folded half of stars into her glove. "Remember it. Remembering is the dangerous part. Anyone can forget."

Hoofbeats, closer now. Isandro swung into the saddle with her heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with the cold and everything to do with the fact that she had just, irreversibly, chosen a side — or perhaps abandoned sides altogether, which amounted to the same thing in the eyes of everyone counting.

She rode for the back gate at a dignified, inspecting, thoroughly suspicious pace, half a blanket against her ribs and the taste of a promise in her mouth.

Behind her, in a dead orchard, a man stood in the snow holding the other half.

And ahead of her — though she could not yet know it — Consul Beretha Hale was that same afternoon sharpening an offer like a knife, and Deputy Courier Sef Andrel was learning what ambition cost by the ounce, and the sealed orders in her saddlebag ticked toward their coordinates like something patient, something certain, something waiting to be carried the wrong way by the right person.

The first shell would fall in nine days.
