## 10. Hale's Bargain

Consul Beretha Hale had a theory about candles.

A cheap candle, she liked to say, burned fast and bright and told you nothing. A good candle — wax rendered properly, wick trimmed by someone who cared — gave you its whole life in slow installments. You could read the quality of a thing by how it spent itself. She had said this often enough over the years that her clerks had begun leaving better candles on her desk without being asked, which she chose to interpret as respect rather than fear of another lecture. Either way, the light improved, and Beretha Hale had always believed in taking improvement where you found it.

Tonight's candle was a good one. She watched it spend itself and thought about arithmetic.

The consulate at Lantern's Reach had once been a merchant's house, all high ceilings and imported tile, back when merchants could afford optimism. Now it was a fortress of paperwork. The grand receiving hall was a ration office. The music room stored grain requisitions. Hale herself worked from the study, which still had its original windows, though they were now cross-hatched with timber braces against shellfire, so that looking out at her city meant looking through a lattice — as if the view itself needed splinting.

Through the lattice she watched the last light go off the rooftops of Lantern's Reach. Somewhere below, the bakeries would be banking their ovens for the morning. They did that even now, even in the fifth month of the siege. Flour was down to the dregs of the granary stores, and still old Master Corvin fired his ovens every dawn because, as he put it, a city that stops smelling like bread has already surrendered. Hale found this sentimental. She also found it true, which annoyed her.

She turned back to the desk, to the thing that had brought her here an hour after everyone else had gone to bed: a courier's manifest, freshly logged, listing one Isandro Voss, senior courier, Republic of Vellamare, arrived under diplomatic seal with dispatches for the consulate.

Hale knew Voss by reputation only, which was to say she knew everything about her, reputation being merely gossip that had survived long enough to become file material. Twelve years of service. No blemishes. Not one. In Hale's long experience of reading personnel records, a courier with no blemishes was either genuinely incorruptible or very good at not being caught, and the two categories required entirely different handling. The first kind you trusted. The second kind you trusted too, but you counted the spoons afterward.

She picked up the manifest again and looked at the notation that interested her most: *dispatch unopened, weight verified, seal intact.*

Unopened. Of course it was. That was the protocol, and Voss had followed it to the letter, because Voss followed everything to the letter. But Hale had been a consul through two wars and three changes of government, and she had learned to hear what silence in a report was actually saying. A courier who logs *seal intact* is telling you they thought about opening it. A courier who doesn't mention the seal at all is the one who never considered it. It was a small distinction. Most of governing was small distinctions; the large ones tended to arrive already on fire.

* * *

The next morning, Hale held court at breakfast.

This was a habit inherited from the house's merchant days and retained deliberately. A consul who ate alone was a consul who learned things last. So every morning the long table filled with whoever mattered: the military attaché, the harbor-master when there was still a harbor worth mastering, senior clerks, and — since the siege made ceremony cheaper than security — any courier or officer passing through with something worth hearing.

Isandro Voss came to breakfast on her third morning in the city, and Hale watched her the way she watched everything: sideways, over the rim of a cup, with the patience of a woman who had once waited out a nine-hour parliamentary filibuster without blinking.

Voss sat where the junior end of the table indicated she should sit, which told Hale she understood hierarchy. She ate what was put in front of her without comment, which told Hale she understood scarcity — the bread was half sawdust now and everyone knew it, but complaining marked you as either new or stupid. And when the military attaché made his usual grim joke about the enemy's artillery having worse manners than their infantry, Voss smiled at the correct moment with the correct degree of amusement.

*Correct*, Hale noted. Everything about the woman was *correct*. It was like dining with a well-bound book.

"Courier Voss." Hale spoke across the general murmur, in the tone that was not quite a question. "You'll be wanting to move on soon, I imagine. The roads east won't stay open forever."

"They're closing faster than my instructions anticipated, madam Consul." Voss's voice was level, unhurried. "I'll need perhaps two more days."

"Two days." Hale buttered her sawdust bread with the air of a woman negotiating with destiny. "The republic sends me its finest courier and asks her to linger in a city she has no orders to love. I hope at least the consulate is comfortable."

"It's warm. That's more than most of the city can claim."

"Mm. Yes." Hale let that sit. It had been offered honestly — she could tell; flattery sounded different — and honest observations about the city's suffering were currency she preferred to collect rather than spend. "Warmth is a policy decision these days. We ration it like everything else."

She let the table resume its noise, then, some minutes later, as casually as dealing a card: "Tell me, Voss. What do you know of General Aldric Fen?"

It was a small test, small enough that most people wouldn't notice being tested. The name had begun appearing in intercepted correspondence weeks ago — *the traitor Fen*, moving through the borderlands with a handful of loyalists, hunted by both sides now, which was generally the sign of a man who had done something interesting. Hale wanted to see whether the republic's finest courier had been briefed on him.

Something crossed Voss's face. It lasted less than a heartbeat — a flicker, a candle guttering in a draft — and then it was gone, replaced by the smooth professional blankness Hale had seen a thousand times on a thousand careful faces.

"Only the official account, madam Consul. A decorated officer who defected during the winter negotiations. The bulletins call him a traitor to the republic."

"And do you believe the bulletins?"

"I believe," said Voss, "that I carry sealed orders, not opinions."

Hale laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her. Around the table, the younger clerks relaxed; the consul's laughter was weather, and fair weather was rare. "Well answered. My attaché would have given me a speech." She raised her cup toward the far end of the table. "Two days, then, Courier Voss. Enjoy our hospitality while it lasts."

But she was watching the flicker's ghost, and filing it.

* * *

That afternoon, Hale climbed the consulate's tower — formerly the merchant's folly, currently the signal post — and stood with her hands clasped behind her back, looking north through the timber lattice at the enemy lines.

From up here Lantern's Reach arranged itself into something almost bearable. The smoke rose from a hundred chimneys in thin domestic threads. The market square was a scatter of color where women traded what could be traded. The granary district squatted along the river like a sleeping animal, and beyond it the orchards ran bare and grey toward the walls, row upon row of trees waiting with the terrible patience of things that had outlived several owners.

Her city. Not hers by birth — she'd been born four hundred miles south, in a town so small it appeared on maps mainly as a typographical error — but hers by twenty years of choosing. She had chosen it when it was prosperous and she had stayed when it stopped being, and there was a version of Beretha Hale, a younger version, who believed that staying was the whole of honor.

The younger version had not yet read the dispatches she'd read.

Because here was the truth that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone: she had known what was coming before the sealed orders ever reached her city. You did not hold a border consulate through five months of siege without learning to read the shape of silence from the capital. The republic had stopped answering her requests for reinforcement in the spring. Then it had stopped answering her requests for anything. A government that loves you sends supplies. A government that has written you off sends silence. And a government that intends to trade your city for time sends a courier.

When the dispatch had arrived — routed through her hands before going onward to the artillery coordination office, because protocol demanded the countersignature of the ranking civil authority — she had held it exactly as she imagined Voss held it: weighing it, feeling the weight of whatever was inside.

She hadn't opened it. Protocol. Also fear. She suspected those were the same thing wearing different coats.

But she had decoded the routing marks — she was a consul, not a child, and routing marks were how consuls kept governments honest, or at least informed — and the marks terminated at the granary district's coordinate registry. Terms for negotiation went to negotiation tables. Evacuation authorizations went to harbors and gates. Coordinates went to guns.

Lantern's Reach was not being defended. It was being priced.

And Beretha Hale, fifty-one years old, twenty years in service, keeper of the best candle in the consulate, had sat with that knowledge for eleven days and arrived at the arithmetic.

Here was the arithmetic: the republic was losing the war everywhere else. The eastern armies were retreating in good order, which was the polite phrase for running. The capital was hoarding its remaining strength for the defense of people who mattered — ministers, ministries, the districts with the good marble. If Lantern's Reach held out another season, it tied down enemy divisions the republic could not otherwise occupy. If it fell quickly — if it *burned* quickly — the enemy's momentum carried them straight into prepared defenses a hundred miles south, defenses that existed only because Lantern's Reach had bought the time to build them.

Sacrifice now bought the future later. That was the whole equation. Fourteen thousand lives in the balance column, an uncertain number of lives saved in the credit column, and Beretha Hale standing between them with a pen, deciding which number got to be larger.

She had made harder decisions than this. She reminded herself of that nightly, like a prayer that didn't work. She had ordered the bridge quarter flooded with families still on it. She had signed the requisition that took the winter blankets from the hospital for the front line. Every one of those nights she had slept badly, and slept anyway, because that was the job: you did the arithmetic, and then you lived in the answer.

What made this one different — what made her climb the tower every evening and look at her chimneys and her market and her bare grey orchards — was only that this time she could put faces to the numbers. Old Corvin and his ovens. The harbor-master's granddaughter, who waved at the tower every morning and believed the consul waved back, which Hale had made sure she did.

Arithmetic was easy. Faces were hard. That was why governments sent coordinates instead of letters.

"Madam Consul?" The signal clerk, a boy called Tam with cowlick hair and the nerve of someone too young to know better, hovered at the tower door. "Courier Voss is asking after you. Says she has questions about eastern road conditions."

"Does she." Hale did not turn around. "Send her up. And Tam — bring the good bottle from my private cabinet. The one I pretend doesn't exist."

"The one you keep pretending doesn't exist every time we have visitors?"

"The very same. Go."

* * *

Isandro Voss climbed the tower stairs like a woman ascending to a verdict, which Hale found amusing, since technically Hale was the one on trial — though neither of them would have admitted it. She arrived at the top slightly breathless from the narrow stair, and Hale gestured her to the parapet beside her and poured two fingers of plum brandy into cups that were older than both of them.

"Road conditions," Hale said, handing her a cup. "Let me guess. You want to know if the eastern passes are passable, and you want me to lie to you about it, and you want to be able to report home that you asked."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes again — that flicker, quicker each time, a draft getting into the candle. "I want accurate information, madam Consul."

"Of course you do. Here it is: the passes close within the week. Snow, then patrols, then worse patrols. After that, nothing leaves Lantern's Reach in any direction except by the grace of whichever army holds the gate that day." Hale drank. The brandy was excellent; she permitted herself excellent things precisely twice a year, on principle. "So. Two days, you said. Let us speak frankly, courier to consul, while the wind is loud enough that no one will repeat what we say."

Voss waited. She was good at waiting. Hale approved of it and distrusted it in equal measure.

"You've carried orders all your life," Hale said. "So have I, in my way — mine just arrive slower and come with more committees attached. And you know as well as I do what happens to couriers whose deliveries go astray. There is a record. The record follows you. The record *is* you, after enough years; the person underneath is just the thing carrying the record around." She turned to face Voss fully. "I am offering to make the record disappear."

For the first time, the composure shifted visibly. "Disappear?"

"There is a promotion pending. Deputy Director of Courier Services, capital posting, which means warmth and marble and a salary that lets you stop counting coins. It requires a recommendation from a ranking official in the field." Hale let that hang in the cold air. "I am that official. Deliver your dispatches on schedule, on the record, exactly as sealed, and I will write that recommendation tomorrow. And I will also write — separately, unofficially, into channels that can be quietly cleaned — a note regarding certain irregularities in your early service. The ones from the Marrowford incident."

She watched the name land. Marrowford. Twelve years ago, a young courier, a lost dispatch, a border incident that should have ended a career and somehow hadn't — buried deep, but Hale had read every file that crossed her desk for twenty years, and files talked to each other when no one was listening.

Voss had gone very still. It was the stillness of a person discovering the floor has opinions.

"You knew about Marrowford."

"I know about everything that matters. It's the job." Hale softened her voice by exactly one degree, which for her constituted tenderness. "Understand me, Courier Voss. I am not threatening you. I'm doing the opposite. I am trusting you — publicly, expensively, in writing. Do you know how rare that is? Power trusts almost no one, because power has usually earned the distrust. When it trusts you, it is handing you the only thing it actually owns." She looked out through the lattice at the darkening city, at the first lanterns being lit in the lower streets, little stubborn points of light against the siege-dark. "They will ask you, someday, whether you served a republic or a conscience, as if the two were different animals. They are not. The republic is the conscience — clumsy, cruel, slow, but it is the only conscience fourteen thousand people share. Sometimes keeping it alive costs the parts of it we love best. That is not treason. That is surgery."

The wind gusted through the lattice. Below them, a bakery chimney released a thread of woodsmoke, and somewhere a child shouted something joyful and unintelligible, because children did not read routing marks.

"Deliver on schedule," Hale said quietly. "Two days. Walk out the eastern gate with your seal intact and your record clean, and in five years you'll be directing the entire courier corps from a heated office while this city is a paragraph in someone's memoirs." She finished her brandy and set the cup on the parapet with a click. "Or refuse, and discover what the record does when it isn't erased. I say this with genuine affection, Voss. I've watched better people than both of us learn the difference between doubt and duty. Doubt is a luxury. Duty is a debt. Pay yours."

Voss drained her cup without tasting it, Hale noticed. A woman who loved plum brandy would have tasted it.

"I'll deliver the dispatches, madam Consul," she said. "On schedule."

"Of course you will." Hale smiled, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it. "You're the finest courier the republic has. It says so right here in your file."

She watched Voss descend the tower stairs — steady, correct, unhurried — and felt the peculiar vertigo of a woman who has just handed her knife to someone and cannot decide whether she's made a friend or fed a fire.

Then Tam appeared with the signal ledger, chattering about a flagged message from the western pickets, and Hale turned back to her city, her chimneys, her lanterns coming alight one by one below like a promise the dark intended to collect on.

The flagged message, when she read it, was routine: enemy patrols active near the western orchards, a checkpoint guard reporting unusual traffic on the river road.

Unusual traffic. Near the orchards.

Hale frowned at it for a long moment, the candle of her certainty guttering, and reached for her pen to order increased patrols west of the walls.
