## 11. The Face Remembered

The market district checkpoint was the worst kind: the honest kind.

Isandro had spent eleven years learning to read checkpoints the way other people read weather. The corrupt ones were easy — a courier's satchel and a silver coin moved through them like water through a cracked door. The bored ones were nearly as good; a man who had stood in the same spot for nine hours would wave through anything that wasn't on fire. But the honest ones — the young ones with something to prove, or the old ones with nothing left to lose but their diligence — those required care.

This one was both. A sergeant named, according to his chalked duty board, Ovist Rell, who had the gray at his temples of a man twenty years in uniform and the squared shoulders of one who still believed the uniform meant something. He was checking papers. Actually checking them. Turning them toward the light, comparing faces, doing the whole liturgy of the Republic of Vellamare as though the republic were not currently a candle burning down to its brass holder.

Isandro adjusted Maren's grip on her hand and put on her third-best smile. The first-best was for consuls. The second-best was for customs officers. The third-best was for moments when she intended to be nobody at all.

"Morning," she said. "Two travelers, going out."

"Going *out*?" The sergeant's eyebrows performed a small, skeptical migration. "Nobody goes out, madam. Everybody's trying to get in."

"We're the exception that proves the rule. My niece is frightened of the shelling. We have family in the hill villages." She produced two documents. Hers was genuine, if creatively annotated. Maren's was a forgery she had completed at four in the morning by candlelight, working from a dead colleague's blank travel pass and a memory of how the consulate clerk dipped his pen twice before signing, which gave the ink its characteristic double-weight stroke. It was, she reflected, a very good forgery. She had been proud of it, right up until this moment, standing in front of a man who checked things.

Sergeant Rell took the papers. He looked at hers, nodded, stamped it against his thigh out of habit rather than authority, and then turned to Maren's.

And here was the problem with six-year-olds: they could not be instructed the way dispatch cases could be instructed. Isandro had spent the walk over coaching Maren — *look bored, look like a child who has been dragged somewhere before dawn, which should not be difficult* — and Maren had absorbed perhaps half of it. She was looking bored. She was also looking directly up at Sergeant Rell with the frank, unguarded curiosity of a child who has not yet learned that faces are currency, tilting her head so the morning light fell full across it.

"Your niece is very fair," said Sergeant Rell.

"She takes after her mother."

"Mm." He did not look away. Something was happening behind his eyes — the slow, grinding work of a memory being consulted. Isandro felt it in her stomach before she understood it: the sensation of a floor deciding whether to remain a floor.

He reached into his coat and drew out a folded square of cheap paper, worn soft at the creases, and unfolded it beside Maren's forged pass.

WANTED BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COMMAND. The woodcut portrait was crude — they always were, faces rendered by men who thought of people as categories — but the eyes survived even bad engraving, and Maren's eyes were unmistakable. Wide-set, dark, serious past their years. Below the image: *Daughter of the traitor Aldric Fen. To be detained on sight. Reward: forty crowns and commendation.*

Forty crowns, Isandro thought absurdly. They've priced a general's daughter at forty crowns. You couldn't buy a decent horse for forty crowns. You could barely buy a disappointing mule.

"Interesting," said Sergeant Rell, in a voice that suggested he found nothing interesting about it at all, except professionally. "Very interesting. Your niece here looks a great deal like a fugitive."

"Half the children in Vellamare look like somebody's fugitive," Isandro said. "It's a feature of children. They all have that hunted quality. Mine especially — she broke a jar of preserved plums last week and hasn't stopped looking guilty since."

"This isn't plums, madam." He held the circular steady. His hand was not quite steady itself, which told her he was new to this particular errand. Men who had done terrible things often had steadier hands than men contemplating their first one. "This is the Fen girl. There are soldiers three posts down who've been promised leave if they find her. Real leave. Home leave."

"And what have you been promised?"

He blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. What's your cut of the forty crowns? Or does your commanding officer keep the reward and give you the commendation? Because I've read the commendation schedule, sergeant, and I can tell you exactly what 'commendation' translates to in the current army: a certificate, a handshake, and being sent wherever the fighting is thickest, because clearly you're lucky."

Maren tugged Isandro's hand. "Auntie," she whispered, at a volume audible in the next district, "why is that man holding my picture?"

"*Not* your picture," Isandro said. "Eat your bread."

"I don't have any bread."

"That's why you're hungry."

Sergeant Rell looked from the child to the woman and back again, and Isandro watched him arrive at the arithmetic every honest man at a collapsing border eventually arrives at: the sum of what the Republic owed him versus the sum of what it had taken. Twenty years of gray temples. A war going badly enough that the government was issuing circulars about six-year-olds. Whatever the Republic was paying him, it was paying it in a currency that was visibly losing value by the hour.

"It's her," he said quietly. Not accusingly. Almost apologetically. "I'm not a fool, madam. I know whose face that is. And I know what happens to her if I call it in. I also know what happens to me if I don't and someone finds out."

"Yes," Isandro said. "That's the whole design, isn't it? Everyone frightened of everyone else, so no one has to think." She shifted her weight. Under her coat, against her ribs, hung the thing she had been carrying for eleven years and had never once considered surrendering: her courier's seal. Lead and silver on a chain, worn smooth where her thumb worried it during long rides. It was the mark of her office, older than her rank, older than her name in the rolls. With it, she could open doors, close inquiries, command horses and beds and silence. Without it, she was a woman with a satchel and an increasingly creative relationship with the truth.

She had known since the orchard — since she'd left half a blanket tied to a bare branch like a love letter written in cloth — that it might come to this. She had told herself she was prepared. Preparation, it turned out, was a word people used for the moment before the actual thing arrived and revealed preparation to have been a rumor.

"Sergeant Rell," she said. "May I show you something?"

"If it's another document—"

"It isn't a document." She lifted the chain over her head. The seal swung in the gray morning light, catching it briefly, and she saw his eyes change — the way soldiers' eyes changed when they saw the courier's mark, because couriers were the Republic's nervous system, and everyone knew you didn't touch the nervous system, whatever else you did to the body. "Do you know what this is worth? Not its metal. Its use."

"Anyone caught forging that swings," he said.

"Anyone *using* it doesn't swing for years. Doors open. Questions close. If I handed this to you — if I *gave* it to you, freely, no trickery, witnessed only by a child who eats imaginary bread — you would hold more power in your fist than you'll earn in twenty more years of service. Enough power to get your family out of this city before the spring offensives. Enough to buy passage anywhere the war isn't." She let it hang between them, turning slowly. "Or you can have forty crowns and a certificate."

The checkpoint queue behind them grumbled. Somewhere down the street a baker's boy was arguing with a woman about the price of flour, which had apparently doubled overnight, which meant the siege mathematics were tightening, which meant everyone in this city was already doing this same arithmetic at their own scales — what do I keep, what do I trade, what do I lose.

"Why?" Sergeant Rell asked. Not *why are you offering*. The real question, the one underneath. "Why would a courier throw away everything for—" he glanced down "—for a girl who isn't even yours?"

Isandro considered lying to him. She had gotten remarkably good at lying lately; it was the one skill her career had failed to teach her and the siege had supplied in abundance. But there was something in his face — the same something she'd seen in Aldric Fen's face across a frozen river a lifetime ago — that deserved better than craft.

"Because someone once refused to look at my face when looking would have cost him," she said. "And I've been repaying the debt ever since, badly, in installments. This is just the largest installment." She held out the seal. "Take it."

He didn't move. "You understand what you're giving up."

"I understand it better every second, which is why we should conclude the transaction before I come to my senses."

"Auntie," Maren whispered, "what's a transaction?"

"Hush, darling, the adults are ruining their lives."

And that — absurdly, blessedly — was what broke him. Sergeant Rell laughed. It was a short, startled laugh, the kind a man produces when grief and comedy collide inside him at speed, and while he was laughing Isandro pressed the seal into his palm and closed his fingers over it, and the weight of eleven years left her hand so gently she almost missed the moment it happened.

He stared at his own fist. Then he tore Maren's forged pass in half, dropped the halves into the brazier beside his post, and waved them through with a stamped chit for the outer road. "Niece frightened of shelling," he recited flatly, for the record of anyone watching. "Family in the hills. Good day, madam."

"Sergeant." She paused, one step past the barrier, and turned back. "When the spring comes — take the passage. Don't wait to see how it ends. Men who wait to see how it ends always end up explaining it to a tribunal."

"Is that what you're doing? Waiting to see how it ends?"

"No," Isandro said. "I'm carrying something the wrong direction. It's different."

They walked. The outer road unspooled ahead of them, rutted and pale, running along the frozen canal toward the orchard country. Behind them the city made its small stubborn noises — ovens, arguments, a bell that had been cracked since autumn but rang anyway, out of principle. Isandro kept waiting for the grief to hit, the loss of the seal, the end of the woman she had been at breakfast yesterday. Instead what she felt was lighter than grief and stranger: the specific vertigo of having stepped off a ledge and discovered that falling, at least so far, resembled flying closely enough to be mistaken for it.

Maren walked beside her, swinging her arms, and after a quarter mile said, "You gave that man your shiny necklace."

"Yes."

"For me."

Isandro looked down. The child was watching the road, not her, which was either tact or coincidence, and with six-year-olds the two were indistinguishable.

"Yes," Isandro said. "For you."

"My papa says you shouldn't owe anybody anything."

"Your papa is a very wise man who owes me a river."

"What river?"

"The kind you freeze in January and fall through in February." She squeezed the small hand. "He'll explain someday. Or I will, when you're older and less likely to repeat things to soldiers."

"He already repeats things to soldiers," Maren said. "That's how we got here."

Isandro stopped walking. "What do you mean, sweetheart? When did you talk to soldiers?"

Maren shrugged the elaborate shrug of a child being asked to perform memory on demand. "Before the farm. Papa's friends came, and then soldiers came asking questions, and Papa got quiet the way he does, and then it was night and the fire and he said *run* and I ran." She said it the way she reported everything — plainly, without the adult machinery of dread — and somehow that made it worse. "Papa talks to everybody. That's his whole job. Talking until people stop being angry."

*Yes,* Isandro thought, walking again, faster now. *That was always his gift. And look what the Republic made of it.* A peacetime diplomat turned traitor; a man whose voice had once settled disputes between nations now hiding in orchards, sending his daughter through enemy lines because a child was the one courier no one searched.

The thought snagged, as it had been snagging for days, on a single splinter: *no one searched.* No soldier would intercept a child. That was Fen's whole design, the note had said as much — forget the face, the message too dangerous to write, delivered in skin and bone. Which meant the child was the message. Which meant—

Which meant whoever had carried Maren this far knew things. Knew routes, knew names, knew what the Republic was planning for Lantern's Reach. And Sef Andrel had been asking questions all week in that bright, eager, ambitious voice of his, questions dressed as concern, concern dressed as duty. Isandro had watched him watch her across the consulate dining table and told herself it was professional jealousy, the ordinary friction between a senior courier and a hungry one.

But Sef had access to the routing rooms. Sef had a wax impression kit and nimble hands and a future he believed the war owed him. And if Sef ever grew curious enough to press a duplicate seal into soft clay and open the dispatch she had sworn to deliver unread —

She looked down at Maren, trotting along beside her with the wanted face of a traitor's daughter and the unbothered stride of a child on an outing.

"There's no un-carrying you," Isandro murmured. "I hope you know that."

"Un-carrying what?" said Maren.

"Nothing, darling. Keep walking. We have an orchard to reach before dark."

Behind them, at the checkpoint, Sergeant Rell stood a long moment with a courier's seal burning a hole in his pocket like a coal of a different fire — and then turned back to his post, his circulars, and the long gray morning, saying nothing to anyone.

Yet.

* * *

They made the tree line by late afternoon. Isandro checked the sky, checked the road behind them, checked the small warm weight of Maren's hand — and found, tucked into the bark of the boundary oak where no traveler would ever look, a strip of linen matching the half-blanket she had left a week ago, knotted in a way she recognized.

Fen had received her signal.

And beneath the knot, pressed flat into the wood as if with a knife, was a second mark — one Isandro did not recognize, precise and official, the kind of mark a trained courier leaves without thinking.

Someone else had found the orchard first.
