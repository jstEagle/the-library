## 12. Sef Opens the Seal

The theft, when Sef Andrel finally committed it, took less time than choosing the wine at Consul Hale's table had. That was the thing about dishonesty nobody warned you about: the anticipation was a mountain, and the deed itself was a doorstep.

He had spent three days telling himself he wasn't going to do it.

He spent the fourth day doing it.

It happened in the courier's annex of the Lantern's Reach consulate, in the grey hour between the night watch changing and the kitchens waking — an hour Sef had learned, over eleven years of service, belonged to no one. The braziers burned low. A clerk named Ovist slept at his desk with his cheek on a ledger, snoring in a rhythm that suggested he dreamed of arithmetic. Sef stepped past him with the exaggerated delicacy of a man who believed his own guilt could be heard.

Isandro Voss's seal sat where she always left it: in the felt-lined tray by the dispatch window, because she was the sort of courier who believed a seal should rest when its bearer did. *Superstitious*, Sef had thought once. Now he understood it differently. She trusted the building. She trusted the institution. She trusted the republic the way other people trusted the ground beneath their feet.

Sef had stopped trusting the ground some years ago. He'd simply never told anyone, least of all himself.

The duplicate impression was already made — wax pressed into plaster on Tuesday, while Isandro stood in the courtyard arguing with a mule driver about grain tariffs, her back turned for perhaps forty seconds. Forty seconds. Sef had counted them twice afterward, horrified at how much of a life could be counterfeited in forty seconds.

Now he set the duplicate beside the sealed dispatch she had locked in her document case, and the lock — this was almost insulting — yielded to the same key that opened every courier's case in the Republic of Vellamare. Standard issue. Uniformity, the manuals said, prevented confusion in the field.

Sef thought: *uniformity prevents nothing except the discovery of theft.*

* * *

He did not open it immediately. That was the strange part, the part he would turn over in his mind for years afterward like a stone with something underneath it. He had the seal impression in one hand and the dispatch in the other, and instead of pressing wax to paper he sat down on Ovist's spare stool and looked at the fire and thought about his mother.

This was not sentimentality, he told himself. This was method. A courier who acted without deliberation was a courier who made errors; the manuals were clear on this. Deliberation was simply professionalism wearing a dressing gown.

His mother had sold fish in the Terraza market in Vellamare city, and she had a saying for everything, most of them unrepeatable, but the one that surfaced now was this: *"A man who opens a letter he shouldn't has already read it. The rest is just deciding what kind of liar to be."*

She had said it about his father, actually, and a letter from a woman in Halvern, and the memory was not entirely comforting. But the shape of it held.

Because here was the truth Sef had been circling for days: Isandro Voss had hesitated. He had watched her hesitate the way a man watches weather. She'd arrived in Lantern's Reach with her usual economy, her usual precision, and then — small things. She'd taken two hours longer than necessary on a route that ran through the ration quarter. She'd asked the consulate steward, casually, how many civilians remained inside the walls, and then looked surprised at herself for asking. She'd stood at the dispatch window last night holding the sealed orders against the lamplight for a long moment, as if weight alone might tell her what was inside.

And she had not delivered them. Four days in the city, four days of "awaiting confirmation from the field office," which was a phrase couriers used the way physicians used *the patient is resting*: technically true, and hiding everything that mattered.

Sef Andrel was twenty-nine years old, deputy courier, third in line for a district posting if the war didn't eat the district first, and he had built his entire career on being the man who noticed things. Noticing was his loyalty. Noticing was his ambition's instrument. And what he had noticed was that Isandro Voss — the finest courier the republic had produced in a generation, the woman whose record was quoted in training lectures — was stalling.

There were only two explanations. Either she was compromised, or she knew something.

Both possibilities required him to know something too.

*You can't report hesitation*, he reasoned, pressing the warm plaster gently to fresh wax, warming it first over the brazier until it softened to the exact temperature he'd practiced on discarded seals. *Hesitation isn't evidence. But the contents of those orders — those are evidence.*

That was the sentence he would remember. *Those are evidence.* As if he were opening the dispatch for the republic. As if this were diligence. Loyalty revealed, he would think much later, is often just ambition that has learned to wear a uniform, and Sef was buttoning the collar even now, even here, telling himself stories with official-sounding words.

The wax took the impression cleanly. Too cleanly. It looked exactly like hers, which meant that whatever he did next, he had already done it as far as any tribunal would ever care.

He broke the seal on Isandro Voss's final mission.

* * *

The dispatch was short. That was the first wrongness. Diplomatic orders ran long — protocols, contingencies, phrases like *in the event of negotiation failure* repeated until they lost meaning. This was a single sheet, dense, and it began not with terms but with coordinates.

Sef read it standing up. Then he read it sitting down. Then he read it a third time with his finger under each line like a boy sounding out scripture, because comprehension kept sliding off him, refusing to land.

It was a targeting grid.

Not a treaty. Not terms. Not the last diplomatic appeal of a collapsing republic to a border city it claimed to love. It was a map of Lantern's Reach divided into numbered squares, each square annotated with ordnance type and firing solution, cross-referenced to artillery positions he recognized from the siege lines south of the river. The granary district — where the ovens still lit every morning, where the baker's boy waved at couriers from the loading dock — was marked in red ink with a notation Sef had only ever seen in theoretical exercises:

*Priority saturation. Expected structural collapse. Civilian presence assessed as unavoidable.*

*Assessed as unavoidable.*

He read that phrase four times. Each time it got heavier, the way wet wool gets heavier. Somewhere behind him, Ovist snored on, dreaming his arithmetic dreams, and outside the window the city was waking — he could hear the water carts, the first argument of the day at the gate market, a child singing something tuneless and triumphant.

They had sent Isandro Voss to carry the death warrant of everyone singing in it.

* * *

Sef did the math because math was what he did when the world went wrong. It was his version of prayer.

The granary district held — conservatively, using the census figures posted in the consulate corridor, which he had walked past a hundred times without reading — roughly nine thousand people. Nine thousand, in squares seven through fourteen. Square nine included the orphanage on Candlemaker's Row. He knew the orphanage because he'd donated to its winter fund last year, two silver bits, feeling extremely generous, receiving a paper lantern with his name inked on it in a child's careful hand. The lantern was packed in his trunk. He had been saving it to give to someone someday, though he'd never decided who.

Square eleven covered the consulate itself.

He looked at that for a while.

Consul Beretha Hale was in square eleven. Deputy Courier Sef Andrel was in square eleven. Whatever bargain the republic thought it was striking, whoever had drawn these grids had priced the consulate into the exchange rate. *Expected losses among diplomatic personnel acceptable given strategic value of denial.*

Denial. They weren't defending Lantern's Reach or abandoning it. They were destroying it so the enemy couldn't have it — the granaries, the mills, the deep-water cisterns. Scorched earth, written in the polite language of logistics. The republic was dying, and like anything dying it had begun consuming its own limbs, and the limb currently on the menu contained nine thousand people and one paper lantern with his name on it.

Sef put the dispatch down very carefully on the desk, as though it might go off.

Then he laughed. One short, appalled bark of a laugh, instantly strangled. Ovist stirred, muttered something about column sums, and slept on.

Because here was the joke, and it was a terrible one: Sef had opened the seal to gather evidence against Isandro Voss. He had constructed, over four sleepless nights, a whole tidy architecture of duty. *She's compromised. She's carrying private grievances. Fen, the traitor general — there are rumors, and rumors say she has history with him. My responsibility is to the chain of command. If she breaks her oath, I report her, and I do my job.*

And the contents of the orders said, in the cleanest possible language: *she's right to stall.*

The evidence exonerated the accused. Worse — it convicted the court.

* * *

He sat with it for the better part of an hour, and during that hour he conducted, silently and with great rigor, the trial of Sef Andrel versus Sef Andrel.

The prosecution went first, and the prosecution was good. The prosecution wore his own voice, the official one, the one he used in reports. *You are a courier. You do not choose what you carry; that is the entire meaning of the profession. The moment a courier starts reading the mail, the republic stops having a postal service and starts having a hundred little kings. Isandro may already be reading it. If you cover for her, you're not sparing the city — you're just distributing the treason more evenly. Report her. Report what you found, how you found it, take your lumps for the seal, and be the man who caught the leak before the traitor general's network swallowed the last diplomatic channel out of Lantern's Reach. There is a career in that. There is honor in that. There is a version of this story where you are the loyal one.*

It was a fine speech. He'd have believed it completely, once.

The defense rose, and the defense was tired, and the defense sounded uncomfortably like his mother. *"Loyal,"* the defense said. *"To what? Show me the thing you're being loyal to. Point at it."*

And that was the wound of it. Because Sef Andrel loved the republic — he genuinely did, in the way you love a house you grew up in, with its drafts and its bad corners and its smell of specific bread — but the republic had died somewhere on the road between the capital and this city, and what remained was a machinery grinding through its motions, and the machinery had just handed him a grid with nine thousand people in it and called the deaths *unavoidable*.

Unavoidable. As if avoidance hadn't been the entire job description. As if everything Sef had done for eleven years — the frozen passes, the bribed ferrymen, the night rides through territory that shot at lantern-light — hadn't been one long exercise in making the avoidable avoided.

If the republic wanted him to deliver inevitability, it had hired the wrong romantic.

But — and here his hand shook slightly as he reached for the candle — knowing was not the same as choosing. There was still a door marked *report her*. It led to survival, promotion, and complicity dressed as duty. And there was another door with no markings at all, and he had no idea where it went, and Isandro Voss was standing in front of it, and he suspected she'd been standing there longer than he had.

He could not un-know what the orders said. That was the price of the seal, paid in advance. The question left was only this: did he tell the machinery what he'd learned, or did he tell the woman?

* * *

He restored everything. That was the discipline in him, the old reliable craft. Fresh wax, warmed and matched in color to the original seal's amber-red; the duplicate impression applied with a steadiness that surprised him; the dispatch returned to the case; the case locked; the key returned. He cleaned the plaster mold and buried the shavings in the brazier ash. By the time the kitchen bells rang the morning hour, the room looked exactly as it had, and only Sef Andrel was different.

Ovist woke, blinked at him, and said, "You're here early."

"Couldn't sleep," Sef said. "Worrying about the tariff schedules."

"Tariff schedules," Ovist said with deep sympathy, "have ruined better men than us," and put his head back down on the ledger.

Sef walked out into the courtyard, where the light was coming up grey-gold over the wall, and the baker's boy was hauling flour sacks and singing his tuneless song, and somewhere in the ration quarter nine thousand people were beginning a day they did not know had been priced.

He found Isandro at the dispatch window, checking her case against the manifest — her morning ritual, precise as liturgy. She glanced up. Her eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, to the document case. To the seal.

For half a heartbeat neither of them moved.

Then Sef Andrel, deputy courier, loyal servant of the Republic of Vellamare, holder of eleven years of unblemished service and one freshly broken law, smiled the smile of a man reporting for ordinary duty, and said:

"Voss. Walk with me to breakfast. I think we need to talk about the route south."

Her face did not change. But her hands went still on the manifest, and he watched her understand — watch her arrive, in the space of a breath, at the same terrible country he had just entered — and she closed the case softly, like a door shutting on a room where something precious was sleeping, and said,

"Lead the way."

Behind them, in the felt-lined tray, her seal sat gleaming in the early light, and it was no longer the most dangerous object in the building.
