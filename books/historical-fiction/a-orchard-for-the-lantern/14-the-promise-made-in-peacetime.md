## 14. The Promise Made in Peacetime

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Isandro finally sat at the little table in the consulate's back room and let herself remember.

She did this the way she did everything: deliberately, by habit, as if memory were a dispatch case that could be opened on command. She set her hands flat on the wood — the same posture she used for reading difficult orders — and went back.

Not to the war. Not to the checkpoints, the ration lines, the child sleeping three doors away with her boots still on because she wouldn't let anyone take them off. Isandro went back further, past all of that, to a winter so long ago it belonged to another republic entirely — one that still believed in itself, and in her.

* * *

The river had been called the Merle then, before anyone thought to rename it after a general or a treaty. It ran slow and green through the valley below Vellamare's summer conference grounds, and it froze every year into a road of white glass that the locals trusted and everyone else was warned about.

Isandro was twenty-three, newly badged, and insufferably proud of both facts. She had been sent to the peace talks not as a delegate but as a courier-in-waiting — which meant she carried nothing but her own importance and stood at walls looking available. It was, she would later reflect, the last year of her life in which she was genuinely bored.

The delegation from Osterhale had brought General Aldric Fen.

She remembered being disappointed by him at first. Traitor-general-to-be, though no one knew it yet; in those days he was only the man who had ended the border war, and she had expected someone carved out of bronze. Instead there came across the frozen courtyard a broad, rumpled man in a coat two seasons out of fashion, eating a roasted chestnut with the concentration of a scholar deciphering a text, who nodded to her — *her*, the lowest-ranking person in the courtyard — and said, "You're the one who knows where things are."

"I know where the dispatch room is," she said carefully.

"That's what I said." He held out the paper bag of chestnuts. "Take one. They're terrible. The cook salts them like he hates us personally, and it's important that someone else suffer."

She took one. He was right; they were terrible. She ate three more over the course of the morning, and by afternoon they were arguing about whether the bridge at Kessel was a masterpiece of engineering or a monument to a committee, and by evening she had forgotten entirely that she was supposed to be standing at attention somewhere.

That was how Fen worked, though she didn't have the vocabulary for it yet. He didn't recruit people. He simply made himself impossible not to talk to, and then he listened the way other men gave orders — completely, and expecting to be obeyed.

* * *

It happened on the fourth day, in the way disasters do: quickly, stupidly, and in front of witnesses.

There had been a thaw that afternoon — one of those false springs winter likes to offer as an insult — and then the temperature fell off a cliff at sundown, and the ice that had been merely unreliable became a trap with a fresh skin of water over it. A boy from the village had gone out after his dog. The dog came back. The boy did not.

Isandro heard the shouting from the wall and did the arithmetic without knowing she was doing it: the boy was forty yards out, the ice was groaning in a voice she'd never heard ice make, and none of the soldiers on the bank could swim, because it turned out that a garrison raised in the mountains considers swimming a foreign language.

She was already running before she decided anything. That was the part that stayed with her afterward — that there was no decision. There was just her body doing what her body apparently believed in, flat across the ice, the cold coming up through her coat like a hand closing around her ankle.

She reached the hole. She got one arm around the boy — he was small, blue, clawing at her with the blind strength of drowning things — and then the ice under them said a word in its groaning language and gave way, and the Merle took them both.

People imagine drowning as chaos. It isn't. It's very quiet and very organized. The cold took inventory of her — fingers, feet, breath, will — and confiscated each item in turn. She remembered thinking, with strange courier's clarity, *I am carrying nothing. This is the first day of my life I am carrying nothing,* and finding the thought unbearable rather than peaceful.

Then something closed around her collar from above.

Fen had come out onto the ice lying flat, distributed like a man who understood load-bearing surfaces better than any soldier ought to, dragging a coil of rope from the boathouse behind him. He got her by the collar and the boy by the hood and hauled them both up onto the sound ice with a strength that seemed unfair in a man built mostly of conversation, and then the soldiers finally arrived to help, which is to say they arrived.

They wrapped her in blankets that smelled of horse. Someone pressed a flask to her lips. And Fen crouched down in front of her, soaked to the shoulder, hair frozen into something between a helmet and a joke, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't read at the time and would spend years learning to read.

"You went out without rope," he said.

"There wasn't time."

"There's always time for rope. You just didn't take it." He said it without heat, the way he said most true things. "Next time, you take the rope. Or you wait for me."

"You were eating chestnuts," she said, through chattering teeth.

"I'm always eating chestnuts. It doesn't mean I'm not watching." He pulled the blanket higher around her shoulders, briskly, almost roughly — the gentleness of a man embarrassed by his own gentleness. "What's your name?"

"Isandro. Isandro Voss."

"Well, Isandro Voss. You've got the only kind of courage worth having and none of the sense that keeps it alive. Somebody should look after you until you grow the sense yourself." He stood, joints cracking audibly in the cold. "Fortunately, somebody has nothing but free time and terrible chestnuts."

* * *

The rest of the talks lasted eleven days, and she spent nearly all of them within earshot of Aldric Fen, partly by assignment and partly because everywhere he went, the interesting parts of the day followed like ducklings.

He taught her things no instructor ever had. How to read a room by where the servants' eyes went. Why the third offer is always the honest one. Which of the delegates lied with their hands still and which couldn't keep their thumbs quiet. Once, watching Consul Beretha Hale — young then, sharp then, already dangerous — work a reception, Fen had murmured, "That one believes the ends will apologize for the means. Mark my words, Voss: the means never write back."

And once — this was the night before the delegations dispersed, the last evening of the last easy winter either of them would see — he found her on the wall above the frozen river, looking down at the dark scar in the ice where she had gone through, and sat beside her without asking permission, because he never asked permission for anything except, later, that one thing.

"Do you know why you jumped?" he asked.

"Because a boy was drowning."

"Because you're a carrier," he said. "You've made carrying into a religion. Orders, dispatches, secrets, drowning boys — if someone hands it to you, you'll get it there. It's a fine religion. It'll be honored all your life." He turned the chestnut bag over in his hands, empty now, and folded it into a small tight square. "But someday the republic is going to ask you to carry something you can't survive delivering. Something that weighs nothing at all and still breaks your back. And on that day, the religion won't save you. Only a promise might."

"What promise?"

"The one I'm about to extract from you." He looked out over the ice, and for a moment the humor went out of him entirely, and she saw underneath it the man the histories would eventually describe with words like *implacable* — though the histories, she thought now, would get even him wrong. "Someday I may need to send for you. Not through channels. Not with seals and couriers and all the machinery that can be intercepted, or forged, or bought. Just — sent for. And when that happens, I want you to come. Without asking why. Without waiting for authorization. Can you swear that?"

"Fen," she said, laughing a little because the alternative was to notice how serious he was, "you're a general of Osterhale and I'm a courier who fell in a river. What could you possibly need me for?"

"Exactly the thing I can't tell you, or the asking-why would start there instead of later." He held out his hand. It was a working hand, scarred across the palm from some old business he'd never explained. "Swear it or don't. I'll think no worse of you. Well — slightly worse. But only slightly, and I'll hide it beautifully."

And here was the thing she had been circling all night at the consulate table, the thing the candle had burned low to illuminate:

*She swore easily.*

That was the whole of it, the whole terrible simplicity. No hesitation. No conditions, no clauses, no courier's instinct to verify provenance first. The ice was groaning below them and her teeth were still chattering and a man who had pulled her out of a river four days after meeting her held out a scarred hand, and she took it and said, "Then I'll come. Whenever you send. I'll come without asking why."

He'd squeezed her hand once, hard, and said, "Good. Now come inside before you freeze again. If you die, I'll have wasted a perfectly good rescue, and I hate waste."

They had gone in together. In the morning the delegations left. She saw him twice more in peacetime — once at a review in the capital, once entirely by accident at a ferry crossing, where he bought her lunch and complained about chestnuts in a new province — and then the war came, and the war ate peacetime whole, and Aldric Fen became a name spoken in the tone reserved for traitors, and the promise became a stone at the bottom of her that she stopped visiting.

Until a note appeared on the consulate steps in a handwriting she'd know anywhere, four words long, and the stone rose to the surface and demanded air.

*Forget the face.*

As if she could. As if the face weren't the whole ledger — the chestnuts, the rope, the scarred hand, the voice saying *someday the republic will ask you to carry something that weighs nothing at all.*

* * *

Isandro looked down at her own hands on the table. They were steady. Courier's hands. They had carried everything the republic had ever handed them, without question, without opening, without trembling — and tomorrow morning, if she kept faith with her oath of office, they would carry a set of orders to their destination, and Lantern's Reach would learn what weighed nothing and broke backs.

Maren was asleep down the hall, boots on, one fist curled against her cheek, breathing in the small stubborn rhythm of a child who had run through burning fields and still refused to surrender her shoes.

*If I send for you, come without asking why.*

He hadn't even sent for her properly, she realized. He had sent his daughter. Because a six-year-old girl was the one cargo no checkpoint would search hard, no soldier would shoot at, no censor would dare intercept — because even in wartime, some things are still beneath suspicion. He had spent his child the way she had spent her seal: as currency. The difference, she thought, and the thought cut, was that he had known exactly what he was spending, and she had only just begun to count.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps sending Maren had cost him something no oath could measure, and the note's four words were all he could afford of explanation, because explanation takes time and time was the one thing the siege was spending faster than blood.

Either way, the promise stood. It stood the way the ice had stood — apparently solid, tested only once, and never truly forgotten by the river underneath.

She rose, took her cloak from its hook, and paused at the door, listening to the wind come off the walls and the far, patient thunder of artillery that had learned to sleep lightly between barrages.

Eleven days of peacetime, and she had sworn away her future in one handshake, laughing, certain it would never be called due.

Somewhere beyond the siege lines, in whatever ruin or hollow he was hiding in, a man who hated waste was waiting to find out whether his rescue had been well spent.

Isandro pulled up her hood and went to find out how one sends word to a dead man living in a derelict orchard — and what, exactly, she was willing to carry to reach him.
