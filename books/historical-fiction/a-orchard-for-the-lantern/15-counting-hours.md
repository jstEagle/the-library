## 15. Counting Hours

### Isandro

The forgery began with a cup of tea.

Isandro had learned this years ago from an old courier named Pellam, who claimed that steady hands were made of warm water and patience, and that any man who forged documents with cold fingers deserved the firing squad he got. She warmed her hands around the chipped consulate crockery and looked at what she had assembled on the washstand: a sheet of dispatch paper of the correct grain, a stick of sealing wax she'd melted down and re-poured twice to get the color right, a pen knife for trimming, and the routing book of a dead colleague whose effects nobody had yet claimed.

*Forgive me, Vessik,* she thought, though Vessik had died at Marrow Ford two winters past and could hardly object. His hand had been rounder than hers, more patient with its flourishes, and she had spent three evenings practicing it against his old letters until she could write *Lantern's Reach* without thinking.

That was the trick of forgery, Pellam had said. Not the letters. The not-thinking.

The true orders sat in her satchel like a stone in a bread loaf. She had not opened them — she still hadn't opened them — but she no longer needed to. The routing marks told their own story: *GR-7*, granary district, sector seven; *PRIORITY: SILENT*; a delivery code reserved not for negotiating tables or consular desks but for forward artillery command. The republic was not sending terms to Lantern's Reach. It was sending aim.

So she was writing new orders.

Not new orders, precisely. New *directions*. The same seal, the same weight, the same official gravity — but routed to the eastern orchard road, to a farmhouse ruin where a man who owed her a life might intercept them and see with his own eyes what his republic intended. Let Fen read the targeting grid himself. Let him know she hadn't lied to him. Let him have the proof he'd need to move whoever could still be moved out of the granary quarter before dawn on the appointed day.

It wasn't saving the city. She was honest enough with herself to know that. A city of forty thousand did not fit in a courier's satchel, and no rerouting would carry it away. But the granary district held eight thousand people, maybe nine, packed into the old merchant rows because it was the last quarter with working wells. If even half of them heard the shells coming—

She dipped the pen. Her hand, thank you, Pellam, was warm.

She wrote: *To the commander of forward artillery, by hand of the bearer only.* Then she stopped, because there was a knock at her door, light and arrhythmic, in a code she had taught exactly one person.

"Small mouse," Isandro called softly, "the cat is at dinner."

The door opened six inches and Maren's face appeared in the gap, solemn as a magistrate's. "Sef is back," she whispered. "He came through the kitchen and asked Cook where your room was, and Cook said you were resting, and then he stood in the hall for a long time just standing."

"Just standing?"

"Military standing." Maren demonstrated, spine straight, chin tucked, hands clasped behind her back, which on a six-year-old was so absurd that Isandro had to bite down on a laugh. "I don't like how he stands," Maren concluded. "It's like he's waiting for something to be wrong."

Children saw truly, Isandro thought, because they hadn't been trained out of it. "Thank you, little mouse. Go back to the linen room. Count your hiding places again."

"I have four now," Maren said with pride. "The linen room is only for beginners."

She vanished. Isandro listened to Sef's boots pass her door — slow, deliberate, pausing — and then continue on toward his own room, where, no doubt, he was also doing something with paper and wax.

Everyone in this house was writing tonight. Everyone was counting hours.

She bent back over the dispatch and finished the sentence in Vessik's patient, rounded hand.

### Sef Andrel

Deputy Courier Sef Andrel had drafted his report eleven times, and every draft was worse than the one before.

The first had been honest: *I believe Courier Voss intends to divert or destroy the sealed dispatch entrusted to her.* He had read it aloud in the privacy of his room and heard what it sounded like — the whine of a junior officer denouncing his senior on suspicion alone, on a *feeling*, on the fact that she had looked at him strangely across the consular table and he hadn't liked it.

Hale would eat him alive. Worse, Voss would eat him alive, and she would do it politely, with citations, in front of people.

The second draft hedged: *Certain irregularities in Courier Voss's conduct...* No. Too soft. If he reported and she was cleared, *irregularities* made him a fool.

The third draft accused her outright of treason. He burned that one in the candle flame with a certain satisfaction, watching *traitor* curl up black, because it had felt good to write and therefore could not be trusted.

Now, eleventh draft, he sat with the pen hovering and admitted the thing underneath all the others, the thing he had been circling for two days like a moth around a lamp: he didn't actually know what she was going to do. He knew what she *might* do. He knew what the signs suggested. And he knew — this was the humiliating part, the part he could never put in a report — that he wanted her to be guilty.

Because if Isandro Voss, the celebrated courier, the woman whose name was spoken in training halls as a byword for incorruptibility — if *she* was breaking her oath, then the oath itself was breakable. Then his own hesitation, his own sleepless nights, his own horrible suspicion about what the sealed orders contained, was not weakness but clarity. If she fell, he would not be the most frightened man in Lantern's Reach anymore. He would simply be the honest one.

*Courier Voss has shown marked reluctance regarding her current assignment,* he wrote. *She has questioned the routing, delayed confirmation of receipt, and has been observed—*

Observed doing what? Walking in the evening? Talking to a child?

His pen hovered. Downstairs, faintly, he could hear the consulate settling into night: Cook banking the stove, a shutter catching the wind. Somewhere above him, Voss's floorboards creaked in the rhythm of someone pacing, or writing, or both.

He thought about the duplicate seal impression he had lifted from her desk three days ago — pressed into a scrap of wax while she bathed, a small theft that had seemed like diligence at the time and now sat in his breast pocket feeling increasingly like evidence of his own treason. He had told himself he would use it to verify her seal if forged papers appeared. He had not told himself why he expected forged papers to appear.

He wrote instead: *Requesting guidance. Requesting relief of ambiguity.*

Then he crossed it out, because no report in the history of the Republic of Vellamare had ever contained the phrase *relief of ambiguity*, and a man who wrote such a thing deserved whatever happened to him.

What he finally set down was short, and he hated it:

*I will confirm within forty-eight hours, by direct observation, whether Courier Voss has compromised the dispatch. Until then I request no action, lest we alert her prematurely.*

There. It committed him to nothing and everything. He signed it, folded it, and addressed it to the garrison adjutant, to be sent with tomorrow's regular post — unless, of course, he tore it up in the morning, which depended entirely on what the next day showed him.

He blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to Voss pace above him, counting her steps the way other men counted sheep.

Forty-eight hours. He had given himself forty-eight hours to decide who he was.

### Hale

Consul Beretha Hale reviewed troop movements the way she had once, thirty years ago, reviewed her late husband's accounts: with cold arithmetic and a private refusal to flinch.

The map spread across her dining table after midnight was not the public map, the one with its hopeful blue arrows showing the republic's armies sweeping to relieve the city. This was the real one, sent in cipher by General Ostrava's staff, and it had very few arrows on it at all. The relief column had turned south at Veldt Crossing. The supply barges had been requisitioned. The words *strategic reassessment* appeared twice, which in Ostrava's dialect meant *abandoned*.

And here — her finger found it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — here was the granary district, and here, overlaid in the artillery staff's neat red pencil, were the grid squares. Seven of them. Numbered.

Hale had known, in the way one knows a diagnosis before the physician speaks it. She had known since the dispatch arrived with its *PRIORITY: SILENT* marking and its routing to GR-7. But knowing and seeing the red pencil were different animals. Knowing was an ache. Seeing was a verdict.

Eight thousand souls in the granary quarter. Forty thousand in the city entire. And beyond the city — she made herself look, made herself trace the line east — the whole exposed flank of the republic, which could hold if Lantern's Reach bled long enough to buy it a season.

*Sacrifice now buys the future.* She had written that sentence herself once, in a memorandum ten years ago, about a smaller town and fewer lives, and the memorandum had been right. That was the unforgivable part. It kept being right.

A log shifted in the hearth. Hale poured the last of the wine and did not drink it.

Voss knew. Of course Voss knew; the girl had decoded the routing marks days ago, and Hale had watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes at dinner and say nothing. That silence interested Hale more than protest would have. Protest could be argued with. Silence had to be managed.

She had offered the girl everything she had to offer — promotion, a clean record, a place in the republic that would survive this war when almost nothing else did — and Voss had accepted with a face like still water. Still water worried Hale. Still water meant depth, and depth meant currents moving somewhere she couldn't see.

Her deputy had come sniffing around too, young Andrel, with his questions shaped like loyalty. She had fed him just enough encouragement to keep him reporting. A consul survived on informants the way a stove survived on coal, and she was not ashamed of it. Ashamed could wait until after the war, along with grief, along with sleep.

On the map, her finger rested on the first red square. Somewhere under that square was the bakery of a woman named Tessa Corvel, who had sold Hale bread every market morning for six years and always slipped the heel of the loaf into the bag unasked, "for the soup."

Hale removed her finger. She rolled up the map, tied it, and locked it in the drawer beneath her copy of the city charter, which she had taken out earlier that evening for reasons she declined to examine.

Two days. The artillery would be ranged and ready in two days, and the dispatch would reach forward command on schedule, because it must, because it was the only arithmetic left.

Unless somebody stole it from her table between now and then. She almost wished someone would. She poured the wine out on the fire instead, and the flames went briefly violet, and she watched them like an omen.

### Maren

There were four hiding places in the consulate, and Maren had named them.

The first was the Linen Room, which wasn't really hiding, because everyone knew the linen room, but you had to start somewhere, the way Papa had started her on running by making her run to the fence and back. The second was the space behind the water barrels in the scullery, which smelled of wet wood and was exactly her size if she folded up like a moth. The third was the loose panel behind the coat stand in the front hall, which she had found by accident when she tripped on the rug, and which Isandro had widened with a pen knife the same night and never mentioned to anyone.

The fourth was outside. The fourth was the gap in the conservatory lattice where the ivy had died, too small for soldiers, too small for Sef, exactly big enough for a girl who had learned from Papa that the best hiding place was the one that led somewhere else.

Maren practiced them at night, when Cook was asleep and the floors were hers. She moved through the dark house on sock feet, linen room to scullery to panel to lattice, counting under her breath the way Papa counted: *one for the door, two for the wall, three for the window, four for the fall.* At the lattice she would slip through into the frozen garden and stand among the bare rose bushes looking back at the lit windows, and practice being a shadow, which was mostly a matter of believing it.

She didn't tell Isandro about the garden part. Isandro worried, the way she went stiff and quiet when Maren came near the windows, and worry made people slow.

Tonight the house was strange. Lights burning late in the Consul's rooms, and the smell of hot wax drifting down the stairs, and Sef's boots going up and down and up and down like a clock that couldn't make up its mind. Maren lay in the linen closet among the towels and listened to the house talk. Houses talked; Papa said they did; you only had to learn the language. This house was saying *hurry* in a voice it didn't know it had.

From under the door, a thin ribbon of lamplight, and footsteps stopping outside it.

Maren went absolutely still, moth-folded, breath small.

"It's me." Isandro's whisper. "You can stop being a towel."

Maren unfolded. Isandro came in and sat down on the floor beside the closet, which was unusual — grown-ups didn't usually sit on floors — and pulled something from her pocket. A strip of wool. Maren knew it instantly: it was the fringe off her blanket, the one from home, the one she'd carried out of the burning farm clutched to her chest until the edge singed away.

"You cut my blanket," Maren said.

"A little bit of it. I needed to send someone a message, and this was the truest thing I had." Isandro turned the wool over in her fingers. "Do you remember what I promised you? When you came to the door?"

"That you'd find my papa."

"And that I'd keep you safe until I did. Both those promises are getting heavy, little mouse. They're pulling in different directions." Isandro said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all; they were doing sums, the kind Maren had seen in Papa's face during the bad weeks, the kind adults thought children couldn't read. "So I need you to be ready. Truly ready. All four places, and the fifth one too."

"There is no fifth one."

"The fifth one is me," Isandro said. "If I tap twice on any door in this house, you come to me, wherever I am, whatever else is happening. Can you do that?"

Maren considered it seriously, the way she considered everything now, because seriousness was what had kept her alive on the road while other children cried and were caught. "Even if Sef is there?"

"Especially if Sef is there."

"Then yes." She held out her hand, and Isandro shook it formally, which made it a treaty, which made it permanent.

Isandro stood to go, then paused at the door. "Why did you trust me, that first night?" she asked suddenly. "You didn't know me. You walked up my steps with a note and handed yourself over."

Maren shrugged into the towels. "Your face," she said. "Papa said forget the face, but I remembered it anyway. From the farm, when he talked to the tall lady with the river-grey eyes. He smiled different when he talked about you." She yawned. "People smile different about the ones worth finding."

The door closed softly. Maren burrowed into the towels and closed her eyes, and in the dark she counted her hiding places one last time — linen room, barrels, panel, lattice —

—and then, arriving without being summoned, a fifth: the sound of two taps on a door, somewhere ahead of her, always ahead of her, waiting to be answered.

* * *

Dawn came grey to Lantern's Reach, and the hour-glasses in four separate rooms ran thin together.

In her room, Isandro sanded the last page of the false routing, blotted Vessik's borrowed hand, and slid the forged orders into the dispatch case beside the true ones — two futures, side by side, waiting for her to choose.

In his, Sef Andrel took the unsent report from his breast pocket, weighed it, and did not burn it. Not yet. He tucked it away like a knife kept sharp, and went down to watch her.

In hers, Beretha Hale unlocked the drawer, unrolled the map, and drew a small, precise circle around the first red square — Tessa Corvel's bakery, though the map did not know names — and told herself, once more, that arithmetic was mercy enough.

And in the linen closet, a child slept with her hand curled open, holding nothing, ready to hold anything.

Outside, the siege guns woke one by one in the dark, testing their voices, like men clearing their throats before a speech none of them wanted to give.
