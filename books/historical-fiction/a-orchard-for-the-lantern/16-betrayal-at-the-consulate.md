## 16. Betrayal at the Consulate

The knock came at half past six in the morning, and Isandro knew before she opened the door that it was Sef.

She had known since yesterday evening, when he'd looked at her across the consulate courtyard with that particular expression he wore — the one like a man doing arithmetic on a ledger only he could see. She'd known when he'd asked, so casually, whether her courier's seal had been lost or surrendered, and she'd known when he'd smiled at her answer, because Sef Andrel did not smile at answers. He smiled at openings.

So when she opened her door to find him standing there with two soldiers of the city garrison behind him, wearing his best uniform and his worst expression, she felt something that was not quite surprise. It was more like the moment a tooth you've been worrying finally breaks.

"Sef," she said.

"Courier Voss." He said her name as though reading it off a document. Perhaps he was; he had a folded paper in his hand. "Consul Hale requests your presence at her table within the hour."

"Requests."

"Summons," he amended, with a small shrug that tried to be apologetic and didn't bother succeeding. "There will be an inspection of the consular quarters this morning. Standard security measure. The consul wants everything in order before the dispatches move tonight."

"Tonight." Isandro kept her voice level, pleasant, faintly bored — the voice of a woman with nothing in her wardrobe but uniforms and nothing under her floorboards but dust. "I hadn't heard the schedule changed."

"A great many things have changed overnight." His eyes went past her shoulder, into her room, cataloguing: the narrow bed, the washstand, the trunk with its courier's lock, the window with its view of the courtyard wall. He was looking for a child. She could feel him looking for a child the way one feels a draft from a badly hung door.

"She's not here," Isandro said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to."

Sef's mouth thinned. Behind him, the soldiers shifted, young men with chapped hands and rifles held with the careful grip of boys who hoped they'd never have to use them. One of them sneezed. Isandro found herself absurdly wanting to offer him a handkerchief.

"The inspection begins in twenty minutes," Sef said. "You'll want to make yourself presentable."

"I'm always presentable. It's one of the three things I'm good at."

"And the other two?"

"Carrying things," said Isandro, "and keeping my mouth shut."

Something flickered across his face then — not doubt exactly, but its cousin, the thing that lives next door to doubt and borrows its cups. He turned on his heel and led the soldiers away down the corridor, and Isandro closed her door and stood with her back against it and let herself shake for exactly ten seconds. She counted them. It was an old courier's trick: fear was cargo too, and cargo had to be weighed before it could be carried.

Ten seconds. Then she crossed to the trunk, knelt, and pressed her palm flat against its false bottom, where Maren had been sleeping every night for six days, breathing through a gap Isandro had cut in the cedar lining, waking only when Isandro tapped the old lullaby rhythm against the wood.

*Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.*

A pause. Then, muffled and small: *tap-tap. Tap.*

*Good morning,* said the rhythm. *Stay quiet. Stay still. I love you.*

She had never actually said those last words to anyone in her life. It seemed typical of her circumstances that the first time would be in code, through three inches of cedar, to a six-year-old pretending to be luggage.

* * *

Consul Beretha Hale received her in the dining room, which had been stripped down to a single table, a single lamp, and the particular silence of a room where decisions were made and furniture had learned to keep out of the way. Hale sat at the table with a cup of chicory coffee she plainly wasn't drinking, and Sef stood by the window wearing the expression of a man who has just been handed a promotion wrapped in someone else's skin.

"Sit down, Voss," said Hale.

Isandro sat.

Hale was not a large woman, but she occupied space the way a fortress occupies a hill — by right, by age, and by the simple fact that no one had ever succeeded in moving her. Her hair was iron-grey and cropped short for convenience, her hands were the hands of a woman who signed things, and her eyes were currently performing a slow inventory of Isandro Voss, item by item, as though deciding what could be salvaged.

"Last night," Hale said, "a report reached me. Anonymous. Detailed. Concerning a child hidden somewhere in this building, and concerning the disappearance of Courier Voss's official seal, and concerning certain documents in Courier Voss's possession bearing routing marks that do not match her assigned mission."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below, in the ration line already forming in the street, a woman laughed at something, bright and ordinary, and the sound came up through the window glass like weather from another country.

"Anonymous reports," Isandro said, "are like anonymous letters. Usually written by someone who wants your chair."

"Do you want my chair, Voss?"

"No, Consul. Chairs are heavy and everyone shoots at the person sitting in them."

For one instant — one — something that might have been approval moved behind Hale's eyes, and then it was gone, packed away with all her other unaffordable luxuries. "Search the quarters," she said, without raising her voice, and the soldiers went.

What followed was forty minutes of the longest kind of theater. Isandro sat with her hands folded while men in garrison grey went through her belongings with the thoroughness of people who had been told there was something to find and the desperation of people who couldn't. They emptied her trunk. They pulled up the loose flagstone by the hearth — the wrong loose flagstone; there were three, and only a fool hid anything under the obvious one. They shook out her spare uniforms and found, in one pocket, half a woolen blanket cut raggedly along one edge.

Sef picked it up. Turned it over. Looked at her.

"Torn," Isandro said. "Caught on a gate latch last week. I kept meaning to mend it." She held out her hand. "It's a blanket, Sef. Even the Republic hasn't outlawed blankets yet. Though I understand the winter provisions committee is considering it."

He gave it back. But he gave it back slowly, and his eyes said: *I know what this is, and I am deciding, minute by minute, what to do about it.*

And that was the thing about Sef Andrel that Isandro had understood too late — he wasn't cruel. Cruelty would have been simpler. He was ambitious the way some men are religious: sincerely, earnestly, and with a willingness to burn whatever the doctrine required. He believed in the Republic the way she used to believe in it, whole and unquestioning, and her hesitation — her small, mortal, human hesitations over the past week — had read to him not as conscience but as contagion.

The soldiers finished. They found nothing, because there was nothing to find; Maren had spent the night, as she had spent four of the last six nights, in the crawlspace above the kitchen pantry, where the cook — a broad, silent woman named Odile who asked no questions and baked bread that could raise the dead — left a plate every evening without ever once looking up at the ceiling.

Hale dismissed the soldiers. She dismissed Sef with a look that said *stay close.* Then she leaned forward, folded her hands, and spoke quietly enough that the words seemed almost kind.

"One hour, Voss. You have one hour to produce this child, or produce proof that no such child exists in this consulate, or produce something better than either — because I will tell you now that if the garrison finds her first, she goes into the circulars, the circulars go to the enemy lines, and every man who died holding this wall dies again for nothing. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you."

"There was a time," Hale said, and here her voice did change, dropping into something older and wearier, "when I would have trusted you with my own seal. Your record is clean. Twenty-two years, not one late delivery, not one opened packet, not one lie on a manifest. So I am going to ask you once, woman to woman, officer to officer, and I advise you to consider very carefully what your answer costs. Is there a child in my consulate?"

Isandro looked at her.

Here is what she felt, in that moment: terror, yes, cold and complete, sitting in her chest like swallowed river water. But underneath the terror, rising up through it the way bubbles rise, something else — something almost like relief. Because the question had been asked at last, openly, and a courier's whole life is training for exactly one skill above all others: knowing what to say when the words matter most.

"No," Isandro said.

Hale waited.

"But," Isandro continued, and reached into her jacket, and drew out the folder she had spent two sleepless nights building — forged transit letters, a dead man's name written in a living man's hand, a consular protection stamp copied from memory and aged with tea and candle smoke until it looked like it had survived a war, which, in fairness, it now had — "there is a ward. A colleague's daughter. Courier Tam Vessic, killed at the Marrow Ford retreat — you'll find his name on the casualty lists, third page, second column. Before he went north he asked me, if anything happened to him, to see his girl safe. She arrived Tuesday last. I lodged her privately because the man she's fleeing is General Fen, and I have seen what happens to children whose names appear near his."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Down in the street, the ration line shuffled forward one step, then another, patient as tide.

"You're telling me," Hale said at last, "that Aldric Fen — traitor, defector, the reason half our artillery positions are compromised — sent his daughter to *you*, and you filed her under *dependents*."

"I'm telling you that a dying man asked a favor, and I'm a courier, Consul. Carrying things is what I do. Even heavy ones." She laid the folder on the table between them, open to the protection certificate, and watched Hale read, and felt her own heartbeat perform its work somewhere far away, calm as a clock in an empty house. "Tam Vessic served this Republic for nineteen years. If you overturn his last request, I'll want it done formally. In writing. With reasons."

It was, Isandro reflected later, the boldest lie of her career, and its genius — if it had any genius — lay entirely in its architecture: every part of it was true except the parts that mattered. There had been a Courier Tam Vessic. He had died at the Marrow Ford. He had indeed once, drunk at a midwinter feast, wept about a daughter he'd hardly seen. She had simply borrowed a dead man's grief and fitted it to a living child, and grief, being the one commodity the Republic never audited, passed inspection.

Hale read the certificate twice. Then she looked up, and her eyes were dry and hard and terribly tired.

"If this is false, Voss—"

"Then I've committed treason against the Republic I've served for twenty-two years, in the building where I've served it, under your own roof. Yes, Consul. I know what I'm risking. Do you imagine I did this lightly?"

"I imagine," said Hale, "that you did it for reasons of your own, and that I don't have time to dig them out." She closed the folder. Slid it back across the table. For a moment her hand rested flat upon it, pinning it, as though it might fly away. "The child stays. Under consular protection, under your authority, and under your neck. If she is who your papers say she is, no harm comes to her. If she is not—" Hale stood, and buttoned her coat, and became once more the fortress on the hill. "One hour ago I offered you the chance to prove your loyalty. You've chosen instead to prove your nerve. I haven't decided which frightens me more."

She paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "Tam Vessic did have a daughter. He talked about her constantly. Drove us all mad." And she was gone, her boots ringing down the corridor like a sentence being pronounced.

* * *

Isandro walked back to her quarters slowly, nodding to the soldiers, smiling at the clerk, performing the entire time — the walk of a woman vindicated, mildly inconvenienced, already thinking about breakfast. Only when the door closed behind her did she lean against it and press both hands over her face and breathe, long and shaking, into the dark of her own palms.

*Terror transmuted into performance,* she thought. *Twenty-two years of carrying other people's sealed truths, and it turns out the trick works in reverse.*

She tapped the lullaby on the trunk. *Tap-tap. Tap. Safe. Stay put till dark.*

From inside: *tap-tap. Tap.* And then, after a pause, a third sequence — new, invented, unmistakably Maren: *tap-tap-tap-tap-tap*, quick and urgent, the child's own private alarm.

Isandro frowned. Tapped back: *what?*

The reply came in a rhythm Isandro had taught her only yesterday, spelling out letters against the wood, slow and painstaking, one letter at a time:

*S-O-L-D-I-E-R-S-I-N-T-H-E-P-A-N-T-R-Y*

Isandro went very still.

Because she had moved Maren to the trunk only last night. Which meant the child in the crawlspace above the kitchen pantry — the child the soldiers' search had missed by inches, the child whose hiding place Odile's bread plate had marked for six days — had been discovered. Or was being discovered. Right now. While Isandro sat upstairs winning arguments with forged paper.

Down the corridor, boots. Many boots, moving fast, toward the kitchen stairs.

Isandro took the false-bottomed blanket, the forged folder, and every second of the head start Hale's mercy had just accidentally given her, and opened her window onto the courtyard wall.
