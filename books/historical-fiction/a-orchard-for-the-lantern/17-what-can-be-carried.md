## 17. What Can Be Carried

They took the seal at noon.

Not with ceremony — Isandro had expected ceremony, had braced for it the way one braces for a blow, imagining Hale's officers drawn up in the consulate hall while some functionary read out the words *stripped of office* in a voice like a closing ledger. Instead a clerk came to her room with a leather tray and a bored expression and asked her to place the courier's seal on the felt, please, and also the dispatch case key, and also — he consulted his list — the second key, the one she kept sewn into her coat lining because she was not born yesterday.

She gave him all three. Her hands did not shake. She noted this with a strange, distant pride, the way one might note that a roof held during a storm it was never built to survive.

"Deputy Courier Voss," the clerk said, and then corrected himself with a small cough. "Former Deputy Courier Voss. Consul Hale extends the courtesy of your room until evening. After that, the consulate can no longer guarantee—" He stopped, hearing how it sounded. "You should be gone by dark," he said more quietly, almost kindly, and left with his tray held very level, as though the seal on it were something dead that must not be jostled.

Isandro sat on the edge of her narrow bed for a long time after the door closed.

A courier without a seal. She turned the phrase over the way she had once turned sealed orders over in her hands, measuring weight, wax, urgency. Twenty-two years. She had been fourteen when she first carried a message through the flood districts of Vellamare proper, a scrap from a levee captain to a harbor master, running the whole way because no one had told her yet that speed was not the whole of the profession. She had learned everything since: how to read routing marks, how to sleep sitting upright against a saddle, how to lie to customs men with a yawn and a shrug, how to hold a sealed thing so that its weight became a kind of companionship on long roads. The seal had been pressed into her palm at nineteen by a woman named Odile Corvane who said, *This is not yours. It is only lent to you, and what you do while it is in your hand is who you are.*

And now it sat on a felt tray in a clerk's hands, and she was still herself, apparently. It seemed an oversight somewhere. Someone ought to be notified.

There came a soft scratching at the wall behind the wardrobe — three knocks, a pause, two knocks — the code Maren had invented on her second day hiding here, delivered with the gravity of a general and the rhythm of someone who mostly just liked knocking.

Isandro slid the wardrobe aside. The panel behind it opened onto a crawl space between walls, and Maren's face appeared in the gap, round and solemn, a smudge of plaster dust on one cheekbone where the old house shed itself onto its guests.

"They took your shiny thing," Maren whispered.

"They did."

"Was it important?"

"It was the idea of me," Isandro said. "The rest of me is still here."

Maren considered this with the ferocious literalism of six-year-olds everywhere, then nodded once, satisfied, and wormed backward into the dark. "I saved you the biscuit from lunch," floated back through the wall. "It's under the third floorboard but don't tell the mice I told you about the floorboards."

Isandro laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her, coming up from somewhere below the dread. Then she began to pack.

* * *

Packing, for a courier, is a confession. Everything you choose to carry tells the road who you are.

She laid it all out on the bed like evidence at a trial. Two shirts, one of them beyond saving but useful as rag. The coat with the lining already opened where the second key used to live; she restitched it around a folded knife instead, working the needle clumsily because her hands wanted to shake now that no one was watching. A flint. Half a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in wax cloth, acquired through channels best not examined too closely. And Maren's blanket — the other half of it, the half that had not gone to the orchard as a signal to a man who might or might not still be watching among bare trees. He had answered. That was four nights ago now, and the answer lived in her chest like a swallowed coal: a strip of red cloth tied to the third tree past the broken gate, knotted in the way Aldric Fen had once knotted his fishing lines on a frozen riverbank, laughing at her for asking why.

"You tie them wrong on purpose," she'd said.

"So I know they're mine," he'd said. "Everything worth keeping, you mark so you can find it again."

She packed the blanket last, folding it small.

Maren emerged from the wall when the light went amber, moving through the consulate's hidden anatomy with an ease that would have alarmed Isandro if she let herself think about it — a child who knew the hiding places of a building better than its owners did, because a child who had run from a burning farm learned fast what walls were for. The girl ate her rescued biscuit with ceremonial slowness and watched Isandro work.

"Where are we going?" she asked. Not *are we going*. Where. As though it were settled, which, Isandro supposed, it was. Children decide things faster than adults and then simply wait for the adults to catch up.

"Away," Isandro said. "Through the river gate, probably. There's an orchard past the hills—"

"I know about the orchard." Maren licked crumbs off her thumb. "Papa's letters smelled like apples sometimes. He wrote that there were trees older than the war." A pause, and then, in the tone children use when they are being brave on purpose: "He'll be there, won't he. Because of my blanket."

"Yes."

Maren nodded and asked nothing else, which was its own kind of mercy, and went back into the wall to pack her own treasures — Isandro heard the faint sounds of a tin soldier being wrapped in something soft, and a whispered negotiation with the mice regarding the third floorboard.

Isandro sat down amid her small pile of belongings and let the fear arrive properly, now that the child wasn't watching. It arrived the way winter arrives — not all at once but in a slow graying of everything. Sef had reported her. Soldiers had walked the halls with their boots loud on purpose. Hale had given her an hour and Isandro had stood in front of the consul with forged papers in her sweating hand and claimed that Maren was the daughter of a dead colleague, under consular protection, and Hale had looked at her for a long, long moment and then — worse than anger — *believed her*, or chosen to appear to, because belief was cheaper than investigation and the consul had a war to lose gracefully.

The hour was spent. The performance had held. But performances rot quickly, and Sef Andrel was still in the building with his ambition and his suspicions, and every candle that burned tonight burned down toward exposure.

She had lost her seal. Her post. Her protection. She inventoried these losses the way the clerk had inventoried the tray, and found that beneath them lay one thing unlisted, unconfiscated, still hers.

The orders.

Her blood went cold and then, strangely, warm.

* * *

Here is what she understood, sitting in a stripped courier's room while the city outside baked bread and pretended tomorrow:

The true orders still existed. Not her copy — hers had been taken with everything else, logged, locked in the consulate strongroom. But couriers' doctrine was written in duplication; a republic that trusted any single document to survive a siege would not have lasted two hundred years. There was a duplicate dispatch. There was always a duplicate. It sat in Sef Andrel's keeping now — she had watched the duty roster shuffle after her disgrace, watched his name slide neatly into the vacancy she'd left, watched him wear the promotion like a new uniform a size too hopeful. Tomorrow, or the day after, Sef would ride east with the sealed orders to the artillery command at Kessel Ford, and three days after that the guns would have their grids, and Lantern's Reach would learn what the granary district looked like from above, falling.

Unless someone carried them in the wrong direction.

Isandro stood up. Walked to the window. The street below was doing its stubborn evening arithmetic: a baker hauling in his sign, two women arguing cheerfully about nothing, a boy chasing a hoop past the barricade sandbags as though sandbags were furniture, which, if you were seven, they were. This was the city she had been ordered to treat as a route. It refused. It kept insisting on being a home, even to people just passing through, even now, even with the shells coming — especially now, the way a man sings louder as the ship goes down, not because it helps but because the singing is the point.

She thought about interception. Couriers intercepted things all the time — that was the trade's dirty twin, the thing taught in back rooms after the clean rooms closed: how to take a message out of the world. Burn it. Drown it. Replace it. A message that never arrives is a battle that never happens, and the republic had built her into precisely the kind of instrument that could make that true.

They had built her, and then taken away the seal, as though the taking-away mattered. As though the skill lived in the brass rather than the hands.

If she could reach Sef before he rode —

No. Not reach him. Reach the *dispatch*. Sef slept in the annex, third door, beneath the map cabinet she'd memorized years ago out of professional habit and was only now grateful for. His dispatch case would travel with him, strapped, but cases slept badly on their first night — new custody, new weight, the owner checking and rechecking the lock. There would be a window. There was always a window; she had spent her life standing in them.

And then what? Ride east herself, past the siege lines, past Kessel Ford, and put the targeting grids for a city of forty thousand souls into a stove? Take them north and drop them in a river? Every version ended with her a traitor twice over, hunted by a state that had already decided what she was, and none of it — none of it — got Maren out. The child was the promise. The child had always been the promise. Fen had sent her daughter across a war with a note reading *forget the face*, and Isandro had finally understood, in the checkpoint shadow with a guard's eyes crawling over Maren's features and her own seal changing hands like a ransom, that some deliveries cannot be delegated and some oaths outrank nations.

So: both. The girl to the orchard. The orders into oblivion. One night to do what her whole career had trained her for, performed in reverse, against the people who trained her.

It was madness dressed in logistics. She smiled at it anyway. It was the first plan in weeks that had the correct shape — not the shape of survival, which she'd stopped believing in somewhere around the checkpoint, but the shape of *purpose*, which fit differently, closer to the body, like a coat cut for you alone.

* * *

Maren came out of the wall at full dark, wearing the tin soldier on a string around her neck like a medal and carrying a bundle that clinked suspiciously.

"What's in the bundle?"

"Supplies," Maren said, with the flat finality of a quartermaster.

"Maren."

"Biscuits. And a candle. And the key to the wine cellar that I found but I didn't drink anything, I checked it was all vinegar anyway." She handed it over solemnly. "You might need doors."

Isandro crouched to her height. In the candlelight the girl's face was Fen's face softened by childhood — the same set of the jaw, the same watchfulness behind the eyes, as though patience ran in the blood like eye color. "What we're going to do is dangerous," Isandro said. "I'm going to say true things to you now, because you've earned true things. We're going to walk through a city that's frightened, and frightened cities have soldiers, and soldiers will ask us questions. You remember what you're called if they do?"

"Tessa. Tessa Marrow. My father was a cartographer and he died of fever and you're my aunt and aunts are allowed to be boring."

"And boring aunts?"

"Don't talk unless spoken to, and never look at anyone's face longer than two blinks." Maren recited it like grace before meals. Then her voice dropped, wobbled one degree. "Will Papa really be at the orchard?"

"He'll be there," Isandro said, and made it true by saying it, the way promises work.

Maren studied her, and then did the thing that undid Isandro completely: she reached up and straightened Isandro's collar, patting it flat with two small pats, the way a parent does, the way, perhaps, Fen had done for her once upon a farm that was now ash. "You pack like Papa," she said. "Everything folded like it matters."

"Everything does matter."

"I know." She said it without drama, as settled fact. "That's why we're going."

Isandro blew out the candle. In the darkness she listened to the city breathe — the far-off grind of the siege guns, patient as tide; nearer, the bakery ovens being banked for the night, a smell of woodsmoke and yesterday's bread drifting up through the shutter seams. Somewhere below, in the annex, Sef Andrel slept beside a case containing forty thousand deaths, dreaming, presumably, of promotion.

Tomorrow he would ride east. Tonight, before the moon cleared the eastern wall, Isandro Voss — former deputy courier, current thief of her own profession — would open a lock she'd taught herself to open at twenty, take what the republic most wanted delivered, and carry it in the worst direction imaginable.

She lifted Maren's bundle in one hand and Maren's hand in the other.

"Now," she said, "we go be boring."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, a door opened that had no business opening at this hour, and boots began to climb the stairs.
