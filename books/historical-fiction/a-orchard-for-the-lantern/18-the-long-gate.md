## 18. The Long Gate

The city had a way of breathing at night that Isandro had never noticed by day.

By day it coughed — ration lines and checkpoint bells and the wet rattle of cart wheels on frozen mud. But past midnight, when the lamps were shuttered against the siege and the snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes, Lantern's Reach breathed. Long, slow inhalations of wind off the mountains. Long, slow exhalations of woodsmoke from a thousand chimneys where people who did not yet know they were targets were sleeping anyway, because there was nothing else to be done about it.

Isandro moved through that breath with Maren's hand locked in hers and the true orders pressed flat against her ribs, bound under her coat in oilcloth, riding like a second heart.

"Are we running away?" Maren whispered. She was wearing two coats and Isandro's spare boots, which were four sizes too large and made her walk like a duck wearing a disguise as a different duck.

"We're taking a trip," Isandro said. "A short one. Through the woods."

"At night."

"Yes."

"In the snow."

"You're very good at cataloguing," Isandro said. "That'll be useful when we're famous."

Maren considered this, her small hand tightening. "I'm not scared," she announced, in the tone of someone who was extremely scared and wanted credit for the announcement.

"I know," Isandro said. "I'm doing enough for both of us."

And she was. Her fear had stopped being the cold, abstract dread of the consulate weeks ago and become something hotter, stranger, more alive — a fear that walked beside her and held a child's hand and would not, could not, put it down. Somewhere between forging the routing documents and lying to Consul Hale's face, her terror had transmuted into something with teeth. Not fear *of* losing the girl. Fear *for*. It was a better fuel. It burned cleaner.

They went down the cooper's lane behind the tannery, past the shrine to some minor saint of lost causes whose candle Isandro left burning out of professional sympathy, and came at last to the long slope above the river district, where the wall of Lantern's Reach bent to follow the water and the siege lines beyond it lay like a black stitch across the white fields.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, fit inside a sentence: get through the river gate before the watch changed at the fourth bell, cross the ice road where the sentries' brazier-light didn't reach, and vanish into the pine country south of the walls. From there, the orchard. From there, Fen. From there —

She hadn't let herself think past *from there.* Planning past survival felt like tempting a god who was already annoyed.

What she had not planned for was Sef Andrel standing at the mouth of the cooper's lane.

He was alone, which was somehow worse than if he'd brought soldiers. He wore his courier's grey and no coat over it, which meant he'd come fast and angry, and his breath steamed around him like a kettle deciding something. In one hand he held a lantern. In the other, folded once, was a paper she recognized by its shape: an arrest order, or something wearing an arrest order's clothes.

"Sef," she said pleasantly, because her voice had learned somewhere in the last hour that panic sounded like guilt and calm sounded like paperwork.

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Don't do the courier voice. I've spent six years listening to you do the courier voice to customs officers and I know exactly what it means. It means you're about to ruin my life."

"That seems dramatic."

"Isandro." He stepped closer. The lantern swung and threw his shadow huge up the tannery wall, so that a giant Sef loomed over them both, arms wide, absurdly menacing. "I read them. The orders. Your seal impression — yes, I took it, yes, I'm sorry, no, I'm not actually sorry — I read every grid reference. Do you understand what you're carrying? Do you understand what they gave *us* to carry?"

"I understand better than you think."

"Then give it back." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Give me the dispatch and take the child wherever you want. I'll write my report saying you tricked me, that I pursued you valiantly, that I recovered the orders from your frozen corpse in the snow — I'm flexible on details, Isandro, I always have been. You keep your conscience. I keep my career. Everyone wins except the truth, and the truth has never won anything in this republic."

Behind her, Maren made a very small sound.

Sef's eyes flicked down to the girl, and something in his face wavered — the ambition and the decency wrestling briefly in public, like two cats in a sack.

"She's six years old," he said, quieter. "You're going to die out there. Both of you. The sentries shoot first and file reports later, and nobody files reports about dead couriers anymore because there are too many."

"Then it's lucky," Isandro said, "that I've never once filed a report about anything."

"Isandro—"

"*Sef.*" She kept her voice low and even, though her heart was hammering against the oilcloth-wrapped dispatch like a prisoner on a door. "In six years, have I ever lied to you?"

"No," he admitted bitterly. "It's been infuriating."

"I'm not lying now when I tell you those papers are targeting grids for this city. For the granary district. For the ovens that are lit right now. If you stop me, you don't save your career — you just make sure you're holding the lantern when they burn."

The snow fell between them. Somewhere across the river, a sentry coughed.

"You always did think you were the hero of the story," Sef said at last, and there was no venom in it, only exhaustion. "Go, then. Before I remember what I want more than I want to sleep."

He stepped aside. He turned his lantern deliberately toward the wall, away from them.

Isandro took Maren's hand and walked past him, and did not run until the lane curved, and even then only a little, because dignity mattered, even to the damned.

* * *

The river gate was older than the rest of the wall, a squat arch of mountain stone where the water passed beneath in a channel now half-choked with ice. In peacetime it had been a toll gate for barges; in wartime it was a checkpoint with two bored sentries, one brazier, and a chain stretched across the towpath.

Isandro crouched in the shadow of a fish-smoke shed and did arithmetic.

Two sentries. One brazier, positioned badly — its light reached the gate but not the towpath's far curve. The chain. The watch change at the fourth bell, which was perhaps twenty minutes off, when both men would turn their backs to grumble at their replacements and steal each other's tobacco, as watch changes were contractually obligated to involve.

Twenty minutes. One chain. Two children's boots that clacked.

"Maren," she whispered. "How are you at climbing chains?"

"I'm six."

"Noted. Revised question: how are you at being very quiet while I climb chains?"

"Very quiet," Maren whispered back with tremendous confidence, then immediately sneezed.

Isandro closed her eyes and asked whatever saint watched over lost causes to also cover incompetence.

They crossed the open ground low and fast, Isandro carrying Maren piggyback because the boots had finally betrayed them entirely, clacking like a pair of castanets with ambitions. The chain across the towpath was old iron, waist-high, sagging. Isandro set the girl down, tested the links, and swung herself over with the graceless efficiency of a woman who had climbed a great many walls in service of a republic currently trying to kill her.

She turned to lift Maren.

And the brazier flared.

One of the sentries had fed it a fresh log, and the light leapt ten feet, and it found them — found Maren, small and pale in her borrowed coats, halfway up the chain with her mittens hooked in the iron, caught exactly like a moth in a very bureaucratic lamp.

"Hoy!" The sentry was up, spear in hand, voice cracking across the ice. "Hoy! Stop there! Who goes!"

Time did something strange. It slowed and sharpened. Isandro saw everything: the second sentry scrambling up, the spear point, the twenty yards of open towpath, the ice of the river gleaming beyond, the whole thin membrane between here and safety — and her own body already moving, stepping in front of the girl, hands rising, mind racing through every lie she owned and finding all of them suddenly, catastrophically insufficient, because the sentry wasn't looking at her.

He was looking at Maren.

And Maren — six years old, terrified, mitten-hooked on a chain in the freezing dark — did the single most brilliant thing Isandro had ever seen anyone do under fire.

She burst into tears.

Not polite tears. Not frightened tears. The full operatic catastrophe, the howling, hiccupping, bone-deep misery of a small child who has been dragged out into the freezing night by her horrible aunt to visit her horrible grandmother, and who wants everyone within a quarter mile to know about it, and who is going to make this everyone's problem.

"Oh — oh no," said the first sentry, in the voice of a man watching his entire evening disintegrate.

"My *feet* are *frozen,*" Maren howled, with the precision of an artist. "*Auntie* said it would be a *short* walk and it is NOT a short walk and I want to go HOOOME—" She punctuated this by kicking the chain, which rang, which set her off afresh.

"Auntie?" The second sentry arrived, squinting. "This the Voss woman? The courier? There's circulars—"

"There's a *child,*" said the first sentry, with the weary authority of experience. "You ever met a smuggler's kid? They cry like that too. It's a whole thing. My sister's boy—"

"Sir," Isandro said, stepping forward, and here her courier voice came to her rescue one final time, smooth as waxed paper, "my niece has been traveling since dusk, her mother is ill in Southreach, and I will be eternally grateful if you simply let us pass this gate and stop making her stand in the snow, because if she catches the fever before we reach the ferry I will never forgive myself and neither, I suspect, will you."

The second sentry looked at the first sentry. The first sentry looked at Maren, who looked back at him with streaming eyes and an expression of such profound, world-historical grievance that he visibly aged.

"Fourth bell's in a quarter hour," the first sentry muttered. "Chain comes down for the ferry traffic anyway. Go on, then. Take her to the ferry landing and out of my night."

"Bless you both," Isandro said. "Truly."

"And buy the girl some new boots," the second sentry called after them, as Isandro lifted Maren over the dropped chain and onto the ice road beyond. "Those things sound like a horse falling downstairs."

Maren, sniffling magnificently, waved at them over Isandro's shoulder.

They did not speak until the pines swallowed the gate's light behind them. Then Isandro set the girl down in the shadow of the trees, knelt, and looked at her — really looked — at the red-rimmed eyes and the furious little chin and the mittens still hooked in memory around nothing.

"That," Isandro said, "was the bravest thing I have ever seen."

Maren wiped her nose with devastating dignity. "I wasn't brave. I was mad."

"The best bravery usually is."

* * *

They walked the pine country through the small hours, following the frozen stream south, Isandro navigating by stars she'd memorized from a hundred rooftop couriers' rests. Twice she heard dogs far off to the east, and twice she lifted Maren and cut west without a word, and Maren, bless her, wrapped her arms around Isandro's neck and held on and asked nothing, because children understood instinctively what adults forgot: that questions could wait, but running couldn't.

Near dawn they rested in a hollow beneath a fallen pine, snow banked around them like a fort. Isandro shared the last of the bread and the last of the honey she'd stolen from the consulate kitchens, and Maren ate hers with the solemnity of a judge.

"Isandro," she said, around the bread. "When we find Papa. Will you stay?"

The question landed in Isandro's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for it to hit bottom, and it didn't.

"I don't know, little one."

"Papa says people who keep promises come back." Maren said it simply, the way children say enormous things. "He said you were a promise."

"He said *what*, exactly?"

"That's what he called you. When he told me to go to the door with the note. He said, 'find the promise.'" She shrugged, crumbs flying. "I didn't know what he meant till I saw your face. You looked like somebody I dreamed about."

Isandro looked north, through the black trunks, toward the city she had betrayed and the republic that would call it treason, and thought about how strange it was that the smallest person she knew carried the largest truths, and handed them out like sweets.

"Sleep now," she said, and pulled the blanket — half a blanket, the other half hanging in a bare tree miles back, a message in cloth — around the girl's shoulders.

But she did not sleep. Because as the sky went grey in the east, she heard it again: dogs. Closer now. And under them, faint but unmistakable, the crunch of a single set of footsteps moving through snow with the deliberate pace of a man who had decided something and could not un-decide it.

She rose, drew Maren close behind her, and looked toward the tree line — where, at the orchard's edge, a figure in courier's grey stood waiting, lantern dark, arrest order in hand.

"Sef," she breathed. "Oh, you stubborn, ambitious fool."

The man began to walk toward them.
