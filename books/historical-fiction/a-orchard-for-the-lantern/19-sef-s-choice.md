## 19. Sef's Choice

Sef Andrel had rehearsed this moment for three hours, and in all his rehearsals he had never once imagined the orchard would be so quiet.

He had expected wind, perhaps — the dramatic sort that rattled bare branches and made a man feel the weight of history on his shoulders. He had expected dogs, or sentries, or at minimum the distant thump of artillery from Lantern's Reach to give the scene its proper gravity. Instead there was only frost stiffening the grass, the creak of old apple trees swaying like patient old men waiting for a bus, and somewhere far off a crow making an argument no one was listening to.

Isandro Voss stood at the orchard's edge with a child's hand in hers and a satchel slung across her body, and she did not look surprised to see him.

That, more than anything, made his stomach drop. He had ridden hard through two checkpoints and a frozen creek to get ahead of her, arrest warrant folded against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and she looked at him the way one looks at a colleague who is late for a meeting.

"Sef," she said.

"Deputy Courier Andrel," he corrected, and immediately regretted it, because it sounded exactly as small as it was.

Maren — and he knew her name now, everyone would know it soon enough — peered out from behind Isandro's coat with the frank, unimpressed stare of a six-year-old assessing a new adult. Her verdict, judging by her expression, was not favorable.

"You should go back toward the city," Isandro said. "There's time."

"There isn't, actually." Sef dismounted. His knees cracked. He was twenty-six years old and his knees cracked; he suspected this was what guilt did to a body, aged it from the inside like fruit. "I've been awake for thirty-one hours. I've forged a transit pass, bribed a ferryman, and lied to a corporal whose handshake could crush walnuts. Do you know why? Because I needed to be here before you were. Because if you walked into that orchard and I wasn't standing here, then everything I've done these last two days meant nothing, and I would have to admit it meant nothing, and I am not yet drunk enough for that conversation."

Isandro's expression softened by a degree. It was the look she'd given him their first year, when he'd mislabeled a dispatch pouch and blamed the ink. *You'll learn,* that look said, *and learning will cost you.*

"Sef," she said again, gentler now, "what are you holding?"

He realized his hands had gone to his coat. He drew out both documents and held them up: the arrest order in his left hand, crisp and official, bearing Hale's seal and the republic's broken-column crest. In his right, the true orders — the ones he had unsealed at midnight with a stolen impression of her courier's seal, alone in a rented room with a candle burning down to nothing.

The papers trembled slightly in the cold air. Or possibly in his hands. The distinction had stopped mattering around dawn.

"The arrest order," he said. "And the dispatch you were supposed to deliver. Which I opened." He watched her face for shock, for anger. She gave him neither. She gave him patience, which was worse. "You knew," he said. "You read the routing marks and you knew, and you let me sit at that table and argue loyalty at you while you carried coordinates for—"

"I didn't let you do anything, Sef. You argued beautifully. You always do."

* * *

Here was the thing about Sef Andrel, the thing he had spent his whole career hoping no one would notice: he had never actually believed in anything. Not the republic — he'd been too young when it began collapsing to have loved it whole. Not duty — duty was just obedience with better tailoring. What he believed in was the ladder. The rungs. The clean arithmetic of advancement: serve faithfully, rise predictably, die comfortably with a commendation framed over the fireplace.

Loyalty, he had told Isandro at the consul's table, means delivering without question. Doubt is the first treason. He had said it with real conviction because it was the creed of every man who had ever wanted something he couldn't name and hoped a title might be it.

Then he had stolen the seal impression. Then he had pressed wax into the copy of her seal at midnight, hands steadier than they had any right to be, and lifted the flap of the true orders, and read:

*Priority Artillery Liaison. Grid references follow. Confirmation of receipt terminates at Third Battery Command, granary district.*

Grid references. Eleven of them. He had known what they were before he finished the first line, because he had grown up outside Vellamare City and he remembered the surveyor's marks painted on the granary roofs during the last war, and he remembered what the marks had meant afterward, when the granaries were holes and the bread queues were graves.

Eleven coordinates. A bakery district. A schoolhouse that doubled as a shelter. The clock tower where children still played, where a woman still lit her ovens at four in the morning because the smell of bread was the last thing a besieged city had that resembled hope.

*Confirmation of receipt.* As though the death of a city were a parcel requiring a signature.

Sef had sat until the candle died, holding the paper, listening to his own breathing. And the terrible thing — the thing he could not stop examining, turning over like a stone with something living under it — was that his first thought, genuinely, truly, had been: *If I report this interference, I will be promoted.*

His second thought had been the shape of Maren's face in the consulate corridor, asking him if the soup was always this color.

* * *

"Read it," Isandro said now. "Out loud. If you're going to arrest me for treason, you should hear the treason first. All of it."

"I don't need—"

"You do. That's exactly what you need. You've been carrying those words in your head for two days, Sef, and they've been eating you alive, because things kept in silence grow teeth. Say them into the air. Let the trees hear them. Let her hear them." She touched Maren's shoulder, and the girl stepped forward — stepped *forward*, this small fearless creature, facing a man with an arrest warrant as though he were a mildly interesting exhibit. "You served them your whole life without knowing. You should at least learn what service tastes like."

His mouth was dry. The crow had given up its argument and flown off, leaving him with no witnesses but apple trees and frost and a child.

He unfolded the dispatch. His voice, when it came, sounded younger than he felt — a boy reciting in a schoolroom, praying the master wouldn't ask him to explain.

"'Priority Artillery Liaison,'" he read. "'Effective upon confirmation. Grid one: granary row, north quarter. Grid two: the clock tower square.'" His voice caught. He pushed through. "'Grid three: river gate approaches. Grid four: the consular residence and attached grounds.'" 

He stopped.

"That one's you," Isandro said quietly. "And Hale. The republic doesn't want witnesses either, Sef. We were never couriers. We were the fuse, walking ourselves politely to the powder."

"Grid five," he continued, because stopping felt like drowning and reading felt like — he didn't know what. Punishment. Absolution. Both wore the same coat. "'Grid five through eleven: residential districts, east wall.' Signed, sealed, countersigned." He lowered the paper. The wind chose that moment to arrive, finally, rattling the bare branches overhead so that the orchard seemed to shudder. "I've memorized it," he said. "Do you understand? I can't unread it. It's in me now. Every coordinate. I could recite it in my sleep, and probably will, for the rest of my life, however long the republic decides that is."

"Yes," Isandro said. "Now you know what I've been carrying since the granary district."

"Why didn't you tell me?" And it came out raw, accusatory, the question of a man who had spent two days discovering that his ambition had a body count. "All those years. All those runs together. You trusted me with nothing."

"I trusted you with everything," Isandro said. "I trusted you with my silences. You just never noticed how heavy they were." She sighed, and some of the hardness went out of her, and for a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "Would it have changed anything, Sef? Two weeks ago? A month ago?"

He wanted to say yes. He assembled the yes carefully — *of course, I'd have helped, I'm not a monster* — and held it up to the light of himself and found it hollow as a courier's promise of safe passage through a war zone.

"No," he admitted. "Not then." He looked at the arrest order in his left hand. Hale's seal gleamed. *By order of Consul Beretha Hale: detain Deputy Courier Sef Andrel should he exhibit hesitation or divided allegiance.* He had found that clause appended to *his own* warrant — Hale had written his fall into Isandro's paperwork, hedging her bets, trusting no one, and some part of Sef had admired the thoroughness even as it ended him. "But now isn't then. Now I've read grid seven aloud and heard myself say *shelter*. So." He swallowed. "So."

* * *

What came next took longer than it should have, and less time than it deserved.

He thought about his mother, who had a framed commendation already picked out for him, metaphorically speaking — she had a wall prepared, a spot above the mantel measured by eye. He thought about the promotion that had been promised in all but writing, the corner office in the capital with the good light, the name that would appear in dispatches. He thought about how he had told himself, riding through the night, that he was coming to stop Isandro for the good of the mission, and how the lie had worn thin by the second checkpoint, and how by the frozen creek he had admitted the truth to the water: he was coming because he could not bear to let her carry this alone, and because if she succeeded then some part of him succeeded too, some part he hadn't known existed until it started hurting.

"You'll hang," he said. "Or whatever they do now. There won't be a trial worth the name."

"I know."

"And the girl goes to Fen, and Fen is still officially a traitor, and you'll have handed the enemy general his own daughter in the middle of an active bombardment, and history—" His voice broke, absurdly. He laughed at it. "History will call you a traitor too, Isandro. They'll put us in the same sentence. *Voss and Andrel, the traitors of Lantern's Reach.* My mother will take down the mantel."

"She'll keep the mantel," Isandro said. "She'll just put something better above it. Mothers are efficient that way."

Despite everything — despite the warrant and the coordinates and the shells that would start falling within the hour — Sef laughed. It came out cracked and wet and it went on slightly too long, the laugh of a man setting down something very heavy after carrying it very far.

He held out the true orders.

Isandro didn't take them. "They're yours now," she said. "You read them. You know what they are. I delivered mine the only way I could — by refusing to. Deliver yours."

For a long moment he didn't understand. Then he did, and the understanding went through him like cold water through cloth.

He knelt in the frost — actually knelt, his good trousers in the mud, Maren watching with round serious eyes — and built a small pile of dry leaves and dead apple twigs, and struck the match he had carried since the rented room, telling himself then it was for candles. The paper caught reluctantly, the way important things do, as though it required convincing. The wax seal bubbled and blackened. The coordinates curled — grid one, grid four, the shelter, the schoolhouse — and he fed each piece to the flame with the same careful attention he had once given to sealing dispatches, because if a thing was worth doing badly it was worth doing slowly, and this was the one act of his entire career he intended to perform perfectly.

The smoke rose thin and gray among the bare branches.

"Third Battery Command will confirm non-receipt by nightfall," he said, watching the last corner burn. "They'll assume interception. They may fire anyway — Hale has contingency orders, I'm sure of it. This doesn't save the city, Isandro."

"It buys the morning," she said. "The morning is where people live."

He stood. Brushed off his knees. Looked at the ash and understood that he was looking at his career, his promotion, his name above the mantel, his comfortable death decades hence — all of it reduced to gray flakes drifting between apple trees. And underneath the grief of it, unmistakable, warm as a coal cupped in both hands: relief. Enormous, shameful, magnificent relief.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then." He turned to Maren, who had been silent throughout with the gravity of a much older person, and extended his hand with tremendous formality. "Sef Andrel. Formerly of the Republic of Vellamare Courier Service. Currently unemployed."

She shook it solemnly. "Maren. Also unemployed."

"Between jobs," Isandro murmured, and Maren grinned up at her, and something in Sef's chest turned over painfully, because this — this small joke in a burning world — was what he had been defending all along without knowing its name.

From the direction of Lantern's Reach, faint as a heartbeat, came the first low cough of artillery finding its range. Not the barrage. Not yet. Just the war clearing its throat.

Isandro's face changed. She pulled Maren close, squared the satchel, and looked toward the orchard's interior, where between the rows of bare trees a lantern had begun to glow — a small, steady, impossible light, moving closer through the winter dusk.

"He came," she breathed.

"Of course he came," Sef said. "Men like him always come. It's men like me who take convincing." He stepped back, deliberately, putting himself between the orchard and the road. "Go. I'll watch the path. And Isandro—" She paused. He found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "When they write us into the same sentence, make sure mine comes second. I've always hated going first."

Behind them, through the trees, a tall figure in a weathered coat stopped at the edge of the lantern light — and waited, the way one waits for a promise twenty years late to finish arriving.
