## 20. The Orchard for the Lantern

The orchard had been dying longer than Maren had been alive, and it was still, stubbornly, an orchard. That was the thing about trees, Isandro thought as she led the girl between the rows of bare, black-limbed apple stock. You could cut them back, burn their skirts, shell the farmhouse to splinters — and they would stand there in the frozen ground like patient creditors, waiting for spring to settle accounts.

"Is it far?" Maren whispered.

"You can see it from here. The lantern."

They had been walking since the last gray light of afternoon, out through the smuggler's cut in the aqueduct wall that Fen's coded advertisement had described in the language of fruit prices, down the frozen drainage ditch, along the river's edge where the ice made a sound like a man clearing his throat. Isandro had carried Maren over the worst of it — the girl weighed almost nothing, which was its own kind of heartbreak — and now the orchard rose ahead of them on its low hill, a hundred skeletons of trees against a sky the color of an old bruise.

And in the tallest tree, at the very crown where a boy might have climbed to steal apples in some other century, hung a lantern. Lit. Steady. Small and absurd and unmistakable against the darkening east.

Maren's hand tightened in hers. "Is he there? Papa?"

"He's there," Isandro said. "Or he's watching, and he'll be there before we've counted ten trees. That's how he is."

"How do you know how he is?"

Isandro looked down at her. Six years old, cheeks scoured pink by the wind, wearing Isandro's spare coat belted twice around her middle so she looked like a sausage in uniform. She had spent three days in a courier's safehouse eating hard bread and asking questions with the tireless precision of a customs officer, and Isandro had answered every one of them except the dangerous ones.

"Because he pulled me out of a frozen river once," Isandro said, "and then lectured me about it the whole way back to the fire. Some people you only have to know once."

Maren considered this with the gravity it deserved. "You fell in a river?"

"I was showing off."

"Were you good at it?"

"Terrible. That's rather the point of showing off."

The girl laughed — a small, startled sound, the first Isandro had heard from her in two days, and it went through Isandro like warm water through a cold hand. *Forget the face*, the note had said. As if that were possible. As if Aldric Fen had not understood, when he wrote it, that he was sending Isandro the one thing in the world she could never forget: himself, in miniature, with his exact frown and his mother's stubborn chin.

They were halfway up the hill when the first shell fell on Lantern's Reach.

It came as light first — a bloom of orange behind the city's western wall, silent for the full three seconds it took the sound to cross the frozen fields. Then the sound arrived, not a crack but a *whump*, deep and wet, the sound of something enormous being told *no*.

Maren flinched and grabbed Isandro's leg. Isandro put a hand on her head, the way you'd steady a cup on a tilting table.

"It's all right," Isandro said, and was astonished to find her voice level, because nothing in her was level. The city was burning. *Her* city — not hers, she'd told herself for eleven years, never hers, a posting, a route, a waypoint between seals — and it was burning in the exact pattern she had decoded from those routing marks in chapter and verse, granary district first, then the river gate, then the quarters where bakers still lit their ovens in the morning because the morning was still worth lighting them for.

She had failed. She had rerouted, forged, bribed, fled, and it had all bought was three days, and three days had not been enough. Hale's sacrifice was proceeding. The republic was having its future, purchased at the price of a city, and the interest was being paid in orange light.

"Papa said the loud noises mean it's almost over," Maren said into her coat. "He said that. He said it like it was true."

"Then it's almost over," Isandro said.

It was a lie, and Maren knew it was a lie, and Maren let her have it, which was perhaps the most adult thing Isandro had witnessed in eleven years of diplomatic service. She took the girl's hand again and they climbed.

* * *

He was standing under the lantern tree when they crested the rise, and the sight of him hit Isandro harder than the shelling had.

Aldric Fen had been a broad, easy man once, the kind who filled doorways and laughed from the chest. What stood among the bare trees now was narrower, grayer, wrapped in a farmworker's coat that hung off him like a flag of surrender. His beard had gone half white. His eyes, though. His eyes were exactly the same, and they went from Maren to Isandro and back to Maren, and then Aldric Fen, traitor general of the Republic of Vellamare, sat down right there in the frozen mud because his legs had evidently decided the matter without consulting him.

"Mama's coat," Maren said, and ran.

He caught her and folded around her, and made a sound Isandro had only ever heard once before, the night her own father had come home from the border the last time — a sound that wasn't a word and wasn't a sob but was somehow both, the sound of a man being handed back a piece of himself he'd already finished mourning. Maren was talking into his shoulder, a flood of it: *the farm, the soldiers, the lady with the lantern eyes, she carried me, Papa, she carried me the whole way, she fell in a river once—*

He looked up at Isandro over his daughter's head.

"You told her about the river."

"She extracted it under interrogation," Isandro said. "I'm trained to resist. I lasted four days. You'd be proud."

"I am proud." His voice was rough. He stood, keeping Maren against his side, and looked at Isandro the way you look at a debt you'd stopped believing would ever be called in. "Isandro. Eleven years."

"Eleven years, four months. You're late."

"I know."

"I sent three signals. Three. The fruit prices, the blanket, the—"

"I know. I was in the marshes. There was a man in the marshes who was very interested in my correspondence." He said it lightly, but his hand wasn't light on Maren's shoulder. "I read every one of them. I read them six times. I want you to know that."

Another shell. Closer now, or it only sounded closer — the eastern batteries walking their fire along the wall, methodical as a clerk. The lantern above them swayed on its branch, and Isandro thought, absurdly, of Hale at her dinner table, refilling Isandro's wine with her own hands, saying *you are the last honest courier I have* — and meaning it, and sending the coordinates anyway. That was the thing about the republic at the end. It had stopped lying to itself about anything except its own survival.

"You knew," Isandro said. "When you left her at my door. You knew what the orders were. You knew what I was carrying."

"Yes."

"You used your daughter as a dispatch. The one thing no soldier would intercept. Your own words, I imagine."

"They were my words." He didn't flinch from it, and she hated him a little for that, and loved him a little for it too, and understood that the two had been the same feeling for a very long time. "I have done arithmetic all my life, Isandro. Armies are arithmetic. And the arithmetic said: a courier who has never broken an oath will break it for a child. Every courier. Every oath. I bet my daughter's life on the shape of your conscience, and I have not slept since, and I would do it again, and I am not sorry, and I will spend the rest of my life — however long that is — being sorry anyway. All of that is true at once. I've stopped expecting things to be only one thing."

Maren had gone quiet against his side, watching them both with the flat, total attention of a child who understands that the adults are saying the real words now, the ones that don't have child-sized versions.

"Come away with us," Fen said.

Isandro blinked. "What?"

"There's a boat. A fishing boat, a friend, the marshes, and then the coast, and then anywhere that isn't here. There's a life after this — small, and cold, and I can't promise you'll like it, but it's a life. Come with us. You carried her here. You've paid. Whatever they say you owe, you've paid it." He stepped closer. "Isandro. The city is lost. You know it's lost. Come away."

And the terrible thing — the thing she would carry to whatever tribunal was waiting for her — was that for one full breath, she wanted it. She stood in the frozen orchard with the sky on fire behind her and she *wanted* it: the boat, the marsh, the small cold life, Maren learning to fish, Fen's terrible lectures, an existence with no seals and no routes and no republic at all. It rose in her like the smell of bread, warm and specific and unbearable.

Then, over Fen's shoulder, through the black branches, she watched a shell find the granary district.

The flash was bigger this time. The sound came rolling across the fields like a wave deciding, and with it came a second sound underneath, faint at this distance, almost imagined: the sound of a city screaming.

Bakers still lit their ovens. Children still played in the ration lines. The woman at the consulate who'd slipped Isandro an extra roll every morning for eleven years and called her *skinny thing* — the boy who swept the checkpoint and saluted her with a broom — the whole fragile, stubborn warmth of the place, the thing she had spent a decade refusing to call home because couriers didn't have homes, couriers had *routes* — and she had finally learned the word for it three days too late, and it was burning.

"They'll say it was necessary," Isandro said. Her voice came out strange and calm. "The histories. If there are histories. They'll say Lantern's Reach was a hard necessity, a tragic arithmetic, and Hale will be a footnote and the granary district will be a number, and the only people who will ever say otherwise are the ones who were in it. And I—" She stopped. Started again. "I carried those coordinates for eleven hundred miles. I slept with them under my head. I was the last honest courier, Fen. That was the whole of me. And the honest thing, the only honest thing left in me, is to walk back into that and tell the truth out loud, to whoever's left to write it down. Even if it saves no one. *Especially* if it saves no one. Because if I get on your boat, then the republic gets to be right. Then it was just necessity, and nobody argued."

"You'll hang."

"Probably."

"They'll want a trial. They'll want a spectacle. They'll—"

"Fen." She almost smiled. "You once lectured me for twenty minutes about river ice. Let me have this one."

He stared at her. And then Aldric Fen did the thing he had done on the riverbank eleven years ago, when a half-drowned junior courier had coughed up half the Vell and sworn at him in three languages: he laughed. It was a wrecked, wet, disbelieving laugh, and it cracked something open in the cold air between them.

"There she is," he said. "There she is. I pulled one stubborn fool out of a river and the republic never even noticed what it had."

"It noticed," Isandro said. "That was the problem."

Maren tugged his coat. "Papa. Is she staying for the lantern?"

Isandro looked at the girl. "What lantern, little one?"

Maren pointed up, at the lantern burning in the crown of the dead tree. "Papa said we light it when it's safe. He said, when the war's over, we light the lantern in the orchard and everyone who's lost can find their way home. He told me that when I was little. He said it like a promise." She frowned, working at something. "The war's not over. But it's lit anyway."

"It's lit anyway," Fen said quietly. He was looking at Isandro when he said it.

Isandro crouched down to Maren's height. Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and Isandro's own spare soap, and Isandro's chest did something complicated and painful.

"I have to go back," Isandro said. "Into the city. I have to tell people what was in the papers I carried, and then some very serious men are going to be cross with me, and I might be gone a long time. So I need you to do something for me."

Maren nodded, very seriously.

"Grow," Isandro said. "Grow like these trees. They'll cut you back and burn around you and shell your farmhouse, and you'll stand there anyway, and one spring — one spring, I promise you — someone will come walking up this hill and find you blooming. And when they do, you light the lantern. Can you do that? Can you keep that promise for me?"

Maren studied her the way she studied everything, without mercy. "You'll come back?"

"If they let me."

"That's not a real answer."

"No," Isandro agreed. "It isn't. It's the only kind I have."

Maren considered. Then she stepped forward and hugged Isandro around the neck, quick and fierce, the way children hug — as if affection were a task to be completed efficiently — and pulled back, and it was done.

"Walk fast," Maren advised. "The loud noises are getting closer."

* * *

Fen walked her to the edge of the orchard, because of course he did, and they stood a moment in the shadow of the last tree with the burning city laid out below them like a spilled lamp.

"The boat will be in the marshes until the shelling stops," he said. "After that, I can't promise. But the offer doesn't expire, Isandro. Whatever they do to you — whatever's left after they're done — you come find the orchard. There will be an orchard. I've decided."

"You've decided," Isandro said. "Well. Then it's practically law."

"And Isandro." He caught her arm. His grip was still strong; the rest of him had thinned around it, like a candle around its wick. "The orders. The true ones. Do you have them?"

She put her hand inside her coat, against her ribs, where the oilcloth packet had ridden for eleven hundred miles and three days and one long frozen night. The sealed dispatch. The coordinates. The death warrant of a city, signed and waxed and routed, still in her possession because she had never delivered it and never destroyed it — because some part of her had known, since the night she decoded the routing marks, that the paper itself was the testimony. Evidence doesn't burn well. Evidence has to be *shown*.

"They're going to want to know what was in it," she said. "The tribunal. The histories. And the republic's own seal is on this, Fen. Their wax, their cipher, their hand. When I stand up and say what I carried, I won't be a traitor telling stories. I'll be the republic, testifying against itself."

Fen looked at the packet's outline under her coat, and something moved through his face — not hope exactly, nothing so warm. Recognition. The look of a man watching a debt get called in after all, on the right party.

"Walk fast," he said.

"I always do."

She went down the hill through the bare trees, past the lantern burning its small, stubborn promise into the dark, toward the burning city and the ruined consulate and whatever was left of Hale's officers and Hale's arithmetic and Hale's terrible, necessary future. Behind her, faint over the shelling, she heard Maren's voice, high and clear on the cold air:

"Papa, she's really going. Papa, she's really going *back*."

"Yes," Isandro said to herself, to the city, to the dark. "I really am."

The next shell fell as she reached the aqueduct wall — close now, close enough to taste the smoke — and somewhere in the granary district, a woman was surely still standing in her bakery, deciding whether tomorrow's morning was worth lighting the ovens for.

Isandro put her shoulder to the smuggler's cut and went in.
