## 21. Ash and Account

The consulate had lost its roof the way a man loses his hat in a windstorm — suddenly, and with no dignity at all. Isandro stood across the square from it in the grey light of morning, Maren's absence a warm ache against her chest where the girl had been, and looked at what remained of the Republic of Vellamare's presence in Lantern's Reach.

Half the building still stood, which was more than could be said for the granary district, or the baker's row, or the clock tower that had chimed the hours for two hundred years. The other half was a blackened skeleton, beams jutting like the ribs of some enormous animal that had lain down in the street to die. Smoke still rose from it in thin, tired threads, as if the fire itself had grown bored.

She should go north. Fen had offered. *There's a road through the hills that the artillery hasn't learned about yet,* he'd said, holding his daughter with both arms, his face pressed to her hair. *You could be over the border in three days and no one would ever know your name.* And she had wanted to, with a want so fierce it had frightened her. But Maren had looked up at her over her father's shoulder — that solemn, unblinking look she had, the one that made her seem sixty years old in a six-year-old body — and Isandro had understood that there was one more delivery to make.

Not a package this time. A truth.

She crossed the square. Her boots crunched on glass and plaster. A cat — the same enormous orange tom that had haunted the consulate kitchens all through the siege, the one the cooks had called Chancellor — sat on the surviving doorstep, licking a paw with the composure of a creature who had seen empires fall and found them insufficiently interesting.

"Good morning, Chancellor," she said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't spoken to anyone since leaving Fen in the orchard, and before that she had spoken mostly in whispers and lies, and her throat had apparently filed a formal complaint.

The cat blinked at her.

Inside, the great hall was a ruin of its former grandeur. The long table where Consul Beretha Hale had held her dinners — where Isandro had lied for the first time in her career, over roast quail and watered wine — was split down the middle, its surface littered with plaster and a single surviving candlestick, absurdly upright, as though it had been left there to take minutes.

Three people were in the room. Two soldiers she recognized from the consulate guard, their uniforms scorched and their faces grey with exhaustion. And between them, seated on a camp stool with her arm in a sling and a bandage wrapped around her brow like a laurel crown gone wrong, was Deputy Consul Aris Vane — Hale's second, a thin, precise man whom Isandro had always privately suspected of being held together entirely by ink and resentment.

He looked up at her, and his eyes went very wide, and then very narrow, in the space of a single breath.

"Courier Voss," he said. "We had you listed among the dead."

"I know," Isandro said. "It seemed the polite thing, at the time, to let the list stand."

One of the soldiers made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Vane did not smile. Vane, as far as Isandro knew, had never smiled; there were rumors that the muscles required had been reassigned to other duties.

"You disappeared," Vane said. "The night before the bombardment. The dispatch you carried was never logged at its destination. And now, the morning after our city burns, you walk in through the front door." He rose from the camp stool, wincing. "Where is Consul Hale?"

Isandro had rehearsed this moment on the long walk back through the smoking streets. She had composed sentences, ordered them, polished them. What came out was simpler than any of them.

"Dead, I believe. She was in the consulate when the shells came. I'm sorry."

"She was *here*?" Vane's voice cracked on it. "She stayed?"

"She stayed," Isandro said. "She said someone had to sign the surrender papers properly, or the artillery would keep firing out of bureaucratic spite."

That, at last, got the ghost of a reaction from the younger soldier — a twitch at the mouth, quickly suppressed. Vane closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, they were wet, and he was furious about it, and Isandro found, to her surprise, that she liked him a little for the fury.

"She was a great servant of the Republic," Vane said.

"She was," Isandro agreed. "That's rather the problem."

The silence that followed had weight. The soldier nearest the door shifted his grip on his rifle. Somewhere above them, a beam settled with a groan, and the orange cat, who had followed her in, sat down in the rubble and began washing his face.

"Courier Voss," Vane said carefully, "you are going to explain that sentence."

* * *

They moved to what had been the records room, because Vane was the kind of man who could not have a serious conversation in a room without files. Half the shelves had burned; the rest stood open and gutted, their contents reduced to ash that rose in little grey ghosts whenever anyone moved. Isandro thought it was as good a metaphor for the Republic of Vellamare as she was likely to find, and she was a courier, and metaphors were not in her job description.

They sat — Vane on the one surviving chair, Isandro on an overturned crate, the soldiers standing by the door with the particular stillness of men who wanted very much to be elsewhere. Vane had a clerk's ledger open before him and a pen that had clearly been salvaged from the ruins, its nib slightly bent, so that everything he wrote came out with a small, stubborn flourish at the end of each stroke, as if the pen itself were editorializing.

"State your name and posting," Vane said.

"Isandro Voss. Senior courier, First Rank, attached to the Office of Foreign Dispatch."

"Your seal."

She reached into her coat and set it on the table between them. The brass was dented, and the ribbon that had once been courier's crimson was now a color that could only be described as *survived*. She had traded its twin to a checkpoint guard days ago for a child's life; this one she had carried through the siege lines and the burning streets like a stone she could not put down.

Vane looked at it for a long moment. "You surrendered this at the river gate," he said. "There's a report."

"There is."

"From a Deputy Courier Andrel. Sef Andrel." Vane's pen hovered. "His account is that you abandoned your post, forged routing documents, and fled the city with — " he checked the ledger, and his voice went flat with disbelief — "a child."

"That's accurate," Isandro said. "Except for the word *fled*. I'd have chosen *delivered*. It's the same motion, really. You just care more about what's in your hands."

"Courier Voss — "

"You asked for my account. I'd like to give it in order, if you don't mind, because if I give it out of order I'll lose my nerve, and I've used up rather a lot of nerve in the past week."

Vane set down the pen. Something in his face shifted — not softness, exactly, but the recognition of a fellow creature who had also, once or twice, been afraid. "In order, then."

* * *

She told it from the beginning. The sealed orders on the eve of the mission, and the way they had felt in her hands — too heavy for their size, the way a lie is heavier than the truth it stands in for. The checkpoints and ration lines of Lantern's Reach, the ovens still lit, the children still playing in streets the Republic had already measured for graves. The child on the consulate steps with her note — *forget the face* — in handwriting Isandro had known anywhere, because she had spent one golden peacetime winter learning it across a table of maps and bad wine.

She told them about Aldric Fen. Not the traitor of the circulars — the man who had pulled her out of the frozen river at Corren's Ford during the armistice talks, who had held her under his coat and shouted at her about the stupidity of couriers who took shortcuts over ice, and who had made her swear, laughing, breathless, alive, that if he ever sent for her she would come without asking why.

She told them about decoding the routing marks. The granary district. The realization that the Republic had not sent her to carry terms but to carry coordinates — that the diplomatic mission was a fiction, and she was its postage.

"And you did not open the seal," Vane said. "You deduced all this from routing marks."

"I didn't need to open it. I needed to know where it was going. And a courier knows that a dispatch addressed to a granary district is not a letter. It's an address." She paused. "Sef Andrel opened it, later. He can confirm the contents. Artillery targeting grids. Sector by sector. The whole city, divided up like a pie."

Vane wrote for a while. The bent pen scratched. Outside, someone was calling a name — a rescue worker, maybe, or a woman who had not yet accepted an answer the ruins had already given.

"You understand," Vane said at last, not looking up, "that what you are describing is treason. Not the fleeing. The knowing. You knew the Republic intended the destruction of a city under its own protection, and you concealed that knowledge from your consul."

"I did."

"You could have reported it. To Hale. To the garrison. You could have — "

"To what end? The orders were sealed under the Chancellor's own cipher. Hale couldn't countermand them; she could only be destroyed by them, and she was. And if I'd reported it, the mission would have been reassigned, and the shells would have fallen on the same streets, and I'd be standing here having betrayed a child for nothing." She spread her hands. "I did the arithmetic, Deputy Consul. I've done nothing but arithmetic for eleven years — weight, wax, urgency, route. This was the first sum that came out honest."

"And the child," Vane said. "Maren Fen. Daughter of a declared traitor general. You risked the mission, your rank, your life — for the daughter of the enemy."

"She was six," Isandro said. "She was six, and she was standing on the steps in the cold with a note in her hand, and the note said *forget the face*, which was her father's way of telling me that the only way to save her was to make sure no one looked at her. And I looked at her. That was the whole crime, if you want it plainly. I looked."

The room was quiet. One of the soldiers had turned his face to the wall. The other was studying the ceiling with the intensity of a man memorizing it.

Vane cleared his throat. "And the dispatches. The true orders. Where are they?"

Isandro had known this question was coming. She had walked three days to answer it, and she had decided, somewhere on the second day, walking through fields where the snow was beginning to give up and the ditches were running with meltwater, that she would not lie. Not here. Not in the record. Whatever else happened to her, the Republic's own ledger was going to say what the Republic had done.

"They were destroyed," she said. "Burned at the orchard, by Deputy Courier Andrel's own hand. He read them first. Every grid, every sector. And then he put them in a fire rather than deliver them." She met Vane's eyes. "You'll want that in the record too. Because if you write down only my crimes, you'll write a story about one disloyal courier. And if you write down what those papers contained, you'll write a story about a Republic that ordered the burning of its own border city and called it diplomacy. I would prefer the second story. I've earned very little say in the matter, but I would prefer it."

Vane sat back. For a long moment he did not write anything at all, and Isandro watched him wrestle with it — the clerk's instinct to record everything against the servant's instinct to protect the thing he served — and she recognized the fight, because she had been having it in her own chest for a week, and she knew which side won.

"The Republic," Vane said slowly, "is not — the Republic is an idea. It cannot commit crimes. Only men can."

"Then write down the men," Isandro said. "The cipher that sealed the orders came from the Chancellor's office. The routing was authorized through the Ministry of War. Consul Hale knew, or suspected, and chose to believe that sacrifice buys futures — you'll find her letters, if any of them survived, and they'll break your heart, because she wasn't a monster, she was a believer, and belief is how monsters get their paperwork done." She leaned forward. "I am not asking you to save me, Deputy Consul. I am asking you to write it down. All of it. In the Republic's own hand, in the Republic's own book. Let the record be the one thing in this city the shells couldn't reach."

The pen lay on the table between them, its bent nib pointing at her like an accusation, or a signature.

* * *

They took her statement until the light through the broken windows turned from grey to gold. Vane asked his questions in his dry, precise way — dates, times, seal impressions, the names of every checkpoint guard she had bribed or deceived — and she answered them all, and twice he stopped writing entirely and simply looked at her, as if checking that she was real.

The soldiers brought tea at some point, from somewhere — actual tea, a luxury in a city with no granary district left — and Isandro drank hers too fast and burned her tongue, and the younger soldier, whose name turned out to be Pell, said gravely, "You want to let it steep," and then looked mortified at having spoken, and Isandro smiled at him, and he went pink to the ears, and it was the first moment in a week that had felt even faintly like the world she used to live in.

It was Pell, too, who asked the question the others had been circling.

"The child," he said. "Maren. Is she — did you get her out? Is she safe?"

Isandro looked at him. He was perhaps twenty, with a healing burn along his jaw and his mother's eyes, if the portrait he probably kept folded in his pack was anything like most soldiers' portraits.

"She's safe," Isandro said. "She's with her father. In a place the artillery hasn't learned about yet." She hesitated, then added, "She's the bravest person I've ever carried, and I've carried state secrets through two wars. She learned the consulate's hiding places in a day. She found the loose panel behind the wine cellar that I never found in eleven years. If the Republic had any sense, it would have made her a courier and me a footnote."

Pell grinned. Even Vane's mouth twitched — a seismic event, Isandro suspected, unrecorded in any ledger.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and Vane straightened his papers, and the room became a courtroom again.

* * *

"There remains the matter of you," Vane said.

"I assumed there would."

"You understand the range of it. Abandonment of post. Forgery of official documents. Surrender of a courier's seal to an unauthorized party. Concealment of state correspondence. Aiding a traitor of the Republic." He read the list without inflection, like a man reciting the ingredients of a recipe he did not intend to cook. "Under the Emergency Statutes, several of these carry a maximum penalty of death."

"Yes."

"You don't seem frightened."

Isandro considered this. She was, a little — frightened of the cold cell they'd put her in, frightened of the trial, frightened of the particular loneliness of being made into an example. But underneath the fear was something she had not felt in eleven years of service, and it took her a moment to name it, and when she did, she almost laughed.

"I spent my whole career," she said, "carrying things for other people. Sealed things. Things I wasn't permitted to know, delivered to people I wasn't permitted to question, in service of a Republic I wasn't permitted to doubt. And I was good at it. I was *very* good at it. And then a child showed up on a doorstep with a note in a dead friend's handwriting, and for the first time I carried something I chose." She set down the tea. "I'm not frightened, Deputy Consul, because whatever you do to me, you can't take back the week where I was a person instead of a postage system. You can kill me. You can't un-deliver me."

The silence held for a long time. Outside, the light was failing, and someone had lit a fire in the ruined square — she could smell the woodsmoke, and beneath it, impossibly, bread. Someone in Lantern's Reach had found an oven that still worked. Someone was baking. The city had been shelled to its bones, and someone was baking, because that was what cities did, that was what people did; they lit ovens. She had learned that in her first week here, and she had thought it was tenderness, and it was, but it was also defiance, the oldest kind, the kind that didn't bother with banners.

Vane wrote for a long time. When he finished, he read the page back to her — every charge, every answer, the contents of the dispatches, the names, the grids, the granary district — and then he did something she had not expected.

He signed it. Dated it. Stamped it with the deputy consul's seal, the one that survived because he had carried it in his shirt pocket, against his heart, and the shell fragment that should have killed him had spent itself on the brass instead.

"The record stands," he said. "All of it. The Republic's own hand, in the Republic's own book." He looked up, and his eyes were tired and clear. "I can't promise what the tribunal will do. I can promise that when they ask you why, the answer will be there, in full, under seal, and no one will be able to say you were a coward or a fool. Whatever else they say."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"Don't thank me yet. The tribunal convenes in three days, and the presiding officer is arriving from the capital, and he is said to be a man who has never once been surprised by anything, which I have always thought was its own kind of crime." Vane gathered his papers, then paused. "One more question. Off the record."

"Off the record," Isandro agreed.

"The note. The one the child carried. *Forget the face.*" He hesitated. "Did you?"

Isandro thought of the frozen river, and the coat, and the laughing voice shouting at her about ice. She thought of a girl solemn as a judge, finding hiding places. She thought of bare trees, and a lantern, and a promise made in peacetime by two people who had not yet learned what peacetime was worth.

"No," she said. "I remembered it. That was the whole point."

Vane nodded, as though she had answered a question on an examination, and perhaps she had. He rose, winced, and called for the guard to escort her back to the cell — and then stopped, and turned, and said, in the voice of a man adding a line to a ledger he intended to balance someday:

"The tribunal, Courier Voss. Three days. I'd rest, if I were you."

But as they led her out through the ruined hall, past the upright candlestick and the orange cat, who had taken up residence on the split table as though appointed to it, Isandro found she was not thinking about the tribunal at all. She was thinking about a knock at the consulate door — the real door, the one that used to be there — and the sound of boots, and a voice from the capital asking, in a tone she had heard once before, across a table of maps and bad wine:

*Isandro Voss? The tribunal will see you now.*

And beneath the voice, faint but unmistakable, from somewhere across the ruined city where an oven had been lit against all sense and all orders — the smell of bread.
