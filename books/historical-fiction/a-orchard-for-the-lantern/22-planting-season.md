## 22. Planting Season

The sapling was taller than Maren remembered, which was absurd, because she had carried it herself only that morning, wrapped in sacking like a fussy infant, from the nursery at the edge of town. But trees, her father said, grew fastest in the places where people had once been certain nothing would grow again. He said it the way he said most things — as though it were a joke he hadn't quite decided to tell.

She knelt in the turned earth and pressed her palms flat against the soil. Spring had come late this year, the way it always did now, grudgingly, as if the seasons too had to be talked out of their old suspicions. The orchard stretched around her in long rows of young trees, each one staked and tied with red cord — Fen's idea, so that even a child could see where not to walk. Beyond the last row stood the old wall, patched with stone of three different colors, like a coat sewn from whatever was on hand. It was a good wall now. It had been a terrible wall once.

"Maren." Her father's voice came from somewhere among the pear rows. "If you're burying treasure again, I should warn you the last cache attracted badgers."

"I'm *planting*, Papa."

"That's what you said about the buttons."

"The buttons were an experiment."

"And what did we learn?"

Maren considered. "That badgers will eat anything if you're patient enough."

"We learned," Aldric Fen said, emerging into view with a spade over his shoulder and dirt already up both forearms, "that some things are meant to be kept, not buried. Come here. Show me your hole."

"It's not my hole, it's the tree's hole."

"Of course. Forgive me."

He crouched beside her, and together they looked down into the small dark square of earth. Seven years ago this ground had been ash and shell-crater and the black skeletons of trees that had stood longer than any nation standing today. The first spring after the war, nothing came up. The second spring, weeds came up, defiant and unlovely, and Fen had wept over the weeds like a fool, which he admitted freely. The third spring, someone found a living apple tree at the orchard's far edge — half-burned, crooked, alive — and they had taken cuttings from it the way you might take letters from a house where everyone had died: carefully, with both hands.

Now there were four hundred trees. Maren knew because she had counted twice, and gotten different numbers both times, and been told by old Tomas that this was how orchards defended themselves against being owned.

"Roots first," Fen said, handing her the sapling. "Always roots first. A tree forgives you for everything except planting it shallow."

She settled the pale tangle of roots into the earth while he held the trunk straight. They worked without talking for a while, which was the best way they worked. Her father had never been a man who needed noise. During the war years — the ones she could remember, the ones that lived behind her eyes as smell more than image, smoke and wet wool and her own breath loud in the dark — silence had been a kind of shelter. He had built it around her wherever they went. Even now, when silence was only silence, she felt the shape of it as something made for her, deliberately, by hands that loved her.

"There," Fen said. "Firm the soil. Not hard. Firm. Like shaking hands with something you respect."

"You make everything sound like diplomacy."

"Diplomacy is just gardening with worse weather." He wiped his hands on his coat, which had once been a general's coat and was now simply a coat with many pockets, most of them full of string. "Now. Do you remember what comes next?"

Maren did remember, but she looked toward the gate anyway, because the ritual required it, and because — if she were honest, and Papa said honesty was cheaper than people thought — she wanted to see whether the cart would come.

It came. Of course it came. It always came.

The mule was gray and ancient and named after a treaty that nobody could recall anymore, and the woman driving it sat with the straight-backed ease of someone who had once crossed siege lines with a child under her cloak and a republic burning behind her. Isandro Voss wore a courier's coat still, though no seal hung from it now, and its blue had faded to the color of a winter morning. She had white in her hair. She had laugh lines deep enough to lose coins in. When she saw Maren watching, she raised one hand off the reins in a small salute — the old courier's greeting, two fingers, *message received*.

"She's here," Maren said unnecessarily.

"She's always here," Fen said. "That was rather the point of her."

* * *

They ate lunch on the low wall by the gate, because Isandro refused to eat anywhere else. "I spent six years being welcomed into parlors and dining rooms," she explained, unwrapping bread and hard cheese and the little jar of honey that Fen sent with her every autumn and she pretended not to know came from him, "and I can tell you the wall has better conversation."

"The wall agrees," said Maren, who had appointed herself the wall's spokesperson years ago and had never been relieved of the post.

Isandro laughed — that particular laugh of hers, starting somewhere in her chest and taking its time arriving. She looked older than her years, but then, Maren supposed, everyone who had been anyone during the war looked older than their years, or younger, depending on which direction they'd fled from themselves. Isandro had gone back toward the fire. That was the story Maren had pieced together over the years, from fragments, from other people's careful silences, from the single time she'd asked directly and watched both adults become very interested in their tea.

She had gone back toward the fire, and she had testified, and the tribunal had done whatever tribunals do, which was less than justice and more than nothing, and afterward she had walked out of Vellamare with nothing at all except her freedom and a debt she claimed ran the other way entirely.

"You're staring," Isandro said.

"I'm counting your gray hairs. There are eleven new ones since midwinter."

"Fifteen, if you count the temple. Your father's orchard is very stressful."

"My father's orchard is the calmest place in the province."

"Yes," Isandro said, and the word landed differently than Maren expected, softer, and she understood suddenly that it wasn't a joke. "Yes, it is."

Fen passed down the honey jar. For a moment the three of them sat in the spring light, legs dangling, the mule dozing, the young trees throwing their first thin shadows. Somewhere beyond the wall, the town of Lantern's Reach went about its business — hammers, a dog with opinions, children shouting at each other in the market square with the ferocity of people who had never once been hungry on purpose. Most of them couldn't have told you which side had won the war. Most of them couldn't have told you there had been sides. The histories called it the Border Correction now, two dry words for three years of ash, and schoolchildren learned it in an afternoon between the Founding of the League and the Invention of the Rotary Plow.

Maren had stopped minding. Or rather, she had discovered that minding was heavy luggage, and she had somewhere to be.

"Papa says you almost didn't come today," she said.

Isandro glanced at Fen, who examined the horizon with great diplomatic interest. "Your father," she said, "has a big mouth for a man who preaches discretion."

"He says you wrote that you weren't sure you should keep coming. Now that I'm nearly grown."

"Did he also say why I changed my mind?"

"He said you'd tell me yourself, eventually, when I asked the right question." Maren took a breath. She had rehearsed this on the walk from the nursery, and the rehearsal had gone much better. "What did the orders say? The real ones. Nobody will ever tell me. Papa won't, the books won't. Old Tomas says I should let dead things lie. But you were the one carrying them, and you didn't let them lie. So I think you get to say."

For a long moment Isandro was quiet. The mule shifted its weight. In the orchard, wind moved through the rows and the red cords lifted and fell, lifted and fell, like a hundred small flags of no nation whatsoever.

"They were coordinates," Isandro said at last. "Artillery grids. Every street I'd walked that week, every oven that was lit, the granary, the consulate, the well on Tanner's Row where the children played. The Republic of Vellamare wasn't negotiating for Lantern's Reach. It was measuring it for burial." She turned the honey jar slowly in her fingers. "I'd carried sealed orders my whole life. Weight, wax, urgency — that's how you learn to read them. And those ones weighed wrong. Not heavier. Just... wrong, the way a promise weighs wrong when you already know you'll break it."

"And you chose us instead."

"I chose a promise instead." Isandro smiled, and it was the expression Maren loved best and least understood — grief and warmth in the same face, comfortable neighbors. "Years before, in peacetime, your father pulled me out of a frozen river and made me swear a foolish oath. If he ever sent for me, I'd come without asking why. I swore easily, because it cost nothing. Then one night there was a knock, and a note in his handwriting, and a girl on the steps, and the bill came due." She looked at Maren steadily. "Belonging isn't a flag, little one. I had a flag my whole life. Belonging is a promise somebody kept when keeping it ruined them. You were mine. That's all the history there is."

Maren nodded, because her throat had closed up, and because nodding was what you did when the right question finally got answered and the answer was bigger than you'd budgeted for.

Then, because she was Fen's daughter and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving a tender moment unattended:

"Eleven gray hairs," she said, "and worth every one."

* * *

They planted the lantern at dusk.

This was the tradition, such as it was — three years old now, invented by Maren, tolerated by her father, secretly adored by everyone. At the top of each new tree, on the evening of its planting, you hung a lantern. Not to keep. To give back. Because once, in another life, in a city that no longer existed in quite the same shape, a lantern in a window had meant *someone is home, someone is waiting, come in out of the dark.*

Fen lit the wick with a taper and handed it up to her. Maren climbed the ladder they'd left leaning against the young tree — it was barely tall enough to need a ladder, which was part of the joke, part of the hope — and hooked the lantern onto the highest branch that would bear it. The branch swayed. The light swung out over the darkening orchard, and down the rows she could see the others: dozens of small flames, one for every tree planted since the third spring, burning in the bare branches like a constellation that had decided to take root.

From the wall, Isandro watched. She always watched this part from the wall. Maren had never asked why, but she suspected it was the same reason she herself sometimes woke at night listening for boots: some doors you knock on forever, just to hear them open.

"Higher?" Fen called.

"This is as high as the tree goes, Papa."

"Then we've reached the limit of tonight's ambitions. Excellent. Ambition is overrated. Ask any republic."

Maren climbed down and stood beside him in the grass, and the three of them looked at the little tree with its borrowed star. Tomorrow it would just be a tree with a lantern on it. Next year it would be taller. In twenty years, children who had never heard of Vellamare or Fen or the woman on the cart would pick apples from its branches and complain about worms, and none of it — not one seed of it — would belong to any nation at all.

"Well," Isandro said. She hopped down off the wall and gathered the honey jar and the lunch wrappings with brisk, practiced hands, the hands of a woman who had learned never to leave evidence. "Same time next spring?"

"You know the way," Fen said.

"I always did." She paused, then, and looked at the two of them — the traitor general with dirt under his nails, and the daughter who had been a message once, and was now simply a girl with soil on her knees. Something passed across her face that Maren was too young to name and old enough to recognize: the look of a person checking, one more time, that the thing she broke herself to save was, in fact, saved.

It was. Maren made sure of it. She stepped forward and hugged the old courier hard, smelling of road dust and honey and patience, and Isandro held on a moment longer than strictly necessary, the way you hold on to proof.

"Eleven hairs," Maren said into her shoulder. "Counted honestly."

"Worth every one," Isandro said. "Keep the lantern lit."

And she climbed onto the cart, and clicked her tongue at the treaty-named mule, and the wheels crunched away down the orchard road toward the lights of Lantern's Reach — where, as it happened, an oven had been lit against all sense and all memory, and the smell of bread followed her all the way home.
