## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The tide went out at four o'clock, and Mara Vane went out with it, the way they did every morning the water allowed.

Saltmere harbor emptied in layers. First the ships lifted on their moorings and leaned like tired men against nothing. Then the mud began to show itself, brown-green and gleaming, stitched with the tracks of gulls and the long drag-marks of carts. Last came the smell—brine and rot and coal-smoke and something underneath it all, older, that Mara had never found a name for and suspected no one born above the tideline ever would. It was the smell of everything the harbor had swallowed and not digested.

Mara waded in to the shins, then the knees, then bent and slid under.

* * *

Down there, the world was simple.

Sound arrived as a great soft pressing, the sea's whole weight laid gently against the skull. Light came down in slanted columns, thick with stirred silt, and moved across the harbor floor like the shadows of things too large to be seen. Mara's hands knew the work without the mind's help: fingers walking through cold muck, reading it the way a clerk reads a page. A shell. A stone worn round. The knuckle of some drowned timber. And once in every hundred handfuls, if the luck was in and the pearls were loose—a small hard grit of nacre, warm-seeming even in the cold, catching the filtered light like an eye that had opened underwater and was watching.

Mara had learned to hold their breath long before they learned their letters. Four years old, standing in the shallows off the divers' quarter while Joren Vane floated on his back nearby, teaching by demonstration. *Fill your belly first,* he'd said, or the memory said he'd said; ten years can wear a voice down to its shape alone. *The air is in you already. You only have to be willing to keep it.* Then he'd gone under without a splash, and Mara had counted—one, two, three, all the way to forty, because forty was the age their father claimed for himself, though their mother swore he was thirty-seven—and up he'd come, streaming, laughing, a wet black head and white teeth in it.

Forty. The number rose with Mara now, unbidden, every time the lungs began to burn. They never let themselves surface before forty. It was a kind of prayer, though Mara had stopped believing in anything that answered prayers a long time ago. It was more a kind of arithmetic: this much breath belonged to my father, and I will spend it in his memory, and the ledger will balance.

Everything, in Saltmere, came back to ledgers eventually.

* * *

Mara surfaced near the pilings of the customs pier with two pearls in their cheek-pouch and a third cupped under their tongue, which was a trick no one had taught them and which they had never mentioned to anyone, because tricks like that were the kind of thing that got an indentured diver's rations cut on suspicion of hoarding. The company owned everything that came out of the harbor. That was written somewhere, in some book, in some hand Mara had never been permitted to see: *all yield of bed and strand, being the property of the Halloway Trading Company, to be surrendered at the sorting sheds.* Divers who worked the deep beds for the fleet got wages and a percentage. Mara was not fleet. Mara was harbor-caste, dock-registered, indentured since the age of nine, and what they gleaned from the mud at low tide belonged, technically, entirely, legally, to Halloway's.

So the pearls in Mara's mouth were theft, technically. Legally. Entirely.

They were also breakfast.

There were ways of thinking about this that made it bearable. Sable had a way—Sable had a way for everything, most of them involving stolen rum—but Mara's way was quieter. The way Mara saw it, the company had taken a debt from their family and called it an inheritance, had taken nine years of their life and called it education, took six days of labor out of seven and called it opportunity. Against so vast a thieving, three small pearls in a mouth full of salt water were not theft at all. They were change. Coin owed and pocketed from an enormous, indifferent till.

Mara spat them into their palm at the shadow-line beneath the pier, where the watch didn't look, and wrapped them in a scrap of oilcloth, and pushed the bundle deep into the lining of their boot.

* * *

The sorting sheds stood on stilts over the tidal flats, a row of grey weathered boxes leaning together like conspirators. Inside, the smell of brine gave way to the smell of oyster meat and lamp-oil and the particular sourness of women and men who had been wet since dawn. Mara joined the line at the washing troughs, wrung out their hair, and took their place at a sorting bench between old Petrel and a girl of fourteen whose name changed depending on which overseer was asking.

"Anything?" Petrel asked, not looking up. Her fingers moved through the tray of shell and grit with a blind, uncanny speed, forty years of it, her eyes clouded white at the edges the way all old divers' eyes go.

"A little."

"Mm." A pearl clicked into her keep-pile. "Little keeps a body alive same as much. Slower."

That was Petrel entire. She had pulled Mara half-drowned out of the harbor when they were eleven, after a mooring cable caught their ankle, and had never once referred to it since. She had known Joren Vane, had dived beside him, had held Mara's mother's hand at the burning—which wasn't a burning at all but a leaving-off of the dead man's clothes into the tide, since there was nothing else to burn—and she spoke of him the way she spoke of weather. Gone. Past. Not to be walked into again.

"The beds are thinning," Petrel said now, turning a shell over. "You feel it?"

"I feel it."

"Company doesn't feel it. Company feels numbers." She snorted, a wet old sound. "Numbers don't drown."

"No," Mara said. "People do."

Petrel glanced at them then, quick and sharp from under her clouded lids, and said nothing more, and the shells went on clicking, and the lamps burned low in the grey morning light coming through the slatted walls.

* * *

History, as far as Mara could tell, happened elsewhere.

It happened to people with surnames that mattered—Halloway, Voss, the harbormaster's people, whoever sat on the magistrate's bench across the strait. It happened in rooms with doors, in books with seals, at tables where men wore coats worth more than a diver's boat. History was the founding of the Company; history was the charter signed by a governor whose portrait hung in the ledger room, gold frame, gold chain, gold face; history was wars declared and tariffs levied and fleets commissioned. None of it required Mara's attendance. None of it required the attendance of anyone Mara had ever eaten beside, slept beside, bled beside.

What happened to people like Mara was not history. It was weather. It was accountancy. It was the tide going out at four o'clock and coming back whenever it pleased.

Their mother had taught them this without meaning to, in the last years, when her hands had gone stiff and her voice had gone flat as slate. *Don't put your name on anything,* she used to say, pressing the point into Mara's shoulder with one crooked finger. *A name is a handle. Anything with a handle gets picked up. Get picked up enough times and you get carried off.* She had died unremarked in the infirmary ward, of a cough, at fifty-one, and the Company had entered her death in a book in a fair round hand, and that entry—that single line of ink—was the closest any Vane had come to history in living memory.

Mara had made a philosophy of it. It wasn't resignation, they told themselves. Resignation implied wanting something different and giving up. This was simpler: an understanding, reached early and maintained daily, that the world was divided into the recorded and the unrecorded, and that Mara had been born on the wrong side of that line, and that the wise course—the only course—was to live small, dive quiet, keep your name off things, and take your three pearls from the mud like everyone's ancestors had done since before there were companies or charters or men with gold faces in gold frames.

It was a good philosophy. It held the weight of nineteen years. Most mornings it held without creaking.

Most mornings.

* * *

The bell rang the shift-change at noon, and Mara climbed the stilt-stairs to the shed's walkway to eat. The ration was barley bread and salt fish and a cup of something hot that pretended to be tea. Mara ate sitting on the rail with their boots dangling over the flats, watching the water come back in, and Sable dropped down beside them the way Sable dropped everywhere—suddenly, loudly, displacing air.

"You've got the look," Sable said.

"What look."

"The look of somebody counting something." Sable tore their bread in half and shoved the larger portion at Mara, which was how Sable loved people: by force. "Counting what? Pearls? Days? Your miserable life?"

"My shift."

"Boring answer. Give me the interesting one."

Sable was twenty-two, broad as a door, a smuggler's runner by trade and a liar by religion, with a gap in their grin and a knife in each boot and a laugh that could be heard from the masthead. They had grown up three berths down from Mara in the divers' quarter, and when they were fifteen they had sworn loyalty over a bottle of rum stolen jointly from a customs lighter—sworn it seriously, kneeling on the wet boards, hands clasped, which Sable had ruined halfway through by hiccupping. Ten years of dockside friendship had proven the oath mostly true. Mostly was a generous word on the Saltmere docks, and Mara knew it, and loved Sable anyway, carefully, the way you love a rope you also test.

"I'm counting nothing," Mara said. "I'm eating."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Brooding has a flavor." Sable squinted at them sideways. "It's the tenth, isn't it. Of the month."

Mara looked at the water. The tenth of the month was the anniversary—not marked anywhere, entered in no book, observed by no bell—of the night the Pearl of Anselm's fleet went down. Nine years ago this very tide. Forty-one boats out of the rival fleet caught in a squall that came off the strait too fast for any signal to be run up, so the story went, so the papers said, so the Company's own broadsheet had it: *Act of God, grievous loss, the sea keeps its own accounts.* Forty-one boats, and thirty-eight recovered, and three never found, and forty divers drowned in the sight of their own harbor lights, close enough that the shouting carried across the water, according to those who had been on the quay that night. Close enough, old Petrel said once, drunker than usual, that you could hear them begging.

Mara's father had been among them. Joren Vane, diver, aged forty, entered in a Company book in a fair round hand: *lost at sea, storm, debt forgiven.*

Debt forgiven. As if drowning were a payment. As if a man's death could settle what he owed for the air he breathed.

"I don't mark it," Mara said. "Marking things does nothing."

"Nothing does nothing," Sable agreed cheerfully. "But marking nothing is different from plain nothing. Ask any priest." They bumped Mara's shoulder with their own, an impact like a fender striking a hull. "Come drink tonight. Rum's cheap and grief's expensive. We'll balance the books."

"I have copying."

"You always have copying."

"It pays double rations."

"It pays double rations," Sable mimicked, in a pinched clerk's whine, "and costs you your youth, and what will you have at the end of it? Ink in your veins and a hump?" They shook their head, grinning, and Mara almost smiled, and the water came on in below them, patient, filling the harbor back up over the mud, over the beds, over everything the sea kept and would not surrender.

* * *

Nights, Mara copied.

The ledger room on the third floor of the Halloway counting-house was lit by eight lamps and staffed by twelve clerks, and Mara was none of these and all of theirs. Indentured workers with a steady hand and a clean record could be assigned night-copying, and Mara's hand was steady and their record was spotless, because being unremarkable was not merely their philosophy but their craft. For three years they had sat at the low bench by the window, transferring entries from rough day-books into the master ledgers in their careful, characterless hand, while clerks with surnames that mattered argued about freight rates around them as if Mara were furniture. Furniture heard everything. Furniture was never asked to repeat it.

Tonight the room smelled of tallow and cold coffee, and the rain had started again against the high windows, and the clerks' voices had wound down to the occasional cough and scratch of pen. Mara copied an entry for salted herring. Another for rope. Another for—

They stopped.

Not visibly. Stopping visibly was a death in that room; Mara had seen a copy-boy struck for hesitating over a smudged figure. They stopped invisibly, the pen still moving, finishing the line ahead with the same even pressure, while their eyes went back and read the entry above again.

*Vane, J., diver — acct. closed. Balance settled by casualty. See annex, folio 41. Initialed: E.T.*

E.T. Elias Thorn, senior clerk, a grey man with a grey cough who had never spoken to Mara in three years of shared lamplight. The entry itself was ordinary; Mara had copied a dozen like it, deaths folded into debts, lives closed like accounts. What was strange was the ink. Someone had crossed out the original line—crossed it out with care, not the quick slash clerks used but a slow, deliberate stroke, twice over, as if burying it—and rewritten it above in a different rhythm, tighter, hurried. As though the truth had needed to be made to say something else, and someone had done the making.

Mara copied it as written. Their hand did not shake. Their hand never shook. But something behind their breastbone had begun, very quietly, to move—an unease like the first stir of a swell far offshore, nothing yet, a lifting of the water's shoulder that might be anything, might be nothing, might mean weather coming or might mean nothing at all.

At midnight the clerks filed out, and Thorn paused at the door—the grey man, the coughing man—and looked back at Mara's bench for a moment longer than furniture warranted. His eyes were pale and tired and unreadable. He said nothing. He left.

Mara sat alone with eight lamps and the rain and the ledger open under their hands, and read the crossed-out line again, and again, until the words lost their shapes.

Then they blew out the nearest lamp, closed the book, and went down the stairs into the dark with the swell still rising somewhere far off inside them, too distant yet to name, too steady to ignore.
