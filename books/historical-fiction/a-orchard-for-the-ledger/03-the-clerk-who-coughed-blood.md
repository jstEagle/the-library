## 3. The Clerk Who Coughed Blood

The cough came first through the wall.

Mara had learned the ledger room the way they learned tides—by sound before sight. The building breathed at night: pipes ticking as the boilers cooled, wind worrying the shutters on the seaward side, the long groan of timber settling like an old man into a chair. And beneath all of it, faint and wet and getting worse by the week, the coughing of Elias Thorn in his office behind the glass partition, where he worked past midnight the way Mara did, though for reasons Mara could only guess at.

They had been three weeks into the night-copying rotation when the coughing began to change character. At first it was just a clerk's cough—the dry bark of a man who breathed ledger dust and lamp oil and the cold salt air that came off the harbor through gaps no carpenter had ever bothered with. Then it deepened. It developed a rattle at the bottom of it, like water sloshing in the hold of a ship too heavily loaded. By the fourth week there was color in it, and one morning Mara found a handkerchief forgotten on the copying table, folded small and neat, with rust-brown spots pressed into its center like flowers kept between pages.

Mara refolded it exactly as it had been and said nothing. Saying nothing was the oldest skill they had.

* * *

Elias Thorn was not what Mara had expected from a senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company. The senior men were mostly built like the company itself: broad, red-faced, upholstered in good wool, their voices carrying the particular authority of people who had never once been told *no* by anyone whose opinion mattered. Thorn was none of this. He was narrow and gray and stooped already at fifty-odd years, with ink stains climbing both forearms like ivy gone wrong, and he moved through the daytime offices with a kind of apologetic precision, as if he were sorry to occupy so much space.

Mara knew him the way the indentured know everyone—by function, not by name. He was *the one who checks the tallies*. He was *the one who smells of camphor*. It was Petrel who gave him his name, years ago, pointing with her chin across the sorting floor: *That's Thorn. Old Thorn. Been there since before your da drowned, I'd wager.* And so he became Thorn, and stayed Thorn, and Mara thought of him perhaps once a month, when his shadow crossed the doorway of whatever room they were invisible in.

Now his shadow fell across the copying table every night, and Mara could not stop noticing it.

* * *

It began with the desk.

Thorn's office stood behind a glass partition at the far end of the ledger room, close enough that Mara could see the green shade of his lamp burning late, could hear the scratch of his pen, the cough, the pause, the scratch again. His desk sat beneath the window, and its drawers had always been shut. This was not a detail most people would track, but Mara tracked everything, because tracking everything was how you stayed alive in a place that ate the unwatchful. The Halloway Company ran on locked things: locked ledgers, locked storerooms, locked gates at the end of the indenture dormitories' lane. A thing unlocked was a thing about to change.

On a Tuesday in the gray hour after midnight, Mara looked up from copying a column of pearl weights and saw that Thorn's middle drawer hung open a hand's width. Just that. Nothing spilled out, nothing gleamed inside. The drawer simply stood open like a mouth about to speak.

Mara copied four more lines without looking up again.

The next night, the drawer was open wider. On the third night, Thorn himself came out of his office while Mara worked, walked the length of the room without a word, and stood at the shelf behind them, pulling down a volume of harbor tariffs he plainly did not need—he had taken the same volume down two nights running, and put it back in the same place, having opened it nowhere near its proper page either time.

He was watching them. Not the way the overseers watched, which was the flat watch of ownership, counting hands and hours. He watched the way Mara themselves watched: sideways, patiently, waiting to see what a thing did when it thought no one was looking.

Mara kept their eyes on the page and made their pen move and thought, with a coldness that surprised them: *he is dying, and dying men get strange.*

* * *

The tests started small.

A figure transposed in a shipping total—741 entered where the source ledger said 471—left standing overnight in the fair copy. Mara caught it at the start of the next shift, corrected it in their careful, unremarkable hand, and said nothing. Thorn's drawer was open a little wider that week.

Then a date. The source read *the twelfth of March*; the draft copy on Mara's table read *the twenty-first*, and any copyist working half-asleep would have carried the error forward without a flicker. Mara struck it and corrected it. Across the room, behind the glass, the pen-scratch paused for a long moment, then resumed.

Then something crueler. One of the night foremen's summary sheets arrived with a column of diver's names against a column of shares, and one share calculated short—a widow's portion, the name beside it *Okonkwo, R.*, marked *deceased, balance to estate*. The arithmetic was wrong by eleven shillings. Not much. Enough to matter enormously to a widow with no surname that mattered, which was, Mara suspected, precisely the point.

Mara sat over that error a long time. Their pen hovered. Eleven shillings, corrected, meant the company paid out money it had quietly intended to keep; it also meant announcing, in ink, that the indentured copyist had checked a senior man's figures and found them wanting. Uncorrected, it meant a woman across the strait went eleven shillings poorer and never knew why the world tipped against her.

Mara corrected it. Small letters, neat, unremarkable, the correction initialed with the single letter *V* that stood in for their whole self on company documents. They felt Thorn's attention on their back the whole time, warm as lamplight, and they hated how much it felt less like scrutiny than like approval.

*What are you doing,* Mara thought, pressing the nib clean. *What game is this, old man, and what does it cost me when you lose?*

* * *

The answer came in pieces, the way answers do in Saltmere—never whole, always salted with doubt.

Petrel provided the first piece, because Petrel provided every piece of harbor history worth having, delivered in her cracked voice over the sorting tables while her fingers flew through oyster shell. She had been diving since before Mara was born and had come up from her last dive with a burst ear that left her half-deaf on the left side and entirely unafraid on both.

"Old Thorn," she said one evening, cracking a shell with her thumb, "you've noticed him circling."

Mara kept their eyes on the pearls. "I copy pages. He checks pages."

"Mm." Petrel smiled with half her mouth. "Forty years I've watched that man. Came here young as you, some say younger. Never married. Never drunk. Never promoted past senior clerk, though he knows more of the company's books than the directors do themselves—you don't rise in Halloway's by knowing too much, girl, you rise by not knowing it." She let a handful of shell clatter into the discard bin. "And now he's coughing his lungs into his handkerchief and leaving his drawers open like a man setting a table for someone. Mark me. Something's eating him worse than the sickness is."

"You talk like the weather's coming," Mara said.

"The weather is always coming." Petrel turned back to her shells. "Question is whether you're ashore or afloat when it lands."

That night Mara lay in the dormitory bunk listening to the rain and turned the conversation over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath. Thorn was testing them. That much was certain. But a man tested a lock only when he meant to use the door.

* * *

* * *

The second piece came from Thorn himself, in the strangest form yet.

It was near the end of a shift, the lamps burned low, when Mara finished a fair copy of the quarterly salvage manifest and rose to carry it to the delivery tray outside Thorn's office. As they passed the partition, his voice stopped them—not loud, but pitched to carry, the way a man speaks when he has rehearsed the moment.

"Vane."

Mara turned. No one used the name aloud in the offices. On the documents it was a letter; in the mouths of overseers it was a bark; but spoken quietly, in a lamplit room at midnight, it sounded suddenly enormous, a word with a drowned man attached to it.

"Sir."

Thorn stood at his desk, one hand flat upon an open ledger, and he looked at Mara for a long moment with eyes that were yellowed at the edges and very tired and very awake. "The Okonkwo entry. You corrected it."

"Yes, sir."

"Eleven shillings."

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

It was not an overseer's question. An overseer asked *who told you to*. This question had another weight entirely, and Mara felt the floor of their careful life tilt a degree beneath it. They chose the safe answer, the small answer. "The figures didn't carry, sir. I correct what doesn't carry."

"That isn't what I asked." He coughed then, briefly, into his closed fist, and Mara watched him master it the way a sailor masters a listing deck. When he spoke again his voice had thinned. "You can read more than you admit. You've been able to since you came to this room. I've watched you read."

Mara said nothing. Denial was a door that swung both ways; silence was a wall.

Thorn studied them across the lamplight, and something passed over his face that Mara had never seen directed at them before—not suspicion, not contempt, not the dull accounting look the company gave its property. It took Mara a moment to name it, because it was so out of place in the Halloway Building that it seemed almost foreign: recognition. The look of a man finding another face like his own reflected somewhere unexpected.

"You copy beautifully," Thorn said at last. "The best hand in this room, and this room holds men who went to school in the capital. Do you know what I did with my first wage, Vane? Before you were born. I bought a book. A poetry book, of all foolishness." His mouth bent, not quite a smile. "My father beat me for it. Said books were for men with land." He closed the ledger gently, as if it were sleeping. "Go home. You've worked past your hour."

Mara went. But at the door of the ledger room they looked back once, and saw Elias Thorn still standing at his desk with his hand resting on the closed book, looking at nothing, coughing softly into his collar, a gray man in a gray room keeping some gray fire alive that Mara could not see—and could no longer pretend not to feel.

* * *

After that night, the drawer was always open.

Not wide. A hand's width, steady as a heartbeat, every shift. And the contents changed in ways that could not be accidental: a folio of harbor charts laid where a passing glance would catch the title; a bundle of correspondence tied in string, the top letter franked with the seal of the harbormaster's office, ten years old by its dating; once, unmistakably, a page of the fleet manifests—the 1889 manifests, the season of the storm—left squared neatly atop the pile like bread set out for a bird.

Mara never touched them. They looked, because not looking would have been its own confession, and they memorized what they saw, because memory was the only vault no one could search. But they did not touch, and each night Thorn watched them not touch, and each night something in the air between the glass partition and the copying table grew heavier, charged, like the pressure before a storm that Petrel always claimed to smell coming off the water.

Two invisibles, Mara thought, recognizing each other across a lamplit room. That was the truth of it, and it frightened them more than discovery would have. Because invisibles who recognized each other stopped being invisible together, and the company had ways of dealing with things that became visible. The harbor was full of stories about that. Most of them ended in the water.

* * *

On the last night of the month, the coughing turned terrible.

Mara heard it through the partition—no longer barks but long, tearing pulls, the sound of a man hauling something heavy up out of himself, and after it a silence too long, and then the small bright clink of a bottle against glass. The pen-scratch did not resume. Mara copied two lines, three, listening, and the silence went on, and finally they rose, telling themselves it was only to stretch their legs, and crossed to the partition.

Thorn sat slumped at his desk, head bowed over his arms, and for one lurching moment Mara thought he was dead. Then his shoulders moved with breath, shallow and ragged, and his hand lay open on the desk beside the lamp, and in it, crumpled, was the handkerchief—so soaked now that the rust-brown had spread nearly to its edges, like a map of some country drowning.

His eyes opened. Found Mara through the glass. And held there.

There was no performance left in the look, no test, no clever old man's game. What Mara saw in Elias Thorn's face then was naked and urgent and afraid, and it was aimed at them with the directness of a thrown knife, and underneath the fear, unmistakably, was apology—for the game, for the tests, for the time being wasted that neither of them had.

"Tomorrow," he said hoarsely. "Come early tomorrow, Vane. Before the day clerks."

The lamp guttered in a draft off the harbor. Behind him, through the dark window, the water of Saltmere harbor lay black and patient under the moon, keeping everything it had ever been given.

Mara stood with their heart hammering against their ribs like a fist on a locked door, and heard themselves answer:

"I will."
