## 4. What the Water Keeps

The ledger room smelled of tallow and old paper at night, but by day the harbor smelled of everything else—brine, tar, fish rot, rope gone soft with salt—and it was to that smell that Mara's body returned each morning as though pulled on a line. Nine years of indenture had taught them this much: the body keeps its own schedule. It woke before the bell. It bent toward the water without being asked.

That morning the water was green-glass calm, the kind of calm Saltmere only got in late autumn when the wind forgot its work for a few days. Mara stood at the edge of the sorting sheds with a net bag and a weighted cord, watching light lie flat on the harbor floor like a coin too large to lift.

*History happens elsewhere,* they reminded themselves, the way other people said their prayers. *To people whose names get printed in full.* Vane was not such a name. Vane was four letters crossed out in a ledger with a stroke so careful it might have been an apology—or a signature.

They dove for loose pearls because loose pearls were wages the company could not count. Oysters dragged up by the dredgers shed pearls in the churning, and the company men, who counted everything, did not count what rolled back into the silt. But divers knew where silt gathered like secrets. Mara had learned from Petrel, and Petrel had learned from the drowned.

Mara went down.

The cold took them first, then the hush, then the strange forgiveness of being weightless—a body unburdened of every debt except breath. Below, the harbor floor lay in pale shelves, and the light came down in columns that moved when Mara moved, as though the water were remembering something and couldn't decide what.

It was always down here that the past arrived. Not in daylight, not over tables, but here, where their father had lived half his life and given all of it.

Joren Vane had been a free diver—the last of the old guild trained in the deep beds beyond the point, before Halloway's contracts turned diving into piecework and freedom into a word clerks used. He could hold his breath the length of three bells. He could read the sea the way Mara now read ledgers: not the words on top but the pattern underneath, the places where numbers refused to sit straight. He used to say the water kept what men threw away, and one day it would hand it all back, itemized.

Mara found a pearl in the shadow of a mooring stone, small and gray as a fingernail, and closed their fist around it. In their chest, breath began its slow argument. They stayed anyway. Down here the world above was only rumor—Halloway's flag, the bell, the roll-call, the careful crossed-out name in the third-floor ledger. Elias Thorn's unlocked desk. The way the old clerk watched Mara copy pages with eyes like a man checking arithmetic he already knew the sum of.

*Later,* Mara told the thought, and pressed it down into the silt where they kept everything they were not allowed to carry.

Their lungs insisted. They rose.

* * *

The morning the rival fleet went down, Mara was nine years old and standing on the north quay with wet boots and a stolen apple, which is how memory preserves the innocence of catastrophe—as something small and sweet in the mouth while the world ends politely offshore.

The fleet belonged to the Meridian Pearl Company, Halloway's only rival worth the name. Eleven boats, forty-one divers, most of them guild-trained like Joren, working the outer beds where the water went dark blue and the pearls came up fat as thumbnails. Every diver in Saltmere knew the Meridian men. They drank in the same taverns. Their children swam in the same shallows. Rivalry between companies was a quarrel among men in good coats; rivalry between divers was a shared craft, and craft made kin.

That morning the sky had been the color of unpainted wood, and the wind had come out of the southwest in long slow pulls, the way it does before it decides to become something else.

Mara remembered their father crouching on the quay stones beside them, lashing his knife sheath tighter, squinting south.

"Feel that?" he'd said.

"Wind."

"Wind has manners," Joren said. "This is a question." He'd tapped Mara's chest with one knuckle, right over the sternum. "When the sky asks you a question, you answer it by getting off the water."

But the Meridian boats went out anyway, because the season was short and the company's tally-books were hungry, and because the harbormaster had posted no signal flags. That was the detail Mara would carry for ten years without understanding its weight: no flags. On a morning when every old diver on the quay was reading the southwest pull and muttering, the signal mast at the harbor station flew nothing at all—not storm warning, not caution, not the small black pennant that meant *come in, all hands.* Just the company colors, limp and ordinary, telling forty-one men the sea was open for business.

The storm came at mid-morning, fast as a slammed door.

Mara had been sent to the sheds with the other indentured children, and from there they saw it entire: the horizon going the color of wet slate, the light dying in a single minute as though someone had put a lid on the world, and then the sound—not thunder first but the sea itself, a long tearing roar like cloth ripped from hem to collar. The children were herded inside. Mara pressed their face to a shutter gap and watched eleven boats become nine, become five, become shapes, become nothing, and the rain came so hard the harbor disappeared, and the adults shouted in voices stripped of grammar.

By evening the water had smoothed itself again, the way water does, unrepentant, and the boats began coming in—or pieces of them. Dories with survivors clinging to the gunwales. Nets full of gear. Men carried up the ramp wrapped in sailcloth, some breathing, most not.

Joren Vane was not among the living or the dead that night. He had gone out that morning as a guest aboard the Meridian boat called the *Kindly Tern*, invited by old Captain Ives to help find a new bed past the point—guild knowledge, freely given, because craft made kin. When the storm hit, the *Tern* was farthest out.

For six days the harbor dragged the bottom. Mara waited on the quay each dusk, counting boats, counting faces, learning at nine years old the specific cruelty of a crowd that thins instead of gathers. On the seventh day a dredger brought up a knife sheath, lashing torn, and Mara's mother looked at it once and sat down on the quay stones and did not stand for a long time.

The company's verdict came within the week, printed neatly in the *Saltmere Courier*: *Freak Squall; Heavy Loss of Life; No Neglect Alleged.* An act of nature. A tragedy of weather. The harbormaster expressed condolences. Halloway's directors pledged relief funds for widows—of which there would be a list, administered by clerks, disbursed against debts. Mara's mother signed papers rather than starve, and the papers bound her daughter to the Halloway Trading Company until the age of twenty-one, and the clerk who entered the contract wrote *VANE, MARA* in a fine round hand, four letters, no history attached.

An act of nature. Mara believed it the way you believe the floor beneath you—without thinking, because thinking about it would mean falling. The sea was the sea. It kept what it kept. Grief, if it had nowhere to go, could be folded into that sentence like a pearl into flesh: an irritant made smooth by years of coating it in the same word. *Weather.*

Petrel said otherwise. Petrel said many things, being old and half-deaf and therefore immune to consequence, but on certain nights, after rum, Petrel said it plainly: "No flags, child. You don't forget flags on a day like that unless your hands are busy holding something else."

Mara had learned not to ask what hands held what. Questions, in Saltmere, were a currency spent only by people who could afford the answers.

* * *

Now, nineteen, dripping on the quay with one gray pearl in their fist and the morning bell ringing across the water, Mara shook the memory off the way a dog shakes off the sea. It was an old habit, well-practiced: surface, wring out the past, fold it small, pocket it, walk. The dead did not need them staring at horizons. The ledgers needed copying, and Thorn's desk sat unlocked again, and whatever that meant, it lived in the present tense, which was the only tense an indentured person could safely inhabit.

They climbed the company stairs at noon with their shift wet at the cuffs, and found the ledger room washed in thin light, and Thorn at his window with his handkerchief folded in his lap, watching the harbor.

"You dive," he said. Not a question. Old men who coughed blood had earned the right to skip questions.

"Loose pearls," Mara said. "Off the dredger grounds. It's allowed."

"It's allowed," Thorn agreed mildly. "A great deal is allowed, if no one bothers to forbid it." He turned from the window. His face was the color of the paper he worked with, and there was fresh blood on the handkerchief's fold, a rust-brown crescent like a tiny harbor of its own. "Your father dove too, didn't he. Deep man. Guild-trained."

The room went very still around Mara, the way water goes still before it decides to be something else.

"I wouldn't know, sir."

"No," Thorn said softly, and there was something in his voice that was almost tenderness, or almost pity, and either one frightened Mara more than anger would have. "You wouldn't. That's precisely the trouble with this city—it makes sure nobody knows anything." He looked down at the open ledger on his desk, at the page where, two nights ago, Mara had seen the name *VANE* struck through with a stroke so careful it could have been drawn with a ruler. "Some entries want correcting, girl. And some want reading twice."

He closed the book. Outside, gulls argued over something dead, and the harbor lay green and calm and keeping, keeping, keeping everything it had ever been given.

That night, Mara dreamed of signal masts flying nothing at all, and woke with their father's name in their mouth like a stone they'd slept on—and heard, through the dormitory wall, footsteps in the corridor that stopped outside their door, waited, and moved on.

Down the hall, someone coughed. It did not stop quickly enough to be anyone else.
