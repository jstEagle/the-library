## 5. A Letter Under the Floorboard

The lamp above Elias Thorn's desk had begun to gutter an hour before he did.

Mara noticed it the way they noticed everything in the ledger room—sideways, without seeming to. The flame had developed a stutter, a small arrhythmic hitch like a man climbing stairs who keeps forgetting which foot comes next. Oil was low. Someone would have to fetch more from the storeroom on the second floor, and someone always meant Mara, because fetching was what invisible people were for.

But Thorn had not asked. He sat at his desk with his shoulders drawn up under his waistcoat as though the room were cold, though it was not—September still held the harbor's breath in its lungs, and the third-floor windows stood open to let out the smell of ink and tallow and forty years of paper slowly turning back into dust. His pen moved. Stopped. Moved again. From across the room, where Mara copied shipping manifests at the long table with four other night clerks, the sound of that pen had become a kind of weather. Tonight it kept changing.

*Scratch. Scratch.* Silence. A wet, careful breath.

Mara copied a column of tonnage figures and did not look up. This was the discipline of nine years' indenture: to see without being seen seeing, to know without being known knowing. Old Petrel called it *crab-walking through the world*, sideways and low, and Mara had made an art of it. They knew the exact angle at which their eyes could rest on a thing while their face remained empty as a warehouse wall.

The other clerks had gone home one by one as the clock over the customs house struck eleven. By half past there were only three of them left: Mara, the boy Wrenn who slept with his eyes half-open and could not be trusted with anything but addition, and Thorn.

Thorn, who for two weeks had been leaving things unlocked.

It had started small—a drawer ajar, its brass handle catching lamplight like a raised eyebrow. Then the cabinet of correspondence, which every clerk in Saltmere knew was locked against even the company's own shadow. Then, most strange of all, Thorn himself: watching Mara copy pages with a flat, patient attention that had nothing of suspicion in it and everything of examination. Twice he had introduced errors into the documents he passed down—transposed digits, a date written wrong—and twice Mara had corrected them silently, in their own fair hand, and said nothing. The second time, Thorn had looked at them for a long moment with something moving behind his face like fish under ice.

He was testing whether they could read. Not letters—any indentured drudge could be taught letters, the company saw to that much, because a worker who couldn't read a manifest was a worker who ruined cargo. He was testing whether they could read *between*. Whether they understood that numbers told stories, that ledgers were confessions written in a code so dull no one bothered to guard it.

Mara had decided, carefully, to keep failing the test just enough. To correct the errors but never meet his eyes while doing it. To remain, in all the ways that mattered, unremarkable.

Survival meant being unremarkable. That was the first law of Saltmere, older than any statute: history happened elsewhere, to people whose names appeared in newspapers instead of paybooks. Mara Vane dove for loose pearls in the harbor mud and copied other people's fortunes by candlelight, and if they had a longing anywhere in them, it lived somewhere deep and flooded, like a well no one drew from anymore.

Then the lamp guttered, and Thorn stood up too fast, and the whole architecture of invisibility came down around Mara's ears like a shelving unit struck by a cart.

* * *

He did not fall, at first. That was the terrible part—the part Mara would replay afterward, in the dark of the dormitory, hunting for the moment they might have acted differently and finding none.

Thorn rose from his desk with one hand pressed flat to his chest, and he took three steps toward the long table, and he said, in a voice pitched precisely between command and plea: "Wrenn. The infirmary. Run."

Wrenn ran. Mara heard his boots go down the stairs two at a time and fade into the stone throat of the building, and then there was nothing in the world but the guttering lamp, the tick of the long case clock, and Elias Thorn standing in the middle of the ledger room with blood rising in his throat.

Because it was blood. Mara could hear it now—the wet rattle underneath each breath, the sound of water where water should not be. They had heard it before. The divers brought up from deep water sometimes breathed like that, in their last hours, when the pressure had done its quiet work. Their father had died in the sea, but Mara had grown up among people the sea had half-killed, and they knew the sound of a body filling.

"Boy." Thorn's voice cracked on the word. "Girl. Whatever you are. Come here."

Mara came. Later they would understand that this was the moment—invisibility ended not when Thorn fell, but when Mara crossed that floor. Everything before had been crab-walking. Everything after was standing upright in open ground.

Up close, Thorn was worse than he'd seemed from across the room. His skin had gone the color of the tallow candles, gray-yellow, waxy, and there was sweat standing on his upper lip in fine beads. His eyes, though—his eyes were terribly clear. Clear the way water is clear over a drop-off, where you can see all the way down to the thing waiting.

"You corrected my figures," he said. "Twice."

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"Don't." The word came out with blood in it; he turned his head and spat into a handkerchief, delicately, the way a man does who has been doing it for months and has stopped apologizing to himself about it. "Don't waste my time with that. I haven't got much. Listen."

"Sir, you should sit—"

"*Listen.*" His hand shot out and closed on Mara's wrist—not hard, there was no strength left in it, but with the desperate precision of a drowning man choosing what to hold. And then he was pulling them down, down, both of them folding toward the floorboards beside his desk, and Mara's heart went from steady to hammering in the space of a single heartbeat, because they understood suddenly what he meant to do and every instinct screamed that whatever came next could not be taken back.

"The board," Thorn gasped. "Under the—third board, the one with the knot. I pried it loose in March. It's—it's not nailed right anymore. Feel."

His fingers guided Mara's to the floor. There, under the desk's shadow where no lamp reached, the edge of a board lifted a fraction, a dark seam in the wood. Mara's hands were shaking. They hooked their fingers into the seam and pulled, and the board came up with a groan of old nails, and beneath it lay a cavity smelling of dust and camphor, and in the cavity lay a letter.

Sealed. Wax the color of dried blood, stamped with no crest at all—just a plain thumbprint pressed into the wax, deliberate as a signature. The paper was heavy, good stock, the kind the company reserved for contracts. It had been wrapped once in oilcloth and the oilcloth had been sewn shut with thread, and the whole of it was no larger than a prayer book.

"Take it." Thorn's grip on Mara's wrist tightened. "Not later. Now. Into your shirt. Against your skin."

"Sir, I can't—I'm indentured, if they find me with—"

"If they find it on *me*, it burns, and everyone in it stays drowned." He coughed then, a long tearing cough, and Mara watched blood spot the handkerchief like berries crushed on snow. When he could speak again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, a sound meant for a distance of inches, and his face was very close to theirs, and his eyes were burning with a clarity that frightened Mara more than the blood did. "Forty names. Do you understand? Forty. I entered them myself, years ago, thinking I was keeping accounts. Thinking a ledger was a ledger. It took me twenty years to read what I'd written."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Thorn's head snapped toward the stairwell, then back.

"There are people in this building who would kill us both for that paper, and they would sleep well after. Hale—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Never mind Hale. Take it. Hide it tonight, move it tomorrow, move it *fast*. There's a ship—"

Boots on the stairs. Two sets, quick, and the yellow swing of a lantern light rising through the stairwell like something flooding upward.

Thorn did not hesitate. Twenty years of copying other men's decisions had taught him how fast a decision must be made. He pressed the letter into Mara's hands—warm, impossibly warm, warm as a living thing—and folded Mara's fingers closed over it with both of his own cold ones, and shoved them backward into the shadow behind the filing cabinets just as the lantern light broke across the doorway.

And then, in the last second before the doctor and the porter filled the room, Elias Thorn looked straight into the shadows where Mara crouched with their heart trying to break its ribs, and he whispered:

"The tide. *The tide.*"

* * *

Afterward, Mara could not say how long they stayed behind the cabinets. A minute. Ten. The doctor—a soft, competent man named Grell whom the company kept on retainer the way it kept dogs—knelt over Thorn and murmured the things doctors murmur. The porter fetched water. Thorn refused the water. Through the gap between two cabinet doors, Mara watched the top of his head, the thinning gray hair, the shoulders that had spent thirty years bent over desks finally bent past straightening.

They watched, and they did not move, and against their sternum the letter lay like a coal that would not cool.

*The tide, the tide.*

What did it mean? A ship—the Morning Tide, Mara thought, the coastal packet that ran the strait twice weekly, the one ship in Saltmere's harbor too shabby and too regular to draw the company's interest. Or the morning tide itself, the hour, the window. Or something else entirely, some instruction cut off by the arrival of boots on stairs, lost now unless Thorn lived to finish it.

*If he lives.*

Grell's voice, low: "Infirmary. Carefully, now—he'll want to lie flat." The sounds of men arranging themselves around another man's ruin. The lantern swinging shadows across the ceiling like weed in current. And then, at the door, Grell paused and looked back at the room—at the long table, the abandoned manifests, the lifted floorboard lying askew beside Thorn's desk like a mouth hanging open—and Mara felt the whole world narrow to the width of that pause.

"He was working late?" Grell asked the empty room, mildly.

No one answered him. No one was there to answer. He shrugged, and the door swung shut, and the footsteps faded, and the ledger room settled back into its ticking silence like water closing over a stone.

Mara counted to two hundred. Then they crawled out from behind the cabinets on hands and knees, replaced the floorboard—pressing it down until the seam showed only to someone who already knew—and stood up in the middle of the room with the letter flat against their chest, sewn inside the lining of their shirt where Thorn's hands had shoved it, and discovered that their legs would barely hold them.

* * *

They walked back to the dormitory the long way, along the seawall, because the direct route passed the night watchman's post and the watchman knew Mara's face and liked to talk.

Saltmere at midnight was a different city. The harbor lay black and breathing, masts swaying against the stars, the pearl sheds locked and blind. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy tolled its slow idiot note, and the smell of brine and tar and rotting kelp hung over everything like a second darkness. Mara walked with their arms folded, one hand pressed flat over their own heart, and to anyone watching they were only a tired indentured worker going home, shoulders stooped, gait uneven—one of hundreds, unremarkable, nothing.

Inside, they were screaming.

*Forty names,* Thorn had said. *I entered them myself.*

Mara did not let themselves think it. That was the discipline, the crab-walk, the whole art of survival: feel nothing now, feel it later, in a safe place, in pieces, on your own schedule. But the thought kept surfacing anyway, the way drowned things surface, bloated and undeniable, and each time Mara pushed it down and counted paving stones instead. One. Two. Three. The letter rode against their skin with every step, and it seemed to grow heavier, as though the words inside were lead shot sewn into the cloth.

In the dormitory, twelve bodies breathed in the dark, the familiar chorus of the indentured—Petrel's whistling snore, the boy Tammy's sleep-talk, the creak of bunks that had held tired workers for sixty years. Mara lay down in their narrow cot without undressing, which was not unusual; dock workers learned to sleep clothed the way sailors learned to sleep through storms. They pulled the thin blanket to their chin and stared at the ceiling beams and listened to their own heart.

Against their chest, the letter waited.

*Everyone in it stays drowned,* Thorn had said. Everyone in it. Which meant the letter held names. Names of the dead. And there was only one fleet, one storm, one night that Saltmere's poor still spoke of in the lowered voices reserved for God and the company—nine years ago, the autumn squall that came down the strait too fast for warning, that took the rival pearler's whole diving fleet with forty souls aboard while Halloway boats rode safe in the lee of the point. Forty widows. Forty families scattered into other people's ledgers.

And one of those names—

No. Mara shut the door on it. Not here. Not lying in a room full of sleeping people with a watchman walking his rounds outside. Tomorrow. Tomorrow they would find a place with walls and no eyes, and they would break the seal, and they would know.

But sleep, when it finally came near dawn, brought no mercy. It brought the harbor in winter, gray-green and vast, and their father's voice saying *the water keeps what it takes, little fish, that's why we give it only what we can spare*—and then the voice changed, became Thorn's, ragged and urgent: *the tide, the tide*—and Mara woke with their hand pressed hard over their heart as though holding a wound shut.

Gray light stood in the windows. The dormitory was stirring. And from the yard below, carried up through the glass with terrible clarity, came the voice of the day steward calling the infirmary's news across to the kitchens:

"—clerk, name of Thorn, taken bad in the night. Doctor says it's his lungs. Says he may not see the week out."

Around Mara, the room went on waking—boots dropped, bunks creaked, someone laughed about the porridge. Ordinary sounds. The ordinary world, grinding on.

Mara lay still, the letter a live coal against their breastbone, and understood with perfect, vertiginous clarity that the days of being no one were finished. Somewhere in this building Corvus Hale would soon be asking questions, and the answers would have to include a dying clerk's last hours, and Mara Vane—dive-girl, copy-drudge, nobody—was now the only person alive who knew what Elias Thorn had pressed into the dark.

*The tide, the tide.*

Two days. The Morning Tide sailed on the morning tide, day after tomorrow.

Mara got up, folded their blanket with hands that almost didn't shake, and went down to find out whether they could survive being seen.
