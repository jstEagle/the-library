## 6. Reading by Candlestub

The dormitory breathed around Mara in the dark—forty sleepers exhaling salt and tallow and the sour sweetness of bodies worked past their limits. Someone coughed in the third row. The roof beams ticked as the night cooled, contracting like a closing fist.

Mara lay in their narrow cot with the letter against their chest and did not move for a long time.

It was not fear that held them still, or not only fear. It was the strange arithmetic of the thing: a piece of paper no heavier than a gull's feather had landed on them out of a clear sky, and now everything they were—the careful smallness, the practiced dullness, the nineteen years of being furniture—was balanced on top of it like a cup on a knife's edge. One wrong breath and it would all come down.

They had waited until the dormitory settled into its deep hour, the one when even the restless went under, when the watchman's boots passed the far end of the corridor and faded toward the yard. They had counted his footsteps the way other people said prayers. Forty-one steps to the stair. Then nothing but breathing.

Now Mara sat up, swung their legs over the side of the cot without a sound, and reached beneath the straw mattress to where they had wedged the letter flat between two boards, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth torn from an old dive-coat. Their fingers found it and stopped there, resting, as if it were a sleeping animal that might wake and bite.

*Open it,* said the sensible part of them, the part that had learned to copy figures by candlelight and keep its face smooth as harbor water. *You cannot carry what you do not know.*

*Don't,* said the other part, the child part, the nine-year-old who had signed her father's name in the indenture book because he was dead and someone had to make the mark. *Some papers are doors, and once you walk through you can't walk back.*

Mara took the candlestub from the tin box under the cot—the stub they were permitted, three hours of light a week for mending and reading scripture—and struck a match against the floorboard. The flame caught. The wick guttered, steadied, threw a coin of light no bigger than a dinner plate onto the wall.

They shielded the flame with their body, the way they'd been taught, the way every indentured worker was taught: *light costs money; your darkness is a kindness we allow.* And then, kneeling on the cold boards with the blanket drawn over their shoulders like a hood, Mara unwrapped the oilcloth and broke Elias Thorn's seal.

* * *

The wax cracked with a sound too loud in the silence, a small dry snap like a knuckle. Inside were four pages, folded twice, written in Thorn's cramped clerk's hand—smaller than his ledger script, hurried in places, pressed so hard in others that the nib had nearly gone through the paper. This was not a document composed for filing. This was a man writing against the clock inside his own chest.

Mara read.

*I, Elias Thorn, senior ledger-keeper of the Halloway Trading Company of Saltmere, being of sound mind and failing body, set down what I have known for twenty-three years and kept silent about for twenty-three years, which is longer than I could bear and shorter than I deserved.*

The first page was dates. Thorn had been precise even in treason—dates, amounts, account numbers, the grammar of money that Mara knew better than any prayer. A payment entered in the spring of 1875 under the heading *Harbor Works, Miscellaneous*, forty pounds to an account marked only with a cipher. The same cipher appeared again in a private memorandum Thorn had copied from the founders' correspondence, and beside it, spelled out in full in a letter from Aldous Halloway himself to his partner Greaves:

*The harbormaster is bought and will see to the flags. Let the storm do the rest. Grief makes poor litigants.*

Mara read the sentence three times. The words did not change. That was the horror of writing—that it stayed put, that it meant exactly what it said, that unlike people and weather and memory it could not be coaxed into meaning something kinder.

*The harbormaster is bought and will see to the flags.*

The storm signal flags. Mara's mind went back—it could not help going back—to that morning twenty years ago, or rather to the morning after, when the whole town had stood along the quay looking at the wreckage coming in on the tide. Boards. A boot. A cargo net swollen with sea. And old Petrel, soaked to the ribs, screaming at anyone who would listen that there had been no warning, that the storm had come down the strait like a thief and no signal had ever flown from the tower, though every other fleet on that coast had gotten its flags up in time.

*Weather,* the company had said. *A freak squall. God's own act.* And the town, which owed Halloway its wages, had nodded, because grief makes poor litigants. Thorn had understood that before they did.

Mara turned the second page.

*The rival fleet belonged to the Meridian Pearl Cooperative, eleven luggers, forty-one divers and crew. The Company could not outbid them for the outer banks, so the Company ended them instead. The harbormaster, one Silas Cray, accepted forty pounds and a lifetime stipend to withhold the storm signals on the night of the ninth of October. He retired the following year to a house across the strait, comfortable, and has never, to my knowledge, lost a night's sleep.*

*The divers never had a chance. The storm was ordinary for that coast—a bad night, not a killing one, if a man saw it coming. Most of them died within sight of shore. Some drowned in the shallows trying to swim for the breakwater while the flags above them stayed furled.*

Mara's hands had begun to shake. They noticed it distantly, as if the hands belonged to someone else, some other kneeling figure in a dark room. They read on, because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

*Below are the names of the lost, taken from the Meridian rolls by a friend of mine who did not live long enough to regret it. I copied them into this letter twenty-three years ago and have carried them through six changes of address and two searches of my rooms. The Company believes paper burns. Paper does burn. What does not burn is a clerk who can write.*

And then the names.

Forty-one of them, in a column, the way Mara had seen ten thousand names in ten thousand ledgers—but never a column like this. *Aldous Merrin, captain. Tam Broadbelt. The brothers Fenn. Old Sefa and her daughter, who dove together.* On and on, ordinary names, dock names, the kind of names that got entered into company books as numbers and crossed out when the number no longer drew wages.

And near the bottom of the third page:

*Vane, Joren. Diver, aged thirty-four. Of the orchard road.*

* * *

The candlestub spat. Mara did not move.

Their father's name sat on the page in front of them, written in a dead man's hand, in ink that had dried twenty-three years before Mara was born, and it looked—this was the unbearable thing—it looked exactly like every other name on the list. No larger. No brighter. Just letters, just *Vane, Joren*, filed among the drowned the way he had been filed among the debts, and something in Mara's chest tore along a seam they had not known was there.

Because they had spent their whole life believing in the weather.

That was the catechism of Saltmere, the one the company taught free of charge: the sea gives and the sea takes. Your father chose the water; the water chose him. There is no one to blame, and blame is a luxury of people with surnames anyway. History happens elsewhere, to other families, in other rooms. Here there is only work, and weather, and the mercy of being overlooked.

Mara had built their entire self on that foundation. Being unremarkable was not cowardice; it was architecture. It was the wall that kept the sea out.

And now a dying clerk had handed them a single page of proof that the storm of '75 had been no more natural than a wage cut. That men had sat in a warm room above the harbor and decided, in the language of accounts, that forty lives were cheaper than competition. That the flags had stayed furled not by accident of wind but by arrangement of men, and that Mara's father had swum for the breakwater—Petrel swore he'd seen him, always had sworn it, and everyone had patted Petrel's arm and poured him another drink—drowned in sight of home, under a sky that someone had paid to keep silent.

*Grief makes poor litigants.* Aldous Halloway had written it with his own pen, and then he had built a counting house on top of forty graves, and put orphaned children to work in it, and taught those children to call the murder weather.

The candle flame bent. Mara realized they were crying—not sobbing, nothing so loud, just water running off their chin onto the page, and they snatched the letter up in terror, blotting the tear with their sleeve before it could blur a word. *Careful,* said the copy-drudge reflexes, absurd and faithful even now. *Ink runs. Originals are irreplaceable.*

They laughed then, one dry silent breath that was almost a sob, alone in the dark with a dead man's testimony and wet eyes, laughing because if they didn't they would scream, and forty sleeping people could not afford their scream.

* * *

They read the rest slowly, forcing themselves, the way you force yourself to finish a dive when the air is gone and the surface is a rumor.

The fourth page was instruction. Thorn had been a clerk to his marrow; even dying, he organized. *The magistrate Corben sails from across the strait on the morning tide two days hence aboard the packet Morning Tide, summoned by petition regarding Company shipping practices. He cannot be bought—he has tried and failed to ruin Halloway before, and knows it. But petitions are paper, and paper can be denied. Testimony under seal, delivered into his hands before the ship sails, cannot. Get this letter to him. Do not trust the gate guards—they are Hale's men to a one. Do not trust the customs house—its ledgers are rewritten weekly; I wrote half the lies myself. Trust no one whose freedom the Company holds. And destroy this if capture seems certain, for a burned truth serves the living better than a confiscated one.*

At the bottom, in a hand gone suddenly loose and large, as if the pen had slipped out of control:

*I did not sink the ships. I only kept the books. I tell myself this has made a difference all these years. It has not. Make it make a difference. —E.T.*

Mara folded the pages along Thorn's creases, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and knelt there holding them while the candle burned down toward the tin.

The floor had dropped out of the world. That was the only way to say it. For nineteen years the world had been a solid thing with rules—you work, you keep quiet, you survive, and grief is weather—and now the floor was gone and beneath it was not more floor but open black water, the same water that had closed over Joren Vane, and Mara was falling through their own history toward a truth waiting at the bottom like a hull.

Their father had been murdered. Not lost. *Murdered*, in the only language the powerful respected, which was ink.

And the proof of it was warm in their hands—warm, still, from their own body heat, from being carried against their skin all day, and Mara thought: *this is what he is now. A warmth. A weight. A name at the bottom of a page.* Twenty years of mourning weather, and here was the murderer's signature, and it weighed less than a pearl.

* * *

Outside, far off, the harbor spoke to itself—the groan of mooring lines, the slap of water on pilings, a night bird crying over the masts. Sounds Mara had heard every night of their life and never listened to. Now every ordinary noise sounded like a footstep.

They thought about Thorn's face as he'd pressed the letter into their hands, the gray lips shaping those last words. *The tide, the tide.* He had known he wouldn't last. He had chosen Mara—why? Because Mara could read beyond their admission? Because invisibility was the best disguise in Saltmere? Or simply because Mara had been nearest, and dying men do not interview their couriers?

It didn't matter. What mattered was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had changed forever: two days until the Morning Tide sailed with a magistrate aboard. Between now and then, three walls—the harbor gate, the customs house, the gangway—and somewhere behind them, patient as debt, Corvus Hale.

Mara pinched out the candlestub. Darkness came down like a lid.

In the black they rewrapped the letter and slid it back beneath the floorboard, and then, after a moment, drew it out again and held it against their chest, because putting it away felt like putting him away, and they were not ready to do that yet. Not tonight. Tonight they would sit in the dark and say the name silently, over and over, not the way it appeared in ledgers—*Vane, Joren, diver, aged thirty-four*—but the way it had sounded when it was a voice calling from a lugger deck, telling a small girl to check her knife blade and mind the current.

*Joren. Joren Vane. My father, who was murdered on a clear-sky night by men who wrote tidy figures.*

Somewhere below, in the sleeping town, a dog barked twice and quit.

And it was then—kneeling in the dark, the letter a heartbeat against their heart—that Mara heard, faint through the dormitory window, the sound of boots crossing the yard. Slow, unhurried, deliberate boots. The watchman had passed an hour ago; these were different, heavier, and they stopped directly beneath the window and did not move again.

A match flared outside, brief and orange, silhouetting the brim of a hat against the frosted glass.

Someone was standing in the yard below the workers' dormitory, at this hour, smoking a cigar and looking up.
