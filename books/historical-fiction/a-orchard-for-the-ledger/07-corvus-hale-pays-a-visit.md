## 7. Corvus Hale Pays a Visit

The summons came at dawn, folded into the morning bread ration like a stone into a swallow's crop.

*Ledger room, third floor. Report by six bells.*

Mara read it twice, then fed it to the stove in the divers' dormitory and watched it curl. No name signed. No reason given. That was how the Halloway Trading Company spoke when it wanted you to understand that reasons were a courtesy extended upward, never downward.

They dressed in the dark, fingers finding the coat seams where nothing was sewn—nothing yet, though last night Mara had sat with a needle for an hour, turning the fabric over, imagining what it would take. The letter was still under the floorboard of their old bunk in the sorting sheds, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose plank that had been loose since before they were born. It was not safe there. Nothing was safe anywhere now. But it was *there*, and knowing that was a kind of ballast.

Six bells found them climbing the stairs to the ledger room with their heart keeping its own ledger—fear on the left page, discipline on the right, and between them the thin red line of *do not let him see which column is winning.*

The room was wrong.

Mara felt it before they understood it: the particular silence of a space that has been handled by strangers. Thorn's desk stood with its drawers pulled out and set upside down on the blotter like gutted fish. His chair was angled toward the window instead of the door. The ledgers he'd kept had been re-shelved—not badly, but by someone who thought alphabetically rather than chronologically, someone who knew ink but not the room's own grammar. Even the candlestub on his desk had been moved two inches east, its wax puddle broken from the wood and reset, a pale scar.

Someone had been through everything Elias Thorn owned while he lay dying or dead in the infirmary, and they had done it carefully, and they were coming back.

"You're punctual."

The voice came from the window, and Mara's body did the thing it had trained itself to do since age nine: it went small. Shoulders down. Eyes lowered to a respectful middle distance—not the floor, which looked servile, and not the face, which looked insolent, but that nowhere-place between that said *I am furniture with good posture.*

"I was told six bells, sir."

"Corvus Hale." He crossed the room without hurrying. Hale did not seem like a man who ever hurried; hurry was for people whose plans could fail. He was tall the way a mast is tall—functional, unornamented—and he wore gray so precisely matched to the fog outside the window that when he stood still near the glass he seemed to be wearing the weather. His face was pleasant. That was the first thing, and the worst thing. Mara had expected a scar, a crooked tooth, some flag of cruelty. Instead there was only a face like a well-run office: tidy, attentive, giving away nothing except competence.

"You copied for Thorn." Not a question. He consulted a small black notebook as he said it, as if checking whether *copied for Thorn* were true, whether *Thorn* were true, whether Mara standing before him were true.

"Nights, sir. Four years."

"Four years." He closed the notebook. "And in four years, what did you learn?"

"Ink costs more than paper," Mara said. "Figures lie less than clerks. And a ledger remembers what a man forgets."

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes—approval, or appetite, and Mara could not have sworn which was worse. "Charming. Did Thorn teach you that?"

"He taught me the hand we copy in. The rest I learned from the work."

"Mm." He walked a slow circle around them—not menacingly, not quickly, just thoroughly, the way an appraiser circles a lot. "You'll assist me this morning. We're making an inventory of Mr. Thorn's effects. Company property must be accounted for. Papers, instruments, personal items." He paused. "Especially papers."

"Yes, sir."

"Have you seen any papers of his? Loose ones. Letters. Drafts. Anything not part of the official record."

"No, sir."

"Nothing he gave you? Nothing he asked you to hold?" The smile arrived then, and it stayed too long—the way a hand stays too long on your shoulder, the way a lamp stays lit in a window after everyone inside has gone to bed. It was a smile doing work that smiling should not do. "He was fond of you, I think. He watched you work. He left his desk unlocked at night, which for Thorn was practically an embrace."

"My business was the copying, sir. Not his desk."

"Of course." Hale turned back to the ruined order of the room and began, briskly, to restore it—or to perform restoration. He set Thorn's pen case upright. He squared the drawer contents onto the blotter in rows: nibs, a magnifying lens gone cloudy with age, a tin of throat lozenges, a prayer book with a cracked spine. "You'll write the list. In your own hand. I want to see it."

So Mara wrote, and while they wrote, Hale talked—a low, easy stream of talk about the company, the harbor, the season's pearl yields, the weather coming in off the strait. It was the talk of a man laying carpet over floorboards so he could listen for the hollow ones underneath. Every third sentence contained a hook dressed as small change:

*"Thorn kept late hours. A man alone with ledgers all night—he must have talked to someone."*

*"You were here the night he collapsed. You must have been the last person to speak with him. What did he say?"*

*"Strange thing, a clerk who locks nothing. Either he trusted everyone, or—" a mild chuckle "—he trusted one person entirely."*

To each, Mara gave the same answer wearing different clothes: *He coughed. He fell. The doctor came.* They kept their voice level by keeping their breath shallow, and kept their breath shallow by keeping their mind on the list, the nibs and the lozenges and the lens, item after item, the inventory a rope hand over hand up a cliff face.

It was when they reached the bottom of the second drawer that they saw it.

A rectangle of paler wood. Dust shadow. The ghost of something that had lain under the false bottom of the drawer for years and been lifted out recently—lifted out by whoever had turned the room inside out before dawn. The shape was long and narrow. Letter-shaped.

Mara's pen did not stop. This was the whole art of survival in Saltmere, learned at nine years old beside a drowned father's empty bed: *the surface keeps moving even when the water underneath turns to ice.* Their hand went on writing *1 brass letter-knife, horn handle,* and their voice went on answering Hale's weather-talk, and inside, somewhere below the waterline, a cold current slid through them and kept sliding.

Hale had already searched the desk. Which meant whatever had lived in that dust-shadow was either in his possession—

—or it was the letter, and Thorn had moved it, and the letter was not where Mara had believed it safe, and the floor of the world had shifted again.

Or—third possibility, arriving unwelcome—Hale had found nothing because Thorn had never kept anything there, and the dust-shadow meant nothing, and Mara's imagination was building gallows out of furniture.

"Do you know what a fixer does, Vane?"

Mara looked up. They hadn't heard him come close. He was standing at their shoulder, reading their list upside down with an ease that suggested he'd made a profession of reading things upside down.

"Fixes things, sir."

"What things?"

"Whatever needs fixing."

"And do I strike you as a man who breaks much in the course of fixing?" The smile again. Held. Held. Released. "I ask because people get the idea, sometimes, that my work involves threats. It doesn't. Threats are inefficiencies. My work is arithmetic. A problem enters the column on one side"—he touched the left edge of the desk lightly, with two fingers—"and leaves the column on the other side resolved. Balanced. People who cooperate are very cheap to balance. People who conceal themselves are expensive." He straightened. "The company dislikes expense."

"Yes, sir."

"Elias Thorn became expensive. Do you know why?"

"No, sir." And then, because silence of the wrong length was itself a signature, Mara added: "His lungs, they say. The infirmary—"

"His lungs." Hale considered this as though tasting it. "Yes. Everything is lungs, eventually." He gathered the black notebook from his pocket but did not open it. "You've done good work this morning. Your hand is very fine. Very controlled." He leaned in slightly—not close enough to touch, close enough that Mara could smell bay rum and beneath it something fainter, chemical, like the air in a room where documents have been burned. "Control is a valuable quality in an indenture. It means fewer surprises. I dislike surprises, Vane. Almost as much as I dislike people who mistake me for a man who can be surprised."

He left at half past seven, taking Thorn's prayer book and leaving everything else, pausing once at the door.

"The infirmary will send word about Thorn today or tomorrow," he said to the hallway, or to no one. "When it comes, try not to grieve too loudly. Grief attracts attention."

Then the stairwell swallowed him, and Mara sat alone among the dead man's effects with the pen still in their hand and their pulse counting faster than the harbor bell.

* * *

Corvus Hale descended the stairs at an unhurried pace and permitted himself, in the privacy of the fog-thick lane behind the counting house, a small breath of satisfaction.

The girl—or boy, or whatever Vane was; Hale had stopped needing to know such things years ago—had performed adequately. Better than adequately. The hands had shaken only once, at the mention of the unlocked desk, and the correction had come fast and smooth. Trained. Trained by Thorn, or trained by terror, and Hale had long ago concluded that the two curricula differed mainly in tuition.

He turned the facts over as he walked, the way a jeweler turns a stone against the light.

Elias Thorn, senior clerk, twenty-two years of service, exemplary record, quiet as a held breath—and then, in the space of nine weeks, a changed man. Late hours. Locked drawers that were suddenly unlocked. Requests to the archives for fleet records eleven years old, the year of the storm, the year of the *Sea Lark*'s forty drowned divers. And then blood on his handkerchief, blood in the cuspidor, blood—Hale suspected—in more places than anyone had bothered to look, because a dying man's papers tell you what a living man would never confess.

Hale had found the hiding places. There were always hiding places; Thorn had been thorough, but thoroughness is a habit, and habits leave shadows. Under the false bottom of the desk drawer: ash. Burned paper, recently, carefully—but not completely, because burning is an emotional act and emotion is sloppy. From the ash, with tweed and patience, Hale had recovered fragments. Words. Half-words. *...harbormaster... signal flags... J. Vane...* and a phrase that had made Hale sit very still in the gray dawn light: *...forty souls, and I entered them as weather.*

So Thorn had written it down. Written testimony, drafted, revised—and then burned. Why burn it? Because he'd finished copying it. Because he'd passed it on.

Because he'd given it to the only person in that building he had ever left a desk unlocked for.

Hale reached the quay and stood watching the pearl boats come in, gray hulls in gray water, and allowed himself the pleasure of the hypothesis. It fit. The child had been in the room when Thorn collapsed. The child had been dismissed from the infirmary within minutes of the doctor's arrival—unusual; the doctor liked witnesses to linger. And this morning the child had stood in that wrecked room with a face composed so carefully that the composition itself was a confession. Innocent people are boring. Vane had been interesting, and interest, in Hale's line of work, was nearly proof.

But nearly was not enough. Hale dealt in columns, in entries, in things that balanced. To move against an indentured worker without proof was expense; to move with proof was arithmetic. Somewhere between Thorn's deathbed and this morning's inventory, a piece of paper existed—or had existed, or had been memorized and destroyed, which was the tiresome possibility Hale refused to enjoy considering.

There was also the other thread, thin as spider silk: the deed. He'd pulled the property records himself, three nights ago, after Thorn's first hemorrhage. A coastal parcel north of the bluffs, granted provisionally to one Joren Vane, diver, perished before grant confirmation; lapsed, sold, settled. Clean on paper. But Thorn had spent his last lucid weeks pulling the same records, and men did not pull land records out of sentiment. Unless—Hale smiled at the gulls wheeling over the fish market—they did. Unless the diver's orphan child had a mother's tin hidden somewhere with a seed pod and a scrap of vellum in it, and mistook a dead man's promised orchard for leverage.

People always mistook their treasures for weapons. It was touching, in its way.

Hale bought a cup of coffee from the vendor at the quay head and drank it standing, watching the harbor gate, watching the customs house steps, watching—without appearing to watch—the workers streaming toward the sorting sheds, among them a slight figure in a company coat walking with the deliberate ordinariness of prey that has decided to become scenery.

*Let them run their errands,* Hale thought. *Let them visit their little friends. A fixer does not chase. A fixer narrows the water until everything in it has nowhere else to be.*

He finished the coffee, tipped the vendor generously—for generosity was also a tool, and cheaper than most—and went to make arrangements. There was a roll-call to institute. There were guards to double at the gate. And there was a matter of simple hygiene: the infirmary's report on Elias Thorn was due today, and Hale intended to be present when the body was moved, because dead men's belongings had a way of growing new compartments when no one respectable was looking.

Behind him, the tide began to turn. It always did. It was the one employee the company had never had to fix.

* * *

Mara did not go back to the dormitory. Going back meant being findable, and findable was a word that had changed meaning overnight.

Instead they took the long way to the sorting sheds, through the fish market where the gulls argued over guts and the cobbles ran silver with brine, and they let the noise fill their ears and the stink fill their nose and the crowd press around them, ordinary, unremarkable, one more body in a city of bodies—and still they felt the attention on their back like sun through a lens. Twice they stopped at stalls and pretended to examine dried cod, and both times the lane behind them emptied of anyone specific and filled with no one at all, which was worse.

At the sheds, they worked the sorting trays with the others, fingers moving pearls by feel—luster, weight, the little lies of nacre—and said almost nothing. Old Petrel worked the tray across from them, her knuckles like driftwood, and once she glanced up and held the glance a half-second longer than usual. She didn't ask. Petrel had survived forty years in the company's water; she could read a weather change in a person's face the way she read it in the sky.

"Tide's odd today," she said at last, conversationally, to the tray.

"Is it," said Mara.

"Running harder than the almanac says. Water knows something." She dropped a pearl into the low-grade bin. "Water usually does."

Mara kept sorting. Inside their chest, beneath the ribs, beneath the fear, the letter waited under its floorboard in the shed's back wall—waited, or didn't. They would check at the noon bell. They would check with a hundred workers moving between stations, in the churn and shuffle, where one person kneeling by a loose plank was nobody.

But all morning, one image kept surfacing through the work like a drowned thing rising: a rectangle of clean wood in a dusty drawer, and Corvus Hale's smile holding on, and holding on, and holding on.

The bell rang noon. The lines began to move.

And through the shed's high windows, Mara saw him—small with distance, unmistakable anyway—walking the quay below with two company men behind him, heading not toward the offices, not toward the harbor gate, but toward the infirmary, where Elias Thorn lay breathing through drowned lungs, if he was still breathing at all.

Toward the report. Toward the body. Toward whatever the dead man had left behind that Hale hadn't found yet.

Mara put down the tray and joined the lunch line, and began, quietly, inside the noise, to plan how to reach the infirmary before he finished whatever he had gone there to finish.
