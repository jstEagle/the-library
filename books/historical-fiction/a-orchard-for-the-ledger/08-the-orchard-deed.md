## 8. The Orchard Deed

The letter had lived under the floorboard for three days, and in those three days Mara had learned that a secret has weight—not the weight of paper and wax, which was nothing, but the weight of knowing. It sat against their ribs like a swallowed stone. It made the copying of figures slower. It made the taste of the harbor water different: brackish where it had been merely salt, bitter where it had been merely cold.

They could not keep it there. The floorboard was Thorn's hiding place, and Thorn was gone—carried out of the ledger room on the company doctor's stretcher, his hand still gripping the air as if the letter might return to him by its own tide. Whatever came next, it would begin with a search of that room. Hale would see to it. Hale counted things for a living.

So on the fourth night, after the last lamp in the clerks' quarters had guttered down to a red eye and the dormitory breathed its collective breath of forty sleepers, Mara rose without sound and went barefoot into the dark of Saltmere.

* * *

Their mother's tin was not hidden in any place a searcher would look, because their mother had understood something fundamental about hiding: that the best place is the one that shames the hider. She had sewn it into the lining of the privy wall behind the lime bucket, in the reeking dark where no hand would linger longer than duty required. *No man searches where he holds his nose,* she had said once, laughing her low laugh, the one that seemed to come up from underwater. Mara had been eleven. Their mother had been dead within the year, taken by the same fever that emptied three rows of the divers' dormitory, and the tin had waited in the dark with its secret through six years of Mara's indenture like a held breath.

Getting to it meant the privy shed at the edge of the workers' compound, past the sleeping barracks, past the drying racks where the pearl shells hung like rows of pale moons, past the watchman's brazier burning low. The night was windless. The harbor lay flat and black beyond the palisade, holding the stars so perfectly that Mara felt, walking toward it, like a person approaching the underside of the sky.

Inside the shed, the smell closed over them—lime and ammonia and old wood—and they worked quickly, fingers finding the loose board by memory, prying it with the flat of a shell knife. Behind it, wedged into the gap between studs, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: the tin. A biscuit tin, once, painted with a ship that no longer existed, sailing a sea of faded blue.

Mara carried it out into the open rather than open it there. Some things wanted light. Some things wanted air after nine years in the dark, the way a diver wanted air, the way their father had wanted air at the end of every descent, hauling himself up the anchor rope hand over hand while the harbor pressed its whole grey weight upon him.

They climbed the path behind the shell midden, up to the rocks above the tideline where the watchman's lamp could not reach and the sea spoke its one long sentence below. There, knees drawn up, coat buttoned against the chill, they opened the tin.

* * *

The first thing inside was the smell. Not rot—dryness. Camphor and paper and something faintly sweet, like the ghost of fruit. It went through Mara like a hook through the gills. Because they knew that sweetness. They knew it from before—from the time before the Company, before the indenture papers, before the word *Vane* became an entry in a ledger instead of a name in a mouth. It was the smell of their mother's hands after she had been to the orchard road. It was the smell of the dried apples she kept in a cloth above the stove, hoarded like jewels, doled out one slice each on the darkest nights of winter. It was the smell of a life that had almost happened.

Mara's hands shook. They let them shake. No one watched here but the moon and the water.

Beneath a layer of wool rags: the deed.

It was a single sheet, folded twice, written in a clerk's careful copperplate—the same style of hand Mara copied every night on the third floor, which struck them now as a kind of blasphemy. That hand belonged to this. To contracts and conveyances and the quiet machinery by which land changed custody. It read:

*Know all men by these presents, that the Halloway Trading Company, in consideration of faithful and extraordinary service rendered by Joren Vane, diver, does grant and convey unto the said Joren Vane and his heirs, four acres bounded by the coastal road and the sea, planted to apple and quince, together with the cottage standing thereon...*

Dated eleven years ago. Signed with a flourish that Mara recognized from a hundred other documents—the founder's own hand, Aldous Halloway, whose portrait still hung in the counting room above the mantel, gazing out at the harbor he owned with the mild eyes of a man who had never once gotten wet.

*Extraordinary service.* Mara read the words until they blurred. Four acres. Apple and quince. A cottage. Their father had earned it, then—earned it the way divers earned everything, with lungs and years and the slow spending of his body against the pressure of the deep. And the Company had written it out fair and full, with all its legal furniture of witnesses and seals, and then—

And then nothing. No recording at the customs house. No registration. Just this folded promise in a biscuit tin behind a privy wall, because their mother had known—had somehow known—that a promise from Halloway was a coin that only spent in one direction. Their father had died believing the orchard was waiting for him. Had spoken of it on his last morning home: the trees coming into blossom, the boy or girl—he'd never learned which Mara would be, hadn't lived to learn—running between the rows with the fallen petals sticking to their feet. He had died forty fathoms down in the grey water off the point, and the orchard had never existed for him except as ink.

Except as ink.

Mara sat very still while the thought arrived, the way a big fish arrives—suddenly, all at once, taking the whole line.

Ink. Copperplate. A signature. A document promising one thing while the ledger recorded another. They had spent five nights now copying the third floor's true accounts, and they knew what lived in ledgers: whatever the Company wished to have lived there. Somewhere in those bound volumes was the second half of this deed's story—the entry where four acres of coastal land quietly became Company land again, or never left it at all, or was granted to someone whose service had been less extraordinary and more expensive. Papers lied to each other all the time. Mara had simply never before held two of the liars in the same pair of hands.

Their father had been paid in paper that was never meant to be honored. And now his child held both the paper and, folded away in memory, the pages that showed how the Company honored its debts.

The wind moved along the cliff grass with a dry whisper. Below, the harbor breathed against the stones. *The tide, the tide,* Thorn had said, and Mara had thought he meant only the ship, the sailing, the magistrate across the strait. But sitting there with the deed open on their knees, they understood that Thorn had meant something larger: that everything in Saltmere moved on tides—money, guilt, truth—all of it going out and coming back, and if you stood in the right place at the right hour, the water gave up what it had taken.

* * *

Under the deed, in the bottom of the tin, lay the last thing: a seed pod.

It had gone papery with age, this husk from a quince or an apple—Mara couldn't remember which tree their mother had said, only that she had cut it from a branch on the day she hid the tin, saying, *So you'll know what they took isn't only paper.* Inside the pod, Mara knew without opening it, were seeds. Dark, hard little seeds, patient as anything that has ever slept in the ground. Trees that no longer grew anywhere on that coast. The Company had razed the orchard rows within a year of their father's death—or so the divers whispered; no one had been allowed past the boundary posts to see—and put up instead a curing shed and a fence with signs that no diver could read anyway.

Mara turned the pod over in their palm. It weighed almost nothing. A bird could carry it. A child could crush it. And yet it was heavier than the letter upstairs, heavier than the deed, heavier maybe than the whole tin, because it asked a question the other documents didn't ask.

The deed asked: *what is owed?*

The letter asked: *what was done?*

The seed asked: *what will you grow?*

They thought of their father's hands. Everyone remembered a drowned man's face, if they were lucky enough to have seen it at all, but Mara remembered the hands—cracked and bleached white at the fingertips from years of salt, scarred at the knuckles where the oyster shells had bitten. Hands that had held a seed once too, surely. Hands that had planned to press something living into soil and stand back and watch it rise. That had been the whole dream, Mara realized. Not wealth. Not even freedom, exactly, though freedom was the door it came through. The dream was to put a thing in the ground and have it stay. To be somewhere the water couldn't reach and the Company couldn't count.

And the Company had answered that dream with a crossed-out entry and an unrecorded deed and forty dead divers, and had called it weather.

Mara closed their fist around the pod until the dry edges bit.

Here was the thing they had understood since childhood, the philosophy that had kept them alive: history happens elsewhere, to people with surnames that matter. Vane did not matter. Vane was a line item, a debt marked paid by a death. But a letter signed by a dying clerk, a deed signed by a founder, a testimony naming names—these were the tools the elsewhere-people used, and they could be stolen, they could be forged, they could be carried. Their whole life Mara had believed themselves unarmed. Now they looked down at their lap and saw a small armory: proof of murder, proof of theft, and a seed.

Proof of murder bought justice. Proof of theft bought leverage. But the seed—

The seed bought nothing. That was its power. It was the one thing in the tin the Company could not cross out, because it had never been entered anywhere. It was outside the ledger entirely. It was the future, and the future was the one country where Halloway held no charter.

Mara laughed—a single short sound that startled a gull off the rocks below—and the laugh hurt their throat, because underneath it was grief, all of it at once, the nine stored years of it. Their father murdered for pearls and called a storm. Their mother hiding promises in filth because she trusted no clean place. An orchard sold out from under a dead man's name. Inheritance, they thought, wiping their eyes with the heel of one hand. This is inheritance. Not land. Not coin. A wound shaped like a weapon. You carry it or you bleed out.

* * *

They repacked the tin with the care of a person handling relics—wool over deed, deed over pod—and then stopped, hands hovering.

The tin could not go back behind the privy board. If Hale searched—and Hale would search, starting tomorrow, starting perhaps tonight—it must be somewhere no inventory would ever think to look. Mara considered the shell midden, the cistern, the rafters of the net loft. Then they thought of the one place they had already proven no one watched: the third floor. The ledger room itself. The heart of the counting house, where every object had a recorded purpose and therefore no object was ever truly examined.

Tomorrow night, during copying hours, the tin would come back up to the third floor and go into the space beneath the loose floorboard—Thorn's space, now theirs. Let Hale turn that room over. Let him find the letter gone and wonder. A missing thing tells more than a hidden one; Thorn had taught them that much with his unlocked drawers and his watching eyes. The empty hiding place would be a message only Mara could afford to send: *I know. I have it. Come find me—if you can.*

It was either courage or madness. Mara suspected, folding the plan away like the deed itself, that the two were related the way the tide and the drowning were related.

They slid the tin into the breast of their coat, where it rode against their chest like a second heartbeat, and started down through the dark toward the compound, toward the sleeping dormitory, toward the long gray hours before the whistle. The harbor lay calm as poured glass. Somewhere out past the point, past the strait, a magistrate sat in a lamplit room, not yet knowing what was coming to him across the water, sealed in wax and carried by a diver's child who had just discovered they were also a founder's creditor and a murdered man's heir.

Three checkpoints between here and that lamplit room. One fish cart, one stamp, one gangway.

Mara touched the tin through the cloth, then the seed pod in its inner pocket, then—briefly, like a vow—the spot below their ribs where the letter's absence ached.

*The tide, the tide,* they thought, and went down the hill to make ready.
