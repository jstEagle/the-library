## 9. Sable

The letter had lived under the floorboard for two nights, and Mara had not slept for either of them.

They lay in the dormitory with forty other indentured bodies breathing in the dark, listening to the building settle around them like a ship at anchor, and every creak was a boot, every settling beam a hand lifting a board. The letter was not there anymore—Mara had moved it on the second night, sewn it into the lining of their coat where the padding was thickest, against the small of the back, where a body carrying weight learns to carry it without complaint. But the floorboard still felt loud. Absence, Mara was learning, could be louder than presence. The empty space where a thing had been seemed to call out its own name.

*The tide, the tide.* Thorn's fingers had been cold as harbor water when they pressed the seal into Mara's palm. His lips had been gray. He had looked at Mara—not through them, not past them, the way everyone in the Halloway Company looked through and past the indentured—but *at* them, as if he were setting down a heavy load and needed to see who caught it.

And now Mara carried it, and the weight of it changed how they walked. They had spent ten years learning to be unremarkable, to move through Saltmere like smoke, like weather, like something no one thought to name. Now every step felt like an announcement. When Hale's clerks passed them in the yard, Mara kept their eyes down and counted their own breaths and thought: *he knows someone knows. He is counting heads already, even if he hasn't started yet.*

There was one person in Saltmere who might help. One person only.

* * *

Sable kept a room above the rope-walk, at the end where the tar vats steamed all day and the smell soaked into everything until even the gulls smelled of pitch. It was not a room anyone rented; it was a room Sable had simply been in long enough that no one remembered a time before. To reach it you climbed an outside stair whose boards had been worn to a soft gloss by feet that preferred not to be seen, and you knocked twice, paused, and knocked once more—a rhythm Sable had invented years ago, drunk, laughing, calling it *the tide knocking at the door*, and neither of them had ever used it since without feeling, briefly, like children again.

Mara climbed the stair at dusk, when the lamps along the quay were being lit one by one and the light came up out of the water gold and trembling. Their coat hung heavy on their shoulders. All the way across the dockyard they had been aware of it—the letter riding against their spine like a second heart, beating when they beat, warm when they were warm. Papers could not be warm. Mara knew this. And yet.

They knocked. Twice. Pause. Once.

"Door's not locked," said Sable's voice, "because locks are for people with things worth locking."

Inside, the room was exactly as it always was: a hammock slung in one corner, a table made from a hatch cover, a single candle burning down in a dish of salvaged brass, and everywhere—everywhere—objects of no certain origin. A ship's bell with the maker's name chiseled off. A crate of oranges that had definitely not been bought. A coil of silk line finer than anything the Halloway warehouses stocked. Sable's whole life was a collection of things that had fallen off the backs of ships, and Sable had never once pretended otherwise.

Sable themselves sat cross-legged on the table, mending a net with quick dark fingers. They were small and wiry, with hair cropped close against lice and salt, and a face that was hard to hold onto—features that seemed arranged differently depending on the light, so that witnesses could never quite agree on what they'd seen. This was not accident. Sable had cultivated forgettability the way Mara had, but where Mara's invisibility was a kind of erasure, Sable's was a performance. Mara disappeared because no one looked. Sable disappeared because everyone looked and saw nothing worth keeping.

They looked up now, and their eyes—quick, black-bright, always slightly amused—went over Mara's face the way they went over cargo: assessing weight, assessing damage.

"You look," Sable said, "like a person carrying something."

"I look like I always look."

"No. You usually look like nothing at all. Tonight you look like a nothing with a stone in its shoe." Sable set down the netting needle. "Sit. There's rum."

"There's always rum."

"There's always stolen rum. It tastes better. Guilt is a seasoning." Sable poured two measures from a bottle without a label into tin cups and pushed one across the hatch-cover table. "Drink first. Talk after. That's the rule."

Mara sat. The cup was cold in their hand. Through the wall came the smell of the tar vats and the far-off sound of the harbor—water slapping pilings, a winch groaning somewhere, the endless small industry of a port that never fully slept. They drank. The rum burned going down and then bloomed warm behind the breastbone, and for a moment the letter against their spine felt less like a coal.

"Sable." Their voice came out lower than they intended. "Do you remember the night we swore?"

Sable's eyebrows went up. In eleven years of friendship, Mara had never once referred to the oath aloud. It existed between them the way the drowned exist beneath the harbor's skin—acknowledged by silence, never named.

"The night of the *Kestrel's Pride*," Sable said slowly, watching them. "Stolen rum. You were fourteen. You cried, but you'd deny it."

"I didn't cry."

"You wept like a gutted fish. Very dignified about it, though." Sable's grin faded as quickly as it had come. They set down their cup. "I remember. We swore on the rum and on the water. Loyalty till the tide stops coming. Which, if you think about it, is a stupid oath, because the tide always comes. It's the one thing in this world that keeps its word."

"That's why I'm here."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, a dog began barking at the lamplighter and was told to stop by a voice too tired to mean it.

Mara reached back and, with fingers that shook only a little—they were proud of that, only a little—worked loose the stitches they had put into the coat lining three days ago. The packet came free wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed thread. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. They set it on the hatch cover between them, next to the rum bottle and the half-mended net, and watched Sable look at it.

Sable did not touch it. That was the first thing. Most people would have snatched at it. Sable looked at it the way a diver looks at something glinting on the bottom—measuring depth, current, the risk of the dive—and then looked up at Mara with all the amusement gone out of their face, which made it suddenly very old.

"What is it?" Sable asked.

"A letter. Sealed. From Elias Thorn—you've seen him, the senior clerk, coughs blood into his handkerchief on the third-floor landing."

"Everyone's seen him. He's a ghost that still does sums." Sable's eyes dropped to the packet again. "What's in it?"

Mara hesitated. Here was the edge of the plank. Everything after this step was falling.

"It's testimony," they said. "It says the Halloway founders paid the harbormaster to sink the rival fleet. Nine years ago. The storm that wasn't a storm. The forty divers."

The words went into the room and did not come back. Sable sat very still. Outside, the tide was turning; Mara could hear it in the changed note of the water against the pilings, the harbor exhaling and drawing breath.

"My father was one of them," Mara said. "Joren Vane. It's in the letter. Named. Forty names, and his is one of them, written in a clerk's careful hand like he was a shipment that got mislaid." Their voice held. They were surprised by that. Ten years of holding had built a muscle for it. "Thorn gave it to me two days ago. He collapsed right after. They took him to the infirmary. I don't know if he's alive, and I don't know if it matters, because whoever reads that letter will know someone carried it out of the ledger room, and there was only one invisible person in that room at midnight, and Corvus Hale smiles at me now. Did you know that? He smiles at me. Like a man smiling at a rat he hasn't decided whether to drown."

Sable was quiet a long moment. Then they did something Mara had never seen them do: they picked up the packet, turned it over once, and set it down again gently, as if it might bruise.

"You want to get it out of Saltmere," Sable said. Not a question.

"There's a magistrate coming across the strait. On the *Morning Tide*. She sails on the morning tide—day after tomorrow, if the weather holds. Thorn knew. That's what he meant when—" Mara stopped. Swallowed. "*The tide, the tide.* He meant the ship. He meant get it to her."

"And the checkpoints?"

"Three. Harbor gate, customs house, gangway. Every cart searched at the gate since the fleet scandal rumors started moving. Every manifest stamped at customs. Every worker patted down at the gangway." Mara laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked. "Ten years learning to be nobody, and now I need to be nobody harder than I've ever been anybody in my life."

Sable stood up. They paced the length of the room—four steps, turn, four steps back—their bare feet silent on the boards, the candle throwing their shadow huge and wavering against the tar-stained wall. Mara watched them and understood, with a sinking clarity, that they had just done something irreversible. You could not hand a secret to another person and take it back. It was like opening a cage; the bird might return, but not as the same bird.

Then Sable stopped pacing and turned, and the grin was back—but it was the other grin, the rare one, the one that had no humor in it at all, only appetite.

"The harbor gate," Sable said. "That one's mine."

"Sable—"

"Hear me out. The gate guards search carts, yes. They poke sticks into fish barrels and feel smug about it. But they don't search *my* cart, because my cart comes through twice a week and every time it smells like three-day-old herring, and no man alive volunteers to put his hands deeper into that than his stick has to go." Sable tapped the table. "You sew your letter into the false bottom under the fish trays—I have a false bottom, obviously, I have several—and I roll through the gate at dawn with the morning catch, and I come out the other side smelling terrible and entirely innocent. The letter crosses. Then you take it from me on the quay side and carry it the rest of the way yourself."

"Why not carry it all the way? Why not take it to the ship?"

"Because the customs house and the gangway are yours, not mine. My face is known at the gate—known and waved through, which is different from trusted. If I'm seen near the *Morning Tide*'s berth asking after passengers, questions start. Questions are the one cargo I can't smuggle." Sable leaned across the table, and their voice dropped to almost nothing, though there was no one to hear. "We split it. Gate is mine. Customs and gangway are yours. Neither of us carries the whole road. That way if one of us breaks, the other can still finish."

Mara stared at them. "If one of us breaks."

"If one of us is caught," Sable corrected smoothly, but their eyes flickered, just once, toward the rum bottle, and Mara chose not to see it. "Which won't happen. Because we're both nobodies, and nobodies are the only people in Saltmere who ever get away with anything."

For a moment—just a moment—Mara let themselves feel it. Relief, real relief, the kind they hadn't allowed since Thorn's cold fingers pressed the seal into their palm. It moved through them like warmth through frozen hands, painful and sweet. Someone else knew. Someone else was carrying part of it. The letter was no longer a coal burning alone against their spine; it was a burden shared, and shared burdens weigh half.

This was the dangerous comfort of trust, though Mara did not yet have the words for it. They only felt it: the loosening, the gratitude, the terrible sweetness of not being alone with a thing that could kill them. Later—much later—they would remember this exact moment, this candle and this rum and Sable's bright certain face, and understand that this was the moment they handed over not just a secret but a weapon, and that weapons do not care whose hands they warm.

"Swear it again," Mara said quietly. "The old way."

Sable picked up the rum bottle and refilled both cups. They held theirs out over the packet, over the sealed testimony of forty drowned men and women, and Mara brought theirs to meet it, and the tin rims touched with a small clear sound like a coin dropping into deep water.

"Till the tide stops coming," Sable said.

"Till the tide stops coming."

They drank. Sable took the letter and, with a fish-gutting knife worn thin as a leaf, slit the stitching of the cart bag's false bottom and sewed the packet in with quick sure strokes, humming something tuneless, and Mara watched their father's murder disappear into a bed of herring barrels and felt—for the first time in three days—something like hope.

It was nearly full dark when Mara climbed back down the outside stair. Behind them, above, the candle in the brass dish still burned, and Sable stood at the window watching the harbor lights, and if their expression had changed—if the brightness had drained out of it, leaving something calculating and tired and afraid—there was no one left on the stair to see it.

At the bottom, Mara paused and looked back once. The little window glowed. Their friend was up there, guarding the most dangerous thing in Saltmere, sworn on rum and seawater.

Till the tide stops coming.

Two mornings now until the *Morning Tide* sailed. And somewhere across the dark harbor, in a house with more lamps than the rest of the street combined, Corvus Hale was surely awake too, doing his own arithmetic—counting heads, counting papers, counting the hours until whatever Thorn had hidden surfaced like a body that would not stay down.

Mara pulled their collar up against the sea wind and walked back toward the dormitory, unremarkable, invisible, a nothing with a stone in its shoe.

The tide, they told themselves. Two days. Hold fast until the tide.

They did not yet know that the tide had already begun to turn against them—or that the first crack would come not from Hale's side of the water at all, but from the candlelit room above the rope-walk, where a friend sat counting, in the dark, the price of everything.
