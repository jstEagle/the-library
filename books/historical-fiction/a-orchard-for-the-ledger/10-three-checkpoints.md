## 10. Three Checkpoints

Mara drew the route in salt on the floor of the drying shed, where the boards were old and pale and a fingertip of brine left no trace once it dried. They had learned that trick at nine years old, drawing letters they were not permitted to know, watching them vanish as though the shed itself swallowed evidence. Now they drew a city.

The harbor gate first: a low arch of blackened stone at the neck of the quay, wide enough for two fish carts abreast, narrow enough that every face passing through it passed alone. A lantern hung there even in daylight, burning sullenly behind smoked glass, because Halloway believed light was something you rationed. Two guards by day, four by night, and a third man who stood apart with his hands folded, doing nothing visible except seeing everything. Mara had walked through that gate six thousand times carrying nothing but their own small name, and had never once been stopped. That was the trouble with being invisible: it worked until it didn't.

The customs house second: a long brick building squatting between the chandlery and the rope-walk, its windows fogged with salt so that the clerks inside moved like figures drowned in green water. Everything leaving Saltmere by sea required a manifest stamped there — cargo, crew, passengers, post. The Morning Tide would need her papers in order before a single crate crossed her rail, and every paper would pass under eyes trained to catch what did not belong. Mara knew those eyes. They had sat beneath them for ten years, copying columns until the numbers swam like minnows. They knew which clerks read what they copied and which merely watched their pens move.

And third, the gangway itself — the narrow plank bridge between the quay and the Tide's deck, where a guard would stand on sailing day with a list in one hand and the other hand free to turn out pockets, shake out hems, run fingers along seams. Thirty steps of bare wood, in full view of anyone who cared to look, with the harbor glittering below like a coin too bright to trust.

Mara looked down at the map drawn in salt. Three gates between the letter and the magistrate. Three chances to be seen.

They pressed their palm flat over the whole of it and let the salt stick to their skin, then carried it to the door and shook it out into the dark. The map was gone. The map was also, now, entirely in their head, laid out in lines as clean as ledger entries. Thorn had taught them that much without meaning to: anything you can hold in your memory, no search can find.

* * *

They walked the route that night, off-shift, in the gray hour when the lamps burned low and the tide breathed out of the harbor. It was not unusual for an indentured diver to walk the quays after hours; the company preferred its workers tired and the workers preferred anywhere but the dormitories, and so the arrangement held like a rusted hinge. Mara kept their shoulders rounded, their stride dull, their face set to the expression they wore like a coat — the look of someone thinking about nothing, going nowhere, worth no one's attention.

The harbor gate first. They drifted past it slow, the way a loiterer drifts, and counted without appearing to count. The day guards changed at the sixth bell; there was a gap of perhaps four minutes when only one man stood watch, and he spent most of it warming his hands at the brazier, his back half-turned to the cart lane. Carts went through with a wave of the lantern. Fish carts especially. No one wanted to inspect a fish cart closely, and the men who worked them had a way of standing beside their carts like the carts were extensions of their own bodies — to search one was to insult both.

Sable could get a cart through that gate with a sermon about herring prices and the guard would thank him for the education.

But Sable could not carry it alone. If the letter were found on him, it would end there, in his pockets, in his mouth, in whatever room Hale took people to when they stopped being useful. The letter needed to travel in pieces, or in shadows, or — Mara turned this over as they walked — not travel at all, in the way that mattered. What if the thing that crossed the checkpoints was worthless, and the true testimony never left Mara's body until the moment it had to?

They filed the thought away like a page marked with a thumb.

The customs house next. Its doors closed at dusk, but the loading window on the harbor side stayed open late for ships' chandlers bringing manifests to stamp, and Mara lingered near it, pretending to study the mooring lines of a ketch. Inside, lamplight moved across a counter. A clerk — Voss, the heavy one, the one who signed his initials with a flourish like a man signing his own praises — was working through a stack of outgoing papers, licking his thumb, stamping, initialling, passing each sheet to a runner who carried it down to the waiting vessels. He checked almost nothing. He matched the ship's name to the manifest's heading and the stamp came down like a gavel on a trial nobody attended.

Almost nothing. But the almost was where people died. Somewhere in that building was the master register of what could leave Saltmere and in whose name, and somewhere in that register was a column for parcels and documents, and somewhere in that column a clerk might, on a bad night, decide to look twice at a diver holding paper.

Mara would need a reason to be at that window. A reason with a stamp already on it, ideally. Their mind went, unbidden, to the ledger room, to the neat forgery of routine, to Thorn's voice in memory: *A lie holds best when it's built out of true things, Vane. Build your false entry from real columns and no auditor alive will feel the join.* They had thought he meant ink. Standing at the fogged window, watching Voss stamp his way through the evening, Mara understood he had meant everything.

* * *

The Morning Tide lay at her berth on the far quay, riding low with cargo already stacked in her hold, her masts bare against the sky like needles waiting for thread. Mara walked the length of the quay beneath her hull, close to the waterline where the tar-stink was thick enough to chew, and looked up at the gangway.

It was exactly as bad as they'd remembered. Thirty planks, no railing on the seaward side, a clear sightline from the harbormaster's office, from the gate, from the customs house windows if any clerk cared to lift his head. On sailing morning there would be a queue: stevedores with crates on their shoulders, cooks with baskets, the bosun shouting tallies. Every worker boarding to load would be checked against the duty roster — name, shift, purpose. Mara's name would be on that roster, because Mara would make certain of it; a diver summoned to sort pearl cargo was ordinary as rain. Ordinary got you onto the plank. It did not get you across it with a sealed letter sewn anywhere a hand might press.

So: not sewn. Not carried, even. Carried things could be taken.

They stood a long time under the Tide's shadow, watching the water slap the pilings, and let the plan assemble itself the way a tide assembles — not all at once but in pulls, each piece arriving and refusing to leave. The letter would go inside something that belonged on a pearl ship, something a guard's hand would touch and dismiss. The hollow handle of a sorting knife came to mind and stayed there, fitting itself into the shape of the problem like a key. Divers' knives went aboard all the time, bundled in canvas with the sorting gear. A knife handle was wood, wrapped in cord, solid to any squeeze. Solid, if you made it solid around the emptiness.

Three checkpoints. Gate, customs house, gangway. And threading through all three, the same truth: the letter must cross without seeming to cross at all, and the person who carried it must remain, until the very last step, a person no one had ever bothered to learn the name of.

Invisibility had been their cage for nineteen years. Now it would be their disguise. There was a bitter symmetry in that which Mara chose not to taste.

* * *

The magistrate was the part that made it more than a runaway's fantasy. The Morning Tide sailed on the morning tide Thursday, and she would not sail empty of consequence: word had come down the dockside gossip like oil down water that a circuit magistrate, one Aldous Renwick, had taken passage on her, bound for the assizes across the strait. Magistrates did not ride merchant packets often. When they did, they brought with them a strange, portable island of law — authority that existed on the deck itself, beyond the reach of Halloway's constables and Halloway's fixers and Halloway's careful, drowning arithmetic.

Everything depended on reaching that floating island before the ship cast off. Once the Tide cleared the harbor mouth, she was Renwick's ground, and Hale's writ ended at the waterline.

Mara recited it to themselves on the walk home, silently, the way they'd once memorized tide tables: *Gate at the sixth bell gap, in a fish cart, by Sable's hands or no hands at all. Customs house by stamped paper, forged clean, built from true columns. Gangway in the knife handle, in plain sight, among forty other knives.* And underneath it all, the deeper current: *and if every vessel fails, the words are in me, and I am boarding that ship.*

A plan is a kind of hope, Petrel had said once, splicing line in the shed, not looking up. It's just hope with its boots laced.

Mara had almost smiled at that. Almost.

Because there was a fourth checkpoint, unwritten, unmapped, drawn in no salt on any floor: Corvus Hale. Hale did not stand at gates. Hale was the reason gates existed. Somewhere in the city tonight he was asking his quiet questions in his quiet rooms, and every answer he gathered narrowed the water around Mara by a fathom. You could map a harbor, but you could not map a man who hunted by patience.

They reached the dormitory door and paused, hand on the cold iron latch, and looked back once at the harbor — the black water, the riding lights of the Tide, the smoky lantern burning its rationed light above the gate. Three days until the morning tide. Three gates. One letter that weighed nothing in the hand and everything in the world.

Inside, the dormitory smelled of wet wool and tallow, and Mara lay down in their narrow bunk with their boots on, as they had begun to do, and stared at the beams overhead, running the route again, and again, until it wore smooth as a stone in the current.

They were still awake, deep in the dead hours, when boots sounded in the corridor — too many boots, moving with purpose — and a knock fell on the wardens' door at the end of the hall, and a voice they knew too well said, quietly, that Elias Thorn had died in the infirmary before dawn, and that Mr. Hale would be along directly to see to the man's effects.
