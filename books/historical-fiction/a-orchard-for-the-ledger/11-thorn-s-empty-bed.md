## 11. Thorn's Empty Bed

The boots went away again, and the corridor settled back into its dark like a wound closing over a splinter.

Mara lay in the bunk and listened to the building breathe around them — the tick of the water pipes, the groan of the old timbers taking the tide, somewhere below a door swinging on its hinge with the slow patience of things that have outlived their owners. Elias Thorn had died before dawn. Died in the infirmary, which was four floors up and two wings over, in a room that smelled of carbolic and camphor, in a bed with iron rails and a blanket washed thin as paper. Died alone, or nearly so, unless the night nurse had stayed to hold his hand, and Mara doubted it. The company did not pay nurses to hold hands.

*The tide, the tide.* That was what he had said. Not *forgive me*, not *run*. The tide. As if even at the end, with blood coming up his throat in rust-bright bubbles, he had been thinking not of himself but of the schedule of a ship he would never board.

Mara turned onto their side, slowly, the way one turns in deep water, and pressed the letter's memory against the inside of their ribs. They had not opened it since that first night by the candlestub; they had not needed to. Every line lived behind their eyes now. *On the night of the seventeenth of October, in the year of our Lord 1888, I did carry to the harbormaster's office a sealed instruction from Messrs. Halloway and Sons...* Forty names followed, set down in Thorn's small, careful hand, each one a diver who had gone into the water on a morning when the sky was clean and come no more out of it. Joren Vane stood eleventh on the list. Eleventh. Mara had counted, and recounted, and hated themselves for counting, because what difference could position make to a drowned man except that it meant his daughter knew exactly where he fell in the order of the murdered.

And now Thorn was dead too, and Mr. Hale was coming to see to his effects.

Mara had heard that phrase before, *see to a man's effects*, spoken in the flat administrative voice the company used for everything it wanted to make sound like weather rather than will. A man died; his effects were seen to. A fleet sank; its debts were written down. A child was indentured; her name was entered in a ledger in a fair round hand and there she stayed, year after year, ink drying into scar tissue. The company was very good at seeing to things. It had seen to forty divers and called it a storm.

They got up without light. Ten years in this dormitory had taught them every board that spoke and every one that kept silent, and they crossed to the narrow window between the third and fourth bunks and put their eye to the frost-furred glass.

Below, in the yard between the infirmary wing and the counting house, a lamp moved. Then another. Two men walking with the unhurried gait of men whose errand would still be waiting whenever they arrived. And behind them, under the eave where the lamplight pooled and stopped, a third figure who did not walk so much as arrive wherever he chose to be standing: broad-shouldered, hatless despite the cold, his pale hair silvered by the lamp above him so that he seemed lit from within, like something preserved in glass.

Corvus Hale, come to see to the effects of Elias Thorn.

Mara watched until the figures passed beneath the window and out of sight, into the doorway of the infirmary wing, and the dark took them back one by one, Hale last, as though the building swallowed him reluctantly and only after he permitted it. Then they stood a long while with their forehead against the cold pane, and felt the dread in them change its shape.

It had been terror before, these last days — the animal kind, the kind that makes the hands stupid and the bowels loose and the mind run in circles like a crab in a bucket. Terror said: *hide, freeze, be small, be nothing.* But watching Hale cross that yard, Mara understood with a clarity that felt almost like calm that hiding was finished. Thorn was dead. Thorn had been the other witness, the older hand, the man whose testimony carried weight because he had worn a company collar for thirty years. Whatever else remained now wore an indenture brand instead, and if Hale found it — when he found it — there would be no infirmary bed, no doctor sent for, no decent interval of dying. There would be a question asked quietly in a room with a locked door, and after the question, water.

So the fear did not leave. It hardened. It went from water to ice, and ice, Mara thought, looking down at their own hands ghost-pale against the black glass, ice can be walked upon.

The letter had to sail. Nothing else mattered. Not sleep, not caution, not the thousand small prudences that had kept them alive for ten years. If the Morning Tide left on tomorrow's tide without that letter in her hold, then Elias Thorn would have coughed his life out onto a desk for nothing, and forty names would stay buried in a ledger nobody read, and Corvus Hale would go on seeing to effects until the end of his natural days, fat and smiling and unpunished, and Mara Vane would spend whatever years remained being unremarkable in exactly the way the company preferred.

They went back to their bunk. They did not sleep. They lay with their boots on and rehearsed the route until dawn came gray through the window, and when the bell rang for the first shift they rose with the others and went down to the harbor to dive for loose pearls with cold eating up their arms, and no one looking at them saw anything but a lean indentured worker doing what indentured workers did, which was precisely what Mara intended them to see.

* * *

Word traveled through Saltmere the way water travels through a hull: silently at first, and then all at once, everywhere.

By midmorning everyone at the sorting sheds knew that Senior Clerk Thorn was dead. By noon they knew he had died of the lung-rot, poor devil, that he'd been coughing blood since autumn, that the company doctor had given him till Christmas and been proved right to the day. The story sat comfortably in every mouth. Men died of the lung-rot all the time in Saltmere; the town produced it along with pearls and salt fish. Nobody asked why a senior clerk had been working nights lately, or why he'd taken to leaving his desk unlocked, or why, in his last week, his eyes had gone hunting through the ledger room for a face he could trust with something heavier than ink.

Mara shucked oysters and sorted shell and let the talk wash past them, nodding here, murmuring there, contributing nothing. Old Petrel worked the next bench over, her knuckles swollen big as walnuts from forty years of cold water, her mouth going the whole time the way it always went, and it was Petrel who brought the news that mattered.

"They've shut his office already," she said, pitching her voice under the clatter of shells. "Sealed it with wax and a string, like it was contraband. And Hale himself is up there now — my nephew sees him carrying boxes down to the counting house, his own self, not trusting a soul below clerk grade to touch them. First time I ever heard of a fixer doing porter's work." She snorted. "Man must think the dead man's papers are made of pearl."

Mara kept their eyes on the shell in their hands. "Maybe they're worth a lot."

"Papers are worth what they prove," Petrel said, which was an odd thing for her to say, and she said it looking straight ahead at the sorting table, and then she reached over and squeezed Mara's wrist once, hard, with those walnut knuckles, and said nothing more about it.

But Mara had seen the look that came with the squeeze, brief as a fish turning under a boat, and it was not the blank look Petrel gave to supervisors. It was the look of a woman measuring a child against a danger and finding the child grown enough to stand in it. Mara's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling in surf. How much did Petrel know? How much did anyone know? Trust, they were learning, was not a wall you built once; it was a leak you could never entirely stop, and every person who guessed at the truth of the letter was another seam in the hull.

Still. When the noon whistle blew and the benches emptied, Mara did not go toward the mess hall. They went the long way, along the quay, past the net-menders and the tar vats, and stood where they could see the third-floor windows of the counting house, and waited.

* * *

They did not have long to wait.

Hale appeared at the window nearest the corner — Thorn's own window, Mara realized, the one that looked down on the yard where the ledgers were carted in from the warehouse. He stood there with his back half-turned, and though the distance was too great to read his face, Mara knew his posture the way sailors know weather. He was reading the room. He was standing where Thorn had stood, looking at what Thorn had looked at, learning the dead man's habits by wearing them.

Then, unhurried, he raised one hand and drew the curtain closed.

It should have meant nothing. Curtains were drawn in that building a hundred times a day. But the gesture had a quality Mara recognized from the fish market, from the moment the knife goes in and the stallholder draws the cloth over the tank so the other lobsters won't see. It was not modesty. It was housekeeping.

Mara walked back to the sheds with their heart going hard and steady, like a hammer on a keel, and found that the fear had finished its work. It had become arithmetic now. *One:* Thorn was dead and his rooms were sealed. *Two:* Hale was inventorying the effects personally, which meant he believed something among them was worth a fixer's personal attention, which meant Thorn's precautions had not gone unnoticed. *Three:* the roll-call was coming — it had to be coming, because a man like Hale, having failed to find what he wanted in a dead man's boxes, would turn next to the living, and the living could be counted. And *four*: the Morning Tide sailed tomorrow on the morning tide, with a magistrate aboard bound across the strait, and between this hour and that hour stood three checkpoints and one chance.

Sable. Tonight. Everything depended on Sable and the fish cart and the harbor gate at first light.

Mara spent the afternoon diving, because diving was expected of them and because the cold water was the only place their thoughts would sit still. Down there, three fathoms over the harbor muck, with the world reduced to green murmur and the pulse in their ears, they could almost believe they were diving for pearls and nothing else. Almost. But the bottom of Saltmere harbor held more than pearls. It held ballast stones off ships a hundred years dead, and boot-soles, and bottle glass, and — Mara had always privately believed — the silted-over memory of everything the town preferred to forget. Their father had worked these waters. Their father had died in open sea beyond the point, in a storm that had been purchased like any other cargo, and his bones lay wherever the current had chosen to file them, uncatalogued, unseen-to.

*You're next,* Mara thought, hanging in the cold green, breath burning. *Unless the letter sails.*

When they surfaced, the light had gone the color of pewter and Petrel was waiting on the ladder platform, holding out a twist of oilcloth.

"Bread and salt herring," she said gruffly. "You look like something the tide rejected. Eat."

Mara ate. The herring was good. Around them the harbor lamps came on one by one, halos of yellow in the gathering fog, and beyond the breakwater the Morning Tide rode at anchor with her riding lights doubled, a ship full of cargo and one magistrate and, if the next twenty hours went right, the whole drowned truth of the Halloway Company folded small in a packet of oilskin.

"You'll be careful," Petrel said. It was not a question, and she did not explain it, and Mara did not ask her to. Some understandings were safer unwrapped, like packages marked fragile that both parties pretended contained nothing at all.

"I'm always careful," Mara said.

Petrel looked at them for a long moment, firelight from the brazier moving on her seamed face. "Aye," she said at last. "That's what your father said. The morning he went out." She turned away to her nets, and left Mara standing with the words settling into them like cold water into a boot.

* * *

Night. The dormitory again, the wet wool and tallow smell, the beams overhead. Mara lay dressed and did not close their eyes.

They had sewn the seed pod into the lining of their coat that evening, in the privy, by feel, with thread pulled from a hem — the pod and the deed together, flat against their left side, over the ribs, where a knife might go and where a searchers' hands, patting briskly for coin or contraband, had never yet lingered. The letter itself they had wrapped in oilskin and tucked into the hollow heel cavity of their left boot, wedged with wool. It was the obvious place and therefore perhaps the safe one; Mara had learned from Thorn that people searched where searching was comfortable, and a boot heel was uncomfortable, requiring unlacing and patience, and patience was not a quality the night wardens possessed in abundance.

Tomorrow, Sable would take a copy — no. No copy. There was no copy; there had never been time, and copies multiplied danger. Tomorrow Sable would take the letter itself through the harbor gate in the false bottom of the fish cart, under the herring, and Mara would make noise at the sorting sheds to pull the gate guards' eyes, and by the time the Morning Tide warped out on the morning tide the letter would already be aboard in Sable's keeping, stowed in whatever hole Sable had prepared, and Mara would board afterward as a hired cargo-hand with clean hands and an empty face.

That was the plan. It had the beautiful simplicity of plans made by desperate people, and Mara turned it over and over in the dark looking for the flaw, and found several, and could fix none of them. The flaw was trust. The flaw was always trust.

Across the dormitory someone sighed in sleep. Rain had started against the roof, soft as sifted flour. Mara listened to it and thought of Thorn's empty bed upstairs, the blanket folded back, the iron rails gleaming, the mattress already turned in accordance with regulation — because the company wasted nothing, and a mattress was company property, and grief was not an entry in any ledger they kept.

Then boots again in the corridor. Slow this time. Two sets, and a third that rang differently, heavier at the heel, and Mara's whole body went still in a way that stillness itself would have envied.

The boots stopped outside the wardens' door. A low exchange of voices. Then — and Mara would swear this to their dying day — a knock, soft, almost courteous, on the door of the dormitory itself, though no one entered, and no word was spoken, and after a moment the boots moved on.

But Mara had heard the knock. And Mara understood it. It was the knock a cat gives a mousehole. It was Hale saying, to whoever lay awake in that dark listening: *I know you are in there. Sleep well. We will speak soon.*

The boots faded down the stair. The rain thickened. Mara lay in the black with their heart drumming and their hand pressed flat against their coat lining, over the seed pod, over the deed, and made the only vow that had ever come to them whole and entire:

Tomorrow the letter sailed. Whatever it cost — however many checkpoints, however many lies, whatever Hale suspected and whatever he knew — Elias Thorn's tide would go out on time, bearing forty names across the strait to a magistrate's desk.

They were next unless it sailed. So it would sail.

Outside, far off, through the rain and the fog, the harbor bell tolled the half hour, and Mara began, silently, to count the hours until dawn.
