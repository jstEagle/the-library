## 13. Hale Counts Heads

The bell began at dawn.

Not the work bell—that one had a voice Mara knew the way they knew their own pulse, a flat iron note that meant *up, up, into the water*. This bell was wrong in every particular. It came from the company yard instead of the harbor stairs, and it rang twice, stopped, rang twice again, like something learning to speak. By the third repetition, every diver in the long dormitory was awake and listening, and nobody moved, because in Saltmere an unfamiliar bell at dawn had only ever meant one thing: someone wanted to count what belonged to them.

"They're mustering us," Petrel said from the cot below. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "All of us. Every name on every page."

Mara sat up in the dark. The room smelled of wet wool and lamp oil and the brine that lived in the walls themselves, that no amount of scrubbing would ever drown. Through the shutter slats the sky was the color of a pearl with the nacre scraped off—gray down to the bone of it. Somewhere out in the harbor the water lay flat as poured lead.

"Roll-call," Mara said. The word felt foreign in the mouth, a word for soldiers, for convicts, for people whose names mattered enough to be counted. Indentured workers were counted like herring—at the gate, at the pay desk, at the infirmary door when the fever came through. But not like this. Not called out, one by one, made to stand under a face and answer.

"Thorn's bed is cold," Petrel said. She said it without looking up, the way she said most true things—sideways, into her hands, as if the words might do less damage if they didn't travel by air. "You hear? They took him to the infirmary yesterday and his bed's been empty since."

Mara's hands, already moving to dress, went still. Under the floorboard beneath their cot—the loose one, third from the wall, the one Petrel had shown them how to lift without sound when Mara was eleven and feverish and hiding from the company doctor—the letter lay where it had lain for six days now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a living thing. Mara had not touched it since the night it came into their hands. They thought about it constantly anyway. It had become less an object than an organ, something beating inside them that the whole dormitory could surely hear.

"I don't know anything about Elias Thorn," Mara said carefully. "He was a clerk. Clerks cough. That's all clerks do."

Petrel looked up then. Her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of old rope, and they held Mara's the way a mooring line holds—not gently, but without letting go. She was fifty if she was a day, both hands ruined past feeling from thirty years of cold water, and she had buried a husband and two sons in the harbor and never once spoken their names aloud where the overseers could hear. She knew what it cost to keep things down.

"No," she agreed. "That's all clerks do."

* * *

The yard was filling when they came out. Four hundred souls or more, divers and sorters and shed-hands and the boys who gutted fish for the rendering vats, all funneling through the yard gates in the gray light, breath smoking, shoulders hunched against a wind that smelled of storm somewhere far off. The company had put lanterns on poles around the perimeter even though dawn was coming, and the light they threw was yellow and unsteady, and under it stood men who did not belong to the ordinary order of the yard: constables in Saltmere blue, and beside each constable a Halloway man with a ledger open against his forearm, pen already wet.

And at the head of the steps of the counting house, where the founder's portrait hung behind glass—old Ambrose Halloway himself, painted gazing nobly out at a harbor he had helped make a graveyard—stood Corvus Hale.

Mara saw him and did the thing they had learned to do at nine years old, the thing that had kept them alive through eight years of servitude after their father drowned and the company folded a child into its books like a debt: they became small. Not crouched small—*interior* small. The face emptied. The eyes went mild. The body arranged itself into the posture of a person thinking about breakfast, about the ache in their shoulder, about nothing at all. Mara Vane, nineteen, loose-pearl diver, second shift, no surname worth ink: a figure so unremarkable that the eye slid off them like rain off waxed canvas.

Hale was dressed better than any man had a right to dress at a dockyard muster. Gray coat, black gloves, his hair oiled flat as the harbor water. He held no ledger. He held nothing at all; his hands were clasped behind him in an attitude of patience, and somehow that was worse than a whip or a list. He was not here to check names. He was here to watch faces while names were checked, and everyone in the yard understood this within the first minute, because fear moves through crowds faster than cold.

The roll began.

They worked alphabetically through the ledgers, which meant the A's first—Ackerman, Aldous, Amory—and each name was called in a flat clerk's voice by a thin man with ink-stained fingers whom Mara recognized vaguely from the third floor, some junior copyist promoted overnight into power. Each worker stepped forward when called, gave their number as well as their name, and passed before a constable who looked at their face and then at a paper the constable held, and there was a pause, always a pause, long enough to be felt.

"What's the paper?" Sable whispered. Sable had materialized at Mara's elbow the way Sable always did, smelling of tar and last night's rum, hair tied back with string. "What're they matching faces against?"

"A likeness," said Petrel, on Mara's other side, not whispering at all. "Thorn's description, I'd guess. Or a description of whoever Thorn trusted." She turned her head and spat, delicately, into the mud. "Half the yard can't read and half the rest have never seen their own description written down. They'll hang half of Saltmere on a wrong nose before the week's out."

The line crawled. The sun came up behind cloud and made no difference anyone could feel. Mara stood in the middle of it with their heart going slow and enormous, a diver's heart, the heart they'd trained in deep water to beat like a dripping tap, and made themselves count the lanterns instead of think: nine lanterns, four gates, three constables at the yard's seaward wall where there was no gate at all, which meant someone expected running.

Ackerman. Aldous. Amory. Ansel.

The B's took forever. The C's longer. Mara watched workers step forward and be looked at and be waved back into the mass, and watched two of them—a shed-boy with a burn-scarred cheek and a woman from the sorting tables whose name Mara didn't know—be *not* waved back. Be asked to stand aside. Be walked, gently, between two constables to a doorway in the counting house wall, from which they did not emerge. Nobody said anything. The crowd absorbed it the way water absorbs a stone, closing over the place where they had been.

"That boy's never been inside the ledger room in his life," Mara breathed. "He sorts kelp."

"He has a scar," Petrel said. "They've got 'a scar on the left cheek' written down, likely, from somebody's memory of somebody. It's not a hunt, child. It's a net. You throw a net wide enough you catch whatever swims."

"And what happens to what it catches?"

Petrel didn't answer, and the not-answer was worse than anything she might have said.

* * *

It happened between the M's and the N's, and afterward Mara could never decide whether it had been accident or design, whether the day itself had conspired or whether God—or the sea, which was more reliable than God in Saltmere—had simply reached out a hand.

The foreman of the sorting sheds came along the muster line with a slate and a face like a dropped anchor, looking for someone, and found Mara.

"You. Vane. Where's your knife?"

"My knife?" Mara's hand went to their belt, where the pearl knife hung as it always hung, its handle worn smooth by their father's grip before it was ever theirs.

"The long blade. The opening knife, the one signed out to you from the tool store Tuesday." The foreman consulted the slate with theatrical disgust. "Store says it's not returned. Storemaster wants it accounted before your name's called, or it goes on your indenture as theft, and theft gets read out at muster."

Mara's stomach dropped through the mud of the yard and kept going, down through fill and pilings and forty feet of green water. Because the knife *wasn't* returned. The long opening knife, the good one with the hooked tip, was sitting exactly where Mara had hidden it two nights ago, wrapped in sacking at the bottom of the disused cistern behind the boiling sheds—hidden because it had been part of a plan, part of the plan taking shape in the sleepless arithmetic of the last week, a plan that needed a blade that could not be traced to Mara's belt on the night it was used.

"It'll be in my kit," Mara said. "I'll fetch it now—"

"Now's muster time. Nobody leaves muster." The foreman's eyes were flat with the pleasure of small authority. "So either it turns up, or your number goes in the book with *theft* written next to it, and you can explain that to Mr. Hale yourself."

The line moved forward. The M's were being called. Mara stood in the crowd doing the fastest, coldest figuring of their life: the cistern was four hundred yards away, behind the boiling sheds, past the cooperage. Going there meant leaving muster. Leaving muster during a roll-call instituted by Corvus Hale to find one missing person meant stepping directly into the shape of the person Hale was hunting. Staying meant a theft mark on the indenture—three years added, or worse, a hearing, a reading-out, Hale's smiling attention settling onto them like a gull onto a dying fish.

"Vane!" The clerk's voice, thin and flat. "Mara Vane! Number 4471!"

The crowd parted its smallest part. Mara stepped forward into the open ground before the steps, into the yellow lantern light, and lifted their face to be looked at.

The constable held the paper. His eyes went from the paper to Mara's face and back, and Mara stood in the stillness they had perfected over nineteen years, the stillness of a thing not worth looking at, and waited for the pause to end.

It ended. The constable nodded, almost bored, and waved them back.

But Hale had seen them.

There was no way to know how much he'd seen—whether his gaze had merely passed across one more gray body in a gray crowd, or whether it had snagged, caught, marked—but as Mara turned back toward the ranks, Hale's head turned too, unhurried, a man watching a bird cross the sky, and his eyes followed Mara all the way back into the crowd, and stayed on the place where Mara stood for three full breaths after.

Then the N's were called, and the moment drowned like everything else in Saltmere.

Except that the knife was still in the cistern, and theft was still going into the book.

* * *

It was Petrel who fixed it, and Mara didn't learn until later how completely, how casually, how enormously she had done it.

The M's and N's finished. The O's began—Okafor was not a name Saltmere had; the ledgers here were heavy with Ackermans and Quills and Vanes—and Mara stood in the crowd with the theft mark coming for them like a tide coming for a sandcastle, running the map of the yard in their head, measuring the distance to the cistern, the time, the impossibility, when Petrel shifted her weight off her bad hip and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the foreman and no farther:

"Opening knife. Long blade, hook tip." She was looking at the sky. "That'll be mine, then. Took it Tuesday to cut the net weights free—my own knife's edge is gone, and the storemaster knows it, I told him twice. Must've left it down the sheds. Child," and here her ruined hand closed on Mara's arm, gentle as a manacle, "run and look in my kit by the north rack, will you? Third shelf. If it's there, bring it."

The foreman frowned. The clerk was calling Olliver. And Petrel, fifty years old, both hands dead from the water, a widow twice over, stood in the lantern light with her chin up and her lie smooth as a beach stone, and dared the morning to question her.

Because that was the thing about Petrel: she had been diving since before the fleet went down. She had been in the water the night the storm came too fast. She had pulled bodies out of the shallows for two days and been told it was weather, weather, an act of God, sign nothing, say nothing, get back to work—and she had spent twenty-six years swallowing that, and whatever else lived down inside her alongside it, she had decided, apparently, sometime in the last week, that Mara Vane was worth one smooth lie told in front of the whole company.

Mara ran.

Through the gap in the ranks, past the cooperage, the boiling sheds breathing their sour steam, the cistern's broken rim rising ahead—and there, in the sacking at the bottom, the knife, cold and waiting. Mara's hands shook so badly getting it out that they nearly dropped it into the black water. Then north rack, third shelf, Petrel's kit—a bundle of rag and bone and a tin cup—and into it, quick as sin, the knife went, and back out, and back across the yard with the knife in Mara's fist and their lungs burning, arriving at Petrel's side just as the R's began, holding the blade up to the foreman like a fisherman's trophy.

"There it was," Petrel said, satisfied. "Old woman's memory. Take it to the storemaster and tell him Petrel borrowed it and he can write that down."

The foreman wrote it down. The theft mark died before it was born. And Mara handed the knife over and stood back in the ranks and did not look at Petrel, because looking at her would have meant something, and everything, and there were four hundred people watching and a man on the steps in gray gloves who missed nothing.

* * *

The roll-call took until noon. When the last Z was called—Zephram Quill, a boy of twelve who answered his name in a voice cracking with terror and was waved back to his mother—the thin clerk closed his ledger, and the constables folded their papers, and the crowd began, by inches, to breathe.

Hale descended the steps.

He did not address the crowd. He walked along the front rank instead, slowly, his gloved hands still clasped behind him, looking at no one and everyone, and the rank rippled subtly away from him like water from a prow. He paused once—to examine the boil on a stoker's neck, of all things, tilting his head at it with clinical interest—and once more, near the north end of the line, where Petrel stood.

He stopped in front of her.

Mara, six places down, felt the entire morning tilt.

"Petrel," Hale said. He said it the way a man reads a label. "That's the name you go by, isn't it? No Christian name recorded. Just—" he smiled, and the smile was a beautiful, terrible thing, "—a bird that follows ships."

"Been called worse by better," Petrel said.

"I'm told you lent out a knife on Tuesday." Hale's tone was conversational, pleasant, the tone of a man making idle talk at a funeral. "Generous of you. You're known for generosity. Known for other things too." He leaned in slightly, and though his words did not carry, his smile did. "Known for being in the water a long time ago, on a certain night, and knowing things about that night that were never written down anywhere. Isn't that right, Petrel?"

The wind moved the lantern flames. Somewhere beyond the sea wall, the flat gray harbor slapped softly at stone.

"I'm an old woman," Petrel said. "I know where my kit is and where my bed is and where my next meal's coming from. That's all the knowing I've got left."

"Mm," said Hale. He straightened. He looked at her for one moment longer—one moment in which his eyes, pale and patient and absolutely without warmth, moved from her face down the rank, six places, and rested on Mara Vane with the weight of a hand on the back of the neck.

Then he walked on, and out through the yard gate, and was gone.

The crowd exhaled. The bells went back to their ordinary voices. The work bell rang, flat and familiar, and the yard broke apart into its thousand small motions, and the world resumed pretending to be the world.

But Mara stood where they were, cold to the marrow despite the noon light, understanding three things with perfect clarity.

First: Hale suspected Petrel, or pretended to, or was letting Petrel think he suspected her—which amounted to the same thing.

Second: Petrel had lied for them in front of the whole company, and Hale knew it, and now Petrel's old knowing—the night of the fleet, the water, the things never written down—had become leverage, a hook already set in her flesh.

Third: the letter under the floorboard had to move. Not next week. Not on some careful future tide. Soon. Because Hale was not throwing a net anymore. Hale was counting heads, and finding out exactly whose heads bent together, and the space around Mara was shrinking by one loyal person per day.

"Child." Petrel's hand, the dead one, settled on Mara's shoulder. Her voice was as rough and calm as ever, but her pale eyes were fixed on the gate where Hale had gone out. "Whatever you're carrying—it's heavier than you told me. I can see it dragging at you like a stone in a bag."

"Petrel—"

"Don't thank me and don't apologize." She squeezed, once, with fingers that couldn't feel her own squeeze. "Just do me one kindness. Whatever it is—get it out of this yard before he comes back for me. Old women float, child. But we don't swim fast."

She turned and limped away toward the drying racks, and Mara stood alone in the great gray light of the yard with the letter burning under a floorboard four hundred yards away and the morning tide, their one road out, three days distant—and understood that three days had just become a very long time indeed.
