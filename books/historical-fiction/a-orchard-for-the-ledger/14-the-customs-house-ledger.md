## 14. The Customs House Ledger

The customs house smelled of tallow and old paper and the particular sourness of men who had sat too long in one chair. Mara had walked past it a thousand times on errands for the Halloway Company—carrying manifests, carrying receipts, carrying other people's names from one desk to another—and never once had the doors opened onto anything but boredom. Now they opened onto danger, and the difference was only ink.

Mara stood at the foot of the wide stone steps with a satchel of pearl-sacks over one shoulder and a stamped manifest folded against their chest like a second heart. The morning fog was burning off the harbor in long gray ribbons. Somewhere behind them, at the harbor gate, Sable was already through—that was the story Mara told themselves, anyway, because believing otherwise was not survivable. The letter had crossed the first checkpoint inside a false bottom of a fish cart, under a layer of herring that could gag a customs officer at ten paces. One checkpoint down. Two remained.

*The tide,* Thorn had whispered, blood on his lips, his fingers cold as coin. *The tide.*

The Morning Tide would sail tomorrow morning, if the weather held, carrying a magistrate who had come across the strait to hear grievances no one in Saltmere dared speak above a murmur. The letter had to be aboard before the gangway rose. And between Mara and that gangway stood this building, this smell, this ledger.

They climbed the steps.

* * *

Inside, the great hall of the Saltmere customs house was dim as the inside of a barrel. High windows let down shafts of light thick with dust, and beneath them sat rows of clerks bent over their books like penitents, scratching away in the company hand—the same hand Mara had been taught to write at nine years old, when Halloway's instructors had taken their fingers and shaped them around a nib as though fingers belonged to the company too.

That was the joke of it, Mara thought, joining the queue before the receiving desk. That was the bitter, beautiful joke: everything they were about to do, they had learned here. The evenness of line. The discipline of the entry. The way a forged figure must not be *too* perfect, because perfect figures raised questions, whereas slightly imperfect ones simply passed, the way water passes a stone it has worn smooth itself.

Thorn had taught them that. Not deliberately—not in any lesson either of them would have named aloud—but in the small corrections he'd made over those nights in the third-floor ledger room. *You press too hard,* he'd said once, standing behind their shoulder, close enough that Mara could smell the sickness on him, sweetish and wrong. *A clerk who presses too hard is a clerk with something to prove. Ink should go where you want it, quiet. Like a diver going down.* He had demonstrated, taking the pen from their hand, and his letters had come out so unremarkable, so perfectly forgettable, that Mara had stared at the page and understood, for the first time, that handwriting could be a kind of diving too—a descent into invisibility, holding your breath.

*A clerk is trusted precisely to the degree that he is dull,* Thorn had said another night. *Remember that. Dullness is armor. It has protected worse men than me for longer than I've been alive.*

And then he had coughed into his handkerchief, and folded the cloth so the red didn't show, and gone back to his columns.

"Next."

Mara stepped up to the receiving desk. The officer there was heavy-jowled, half-asleep, a stamp lying by his elbow like a sleeping animal. Behind him the shelves ran back into shadow, hundreds of ledgers bound in gray leather, each one a year of Saltmere's comings and goings, each entry a small act of faith that the paper told the truth.

"Purpose?"

"Cargo transfer," Mara said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Halloway Company. Twelve sacks of shell pearl, sorting-house grade, consigned to the *Morning Tide*, sailing tomorrow's tide."

The officer held out a hand without looking up. Mara gave him the manifest.

This was the moment. This was the whole of it, balanced on a rectangle of paper.

Because the manifest was real—Halloway really was shipping twelve sacks of shell pearl to the ship; Mara had carried the requisition themselves three days ago, before any of this began, back when their life had still fit inside its old shape. But a manifest was not enough. The customs house required a matching entry in the receiving ledger, made by the consignor's clerk, initialed, dated, and stamped—and Mara was not the consignor's clerk. The consignor's clerk was a man named Aldous Grebe, who was presently laid flat with a fever in the company dormitory, and whose initials Mara had spent two hours last night learning to make from a dozen old requisitions spread across their cot blanket by candlelight.

Grebe wrote his G like a man strangling a goose. Mara had practiced until theirs strangled it identically.

The officer read the manifest. He turned it over. He looked at it longer than any man had ever looked at anything, or so it seemed to Mara, whose heartbeat had climbed into their throat and was beating there like a fist on a locked door.

"This says the entry's already been made." He tapped the bottom of the page, where, in Grebe's strangled goose of a G, appeared the notation *entered, vol. 41, fol. 117*.

"Yes, sir," Mara said. "Clerk Grebe entered it yesterday afternoon. He's taken ill since. I'm sent to confirm the stamp."

Something flickered across the officer's face—not suspicion, exactly. Something more tired. "Entered yesterday and not stamped."

"He was called away, sir. The bell rang for the counting of the sheds."

"The bell rings every hour in this town and nothing gets done because of it." The officer sighed, a sound like a hull settling, and pulled volume forty-one toward himself across the desk.

Mara watched him turn pages and understood suddenly, viscerally, that there was no folio 117. There could not be. Grebe had never made the entry, because Grebe had never seen the manifest, because Mara had written the notation themselves, last night, in a fever of fear and candle smoke, betting everything on a single assumption: that a customs officer, given a choice between walking to a shelf and pulling a heavy ledger and finding a page, and simply stamping what was put in front of him—

The officer found folio 117.

Mara's whole body went still the way the harbor goes still before wind, every surface flat and bright and lying.

Because there *was* an entry. In Grebe's hand—or something near enough to it that the lamplight made no argument—twelve sacks, shell pearl, sorting-house grade, consigned *Morning Tide*, initialed A.G., dated yesterday.

Sable. It had to be Sable. Mara had given Sable the true details of the shipment two nights ago, over stolen rum, as part of the plan's architecture—*the cart needs real cargo to hide the real cargo*—and Sable, who could forge a harbormaster's chit as easily as other men whistled, had come into this hall yesterday and sat down at an unattended side desk and written the entry themselves, in a hand they'd copied off company requisitions for years, and walked out again past a dozing officer, because the whole world assumed that anyone sitting at a desk with a pen belonged there.

Dullness is armor. Thorn had said it about clerks. Sable had proved it worked for thieves.

"Looks in order," the officer grunted. He took up the stamp, breathed on it out of some habit older than himself, and pressed it down beside the initials. Red wax-black ink, the crown-and-anchor of the Saltmere customs house, biting into the page. Then he stamped the manifest to match, blew on both, and slid them back across the desk.

"Sign the receipt book."

Mara signed. Not their own name—never their own name, that name lived nowhere but in ledgers owned by other people—but the name the requisition bore, the junior clerk's name, a small unremarkable name belonging to a boy Mara barely knew who would never learn how his signature had traveled today. They felt the falseness of it in their fingers and then, horribly, they did not feel it anymore. The letters came out even and dull and perfect in their imperfection. Thorn's ghost guided the nib.

"Next," the officer said, already reaching for someone else's paper.

Mara walked out of the customs house into the white morning light, and their knees did not give way until they reached the shade of the fish-market awning, where they stopped among the crates and gulls and shook like a landed eel.

* * *

They had meant to feel triumphant. They had rehearsed the feeling on the walk over, the way one rehearses a speech: *I will have beaten them with their own tools.* But what came instead was stranger and colder—a kind of vertigo, standing in the market noise, holding a stamped paper that was now, officially, irrevocably, part of the record of Saltmere.

Somewhere in a gray-bound ledger on a dusty shelf, a lie sat drying between two truths, indistinguishable from them, protected by the same stamp, governed by the same law. And Mara had put it there. With a hand trained by the Halloway Company. With skills bought at the price of their childhood, nine years old and bent over practice lines while other children learned the names of trees.

*The tools of servitude turned against the master.* They heard it in Thorn's voice, though he had never said it. He hadn't needed to. Every correction he'd ever made of their hand had been saying it: *they taught you to copy, child, but copying is power; whoever holds the pen holds the past, and the past is all a drowned man has.*

Mara thought of their father then, unexpectedly, standing in the fish-smell and the gull-cries. Joren Vane, who could hold his breath four minutes and had once brought up a pearl so fine the Halloway assessor had gone quiet looking at it. A man who had never signed a document in his life except with a cross, witnessed, because no one had ever taught him his letters—who had died nameless in a company ledger, *Vane, J.*, crossed out with unusual care, debt marked paid by death.

Well. His child had letters. His child had the finest copy-hand in three floors of clerks, and a stamp's blessing on a lie, and a sealed testimony sewn where no search would find it, and eleven hours until the tide.

Mara straightened the satchel on their shoulder and went to find Sable, to tell them the second checkpoint had fallen.

* * *

They found the fish cart where Sable always left it, tucked behind the net-menders' shed at the harbor's edge, but Sable wasn't with it. Mara waited, counting gulls, counting breaths, watching the harbor gate in the distance where carts trundled through and guards leaned on pikes with the boneless ease of men paid too little to care. Twenty minutes. Thirty. The sun climbed and the fog burned off entirely and the water went glittering and indifferent, the same water that had taken the fleet, the same water that kept their father, the same water that did not care what was written in anybody's ledger.

When Sable finally came, slipping out of the alley behind the net-shed with their collar up and their grin slightly crooked, relief hit Mara so hard it was almost anger.

"You're late."

"I'm *rich*," Sable said, which was their answer to most things. "Do you know what herring fetches this week? I sold the cartload twice, once to the customs house and once to a chandlery cook who didn't ask questions." They dropped onto a crate and produced, from somewhere in their coat, a twist of paper containing two fried fish, and offered one with the magnanimity of a king distributing titles. "Your entry's in. Folio 117. I even spelled 'consigned' right, which is more than Grebe ever managed."

"I saw. It nearly undid me—I thought for a moment I'd walked into a trap."

"If it were a trap, little diver, you'd have known by the number of guards and the shortness of the conversation." Sable bit into their fish and chewed, watching the harbor gate with quick dark eyes. "It went through clean. The fat one at the desk barely looked. You were right about that much—" a jerk of the chin toward the customs house— "they trust their own paper more than their own eyes. A stamped lie weighs more than an unstamped truth. Whole city runs on it."

Mara took the fish and ate without tasting it. Beyond Sable's shoulder, the *Morning Tide* lay at her berth, masts bare, riding low with cargo already aboard. Tomorrow she would take the morning tide, and on her passenger list, in a cabin with a magistrate behind the door, the truth would cross the strait where no Halloway fixer could reach it.

"One checkpoint left," Mara said.

"The gangway." Sable's grin faded a degree. "That's the hard one. Gate guards are sleepy. Customs clerks are lazy. But the gangway tomorrow will have Hale's men on it, and Hale doesn't sleep and doesn't blink, and everyone boarding gets looked at, worker or magistrate, everyone."

"I know."

"And you'll be walking on with the rest of the cargo hands, calm as Sunday, carrying—" Sable waved vaguely at Mara's chest, at the secret sewn there, at the thing they had never said aloud between them but both knew. "You're sure you won't split it. Two packets, two routes, like the boatswain's bundle. Safer."

"No." Mara heard how flat their own voice came out, and did not soften it. "One letter. One carrier. Me. If it's split, it can be lost twice."

Sable looked at them a moment too long. Something moved behind the quick eyes—not hurt exactly, but its cousin. "Fine. Your funeral, little diver. Though I notice it's my funeral twice already this week, what with the cart and the folio."

"And I'll owe you," Mara said. "I keep accounts."

"That," said Sable, standing and brushing crumbs from their coat, "is exactly what worries me."

They laughed then, both of them, briefly and too loudly, the laughter of people standing on a high place in a high wind. Sable clapped Mara's shoulder and slipped away down the quay, collar up, moving through the crowd the way water moves through pilings—everywhere admitted, nowhere held.

Mara stayed a while longer on the crate, finishing the cold fried fish, watching the tide begin to turn. The water at the harbor mouth slackened, shivered, and started its long pull seaward. Tomorrow it would carry the *Morning Tide* out on its back like something born.

Then, as they rose to go, they saw him.

Corvus Hale, at the far end of the quay, standing in the shade of the customs house steps they had just left. He was talking to the fat receiving officer, and as Mara watched, the officer gestured—vaguely, unhurriedly—toward volume forty-one on its shelf. Hale took the ledger down himself. Turned pages. Stopped.

Even at fifty paces, even across the whole bright width of the quay, Mara felt the moment Hale's finger touched folio 117. It went through them like cold water through cloth. Hale stood very still over the open book for a long time. Then he looked up from the page, slowly, the way a man looks up from a trail he has just realized is fresh, and began—without hurry, without any expression at all—to scan the crowd along the quay.

Mara did not run. Running was a confession written on the body. They picked up their empty fish paper, turned with the unhurried dullness of a worker with nowhere in particular to be, and walked into the market crowd, heart slamming, face smooth as harbor water.

Behind them, faint under the gull-cries, they heard Hale's voice ask the officer a question they could not make out—and the officer's answering shrug, which might mean nothing.

Or might mean, by tomorrow's tide, everything.
