## 15. Sable's Price

The fish market opened before the sun did, the way it always did, and by the time Mara reached the quay the stalls were already bleeding silver into the gray water. Mackerel in heaps, their eyes flat as coins. Oysters pried open with a violence that sounded, from a distance, like small doors being broken. The gulls screamed over it all, wheeling and dropping, wheeling again, as if the whole morning were a thing they could not decide to land on.

Mara had learned the market's rhythms the way they had learned the ledger room: by being beneath notice. A diver's child among the fishwives was no stranger than a gull among them. They carried a basket that said *errand* and a face that said *nothing*, and between those two shields they moved down the row of stalls until they found the one they wanted.

Sable was there, of course. Sable was always there, or always somewhere near there, in the way that smoke is near a fire. They stood behind a trestle table heaped with eels, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms slicked to the wrist with river water and fish blood, arguing cheerfully with a woman about whether an eel could be called fresh if it had spent two hours on ice.

"Fresh," Sable insisted, "is a matter of opinion. Lively is a fact. This one bit me not ten minutes past."

"Everything on this quay bites you, Sable Quill. You'd call a dead man fresh if he owed you money."

"I'd call him *paid*," Sable said, and the woman laughed despite herself, took her eel, and went.

Mara watched the exchange from three stalls away, basket held against their hip, and felt the old dangerous warmth rise in their chest. That was the trouble with Sable. That had always been the trouble with Sable, since the night over stolen rum when they'd sworn loyalty with salt water poured between their cupped hands—*the sea hears me, the sea will hold me to it*—and Mara had believed it because wanting to believe a thing is half of believing it.

*Careful*, said the voice in their head that had been growing louder since Thorn pressed the letter into their hands and said *the tide, the tide*. *Careful, careful.* But Sable's laugh carried across the stalls, bright and unguarded, and the voice quieted the way voices do when you feed them what they want.

Sable saw them then. Something crossed their face—quick, there and gone, quicker than the gulls—and Mara told themselves it was only surprise at seeing them so early.

"You're up with the fish," Sable said, wiping their hands on a rag that made them no cleaner. "The harbor must be paying you double."

"The harbor pays me nothing. I'm paying *it*." Mara set the basket down. Inside, under a cloth, lay six pearls, loose and small, the pick of a week's diving. Not for sale. For the boatswain of the Morning Tide, eventually—but today, for Sable, they were only ballast, weight and secret both.

"Still at it? Still feeding your coins back to the water?" Sable leaned on the table, and for a moment their eyes did something Mara had never seen them do: they flicked, once, left, toward the mouth of the quay where the harbor gate stood, then back. "You should be careful with your pearls, little diver. There are men about who'd pay well for a pearl. Or for other things people carry without knowing what they're carrying."

The words landed light as fish scales, but something underneath them did not.

"Other things," Mara repeated.

"Paper," Sable said, too casually, sorting eels as they spoke, not looking up. "A clerk's paper, they're saying. Down the taverns, in the dockside talk. Some fixer of the Halloway Company's asking questions with coin in his fist. Corvus Hale. You know the name?"

Everyone knew the name. Everyone knew it the way sailors know the name of a reef—they speak it quietly and give it room.

"He's offering a reward," Sable went on, still not looking up. "For information leading to a certain document. Enough to buy out an indenture twice over. Enough to leave Saltmere entirely and start a life somewhere the fog doesn't reach." Now they looked up, and smiled, and the smile stayed a half-second longer than smiles should. "Imagine that. One paper, and a whole new life."

An oyster knife lay on the table between them, its blade crusted pale. Somewhere behind Mara a cart wheel squealed. The gulls went on screaming their one endless argument.

Mara understood, in that moment, that this was a test—not the kind Thorn had set with his deliberate errors, inked traps in a ledger, but a rougher one, a net dragged through shallow water to see what it caught. Sable was telling them about the reward. Why tell them? To warn a friend. Or to watch a face.

And so Mara did the only thing invisibility had ever taught them well. They let the words pass through them like weather.

"Hale," they said, tasting the name and finding it bland. "He came round the sheds last week. Tall fellow, smiles like a shut door. What's a paper worth all that coin?"

"That's what I wondered." Sable resumed sorting the eels, one after another, each slap of wet flesh against the boards loud in the morning quiet between them. "A clerk's paper. Old Elias Thorn, they say—he took sick, poor man. Coughing blood all over his ledger entries. Maybe he wrote something down before he went. Maybe somebody has it."

"Thorn taught me my letters," Mara said, and it was true, and true things are the safest lies' clothing. "I copied for him nights, till my eyes swam. If I had anything of his it'd be penmanship, nothing more."

"Penmanship." Sable laughed, and the laugh was Sable's own, warm and cracked and familiar, and the hairline thing inside Mara—the thing that had begun to split the moment Sable's eyes flicked toward the gate—quieted a little. Only a little. "Well. If you find any of his papers lying about, mind you don't get ink on your hands. Ink's hard to wash off. Gets everywhere. Shows up in places you'd never expect."

"Like guilt," Mara said.

"Like guilt," Sable agreed, and handed them an eel, coiled and gleaming, wrapped in brown paper. "For your supper. On the house. You look thin, little diver. Whatever you're carrying, carry some meat with it."

*Whatever you're carrying.*

Mara took the parcel. Their fingers did not shake; nine years of indenture had trained even the small muscles into obedience. "I'll bring the bones back for your stockpot."

"Do that." Sable waved them off, already turning to the next customer, already loud again, already the Sable everyone on the quay knew—generous, sharp-tongued, incorruptible in the way of people who have never been offered enough to test it.

Mara walked away along the quay with the eel against their ribs and the letter's memory sewn into their skull, every word of it, Thorn's careful testimony, Joren Vane among the forty names, and they did not look back. Looking back was a thing people did when they suspected. Invisible people walked forward, always forward, at the same unhurried pace.

But the crack was there now. Small as a flaw in a pearl, the kind you only find when you drill it—and once drilled, the pearl is good for nothing but grinding.

They passed the harbor gate on their way back, and the guards there were different from yesterday. New faces, or old faces arranged differently. And posted beside the gate, nailed up fresh, a printed notice: *INFORMATION WANTED*, it began, and beneath it a sum of money written in figures so large they seemed less like numbers than like a dare.

*Information leading to the recovery of certain documents belonging to the estate of Elias Thorn, deceased clerk.*

*Deceased*. So it was known already. Or claimed. Hale worked faster than storms.

Mara read it the way they read everything—as someone with no reason to read it—and moved on.

* * *

That evening the dormitory smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, forty bodies and their forty small economies of survival, boots drying under bunks, someone's forbidden pipe smoked down to ash in a cupped palm. Mara lay on their cot with the eel eaten and the bones wrapped for return, and listened to the building settle, and thought about Sable's eyes flicking toward the gate.

*Toward the gate*, where the reward notice hung. Toward the gate, which Sable passed a dozen times a day with carts and cargo and other people's secrets. Toward the figure of a number that would buy out an indenture twice over.

No. No, that wasn't fair. Sable had sworn over rum and seawater. Sable had hidden Mara from a wharf-beater once, smuggled them past a press-gang, shared everything down to the last crust and never once kept score—or never once showed the score, which might be the same thing or might be the opposite of the same thing. You couldn't spend nine years trusting someone and then unspend it in one morning because their glance went sideways.

But Thorn had trusted the company for thirty years, and the company had drowned forty divers and called it weather. Trust was just a debt someone hadn't yet come to collect.

Mara turned these thoughts over the way they turned pearls, feeling for flaws, finding them, unable to stop finding them. The lamp guttered. Around them the dormitory breathed.

Sleep came late and thin, and in the thin place between sleeping and waking, Mara heard again Sable's voice: *whatever you're carrying, carry some meat with it.* Kindness. It had been kindness. It had been kindness, or it had been kindness wearing the shape of a question, and there was no instrument fine enough to weigh the difference, and wouldn't be until the morning tide came and the Morning Tide sailed and either the letter was gone across the strait or it wasn't.

* * *

Sable counted the coin alone, after midnight, in the back of the eel stall where the oil lamps burned low and the smell of brine soaked into everything including dreams.

It was not the reward money. Not yet. It was simply money—wages and winnings and the slow accumulation of a life lived on percentages of other people's catches—a small stack of copper and worn silver, sorted by touch in the dark because Sable had learned to count in the dark, as a child, hiding from a father who counted differently.

The stack was pitiful. Sable knew exactly how pitiful. Eleven years on the quay and this was all of it: a pile of metal you could lose in a drain, next to the figure on the notice by the gate, the figure that sat behind Sable's eyelids now every time they closed them. Enough to leave. Enough for a shop of their own in some port where nobody knew the name Sable Quill meant *dockside, cheap, available*. Enough to stop being a percentage of other people's lives.

And Mara had something. Sable was nearly sure of it. Had seen it, maybe, or imagined seeing it—that flicker behind the little diver's stillness, the stillness that was itself a kind of answer, because only people carrying weight stand that carefully. Thorn had favored Mara, everyone knew it. Thorn was gone. And now a man with a soft voice and a long memory was buying information by the ounce, and the coin on the table was eleven years of honest poverty, and the coin by the gate was freedom.

Sable swept the stack back into its pouch and sat in the dark with their hands around it, hands that still smelled of eel and would always smell of eel, and hated themselves a little, and hated the world a great deal more, and did not yet decide anything at all.

Outside, the tide was turning. It would go out, and come back, and go out again, and on one of those mornings soon a ship would sail on it, carrying whatever it carried. Sable lay back on the pallet and listened to the water move in the dark, moving the way it always moved, indifferent, faithful, keeping every promise it had ever made to no one.

The sea hears me, they had sworn. The sea will hold me to it.

The sea heard everything. That had never once stopped anyone.
