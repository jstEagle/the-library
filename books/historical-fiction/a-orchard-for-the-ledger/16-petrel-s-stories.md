## 16. Petrel's Stories

The mending nets came out at dusk, when the sorting sheds went quiet and the lamplighters had not yet made their rounds. Petrel had claimed the same corner of the quay for thirty years—a stretch of weathered planking beneath the rusted hook of crane number four, where the wind came off the water smelling of brine and tar and, on certain tides, of the kelp forests far below where the pearl beds lay sleeping. The young ones called it Petrel's parish. Petrel called it nothing at all. A place you loved was a place they could take from you, and Petrel had learned long ago to keep even their loves unnamed.

Tonight there were three of them at the nets: Petrel, a gap-toothed girl called Minnow whose hands moved too fast for her patience, and Mara Vane.

Petrel had been watching Mara Vane for years, the way an old bird watches weather. There was something in that one—something held very still, very deep, like a stone dropped in dark water that never quite finished sinking. The child had come to the company at nine years old with salt-scoured hands and eyes that counted everything and gave back nothing. Petrel knew that look. Petrel had worn it once themselves, in another port, under another name that no ledger had bothered to record.

But tonight the stone was rising. Tonight Mara Vane sat with a net across their lap and their fingers moving by rote while their mind swam elsewhere, and twice they opened their mouth to speak and closed it again, and the third time—

"Ask me," Petrel said, not looking up from the mesh. "Whatever it is. You've been chewing it since the second bell."

The needles paused. Out on the black water, a buoy bell clanked its slow idiot rhythm, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the Morning Tide rode at anchor, her running lights doubled tonight because the barometer was falling and every captain in Saltmere could taste the storm coming down the strait like iron on the tongue.

"The fleet," Mara said. "The Pearlwing fleet. The one that went down in ninety-one."

Petrel's hands did not stop. That was the discipline: the hands never stopped. "What about it?"

"You were here. Everyone says you were here before the Halloway piers were built, before the customs house, before—" They stopped themselves, as if the list itself were a kind of trespass.

"I was here," Petrel said. "I was here the night it happened, if that's what you're circling."

Mara set the net down. Slowly, deliberately, the way a person puts down something heavy they intend to pick up again. "Nobody talks about it. Seven years, and nobody talks about it. They say *the storm*, they say *that terrible season*, they say *forty souls*, and then they change the subject the way you'd step over a body in the street."

"And what do you want, Mara Vane? Do you want the story? Or do you want what the story does?"

"What does it do?"

"It makes you unable to sleep." Petrel finally looked up. The shed lamp behind them threw their shadow long across the boards, a bent and crooked thing, all angles, like a gull's wing broken mid-fold. "It gets into your chest and sits there like cold ballast. I've carried it seven years and I can tell you the exact weight of it. So ask yourself first whether you want to carry it too."

Minnow, sensing the gravity shift in the air the way children sense weather, gathered her needles and slipped off toward the dormitories without a word. Good girl. Some stories needed fewer ears.

Mara did not leave. Mara pulled the net back onto their lap, but their eyes stayed fixed on Petrel, and in the low light they looked suddenly very young and very old at once—the way the drowned look, Petrel thought, though that was an unkind thought and they pushed it down where unkind thoughts belonged.

"My father dove those beds," Mara said quietly. "Joren Vane."

"I know who Joren Vane was." The name came out of Petrel before the caution could catch it, and once it was out, the rest followed the way water follows a crack. "Everyone who worked the water then knew him. Strongest lungs on this coast. Could stay down past four minutes when the others came up gasping at two and a half. He used to sing on his way down—did you know that? Low, through closed lips, so he could hear his own song in his own skull and know his mind was whole. The other divers said if you were working a bed near Joren Vane and the singing stopped, you surfaced fast, because either he'd found trouble or trouble had found him."

Mara's throat moved. "He sang to me. In the shallows. When I was small. He said—" The voice caught, steadied. "He said the song told the water where you were, so it wouldn't mistake you for driftwood."

"Aye." Petrel bent again to the net, because looking at that face was like looking into a mirror with seven years scraped off it. "That's diver's lore. Old as the beds themselves."

* * *

The storm of 1891 had come in on a Tuesday, Petrel remembered—not that the day mattered, except that memory is a magpie and insists on collecting such things. Tuesday, late September, the pearling season at its fat middle swell. Two fleets worked the outer banks that week: Halloway's own boats, twelve of them, riding high and rich; and the Pearlwing Company's fleet, fourteen boats out of Corvassa, crewed by free divers mostly, families who owned their own boats and their own beds and answered to no trading house. Forty-one divers on the water that day between the two companies' people, counting tenders and skiffmen.

Petrel had been shore-side that day, mending a seam on the *Kestrel's* tender, which was why they lived to tell anything at all.

"The morning was wrong," Petrel said now, and the words came slow, dredged up one by one like stones from deep water. "You'll hear old men say that about every storm, that the morning was wrong, that the gulls flew strange. But I'm telling you true: the light that day came in green. Green as bottle glass. And the tide turned forty minutes early and came in *fast*, faster than any spring tide I'd seen, and when it turned it didn't turn smooth—it turned in steps. Slap. Pause. Slap. Like something big walking on the sea floor a long way off."

"Barometric," Mara said. "Thorn—I read, in the shipping almanacs, that—"

"I know what the almanacs say." Petrel let a silence sit there, long enough for the buoy bell to count five. "Here's what the almanacs don't say. Here's what I've kept behind my teeth for seven years, and I'm only saying it now because something in your face says you've already swallowed worse."

They set down the net entirely. Their old hands, knuckled like coral, lay flat on their knees.

"There's a signal station on Gullwatch Point. Has been since before the war, before my time. Its whole purpose—its whole reason for standing—is to hoist flags when weather threatens the outer banks, so the fleets can run for shelter before the bars close. Every captain on this coast knows the code. Red pennant over black: storm imminent, make for harbor. It takes four minutes to run the flags up. Four minutes. That's all the warning those divers would have needed. Four minutes to surface, four more to weigh anchor and run for the lee of the point."

Mara had gone very still. Stillness like that, Petrel thought, wasn't calm. It was a animal holding its breath in a snare.

"On the afternoon of that Tuesday, the station keeper on Gullwatch—he was new, transferred in from up the coast in June, a man named Ostrey—Ostrey logged in his own book, which I saw later, with these two eyes, that at half past two he recorded falling glass, green light, stepped tides. Everything a man would need to justify raising the flags. And at quarter to three—quarter to *three*, when any fool on the water could see the sky going the color of a bruise—a boat put out from the Halloway pier. Fast launch. Two men in it. It rowed straight for Gullwatch Point, and it sat off the station for the better part of an hour, and when it rowed away, the flagpole stood bare against the sky."

The words fell into the space between them and lay there like lead.

"No flags," Petrel said. "No signal. The Pearlwing fleet was working the outermost beds, six miles out, beyond sight of the point. The Halloway boats—all twelve of them—were back inside the harbor bar by four o'clock. Every one. Called in early, no explanation given to the crews, just *come about, make for home*. The Pearlwingers got no call at all. Nobody signaled them. Nobody sent a skiff. And at ten minutes past five, the front came down the strait like the hand of God closing a lid, and the outer banks disappeared."

Mara's voice came out thin and strange. "Forty divers."

"Thirty-eight divers and two tenders. One body washed up at Cobbler's Beach two days after. Three more over the following week. The rest—" Petrel made a small gesture outward, toward the black water, toward the beds. "The sea kept them. It keeps what it's given time to keep."

* * *

There was a part Petrel had never told anyone. Not the widow's societies that came asking, not the inquiry that never was, not the priests who came sniffing for scandal. Not even in drink, and Petrel had been drink-deep enough times that the whole story should have spilled out of them like bilge. But this part they had kept, and now, with Mara Vane's face turned toward them like a plant toward light, they found it rising in them whether they willed it or not.

"I was on the north mole that night," Petrel said. "After the front hit. Nobody could sleep—every soul in Saltmere was out in the wind, wives and mothers and fathers, staring at the water like staring could pull boats home. The wind was screaming out of the southwest and the harbor bar was a wall of white—you couldn't have launched a rowboat against it without dying in the first hundred yards. We all knew it. We stood there anyway.

"And past midnight, in the gaps between the gusts, we heard them."

"Hear who?" But Mara knew. Petrel could see that they knew, and asked anyway, because the knowing needed to be spoken aloud to become real.

"The divers." Petrel's voice roughened, cracked along some old seam. "Sound carries strange over water in a storm—it comes in under the wind, in the troughs between gusts, like voices through a wall. And the outer bank was only three miles off, close enough on a night like that, and they were surfacing into it. Boats gone over or driven off, no tender to haul them up, swimming in the dark with the swells stacking twenty feet, and calling. Calling for help. Calling names. Calling God. You'd get a gust and then a gap and in the gap—voices. Dozens of them. Faint, but *there*, and every person on that mole heard them, and there was nothing—*nothing*—any one of us could do. We stood there listening to our neighbors drown, and the wind would come back and take the voices away, and then a gap, and more voices, fewer each time, and by dawn the sea was empty and quiet as a church."

Mara had not moved. Tears were sliding down their face, but they made no sound, wiped nothing away, and Petrel understood: this was grief being taken into the body whole, without anesthesia, the way medicine used to be taken before anyone invented mercy.

"My father sang on his way down," Mara whispered. "So the water would know where he was."

Petrel bowed their head. "Then he died doing the bravest thing a diver can do, which is refusing to be silent in it."

* * *

The lamp in the sorting shed guttered and recovered. Down the quay, the watchman made his round, boots ringing on the planks, and passed them by with the nod he always gave—the nod that meant *I see nothing, you are nothing, good night*. Petrel waited until his footsteps faded.

"Afterward," they said, "the company paid out. That's the part that curdles. No inquiry—Halloway's lawyers saw to that, and the harbormaster signed papers saying the storm was unprecedented, an act of nature, unforeseeable. But payments came. Quiet ones. To the widows. To the families. Enough to bury grief, not enough to question it. And every payment came with a paper to sign. A release, they called it. Waiving claim. And the paper said—in fine print, fine as spider silk—that the signatory acknowledged the loss resulted from natural causes and released the Halloway Trading Company from all liability arising therefrom."

"They made them sign," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"Some refused. The Corvassa families—free folk, proud folk—they wouldn't sign, and their payments came slower and smaller, and then came harassment, and within two years every Corvassa boat family had left the coast or gone under. The indentured families signed. What else do you do? Refuse coin to feed your children on principle?" Petrel shook their head slowly. "Grief makes you sign anything. That's the truth of power in this world, Mara Vane. It doesn't need to force you. It only needs to wait until you're drowning and then offer you a rope with a price tag on it."

For a long time neither spoke. The buoy bell counted. Far out, a first spit of rain crossed the lamplight, fine as sifted flour.

"Why are you telling me this?" Mara asked at last. "Seven years you've kept it. Why give it to *me*?"

And here was the crux of it, and Petrel considered lying, or deflecting, or giving the safe answer—the answer about how everyone deserved to know their dead. But there was something in Mara's face that made lying feel like a desecration, so Petrel told the truth instead.

"Because you've been carrying something for weeks now. Something heavy. I've watched you walk like a woman with a stolen thing sewn into her coat—careful of doorways, careful of crowds, checking reflections in shop glass. I've been indentured sixty years, child, and I know the walk of a person carrying paper that could hang somebody." They held up a hand before Mara could speak. "Don't tell me. Don't ever tell me, and don't tell me I'm wrong, because I don't want to know it and neither do you want me to. But hear this."

They leaned close, and their voice dropped to the register they'd used once, long ago, singing shanties in the dark holds of ships that had carried them across an ocean they never named.

"Whatever it is you're carrying—whatever Elias Thorn put into your hands before the coughing took him—it isn't yours alone. It never was. Forty families signed those releases because they had to, and every one of them has wondered for seven years whether the story they were fed was true, and every one of them will die wondering unless someone brings the truth into the light. You think you're smuggling a letter. You're not. You're smuggling *them*—all of them, the thirty-eight and the two tenders, and Joren Vane among them with his song in his throat. Whatever happens, whatever it costs you—and it will cost, it always costs—remember whose hands it's really in. All of theirs. Yours is just the pair that's carrying it tonight."

Mara looked down at their own hands—diver's hands, clerk's hands, nineteen years old and scarred with both trades. When they raised their head, the tears were dried, and in their place was something Petrel recognized from storms: the flat bright calm that comes after fear has burned everything else away.

"The letter belongs to more than one family," Mara said quietly. It wasn't agreement. It was arithmetic.

"It belongs to all of them," Petrel said. "And to the water. And to nobody who wears the Halloway name."

Rain was coming harder now, ticking on the shed roof, and the wind had shifted and strengthened, and out on the dark harbor the Morning Tide's lights swung at their moorings like lanterns in a frightened hand. Petrel began gathering the nets, folding them with the economy of decades, and Mara rose to help, and for a while the two of them worked in the rain-shadow of the crane, silent, shoulder to shoulder.

It was Mara who broke the silence, and when they spoke, their voice had changed—had hardened into something Petrel had never heard from them before, something that sounded almost like a vow.

"Petrel. If anything happens to me—if I don't come back from whatever comes next—will you remember the names? All of them? Will you say them out loud, on this quay, where someone might hear?"

Petrel looked at the young face above them, lit dimly by the shed lamp, and felt the old cold weight in their own chest shift for the first time in seven years—as if something long sunken had stirred, and begun, very slowly, to rise.

"Child," Petrel said, "you tell me the names, and I'll shout them till the day they nail my coffin shut. Now go. And Mara—" they caught the young one's sleeve as they turned, held it one moment, "—whatever you're planning, plan it fast. Because I've watched Corvus Hale for fifteen years, and men like him don't circle forever. When they stop circling—"

Out beyond the breakwater, thunder rolled down the strait, long and low, like something vast clearing its throat.

"—they land."
