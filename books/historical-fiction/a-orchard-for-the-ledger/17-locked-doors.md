## 17. Locked Doors

The summons came at dawn, delivered not by the usual runner but by a boy Mara had never seen before, pale as a candle-end, who would not meet their eyes.

"Mara Vane. Ledger room. Third floor. By order of Mr. Hale."

The words sat in Mara's chest like swallowed stones all through the walk from the dormitory block, past the cooperage where the barrels were already sweating tar into the morning air, past the sorting sheds where the first shift of divers was stripping off their oilskins. The harbor lay flat and gray under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the gulls wheeled over it screaming their idiot grievances, and none of it—not one gull, not one barrel, not one bent back—knew what was coming for Mara Vane.

*By order of Mr. Hale.*

There were a dozen reasons a company might reassign an indentured worker to the ledger room. Mara had copied there for two years, nights, when the clerks went home and the lamps burned low and no one counted the hours of a person whose name appeared in the wage book only as a number. But night work was given in silence, without orders, without names spoken aloud. Day work under supervision was different. Day work meant someone wanted to watch.

Mara climbed the stairs to the third floor with their heart beating slow and heavy, the way it did underwater, when every movement had to be measured against the air left in the lungs. The corridor smelled of ink and cold tea and the ghost of Elias Thorn's cough—though that last was surely imagination, because Thorn's desk had been cleared out eleven days ago, his chair gone, his floorboard pried up and replaced with new wood so pale it glowed like a scar among the old boards.

Hale was already there.

He stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the harbor as though it owed him money. He wore gray today, gray coat, gray waistcoat, the color of weather coming, and when he turned his head the lamplight caught the planes of his face and made them look carved rather than born. He smiled. He always smiled, and the smile always arrived a half-second after the rest of his expression, like thunder after lightning, so that you saw the pleasant thing coming and could do nothing about it.

"Vane." He said the name the way a man tests a coin between his teeth. "You know the fleet ledgers."

It was not a question, but Mara answered it anyway, because silence could be read as knowledge or ignorance depending on what the reader wanted. "I copy what I'm given, sir."

"What you're given." Hale nodded slowly, as if this were wisdom. "That's the whole of service, isn't it. Copying what you're given." He crossed the room and drew out a stack of folios from the cabinet marked *FLEET — 1893–96*, and set them on the long table beneath the lamp. "The old volumes are deteriorating. Water damage. Mice. You know how paper suffers." His smile arrived. "The company wants clean copies made. Every entry transcribed fresh. Nothing lost."

Nothing lost. Mara looked at the folios and understood, with the cold clarity of deep water, exactly what was being asked. Not copying. Erasing. Page by page, in a fair hand, the fleet records would be rewritten—and whatever did not survive the transcription would simply cease to have ever been written down. Paper suffered. Mice suffered. History happened elsewhere, to people with surnames that mattered, and if no page recorded a thing then the thing had never happened at all.

"I'll need the original entries kept beside me," Mara said. "For accuracy."

"Of course." Hale laid a hand flat on the topmost folio, and for a moment he did not move, and the room was very quiet except for the tick of the clock over the door and the far-off cry of the gulls. "Accuracy," he said softly, "is everything."

Then he pulled a second chair to the table—his own chair, set three feet from Mara's, close enough that he could read the page upside down without leaning—and sat down in it, and folded his hands, and waited.

* * *

The first volume opened to March of 1894, and Mara began to write.

The work itself was simple, which was the trap of it. Transcription is a kind of sleep; the hand learns the shapes and the mind goes elsewhere, and Mara had spent two years cultivating exactly that elsewhere-ness, that clerk's trance in which the eyes pass over figures without weighing them. Now they could not afford the trance. Now every line demanded double attention: enough to copy it faithfully onto the new page, and enough to hold it somewhere deeper, in the place behind the place, where memory keeps things the body doesn't know it's carrying.

Because Hale was watching. Because Hale had ordered these copies made. Because a man does not sit three feet away for six hours to supervise a fair hand unless he is hunting something, and the something was either a page or a person, and Mara intended to be neither.

So: discipline. Mara gave themselves to the work the way they gave themselves to a dive—one breath held, one motion at a time, panic drowned before it could rise. The nib whispered. The lamp hissed. Outside the window the light moved from pewter to silver to a thin washed gold, and still the pen moved.

And underneath the copying, the memorizing.

*14 March 1894. Fleet disposition, eastern grounds. Nine vessels. Provisioning accounts, Vesper, Cormorant, Lark's Daughter.* Mara wrote the words and took them in like breath taken in before a dive, filling the chest, holding. *Insurance renewal, Halloway underwriters, premium doubled on hulls east of the shoal line.* Doubled. Why doubled? Who doubles insurance on hulls in waters they intend to fish peacefully?

*22 April 1894. Harbormaster's fee, special services rendered, forty pounds, entered as 'channel survey.'*

Forty pounds. A channel survey. Mara's pen did not pause—it could not pause, Hale's eyes were on the page—but inside their ribs something turned over slowly, like a body shifting in shallow water. Forty pounds paid to the harbormaster in the spring of 1894, and in the autumn of 1894 the rival fleet had gone down in a storm that came too fast, and the storm signal flags had never been raised. Petrel had said so. Petrel had been on the quay that night and sworn it until his voice cracked: no flags, no bells, the hoist empty in the wind while men screamed from the water.

Forty pounds for a channel survey. Mara copied the figure in a fair, obedient hand, and carried it down into the dark place, and let it sink.

* * *

By the fourth day Mara understood the shape of the thing they were doing.

Hale did not sit idle. He read correspondence, sometimes. He sharpened pencils with a small bone-handled knife, the shavings curling down onto a saucer. Twice he left the room for ten minutes at a stretch, and both times Mara heard his boots stop just outside the door before continuing down the corridor—he was testing whether the pen would stop when he left, whether the copyist would seize the chance to turn back pages, to compare, to search. Both times Mara's pen never faltered. Let him find nothing. Let the trance be perfect.

But the third time he left—the third time, on the sixth day, when the folio lay open at August of 1895 and the lamp had been lit against the early dusk—Mara's pen stopped.

Not because they chose it. Because the page demanded it.

*9 August 1895. Roster, dive crews, eastern grounds. Forty-one names engaged.*

Forty-one names. And below them, in Thorn's small precise hand—for these were Thorn's entries, Thorn had kept the fleet books for years, Thorn had written down every name and then, perhaps, spent the rest of his life learning what his own ink had been worth—below the names, a single line:

*Wages suspended per crew loss. See destruction report, storm of 11 August.*

Storm of 11 August. Mara knew that date the way they knew their own heartbeat. Their father had sailed on the 8th. The letter in Mara's memory—burned now, burned weeks ago, every word of it living only in the dark place behind the place—said otherwise than *storm*. Said *bribed*. Said *scuttled*. Said *forty divers*, and named Joren Vane among them.

And here was the roster. Here were the names, forty-one of them, in Thorn's careful hand, each one a person with lungs and a family and a grave that was the sea's to give or withhold.

Mara looked at the list and did the arithmetic of grief. Forty-one engaged. The testimony said forty drowned. One had lived, then—or come ashore late, or never dived that day. Petrel. It had to be Petrel, the old man who told stories on the quay, who swore about the flags, who had covered for Mara at roll-call and was arrested for it, who even now sat in the company lockup on a charge of theft that everyone on the docks knew was a hook baited for someone else.

Forty-one names. Mara read them once at the speed of sight, and then again at the speed of memory, taking each one down separately, the way you take down pearls from a string one at a time so that losing the string loses nothing. *Aldous Brent. Tomas Ferrow. Joren Vane.* There it was. Their father's name in Thorn's handwriting, the letters neat and unhurried, *Joren Vane*, wages due to next of kin, amount struck through, *paid*—paid, the ledger said, paid to the estate, and there had been no estate, there had been a nine-year-old child indentured the same month to the same company that had drowned him, and the debt of his gear had been deducted from nine years of wages that were themselves a fiction—

The rage came up like blood in water.

Mara felt it rise, hot and total, a red bloom spreading through the chest, and the hand holding the pen shook, and one drop of ink fell from the nib and landed on the fresh page—a black star, blooming.

Boots in the corridor. Coming back.

Mara did not think. Thinking was too slow. The body moved the way it moved in bad water, on instinct older than instruction: the blotting sand came down, the page tilted, and with the edge of the sand spoon Mara worked the wet drop outward, feathered it, spread it thin across the margin where a copyist's stray mark was nothing, was routine, was the honest accident of a tired hand. By the time the door opened, the star had become a smudge, and the smudge was drying, and Mara's face was arranged into the blank diligence of a person thinking of nothing but letterforms.

Hale paused in the doorway. He stood there a moment longer than a man needs to stand in a doorway, his gray eyes going from the lamp to the page to Mara's face, reading it the way he read everything, slowly, with that smile assembling itself half a beat behind.

"You've blotted," he observed.

"The sand was damp, sir. I'll recopy the page."

"No need." He came back to his chair and sat, and laced his fingers, and regarded the smudge with something almost like tenderness. "A little weather in the record. Fitting, don't you think? These books have seen their share."

Mara bent to the work. "Yes, sir."

But inside, where the rage still burned, Mara made a decision that felt less like a decision than like a bone setting itself: *they will not erase what I carry.* Let Hale burn every page in the building. Let him feed the folios to the boiler and melt the type and salt the ash. Forty-one names now lived in a place he could not inventory. Aldous Brent. Tomas Ferrow. Joren Vane. Each one taken down and stored, and the storing was a kind of act, was maybe the only kind left, and Mara understood suddenly why Thorn had spent years leaving his desk unlocked, testing, watching—because a man who knows what his ink has done will spend anything to put it right, and the only safe vault in Saltmere is another person's memory.

They were erasing the fleet. Very well. Mara would be the fleet.

* * *

The days found their rhythm, and the rhythm was a discipline, and the discipline was armor.

Up at dawn. Porridge eaten standing. The stairs to the third floor, the lamp, the folios, the watching presence three feet away, gray as weather. Ten hours of transcription. The nib worn to a slant by midday, re-cut with Hale's own knife when he offered it—offered it handle-first, smiling, watching how the fingers took it. Evening dismissed with a nod. Supper in the hall among the other indentured, who had learned not to ask Mara questions since the roll-call, since Petrel, since the searches. Then the dormitory, the blanket, the dark, and the review.

The review was the part no one could see. Lying still under rough wool while the barracks breathed around them, Mara walked the pages again, line by line, checking the holdings the way a diver checks the lines before descending. *Harbormaster's fee, special services, forty pounds, channel survey.* *Premium doubled on hulls east of the shoal line.* *Roster, forty-one names.* *Destruction report, storm of 11 August, entered by E. Thorn, witnessed by R. Callow, countersigned A. Halloway.* Countersigned by the founder himself. The founder's own signature above the word *storm*, and the testimony saying otherwise, and Mara holding both versions in one skull like two tides meeting, and standing steady in the rip.

Some nights the weight of it was enormous. Some nights Mara lay awake feeling the names pressing outward against the walls of their chest, forty-one souls plus a testimony plus a forged manifest plus a bribed boatswain's route plus Sable's blood on the quay boards—Sable, whom Mara had not seen in nineteen days, whose cart rotted at its mooring, whose fate was a stone Mara carried in the same pocket as the seed pod sewn into the coat lining. Some nights the discipline cracked and grief came through the crack, and Mara lay with their eyes open in the dark and let it, briefly, the way you let a wave break over you because fighting it underwater is how divers drown.

Then dawn again. Stairs. Lamp. Pen.

And something else grew in those days, something Mara noticed only around the ninth day, working through the winter volumes of 1895: a strange, cold steadiness. The rage had not gone. It had changed state, the way water changes state, from flood to ice, and ice can be built with. Every falsified figure Mara copied—every *storm* where the truth said *scuttled*, every *paid* where nothing had ever been paid—was also a measurement of the lie, exact to the penny, and the accumulating precision was a weapon being forged one stroke at a time. When the inquiry came—if it came, when it came—Mara would not need the burned letter. Mara *was* the letter. They could stand in any room in front of any magistrate and reproduce the fraud line by line, date by date, signature by signature, and no counter-signature of Hale's could unpick it, because it was not written anywhere. It was written in the only place the company had never learned to search.

On the eleventh day, Hale brought the last volume.

He set it on the table with unusual care, and Mara saw at once that it was thinner than the others, and older, its spine repaired with a strip of leather a shade too dark, and when it fell open the pages exhaled a smell of mildew and salt, as though the book itself had been fished out of the sea.

"This one," Hale said, "is damaged worst of all. Water got into the strongroom some years back. Copy what remains legible." He smiled his arriving smile. "Whatever remains legible."

Mara turned the first leaf. The ink had run in places, the entries blooming into gray ghosts of themselves. But the opening pages were clear enough, and they were dated September of 1894, and the heading read:

*Salvage and recovery accounts — wreck of the independent fleet.*

Recovery accounts. Someone had gone down to the wrecks afterward. Someone had itemized what was brought up. Mara's eyes moved down the columns—hull fittings, cargo pearls, dive weights—and then stopped, and the stopping was a soundless thing, a held breath, because there in the middle of the salvage ledger, entered in Thorn's hand with the same terrible neatness as everything else, was a line that read:

*One diving bell, brass, recovered from the Vesper. Sold to Halloway marine works. Note: seams filed, valve altered prior to sinking. Entered at scrap value.*

*Seams filed. Valve altered. Prior to sinking.*

Proof. Physical, dated, countersigned proof, sitting in a book that Hale had personally carried into this room and asked to have copied—asked to have copied *whatever remained legible*—and Mara understood in one vertiginous instant that Hale did not know what this page said. That the fixer had never read the old salvage volumes closely, or had trusted that water had done his work for him, or had simply grown careless in the confidence that no copyist alive could read a page and forget it.

The pen hovered. Three feet away, Hale turned a page of his correspondence, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world.

Mara thought of Petrel in the lockup. Of Sable's blood dried black between the quay boards. Of forty names spoken into water. Of a father who had taught a child to hold breath and hold truth and hold on.

The pen came down.

Mara copied the line entire—*seams filed, valve altered prior to sinking*—in a fair, obedient, invisible hand, and blotted it, and sanded it, and carried it down into the dark place where the fleet lived now, and turned the page.

Outside the window, far out past the harbor gate, a ship's bell rang the change of tide. Two more days, the dock talk said. Two more days and the Morning Tide would sail on the morning ebb, with a magistrate aboard who had already received one deposition, and was waiting—so the whispers ran along the quay like current along a hull—for a witness willing to stand and speak.

Mara dipped the pen, and kept writing, and kept remembering, and waited for the tide.
