## 19. A Bargain with the Boatswain

The Morning Tide rode at her moorings like a woman pretending not to listen.

Mara had watched her for two days from the sorting sheds, learning her rhythms the way they'd learned the tides: which ropes her crew touched and when, where the officers came and went, how long the boatswain lingered at the rail each evening with his pipe going cold in his fist while the harbor lamps came up one by one along the quay. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard the color of old rope and hands that moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had spent thirty years making rope and canvas obey him. His name, Mara learned from Petrel's endless gossip of the docks, was Odo Merrick, and he was known to take payment for small kindnesses—a crate shifted ahead of its turn, a berth found for a sleeping deckhand—and known equally well never to speak afterward of what he'd been paid for.

That was the whole of his reputation. It would have to be enough.

* * *

Mara counted the pearls out on the third night, alone in the drying loft above the sorting sheds, by the light of a shuttered lantern turned low.

They were not beautiful, these pearls. That was the first lesson of the trade and the last one too: the pearls that fetched coin at Halloway's counters were the misfits, the off-round, the ones with a bruise of gray or a wrinkle near the nacre that no stringing could hide. The perfect ones went into company ledgers under company names. What slipped through the cracks—what divers palmed when the tally-man's back was turned, what Mara had gathered over ten years in increments smaller than forgiveness—was the flawed stock, sold piecemeal to buyers who didn't ask where beauty went to be imperfect.

Forty-one pearls in all. Mara laid them out in rows on the floorboards and looked at them for a long time.

It was everything. Not everything in the world—Petrel would have laughed at that, said a person's everything ought to weigh more than an apple—but everything Mara had. Ten years of held breath and cold water and fingers numb past feeling, converted at last into something portable. Half of it had already gone to the prison guard for the message to Petrel. This remainder was what stood between Mara and nothing at all.

They thought of their father then—not the drowned man of the ledgers, but Joren Vane as he had been in the mornings before a dive, cracking his knuckles one hand against the other, saying the same thing he always said: *The sea doesn't keep score, little fish. She only keeps what sinks.*

Well. The sea had kept plenty. And now Mara meant to make the land keep a little of it back.

They sewed the pearls into a chamois pouch, and the pouch into the seam of a second packet wrapped in oilcloth beside the first—the harbormaster's name written on it in Thorn's careful clerk's hand, copied letter-perfect from the draft confession Mara had found folded inside the man's spare shirt. Two proofs, or none. That was the arithmetic now. One letter told the story; the second made it impossible to call the storyteller a liar. The magistrate across the strait would have the retired harbormaster's own mouth or he would have nothing but the word of a dead clerk and an indentured diver, and dead clerks and indentured divers were exactly the kind of witnesses the world had learned to disbelieve.

Mara wrapped both packets, tucked the decoy knowledge of their weight into their palms until the weight felt ordinary, and put out the lantern.

* * *

Finding Merrick alone was easier than it should have been, which itself was a kind of information. On the fourth evening the Tide's officers went ashore together—they always went ashore together, Thursdays, to the Widow Kess's rooms above the chandlery, if dock gossip could be trusted, and dock gossip about officers' vices was always trustworthy—and left the ship to her skeleton crew and the fog coming off the water in slow white sheets.

Mara crossed the quay with a basket on their arm, because a person carrying a basket was a person with a reason, and reasons were the cheapest disguise in Saltmere. The gangway watch nodded them through without a second look; cargo workers came and went at all hours preparing for the morning sailing, and one more bent-backed figure with a basket raised no more curiosity than a gull.

Merrick was at the rail, aft, where he always stood. Pipe unlit tonight. The fog had gotten into his beard and hung there in beads.

"Boatswain." Mara kept their voice level, pitched under the wind. "You're Odo Merrick."

He looked down at them the way he must have looked at green hands on their first day aboard—measuring, mildly amused, entirely unimpressed. "I'm the man who's about to tell you passengers board at dawn with the rest of them, so whatever speech you've got, shorten it."

"It's not a speech." Mara set the basket on the capstan between them. "It's a question. How much to carry a packet ashore across the strait tomorrow and put it into a particular pair of hands?"

Something shifted in the boatswain's face—not surprise, exactly. More like a door opening onto a room he already knew the shape of. "And what particular hands would those be?"

"The harbormaster's. The old one. Aldous Crane, retired, lives past the point at Greywater." Mara had the name ready and let it sit in the air like bait on a hook, still, patient. "There'll be a reply expected. You'd bring it back before the Tide sails on the morning tide."

Merrick took his time. He knocked the dead pipe against the rail, once, twice, and Mara watched him do it and understood that every motion of this man was a price being calculated. "Crossing and back is four hours' row in open water," he said at last. "Assuming weather. Assuming nobody asks why the boatswain of a Halloway-chartered vessel is calling on a harbormaster who hasn't held office since before you were weaned." He looked at the basket. "Assuming the packet isn't going to come back on me with constables tied to it like barnacles."

"It won't."

"That's what everyone says right up until the barnacles." He leaned on the rail, considering Mara with new attention, the way a man considers a knot he can't immediately identify. "You're one of Halloway's own. Shed girl. I've seen you hauling baskets." His eyes narrowed slightly. "Vane, isn't it? The divers used to say that name. Before my time mostly, but it carried."

Mara kept their face still. Inside, something lurched—their father's name, spoken aloud on this deck, over this water, casual as weather. *It carried.* Of course it had carried. Names did that; they traveled further than the people who owned them.

"You've got me confused with somebody else," they said. "I'm nobody."

"Nobody pays better than somebody, in my experience." Merrick straightened. "Show me what's in the basket, then, since we're being nobodies together."

* * *

Mara lifted out the oilcloth bundle and set it down between them, and for a moment neither of them touched it. The fog moved around the masthead like something breathing.

"Papers," Mara said. "Testimony. For a magistrate."

Merrick's eyebrows rose a fraction. "That's a large word, testimony."

"It's not your word. You don't need it. You need to know three things." Mara held up fingers, the way Thorn had once taught them to structure a ledger entry—item, quantity, value—as if even bribery were bookkeeping, which perhaps it was. "One: what's inside can't hurt you unless you read it, so don't read it. Two: if it goes astray, no one will ever trace it to you, because no one knows you have it except me, and I'll be gone. Three—" and here Mara opened the chamois pouch and let the pearls catch the lantern light from the companionway, forty-one small moons spilled onto the oilcloth, gray and off-round and worth more than the boatswain earned in a season—"three: I pay half now and half on delivery."

The silence that followed was long enough for Mara to hear their own pulse in their ears, and beneath it, far off, the groan of the Tide's hull working against her mooring lines, patient as everything in this harbor.

Merrick didn't reach for the pearls. That frightened Mara more than reaching would have. Honest greed would have been easy to read; this stillness was a man doing sums with bigger numbers than pearls.

"Halloway charters this vessel," he said finally. "Which means anything happens to anything on this deck, I answer to Corvus Hale. And you know who Hale is." It wasn't a question. Everyone knew who Hale was. "So understand what you're asking. You're asking me to bet my berth, maybe my neck, against—" he nodded at the pearls, still not touching them "—that. Which is fine money. But it's fine money I can count, versus trouble I can't."

"I know." Mara made their voice steady. "That's why I'm not asking you to carry anything of mine."

"No?"

"No. You're carrying freight. A parcel, addressed and sealed, delivered to a named party, receipt requested—that's not smuggling, boatswain, that's just the post, done badly. If anyone asks, you were running an errand for a fare who paid passage-money for special delivery. People do it. You've done it. Half the harbor has seen you do it." Mara paused, letting the shape of the lie settle between them like ballast. "What's in the parcel is between the sender and the receiver. You're just the tide that carries it. Tides don't get questioned."

Something flickered at the corner of Merrick's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go on a man who'd forgotten the route. "Tides don't get hanged, either," he said. "Usually."

"Usually is the best odds anyone gets in Saltmere."

He laughed then, short and low, a sound like rope paying out, and Mara felt the bargain turn. It wasn't the laugh of a man persuaded—it was the laugh of a man who had already decided and was covering the decision with humor, the way sailors covered superstition with jokes. But it told Mara which way the wind sat.

"Half now," Merrick said, and held out his hand.

* * *

They transacted the rest quickly, professionally, two people doing business in the only language the harbor respected. Mara counted twenty pearls into his palm—twenty, keeping twenty-one back, because trust was a thing you paid for in installments—and Merrick weighed them in his hand with a jeweler's indifference, then dropped them into a leather purse that clinked heavier than a boatswain's purse had any right to.

"The packet goes in my sea chest," he said. "Nobody opens my chest, not even the mate, not even Hale's people if they come sniffing—chest stays locked, key sleeps around my neck. At Greywater I go ashore alone to see my sister, which I do every crossing anyway, so there's your errand, plain as bread." He tapped the oilcloth bundle once with one blunt finger. "This goes to Crane himself. Not his housekeeper, not a neighbor. Himself. And I don't leave until I've watched him break the seal."

"He'll give you a reply."

"Then I bring the reply, and you bring me the other half, and we've never met." He looked out at the black water. "One thing more, Vane—or nobody, or whoever you are."

"Yes?"

"If this goes wrong—if Hale's people board before we sail, if there's a search—you walk away from me and you never look back and you never knew my name. I won't know yours either. Whatever happens after that happens to whichever of us it finds first." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reads the terms of a charter aloud. "Those are my rates. Take them or find another boat."

"I take them." And Mara found, saying it, that the words cost less than expected. There was a strange relief in a loyalty with no pretense attached. Sable had sworn friendship over stolen rum, had sworn *always*, and look where *always* lay now—abandoned somewhere behind checkpoints and silence and blood on quay boards that might or might not mean what Mara feared it meant. Merrick swore nothing at all. Merrick offered a transaction with clean edges, and clean edges were the only kind of trust Mara could afford anymore.

They shook on it. His hand swallowed theirs whole, dry and hard as wharf timber.

"One more thing," Mara said, gathering the empty basket. "The packet—there are names in it. Forty of them. Divers."

Merrick's face didn't change, but his hand, settling the bundle into his coat, slowed by half a heartbeat.

"My father's among them," Mara said. "Joren Vane."

For a moment the boatswain said nothing. Then: "The fleet that went down in the squall. 'Ninety-two." He was looking at Mara differently now—not softer, nothing so useful as softness, but with the particular attention of a man re-drawing a map. "My brother dove those beds. Starboard side of that fleet, some years before she sank. He'd have been on her if he hadn't lost his lung to a bad season." He settled his coat over the hidden packet. "He always said the storm came on too fast for honest weather."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere forward, a bell struck the half hour, muffled, counting down hours Mara couldn't afford to count.

"So you see," Mara said quietly, "you're not carrying freight either."

Merrick held their gaze for a long moment. Then he took out his pipe, packed it, and struck a match, and the small flame lit his face from below like something rising through dark water.

"Dawn," he said. "Be nowhere near this gangway when I go ashore. And Vane—" he exhaled smoke into the fog, watching it curl and vanish the way everything in this harbor eventually vanished, "—the other half better be real pearls."

"They're real. Everything I have is real. That's the trouble with it."

* * *

Mara walked back along the quay with the empty basket swinging, and the fog closed behind them like water closing over a dive, and for a hundred paces they allowed themselves to feel it—that fragile, terrifying lift, the sense of machinery beginning to move on bearings they had oiled themselves. Two packets now. Two roads across the strait. One letter in their own keeping and one in a stranger's sea chest, and by the next evening the magistrate would hold either two proofs or one, and one was enough, one had to be enough—

They stopped walking.

Because the thought arrived the way cold water arrives, all at once, through every seam: *the boatswain knows your name.* Not the one on the indenture rolls—everyone knew that one, it was worth nothing—but the other one. *Vane.* The name that carried. He'd pulled it out of the harbor's memory like a pearl from a shell, unprompted, unasked, and Mara had given him confirmation free of charge because grief had loosened their tongue, and now there existed a broad-shouldered man with a heavy purse and a locked sea chest who could sell that name for more than twenty pearls any day of the week.

Purchased loyalty. That was all this was. And purchased loyalty, Petrel liked to say, stayed bought exactly as long as the buying.

Mara stood in the fog with the empty basket and made themselves look at it plainly: they had just handed the second half of their proof to a man whose price they knew and whose heart they didn't, and there was no taking it back, no second packet, no reserve. If Merrick talked, the trap would close at Greywater before Crane ever broke a seal. If he didn't talk but simply failed—weather, accident, a sister who wasn't home—the plan would sail on the morning tide half-blind, and blind plans were how fleets ended up on harbor bottoms.

But the alternative was to carry everything themselves, and everything themselves was a single point of failure wearing their own coat. Thorn had taught them that much without meaning to: a ledger kept in one place is a ledger waiting to burn.

*Two proofs, or none,* Mara thought. *And I've just bet the second one on a man who smokes cheap tobacco.*

They walked on. Behind them, faint through the fog, they heard the Morning Tide's bell strike again, and beneath it—so faint they might have invented it—the sound of the gangway boards taking a weight, footsteps descending toward the quay. Steady. Unhurried. Coming their way.

Mara stepped into the shadow of the net-mending shed and stood very still, and watched the fog for what came next.
