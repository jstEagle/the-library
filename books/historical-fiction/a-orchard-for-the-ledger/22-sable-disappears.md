## 22. Sable Disappears

The fog came in off the strait that evening the way it always did in late autumn—thick as wool, smelling of kelp and coal smoke and the sour breath of a thousand gutted fish—and Mara stood at the mouth of Cooper's Alley with their collar up and their heart going like a drum in a parade they had not been invited to join.

Sable was late.

Sable was never late. That was the first thing anyone learned about Sable Ferrow: not that they were loyal, though they were; not that they were clever, though they were that too; but that they were *punctual* with the ferocity of someone who had once lost everything by being five minutes slow. Sable told the story often enough—how their mother had waited at a dock in Corvane for a boat that left early, how she'd sailed without them to a new life and a new name and had never sent for them—and every telling ended the same way, with Sable spitting into the harbor water and saying, *I'd rather be early to my own hanging than late to anything else.*

So when the fourth bell rang and the alley stayed empty, Mara told themselves it was the fog. Fog slowed carts. Fog made even the surest-footed smuggler take the long way around the broken quay boards near the old rope-walk. Fog was an excuse, and excuses were free, and Mara spent one like a miser spending a coin they could not afford.

They counted to two hundred. Then to four hundred. The gas lamp at the alley's mouth hissed and guttered, its light swallowed whole a hand's width from the glass, so that the darkness beyond seemed less like absence of light than like a substance—a thing with weight and appetite, pressing close against Mara's back, breathing on their neck.

*He knows,* said a voice inside them, small and cold and patient, the voice that had lived in Mara's chest since the night Thorn pressed the letter into their hands and whispered *the tide, the tide.* *Hale knows, and he has bought your friend, or he has broken your friend, or he has buried your friend under the new pilings where no one digs.*

Mara shut the voice away the way they shut away all the things that did not help: father's face going down into green water, mother coughing her life out in the company dormitory, the crossed-out entry in the ledger that read *VANE — debt settled by death.* There was no use feeding those things. They grew fat enough on their own.

But the alley stayed empty, and the fog kept coming, and at last Mara stepped out of the lamplight and went looking.

* * *

The route from Cooper's Alley down to the fish-quay took eleven minutes if you walked it honest and six if you took the cut-through behind the salt sheds, which everyone walked dishonest because honesty took too long in Saltmere. Mara took the cut-through, boots silent on the wet cobbles, hands deep in their pockets where the right one closed around the seed pod from their mother's tin—the dried husk of a fruit from trees that had been drowned before Mara could walk, papery and light as a dead moth, and worth more to them now than every pearl in the harbor.

The fish-quay at night was a country of ghosts. By day it roared—gulls screaming, knives flashing, women singing out prices in voices like gulls themselves—but at night it lay emptied and scrubbed only halfway, stinking of the day's slaughter, the scales and heads and tails that the tide would take or wouldn't. The lamps along the quay burned low and orange, each one wrapped in its own private cocoon of fog, islands of light in a drowned world.

And there, at the third berth, where Sable always parked the cart—

Mara stopped walking.

The cart stood alone.

It was wrong in a hundred small ways, the way a room is wrong after a theft, and Mara's eyes went over it detail by detail the way Thorn had taught them to go over a ledger page, hunting the entry that didn't balance. The mule was gone—Sable would never leave the mule, not with the harbor thieves working the quay like pickpockets at a fair. The canvas cover was thrown back crooked, half off one side, as if someone had flung it aside in a hurry or dragged it off. One wheel sat in a puddle, and the puddle was spreading, fed by something dripping from the bed of the cart, slow and steady, drip, drip, drip, into the black water below the quay's edge.

Fish blood, Mara told themselves. It was a fish cart. Fish bled. The whole quay bled; the stones were stained brown-black with decades of it; you couldn't walk the fish-quay without carrying its smell home in your clothes and its color home in your memory.

But the dripping came from the front of the cart bed, near the driver's bench, and fish were stored aft, and Sable was careful, Sable was *scrupulous*, Sable washed the cart down every evening while other smugglers laughed at them for it—*you'll drown us all in cleanliness, Ferrow*—because Sable said a clean cart passed checkpoints and a dirty cart got turned inside out by every bored customs man between here and the headland.

Mara made their feet move. Ten steps. Five. They put their hand on the cart's rail, and the wood was slick.

Not with water.

The blood on the bench was still tacky. Not a fish's blood either—fish blood runs thin and silver-pink and smells of the sea entire. This was dark, and thickening, and it smelled of iron and of the particular animal fear that Mara knew from the killing-sheds, from the days they'd worked the eel vats as a child, from every place in Saltmere where living things were emptied of themselves.

There wasn't much of it. That was the thing Mara's mind seized on and refused to release, turning it over and over like a pearl too good to be true. A pool meant a body left lying a long time. This was a smear, a handprint gone wrong, a splash across the boards and then drag-marks—drag-marks!—leading from the bench to the quay edge, where they stopped. No body. No body anywhere along the black lip of the harbor, and the water below gave nothing back but the orange smears of the lamps and the blind white face of the fog.

Someone had bled here. Someone had been dragged here. And then someone—or someones—had taken that someone away, quietly, in a city where quiet removals happened every week and were remarked upon by no one, because the removed were dock-rats and smugglers and indentured divers, people whose disappearances were not events but weather.

People like Sable. People like Mara.

Mara crouched at the quay edge and looked at the drag-marks until their eyes burned. Two sets of boot prints in the fish-slime, large ones, heavy-heeled, the kind of boots that cost money—the kind of boots that men like Corvus Hale wore, polished and dark and indifferent as coffins. And beside them, smaller, scuffed, unmistakable to anyone who had watched Sable walk a thousand miles of these same quays: Sable's boots, toes turned slightly outward, the left heel worn down from an old injury Sable joked about and never explained.

The small prints led to the edge and stopped. The large prints led away toward the customs house road, unhurried, side by side, two men walking easy, carrying something between them or nothing at all.

Mara knelt there in the fog with the blood drying under their fingers and felt the world tilt, slowly, the way the harbor floor tilts under a diver who has come up too fast.

*Betrayal or capture?*

The question arrived whole, fully formed, wearing its own cold little coat, and it would not leave. Because there were two stories written here in slime and smear, and both of them fit, and Mara could read them both fluently, and that was the torture of it.

In the first story, Hale's men had taken Sable. Taken them quietly off the quay, dragged them bleeding to wherever Hale did his work—in the cellars under the customs house, people said, though no one said it above a whisper—and were asking their questions now, the patient questions, the questions that did not need to be loud because Sable was not a man who needed to be broken so much as *reminded*: reminded of the reward, reminded of the difference between loyalty and suicide, reminded that a person on a rack will say anything at all, including the true thing, especially the true thing. In this story Sable was brave or Sable was broken, and either way the letter's route through the harbor gate was blown, and every hour Mara spent kneeling here was an hour Hale spent tightening the net.

In the second story—and this one slid into Mara's mind with the horrible smoothness of a key into a well-oiled lock—in the second story, Sable had never come to the meeting at all. Sable had gone instead to whoever paid best, or to whoever frightened worst, and had sold the meeting itself: the place, the hour, the fact of it. And what Mara knelt beside now was not evidence of capture but evidence of *payment collected*—or of payment interrupted, a deal gone wrong, a buyer who wanted the goods and not the middleman. Blood either way. Treachery either way. And in this story the letter was already in Hale's soft white hands, and Mara was already a dead thing walking, and the only question left was how many hours of being alive they had left to spend.

Two stories. One bloodstain. And no way—not any way in the world—to know which one was true.

That was the design of it, Mara thought suddenly, and the thought went through them like a hook through the cheek of a landed fish. That was what Hale *did*. Everyone in Saltmere knew the shape of Corvus Hale's work even if they'd never seen the work itself: he did not merely remove people, he removed *certainty*. He left the city full of stories that contradicted each other, so that friends suspected friends, so that wives wondered about husbands, so that no one could trust the ground under their own feet. He didn't need to catch you. He only needed you to believe you might already be caught, and then you caught yourself—you ran, you burned your papers, you fled to the boats, you surfaced exactly where his nets waited.

Mara stood up. Their knees cracked. Somewhere out on the black water a ship's bell rang the half-hour, muffled by fog, sounding like a bell rung underwater, like a bell tolling for the drowned.

*Think,* they told themselves, in Thorn's voice, in the dry ledger-room voice of a dying man teaching a slave to read numbers. *What does the page actually say? Not what you fear it says. What it says.*

What it said: Sable had missed the meeting. Sable missed nothing. There was blood, little of it, fresh, human. There were heavy boots leaving unhurried. There was no body, and Hale's men did not leave bodies on public quays—they were tidier than that, prouder than that. If they had wanted Sable dead as a message, the body would have been arranged somewhere Mara would find it, posed like a sentence in a threatening letter. This was not a message. This was a *removal*.

And if Sable had betrayed them—if Sable had sold the meeting—then why the blood? Why the drag-marks? A traitor walks away from a betrayal on his own feet, counting his coin. Only a captive leaves a stain like this.

Unless the blood was theater. Unless Hale had staged it, knowing Mara would come, knowing Mara would kneel here reading the scene like scripture, and had staged it precisely ambiguous, precisely to plant the twin worms of doubt and terror—

*Stop.* Mara pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark. This was the trap inside the trap: a mind that could imagine every possibility would eventually obey the worst one. Thorn had warned them of this too, in his last weeks, coughing blood into his handkerchief and smiling his gray smile: *A forged page works not because it is perfect but because the reader's fear finishes it for you. Don't be the reader, Vane. Be the writer.*

Be the writer. All right. Then write forward.

If Sable was taken, the questioning had begun tonight and would continue tomorrow and the day after, and Sable—stubborn, bitter, funny, loyal Sable, who had sworn loyalty over stolen rum with rum-blazing eyes and had never once asked Mara for anything except the truth—Sable would hold for a day, maybe two. Long enough. The Morning Tide sailed on the morning tide after next, magistrate aboard, and if the second packet reached the harbormaster and the first testimony reached the magistrate, then whatever Sable said afterward would be ash in Hale's mouth.

If Sable had betrayed them—then the letter was lost anyway, and burning it or keeping it made no difference, and despair was simply a waste of the hours remaining.

Either way, the answer was the same. Either way, the plan did not change. Either way, Mara Vane walked off this quay and finished what they had started, because the forty divers in the ledger—including Joren Vane, pearl diver, drowned twice, once in water and once in ink—deserved more than a girl kneeling in fish-slime, paralyzed by the arithmetic of fear.

But oh, it hurt. That was the thing no one told you about suspicion, Mara thought, walking fast now, cutting away from the quay, taking the long dark streets toward the lodging-house with their head down and their breath smoking: that suspecting a friend was its own wound, whether or not the suspicion proved true. Because if Sable had held, then Mara had spent this whole walk doubting them, and that doubt could never be entirely given back. And if Sable had sold them, then the last warm thing in Mara's cold life had been a lie with a laugh attached to it, and there was no recovering from that, not ever, not in any port, in any life.

The fog thickened. The streets emptied. Behind Mara, the fish-quay lamps shrank to smudges of orange, and the cart stood alone at the third berth, dripping its slow drip into the harbor, keeping its secret the way the harbor kept all of them—the fathers, the fleets, the forty names, the truth at the bottom of the green water where it had lain for ten years, waiting for someone stubborn enough to dive for it.

* * *

The lodging-house door was ajar.

Mara saw it from across the street and stopped in the shadow of a salt shed, every muscle locking. Their room was on the third floor, at the back, over the stable roof. The landlady locked that door at the eighth bell and slept with the key under her pillow and snored like a landslide; Mara had timed her rounds for three nights running before ever bringing the letter inside. The door did not stand ajar. The door had never once stood ajar.

For a long moment Mara simply breathed, watching the dark slot of the doorway, letting the fog bead and freeze on their eyelashes. They thought about the seed pod in their pocket. They thought about the deed sewn into their coat lining, their mother's careful stitches, the only inheritance the Halloway Company had never managed to steal. They thought about the words of Thorn's testimony, every line of them, lodged in memory now deeper than ink, deeper than paper, in the place where a person keeps the things that cannot be searched.

Then they crossed the street.

Inside, the hall smelled of tallow and wet wool. The stairs creaked their familiar complaints, step by step, and Mara climbed them softly, listening past their own heartbeat. On the third-floor landing the air was wrong—there was a smell beneath the tallow, faint but unmistakable, the smell of the killing-sheds again, of iron, and under it something else, something that stopped Mara's breath in their throat:

Rum. Cheap harbor rum, the kind Sable drank, the kind they'd sworn their oath over.

On the floor of the landing, just outside Mara's door, half-hidden by the shadow of the stairwell, lay a small object. Mara picked it up with fingers that no longer shook—they noticed distantly that the shaking had stopped, that fear had burned through trembling into something harder and colder and clearer.

It was Sable's knife. The bone-handled one, the one they used for everything—for gutting fish, for whittling, for the little carved figures they sold to sailors' children. The blade was clean. But the handle was wrapped around its middle with a length of tarred twine, tied in a knot Mara knew, because Sable had shown them it once, laughing, drunk on stolen rum: *a smuggler's knot, Vane. We tie it when we're taken against our will. Any of our people finds it, they know to run and not look back.*

Sable had left it here. Which meant Sable had been brought here—dragged here, bleeding, from the quay—and in the last seconds of whatever freedom remained to them, bound and beaten and certain of worse to come, Sable had thought not of escape, not of bargaining, not of the reward Hale dangled, but of this: a warning, tied in twine, left on the floor where only one person in the world would understand it.

*Run and don't look back.*

Mara stood in the dark hallway holding the knife, and the tears that came were hot and furious and brief, and when they were done, Mara wiped their face on their sleeve, opened their door, and began packing.

Because the knot said *run*—but Sable had tied it wrong. Deliberately wrong. Mara saw it now, turning the knife in the lamplight of their room, seeing everything with terrible clarity: the knot was mirrored, reversed, the way you'd tie it if you wanted the finder to know it was a message but not the message itself. *Run* was what Hale's men would expect the knot to mean. The reversal said something else, something Sable had gambled everything on Mara being clever enough to read:

*I'm alive. I'm held. Come get me—or finish it without me. Either way, don't stop.*

Below, far off toward the customs house, a whistle blew—two short notes and a long one, the watch-change signal—and Mara blew out their candle and stood in the dark with the knife in one hand and the seed pod in the other, counting the hours until dawn, and the morning tide after that, and trying not to hear, underneath the fog and the bells and the blood still on their memory, the sound of a friend's voice saying the true thing one last time before the questions began:

*The tide, Vane. Whatever happens—the tide.*
