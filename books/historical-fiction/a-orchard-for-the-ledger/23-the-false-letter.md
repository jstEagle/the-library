## 23. The False Letter

Dawn came to Saltmere the way it always did—gray first, then the color of old pewter, then a thin, reluctant gold that never quite reached the lower streets. Mara watched it come through the gap in the shutters of their dormitory cot, having not slept, having perhaps slept in fragments too small to call sleep, and tried to decide what the light was telling them.

It was telling them nothing. Light was honest that way. It only showed what was already there.

The knife lay on the blanket where they had held it through the dark hours. The seed pod sat beside it, curled like a sleeping hand. And beneath the loose board under the cot—the third one from the wall, the one whose nail-head they had loosened with a spoon handle over four patient nights—lay the packet.

They had not opened it yet. All night they had told themselves this was caution: open it in daylight, when a candle's glow wouldn't show through the shutter gaps, when the watch-change would have turned over twice more and any ears pressed to doors would belong to the day shift's indifferent snoring. But underneath the caution, in the cellar of themselves where they kept the things they didn't say even to their own reflection, Mara had known the truth.

They were afraid of what the packet would be.

* * *

They waited until the bell rang the half hour after breakfast, when the dormitory emptied and the fog outside thickened enough to blur the windows into milk. Then they knelt on the cold boards, worked the loose plank up with two fingers, and drew out the oilcloth packet Sable had carried through the harbor gate two nights ago—the packet that had crossed the checkpoint inside a fish cart between mackerel and ice, the packet that had been meant for the customs house runner who would pass it to the Morning Tide's second mate, the packet that was supposed to be halfway to freedom by now.

Except Sable hadn't delivered it. Sable had vanished. And the cart had been found abandoned at the quay with blood on the boards, and Mara had spent yesterday in the numb mechanical work of sorting pearls while every face in the sheds seemed to be watching them, and last night they had found this packet where Sable must have hidden it before disappearing—tucked into the hollow of the breakwater stone, the old dead-drop place, wrapped exactly as Mara had wrapped it, sealed exactly as Mara had sealed it.

Which should have been a mercy. The letter hadn't fallen into Hale's hands—or so it appeared. But it hadn't reached the ship either. It sat here, in no one's possession but theirs, which meant it had passed through the worst hours of Saltmere unheld by any friend.

Mara weighed it in their palms. Heavy enough. Sealed enough. The oilcloth bore the same grease-spots their own hands had left on it.

They broke the seal.

* * *

Blank pages.

Not torn pages, not damaged pages—*blank* pages. Five sheets of good ledger paper, cut to the same size as Thorn's testimony, folded the same way, smelling faintly of the same tallow they'd used to soften the wax. Nothing written on them at all. No water damage, no smearing, no desperate half-erased words. Whoever had made this packet had never needed to destroy anything. They had simply copied its shape.

Mara sat back on their heels and the dormitory seemed to tilt around them, the cots and trunks and sleeping-shelves drifting like flotsam.

A decoy. Someone had known about the letter—known its size, its wrapping, its weight—and built a twin. And the twin was here, in the dead-drop, meaning the real packet had been taken from Sable or given up by Sable, and what Mara was holding was either evidence of torture or evidence of betrayal, and there was no way to know which, and both possibilities ended with the same question screaming through them like water through a holed hull:

*Where is the real letter?*

Their hands went to their coat before their mind finished the thought—hands faster than fear, faster than reason, diving into the inner lining the way their father had taught them to dive, straight down, no wasted motion. The seam above the left hip. The thread they'd sewn with waxed sail-twine three nights ago, working by feel in the dark because sewing by lamplight invited eyes.

The deed was there. The folded paper of the orchard deed, soft as cloth from ten years of hiding, their mother's careful handwriting gone brown along the creases. And beside it, in its own little pocket of linen, the dried seed pod, light as a moth.

But the letter—

The letter. The actual letter. Elias Thorn's dying testimony in his own clerk's hand, naming the bribed harbormaster, naming the scuttled fleet, naming forty divers including Joren Vane. Where had Mara put it? Not in the packet—they remembered sealing the decoy question differently now, remembered the night at Sable's loft over stolen rum when they'd made the arrangement, remembered—

They closed their eyes. Made themselves see it. This was the discipline Thorn had taught them without meaning to: a ledger room teaches you that memory is the only book no one can seize. *Copy it until you can close your eyes and read it.*

Three nights ago. The loft above the chandlery. Rain on the roof like applause, like static, like the sea trying to get in. Sable laughing low, saying *you're getting paranoid, Vane, that's how I know you're finally learning something.* And Mara sitting cross-legged on the floor with the letter open between them, reading it aloud once, all of it, because Sable had asked to hear the names—even that, even the names spoken into a friend's listening—and then folding it away.

And then. Then.

Then they had split it. No—that wasn't right either. They had *considered* splitting it, copying it so each of them carried half, and Sable had said no, too risky, two halves means two chances to be caught, keep it whole, keep it simple. And Mara had agreed. And then—

The memory surfaced slowly, the way drowned things surface, turning as they rose.

They had rewrapped the original that same night. And at the last moment, alone in the ledger room afterward, unable to sleep, unable to stop seeing Thorn's gray lips forming *the tide, the tide*, Mara had done something they had never told Sable about. They had taken a second piece of oilcloth and made a second packet, and they had put the real letter inside it, and they had sewn *it* into the coat—not the hip seam, not next to the deed, that was the obvious place, that was where anyone would cut first—but into the collar itself, flat against the back of the neck, between the outer cloth and the lining, stitched with stitches so fine they'd used a pearl needle and worked an hour for six inches of seam.

Hadn't they?

The terror now was not that the letter was lost. The terror was that they could not be certain. Memory was the only book no one could seize—but memory could drown you too, if you trusted a page you'd only imagined writing.

They stood up. Their legs felt borrowed. Outside, the fog pressed white against the glass, and somewhere below, the harbor bells counted the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

They walked to the cracked mirror nailed beside the washstand—the one luxury of vanity the company permitted its workers, a mirror so flawed it showed you mostly your own uncertainty. Mara turned their back to it and looked over their shoulder at the collar of the coat hanging on its hook.

From the outside: nothing. A collar like any other collar, salt-stiffened, mended at the point where years of wear had thinned it. No bulge. No shadow of paper. Even knowing, even staring, they could not see it.

But seeing was not knowing. Knowing required the thing in their hands.

* * *

They could not undress in the dormitory. That rule lived in their bones: never bare the skin or the coat where the company's walls stood. So they took the coat and went out into the fog, down the stairs that smelled of boiled cabbage and lamp oil, past the yard where the morning shift was forming into its silent lines, and out the gate with the practiced slouch of a worker going to the privies—a route that bent, as such routes always did in Saltmere, behind the net-drying racks, where the fog hung so thick the racks became a forest of gray bones.

No one followed. Probably. The word *probably* had become the load-bearing wall of their life.

Behind the racks, in the smell of hemp and brine, Mara took off the coat and turned it inside out and found the seam.

There. At the base of the collar, hidden in the line of stitching: the pearl needle's work, six inches of thread almost invisible against the weave. Their fingers shook so badly it took three tries to pick the knot loose, and the whole time they listened to the fog—to the bells, to the gulls, to the wet slap of water against pilings—for footsteps, for the particular unhurried tread of a man who smiled too long.

The seam opened.

Paper. Folded paper, soft-edged with warmth, real paper, and Mara drew it out into the gray light and unfolded it with the care of someone handling a living thing.

Elias Thorn's hand. The tight, upright script of a man who had spent thirty years making words that would hold up in court. *I, Elias Thorn, senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company, being of sound mind and failing body...*

The words swam. Mara blinked, and the fog got into their eyes, or their eyes got into the fog, and they read the first lines again, and again, until the swimming stopped and the words held still and true:

*I set down what I entered and was ordered to erase.*

Real. It was real. It had been with them the whole time—through the roll-calls, through the searches, through Hale's kindness and his long smile, through every moment since the night they'd made the false packet, sleeping in the very bend of their own neck while they mourned it as lost.

Mara laughed. One sound, short and awful, swallowed instantly by the fog. They folded the testimony along Thorn's own creases and put it back into the collar and began, with fingers steadier now, steadier because they had proof of their own mind, to sew the seam closed again.

And then the other thought arrived. The one that had been circling all along, waiting for relief to lower the gates.

*Sable.*

Sable had carried the decoy. Which meant Sable had swapped packets—or had been made to swap them, or had been searched without knowing it, or—no. No, the decoy was too good for accident. The blank pages matched the size, the fold, the seal. Someone had *made* it, and the only people who knew the letter well enough to make it were Mara and Sable, and Sable had sworn over stolen rum with salt water and rum both in their voice—

Or Sable had broken. Hale's methods were not rumors; they were curriculum. Everyone on the docks knew what happened in the back rooms of the customs annex. A person could hold fast through a beating and still give up everything under worse, and Mara had no right, none at all, to assume their friend's silence was bottomless when their own courage had never been tested past the edge of a knife.

*Betrayed or captured. Captured or betrayed.* The two possibilities circled each other like gulls over a kill, and Mara could not tell which bird would land.

But here was the thing they clung to, the plank in the flood: whoever had the decoy believed it was real. If Sable had sold them, Hale would have the decoy in his hands this minute—and he would believe he had won. He would be congratulating himself on paper that couldn't hang a fisherman. And if Sable had been taken without talking, if the swap had happened some other way—stolen from the cart, lifted from a hiding place by someone who'd watched too closely—then the thief also held blanks and might not yet know it.

Either way, the enemy was holding nothing, and did not know it. Either way, the truth rode in the collar of a worker's coat, invisible, unremarkable—which was, Mara thought, threading the needle, the safest place in Saltmere. The safest place had always been inside something the powerful never bothered to look at.

They finished the seam. They turned the coat right-side out and shrugged it on and felt the letter settle against their nape like a cold hand, like a blessing, like a debt.

* * *

The rest of the day they spent being ordinary, which was now the hardest work they did.

They sorted pearls. They ate the noon bread. They answered Petrel's absence with the lie they'd rehearsed—*she's laid up with her chest, I'm carrying her tally*—to the shed overseer, who grunted and wrote nothing down, and every time Mara said it they felt the shape of the cell Hale had built for Petrel, and hated the comfort of their own freedom a little more.

But underneath the ordinary, the new sense ran like a riptide: *nothing can be trusted except what is carried in the body.* Not the dead-drop—compromised. Not Sable's routes—unproven. Not friends, not seals, not oilcloth, not even the packet's own honest weight in the palm. Only the collar. Only the memorized pages behind their eyes. Only the names, which they now recited nightly like a rosary: Thorn's opening line, the harbormaster's name, the sum paid, the date of the storm that came too fast, and then the divers, all of them, Joren Vane among the thirty-ninth and fortieth lines, and Mara's own heartbeat keeping time.

Near dusk, crossing the quay with the last shift, Mara passed the spot where Sable's cart had been found. The blood had been scrubbed, but the boards were darker there, the way wood remembers. Mara did not slow down. Slowing down was a signature; everything now was a signature.

But at the corner of the chandlery, where Sable's loft window looked out over the street, something caught their eye. The shutters—always crooked, always hanging like a drunkard's jaw—were closed. Straight. Latched.

Sable never latched those shutters. Sable said a locked window was a confession that you had something worth taking.

Someone else was keeping Sable's loft now. Or someone was waiting in it.

Mara walked on without breaking stride, down toward the water, into the deepening blue of evening, while behind them the lamps of the chandlery came alight one by one, and in the loft window above, just visible past the shutter's edge, a thin line of candle-glow appeared—then went out, quickly, as if whoever held the flame had heard a footstep on the cobbles below and was, at this very moment, listening to theirs.

Mara kept walking. The letter pressed warm now against their neck, warmed by their own body, and they counted their steps all the way to the harbor gate and beyond, and made themselves a promise in the rhythm of it: *dawn comes, and the tide after it, and whatever is waiting in that loft will have to be faced with the truth still hidden and the plan still moving—because there is no other way forward but through, and no one left to carry it but me.*
