## 24. Memorized and Burned

The searches came like weather after that—unpredictable, frequent, and impossible to argue with.

The first was a formality. Two company men moved through the women's dormitory with the bored efficiency of men counting barrels, lifting mattresses, running fingers along window ledges. Mara stood in the row with the others, hands folded, eyes down, wearing the face they had worn since age nine: the face of a person with nothing worth taking. It had always worked before. It had been, for ten years, the most reliable thing in their life.

But now there *was* something. It sat against their ribs at that very moment, folded into a oilcloth square no bigger than a prayer book, sewn into the lining of their coat where a patch covered an old burn scar—and beneath it, in a second pocket of their own making, the seed pod and the deed, wrapped together like relics on a saint's body.

The letter had been Thorn's. The seed and the deed were theirs. And Mara understood, standing very still while a stranger's hand patted down the mattress beside them, that these were two different kinds of weight, and only one of them could be carried through what was coming.

* * *

They began the memorizing that same night, after lights-out, lying on their back in the dark with the letter's contents unfolding behind their closed eyes like something seen from underwater.

They did not read it again so much as *walk* it. That was how memory worked for Mara—not lines recited but ground crossed. They had learned it as a child, memorizing tide tables and warehouse codes, punishments waiting for any error. You built the thing into a place and then you lived in it until you knew every stone.

So: the opening address. *To whom it may concern, being all magistrates and honest persons.* Thorn's phrasing, formal and slightly old-fashioned, the handwriting cramped at the margins where he'd run out of room. Mara could see the page. Could see the ink blot shaped like a small fish beside the second paragraph, where his pen had rested too long while he coughed.

*I, Elias Thorn, clerk of the Halloway Trading Company, being of sound mind and failing body, do set down what I witnessed and what I was paid to record falsely.*

Then the heart of it. The winter of '91. The harbormaster, Aldous Wren, bought for three hundred pounds and a quiet annuity, paid quarterly through an account Thorn himself had kept—the bitter joke of it, that the murder had its own ledger line. The storm signal flags ordered furled. The Morning Star fleet, forty-one divers aboard nine boats, sent out with the barometer falling and the harbor master's own glass reading *storm imminent*. Halloway's founders—Marcus Halloway and his brother-in-law, Edmond Pryce—had signed the order themselves. Not a note. A signed order, which Thorn had copied, and which he had hidden, and which was the reason his lungs were bleeding now, or so he believed; he wrote that he had been given cause over the years to fear the company's medicine as much as its enemies.

Mara lay in the dark and walked the page the way you'd walk a shore at low tide, learning where every rock lay.

And then the names.

Forty-one names, because one diver had stayed ashore with a broken hand, and Thorn had recorded him too, marked *survived*, as though survival were a clerical entry. Joren Vane. Third boat, the *Kestrel's Pride*. Age thirty-four. Married to Ilse Vane, deceased. Father to one child, indentured.

Mara had known their father's name their whole life. Everyone has a name; that means nothing in Saltmere. But seeing it in ink—in *evidence*, entered in a fair hand between *Olek Marsh* and *Petra Osei*, equal to them, numbered among them, drowned with them—that was different. That was the difference between knowing a thing and holding it.

They lay awake until the fourth bell, going over the names one by one, refusing to let any of them blur. Forty names plus the man with the broken hand. If the paper burned, the names would not. They would carry them the way the sea carries salt—invisibly, in everything.

* * *

The second search came three days later and was not a formality.

It came at dawn, and it came with Hale.

He did not touch anything himself. He stood in the doorway of the dormitory with his hands clasped behind him, gray-coated and patient, watching his men work with the expression of a man supervising the pruning of trees he owned. When his gaze passed over Mara—and it did pass, deliberately, a full slow pass like a lantern swept across a wall—Mara looked back with exactly the right amount of nothing. Fear would have been interesting. Resentment would have been interesting. They gave him the flat, tired blankness of someone who had been woken at dawn for the eleventh time in their life and expected nothing better.

Hale smiled at them. Just slightly. The smile of a man who enjoyed a long game.

That afternoon, word came down the rows: the company doctor had examined Elias Thorn's effects and found his quarters stripped of papers. Every drawer, every floorboard, every seam of his mattress opened and found empty. Which meant, Mara reasoned with a cold clarity that surprised them, that either Thorn had destroyed everything—which he hadn't—or that whoever searched before Hale arrived had taken the rest, and Hale knew it, and Hale was now searching people rather than places.

Which meant the next search would be personal.

* * *

They chose the third night after, when the fog came in off the strait thick enough to swallow the harbor bells. Fog meant muffled footsteps, meant the watch moving slow and half-blind, meant the coal stove in the dormitory's end room burning low because fuel was counted like everything else.

Mara sat alone by that stove with the letter open on their knees for the last time, and read it through once more, aloud, in a whisper no louder than the coals settling.

They tested themselves. *Aldous Wren, harbormaster, three hundred pounds.* Correct. *The Kestrel's Pride, third boat, eleven souls.* Correct. *Signed under the hands of Marcus Halloway and Edmond Pryce, this ninth day of December.* Correct. The fish-shaped blot. The cramped margin. The way Thorn had pressed so hard on the final line that the words were legible from the *back* of the page—*let the sea give up its accounting*—as if he'd wanted it readable even turned around, even inside out, even burned.

Mara thought about that line for a long time.

Paper could be taken. Paper could be switched—had already been switched, if Sable still lived, if Sable had ever truly carried it. Paper could be produced in evidence and then disputed, forged, discredited, made to hang its bearer. Everything Mara owned that was tangible could be confiscated in a single dawn search, and then they would be empty-handed *and* marked, which was how the company disposed of problems.

But a thing fully learned could not be seized. They could take Mara's coat, their boots, the tin from under the floorboard, the pearls sewn in their hem—they could take Mara themselves, and still the testimony would remain, walking around inside a nineteen-year-old pearl diver with salt-cracked hands, reciting itself whenever asked.

The letter was true. The truth didn't need the paper. The paper only needed the truth long enough to be believed—and belief, Mara was learning, could be carried in a body.

Still, their hands shook. That was the strange part. Ten years of indenture had taught them to surrender things without ceremony—a father, a mother's grave-site claim, every wage-year from nine to nineteen—and here they were, trembling over four pages of another man's confession, as if paper were flesh.

Maybe it was. It was the first thing anyone had ever handed them that said *you were right to grieve; it was done to you.*

"I'll keep it," Mara whispered to Thorn across the distance between living clerk and dead one. "Not the paper. The words."

They fed it to the stove a corner at a time, so the flames wouldn't flare high enough to be seen from the door. The oilcloth caught last and burned greenish, and the smell was sharp and chemical, like the harbor at low tide. Mara watched the ink lift off the page in the heat—the fish-shaped blot curling, the names browning at their edges, Joren Vane's name going soft and then bright and then gone—and felt something split cleanly in their chest, grief and relief in the same wound.

When it was ash, they stirred it into the coals with a poker until no edge of paper survived, and banked the fire, and sat in the dark afterward with their palms pressed flat against their knees, listening to their own heartbeat as though checking whether the letter had successfully moved house.

It had. They could feel it there, behind the breastbone, whole.

What remained was smaller. From the lining of their coat they took out the seed pod—dry, light, rattling faintly with its few surviving seeds, the last fruit of trees that existed now only in a drowned man's promise and a widow's memory somewhere—and the deed, soft as cloth from years in a tin, bearing their father's name and the company's refusal stamped across it like a scar.

These Mara did not burn. These they folded into a rectangle no larger than a playing card, wrapped in waxed cloth, and sewed back into the lining with sail thread pulled tight, working blind by touch in the unlit corner of the dormitory, each stitch deliberate, each stitch a vow. The needle bit. Their fingers, toughened by ten years of shell and rope, barely noticed.

By the time the fifth bell rang the fog was thinning. Mara lay down, coat draped over the foot of their bunk like any garment, containing nothing remarkable, containing everything.

Let them search. Let Hale turn out every mattress in Saltmere. The testimony was memorized and the inheritance was stitched against their heart, and neither one could be inventoried, confiscated, or sold.

They slept, briefly and without dreams, and woke to the sixth bell and the sound of boots in the corridor—many boots, moving with purpose—and a knock at the dormitory door that was not a request.

Old Petrel, rising slowly from the next bunk, met Mara's eyes across the gray morning light. She'd been arrested twice in her life and knew the sound of official boots the way sailors know weather.

"That's for someone," she said quietly. "Eat nothing. Say little."

The knock came again, harder.

"Open in the name of the Halloway Company."
