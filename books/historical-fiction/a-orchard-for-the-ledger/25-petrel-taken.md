## 25. Petrel Taken

They came for Petrel on a Tuesday, in the hour when the pearl-sorting sheds smelled most of brine and rot and the low sun turned the harbor water the color of old brass.

Mara saw it from the sorting tables—saw it the way you see a storm building far out at sea, too distant to run from, too certain to deny. Two constables in company blue walked down the quay boards with that unhurried gait men use when they already own what they've come to take. Between them, flanking them like an honor guard, walked Corvus Hale, his coat brushed, his hat set at the angle of a man attending a social call.

Petrel was mending nets by the third shed, her fingers moving through the mesh with sixty years of habit, her back curved like a question mark against the light. She did not look up when they stopped in front of her. She never looked up at anything she couldn't change.

"Petra Oldwine," Hale said, reading from a paper he did not need to read. "You are detained on suspicion of theft. Company property."

Petrel's hands went still in the netting. For one moment—one long, salt-heavy moment—her head lifted and her eyes found Mara across the yard, across the distance of forty feet and twenty years and one secret held between them like a coal passed hand to hand.

She did not look afraid. That was the worst of it. She looked *resigned*, the way she looked when a dive came up empty, when the season ran cold, when the company docked wages for weather the divers didn't make. She looked like a woman who had been waiting all her life for the knock, and was almost relieved to finally answer it.

Then she stood, slow as a ship taking water, and went with them.

The nets slid from her lap into a puddle of gray water and no one picked them up.

* * *

Mara kept sorting.

That was the discipline Thorn had taught them without ever saying it aloud: keep your hands moving, keep your face empty, keep your eyes on the work in front of you while everything behind your ribs tears loose and floods. A pearl of good size rolled under Mara's thumb—cream-colored, lustrous, worth perhaps a month of wages—and they dropped it into the company tray with the same motion they'd made ten thousand times before, and their hand did not shake, and inside their skull a voice was screaming *run, run, he's found the thread, pull it and everything unravels.*

But running was confession. Running was the thread pulled taut. So Mara sorted pearls and watched Hale's blue-coated constables walk Petrel past the sheds, past the counting house, past the gate where Sable had once wheeled a fish cart through the morning fog with a letter sewn into its false bottom—and the gate closed behind her with a sound like a book snapping shut.

Old Petrel, who had dived since she was eight and carried the sea in her lungs like a second language. Who had covered for Mara during Hale's roll-call with a lie so smooth the fixer himself had nodded and moved on. Who told stories of the drowned fleet in a voice like gravel rolling in a current, who remembered every name, who had once said, *child, the dead don't need us to weep, they need us to remember right.*

Now the company had her, and Hale had her, and the only thing standing between Petrel and whatever happened in rooms with closed doors was Mara's silence.

Which meant the silence had a price now, and the price had a face, and the face had hands that had pulled Mara out of the water twice when they were young and stupid about the currents off the north jetty.

The afternoon light crawled. The trays filled. The overseer called the shift, and Mara walked back to the dormitory with their spine straight and their mind a single white-hot line: *she gave up nothing yet. She hasn't talked yet. Every hour she doesn't talk is an hour bought.*

But hours ran out. Hours always ran out. The tide came in and the tide went out and somewhere across the strait the magistrate sat in a room Mara could not see, and the Morning Tide rode at anchor with its cargo half-loaded, delayed by the storm, sailing tomorrow if the weather held—and Petrel was in a cell tonight, and Mara's memorized testimony did Petrel no good at all locked inside a skull no one could open without breaking the whole thing.

They lay on their cot in the dark and listened to the other indentured girls breathing, and thought: *this is what he wants me to feel. This exact thing. The helplessness with a name attached.*

Hale was clever that way. He hadn't arrested Mara. He hadn't even questioned Mara again since the refused offer, the withdrawn freedom papers, the searched dormitory with its mattresses gutted and its floorboards pried. He had taken the person beside Mara instead, the way you take a hostage not to kill but to weigh—the way you hang a weight from one side of a scale and wait for the other side to tip.

And Mara lay in the dark and understood, with terrible clarity, that they would talk. Not tonight. Not tomorrow. But there was a limit in every person, a depth below which the pressure won, and Hale had spent his whole career finding where that depth lay in people, and he had all night, and all the nights after, and Petrel was seventy years old and her lungs were full of salt history and—

No. Mara pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until sparks bloomed. *Not yet. She hasn't given you up yet. Honor it. Spend the hours she's buying.*

Outside, the harbor slapped against the pilings, patient as a debt collector.

* * *

They let Mara see her on the third day.

That was deliberate too—everything Hale did was deliberate, every kindness a hook, every cruelty a message. A runner in company gray appeared at the sorting shed at midmorning and said, flat as a ledger entry: "Vane. The factor wants you at the counting house." And Mara's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world, because the counting house was where the questioning rooms were, and being summoned there was either the end or the beginning of the end.

The room they took Mara to was small and clean and had one window, high, barred, letting in a column of light that fell on a table and nothing else. Hale stood by the window. At the table, facing the door, sat Petrel.

Mara had prepared themselves for bruises. They had prepared themselves for blood, for split lips, for the marks of a man who worked with his fists. What they had not prepared for was the neatness of it—Petrel washed, her white hair combed, a cup of tea steaming untouched in front of her, looking more cared-for than she had in thirty years of company service. It was worse than bruises. Bruises would have been honest. This was a stage set, and Petrel was the prop, and the play was titled *Look How Well We Treat Her While You Decide.*

"Sit," Hale said pleasantly, and Mara sat, because the chair was there and because refusing small things was how you got broken for the large ones.

Petrel's eyes came up and met Mara's, and in them Mara read a whole language spoken in the space of a heartbeat: *I'm fine. I've said nothing. Don't you dare start now.* Seventy years of diving had taught her to hold her breath a long time. She was holding it still.

"You're wondering why you're here," Hale said. He moved around the table with that unhurried grace of his, the predator's economy, and settled against the windowsill where the light caught him from behind so his face stayed in soft shadow. "I'll tell you plainly, because I've always found plainness efficient. Petra Oldwine stands accused of theft. Certain items missing from the salvage stores. Rope, canvas, a case of trade gin—it hardly matters what. What matters is that theft among indentured workers carries extension of term. Seven years, ordinarily. Fourteen for a second offense." He let that sit in the air like smoke. "She claims she's innocent. She claims a great many things, actually. She's very stubborn. Divers usually are—the water selects for it."

"I don't know anything about it," Mara said.

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "Of course you don't. Here's my difficulty, Vane." He came forward and put both hands flat on the table, and now his face was close and fully lit, and the pleasantness in it had the specific temperature of ice. "Someone in this yard helped Elias Thorn move papers he should not have had. Papers have left this property. I know it the way I know the weather. I cannot prove it, which is a temporary condition. And then—" he straightened, brushed invisible dust from his sleeve, "—a curious coincidence. The old woman covers your absence at roll-call. Within the week, she is seen near the salvage stores at an odd hour. Within the fortnight, she's in my cells. Coincidence is such a faithful servant, isn't it? Always arriving exactly when needed."

"She covered for me because I was sick," Mara said. "Sea-sick. Everyone knows divers get the land-sickness sometimes."

"Everyone knows," Hale echoed, smiling. "Everyone knows a great deal, Vane. That's the trouble with everyone." He glanced at Petrel, almost tenderly. "Your friend has been offered leniency. Complete withdrawal of charges, in fact, in exchange for testimony regarding the movement of documents. She has declined to assist us. Repeatedly." His voice softened into something almost admiring. "Three days of declining. I confess I'm impressed. But stubbornness is a resource, Vane, like pearls or patience. It depletes. And when hers does—" he looked at Mara again, and the smile widened by exactly one tooth, "—she'll give us a name. Names are the last thing the stubborn surrender, because names are the thing they were protecting all along. So I find myself thinking: wouldn't it be kinder, wouldn't it be *cleaner*, if the name arrived voluntarily? Before the cost grew?"

The tea steamed. The barred light lay across the table between them like a blade.

"If you have something to say to me," Mara said, "say it."

"What I'm saying," Hale said, "is that I am offering you a window. A small one. A crack of light. Walk out of here and think about what fourteen years does to a woman of seventy. Think about whether the paper she protected is worth her lungs. Think—" and here his voice dropped, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, gentle as a knife sliding in, "—about the fact that I could have charged her with anything at all, Vane. Theft was merely convenient. The charge was never the point. The charge is just a door. I am showing you the door because I want you to walk through it willingly. People who come to me willingly fare better. Ask anyone."

*Ask anyone,* Mara thought, *except the ones who came to you willingly, because they're not here to ask.*

"I'll go back to work now," Mara said, and stood, and their legs held, which surprised them.

"Of course." Hale stepped aside with a little bow, courteous as a dancing master. "One more thing." He reached into his coat and produced something small and set it on the table in front of Petrel—a dried seed pod, split and hollow, the kind that grows on orchard trees along the coast, the kind that exists in exactly one place in Mara's entire world: sewn into the lining of their coat, against their ribs, where it had ridden for weeks like a second heart.

It was not theirs. Of course it wasn't. There must be dozens in some drawer of Hale's, collected from who knew where, props for the same play. But Mara's body did not know that. Mara's body went cold from scalp to sole, and for one unguarded instant their face must have shown everything, because Hale watched them the way a man watches a fish rise to a lure, and smiled, and said nothing at all.

"Good day, Vane," he said.

Mara walked out. The corridor swam. Their hands, at their sides, curled into fists so tight the nails drew blood from their own palms, and they welcomed the pain, because pain was simple, and nothing else in the world was.

Behind them, through the closing door, they heard Petrel speak for the first time—two words, gravel and current, pitched to carry:

*"Hold fast."*

* * *

That evening, word came down through the yard like oil through water: the old diver would be questioned formally tomorrow, before the factor and a company magistrate, on the theft charge. Everyone knew what company questioning meant. Everyone had known someone who went into those rooms whole and came out in pieces, or didn't come out at all, or came out agreeing to things that extended terms and broke families and buried names deeper than the sea ever had.

Mara sat on the quay in the last light with their knees drawn up, watching the Morning Tide swing at her moorings, black rigging against a bruised-orange sky. The storm had passed; the barometer, the boatswain had said, was rising; she'd sail on tomorrow's morning tide, with the magistrate aboard, with the harbormaster's deposition already delivered by Sable's desperate second route—but a deposition alone was one voice, one thread, and threads snapped. Thorn's letter, memorized and burned, lived nowhere now but in Mara. If Mara broke—if Petrel broke first and gave the name, and Hale came with his gentle voice and his drawer full of seed pods—then everything died here: the testimony, the forty names, Joren Vane among them, drowned a second time in silence.

*Hold fast,* she'd said. As if she were the anchor and Mara the boat.

A gull screamed over the masts. Somewhere below the dark water, pearls lay in their shells making their slow patient nacre around grit and wound—that was all a pearl was, Mara had learned long ago; a pearl was just a hurt the oyster decided to build around instead of dying of. They had built their whole self that way. They could do it one day longer.

Tomorrow Petrel would sit in a room with a factor and a company magistrate, and she would be silent, because she was old and tough and stubborn as keel-wood, but silence had a bottom, and Hale knew how to sound depths.

Unless the bottom never came. Unless, before the questioning began, Petrel looked at the men across the table and understood that she had one possession left worth anything—her silence—and spent it in the only way that mattered: loudly enough to be heard, publicly enough to be witnessed, in front of the one audience whose presence Hale could not control.

Because Mara knew something Hale did not. The magistrate was aboard the Morning Tide, yes—but the Morning Tide's boats came ashore for fresh water at dawn.

And dawn was four hours away.
