## 26. Night Negotiation

The pearls had been Mara's for eleven years, and they had never once been theirs.

That was the arithmetic of an indenture: everything a body gathered belonged to the company that owned the body. Every oyster pried from the harbor's cold shelves in the hour before dawn, every loose pearl sifted from silt where the dredges had torn and missed—these were Halloway property by law, by ledger, by the ink of a contract signed with a child's unsteady X when Mara was nine years old and did not yet know how to spell their own name. The company took the harvest. The company kept the books. The company decided what a diver was worth, which was less than the rope that held them.

But the harbor was wide and the water was dark and no clerk had ever learned to see underwater.

So Mara had kept a second ledger, one written nowhere, held only in the body's memory: forty-one pearls over eleven years, each one earned in the margins of dives, palmed in the blind moment when the basket tipped and the mud bloomed like smoke. Small ones, mostly. Misshapen things with the luster of candlelight through fog. A clerk would have struck them from any manifest as worthless. But worthlessness was a language Mara spoke fluently; it had been their mother tongue since the day the harbor gate closed behind them and the word *indentured* settled onto them like a coat of tar.

Forty-one pearls, sewn into a canvas roll inside the heel of a boot that no longer fit quite right, because nineteen years old was not nine years old, and even boots grew out of their usefulness.

Mara counted them now by touch alone, sitting on the edge of the cot in the women's dormitory with a blanket over their shoulders like a shroud worn backward. Around them the dormitory breathed—thirty sleepers in the sour warmth of bodies and lamp-oil and the salt damp that never left Saltmere, not even indoors, not even in dreams. Someone coughed in her sleep. The roof timbers ticked as the night wind worried at the shingles. And beneath all of it, under the floorboards, down through the foundations, down to the pilings furred with weed—the sea, going about its patient work of keeping everything it had ever taken.

Petrel was in the lockup on Marsh Lane.

They had told Mara that evening in the way news traveled among the indentured—not spoken so much as passed hand to hand, mouth to ear, a coin of information changing owners without ever being displayed. *Hale took old Petrel this afternoon. Theft, he says. Said she lifted pearls from the sorting shed.* Mara had stood very still among the pearl baskets and felt the words go into them like cold water into cloth.

Because it was not theft. Because Petrel had not touched a pearl in thirty years except to sort them for other people's profit. Because Hale did not care about pearls at all; Hale cared about pressure, and pressure required a lever, and the oldest lever in Saltmere was another person's body.

Petrel, who had covered for Mara's absence at the roll-call with a lie delivered so smoothly that Hale himself had nodded and moved on. Petrel, who had dived beside Joren Vane before Mara was born, who had been on the water the night forty divers went down, who carried the drowned inside her like ballast. Petrel, who was sixty years old and had spent her whole life holding her breath.

*Hold fast,* Mara wanted to tell her. *Dawn comes.* It was what the divers said to one another on the rope, in the days when there had been enough divers left to say it—one below and one above, the signal tugs traveling down the line like a pulse: *hold fast, hold fast, dawn comes.* Meaning: I am here. Meaning: you are not alone in the dark. Meaning: whatever the water takes, the ones who surface will remember.

But the lockup on Marsh Lane was a company lockup, and company lockups did not receive visitors. Messages did not pass through walls made of iron and men paid to be deaf.

Unless the men were not entirely deaf. Unless something could be slipped into the small dry space between what a man heard and what he chose to repeat.

Mara finished counting. Forty-one pearls. They sat in the dark and thought about half of twenty, which is twenty, and then about the remainder, twenty-one, which would have to buy whatever came after—if anything came after. Then they lay back on the cot and did not sleep, but listened to the sea beneath the floorboards, keeping its own count.

* * *

The guard's name was Dree, and he worked the night watch at Marsh Lane, and he owed his position to a bad knee that had ended his career hauling cargo and his dignity along with it. He was perhaps fifty, heavy through the shoulders, with a face like a door someone had once tried to kick in and given up on. Mara had watched him for three evenings from across the lane, from the shadow of the cooperage awning, learning him the way Thorn had taught them to learn ledgers: not the entries on top but the corrections underneath.

Every man had corrections underneath.

On the first night, Dree had drunk two cups of arrack from the flask the other guards passed around, and turned his back to the street for the length of a slow song sung by a drunk somewhere near the fish market. On the second night, a woman who was not his wife—for Mara knew his wife's face from the market, a thin sharp woman who sold eels—had met him behind the lockup and left again before the watch bell. On the third night, nothing happened at all, which was its own kind of information: a man who had nothing happen to him was a man waiting for something to happen.

Tonight, Mara stepped out of the cooperage shadow while the drunk was still singing and the watch bell was still an hour away.

Dree's hand went to his truncheon, then stopped, because what approached him out of the dark was small—a slight figure in a laborer's coat, hood up, hands visible and empty, moving the way you learned to move in Saltmere if you were nobody: quickly but without urgency, the way a cat moves along a wall.

"Guard." The voice pitched low, sexless in the way Mara had trained their voice to be since childhood, a voice that clerks' ears slid off of. "A word. Not here."

"I don't take words from shadows," Dree said, but he said it quietly, which meant he took them.

"Behind the salt sheds, then. In the light, where you can see my empty hands and I can see yours." Mara was already moving. It was important, they had learned, never to ask anyone to follow; you simply became someone worth following.

Behind the salt sheds the moon came through a gap in the clouds and laid a gray stripe across the boards, and Mara stopped in the middle of it deliberately, turning, letting the light do what introductions could not. Dree squinted at them—at the face too young and too watchful, at the coat, at the hands—and his expression performed the whole arithmetic of the harbor's hierarchy in three seconds: worker, therefore poor; poor, therefore harmless or desperate; desperate, therefore possibly useful.

"You're one of Halloway's," he said. "I've seen you. Sorting sheds."

"You've got good eyes for a man who sees nothing," Mara said, and let that sit between them, and watched him decide whether to be angry. He chose not to. Men with bad knees and worse wives chose not to more often than not.

"Say your word, then."

Mara brought the canvas roll out of the boot slowly, slowly, the way you'd bring out something living. They unwound it in the flat of their palm under the moonlight. Twenty pearls caught the light the way wet stones catch it—not brilliantly, not like the jewels in the company's catalogs, but truly, the way the harbor itself shone when the sun hit it right.

Dree looked at them the way a starving man looks at bread: openly, helplessly, before he remembered to look bored instead.

"What's this?"

"Twenty reasons for a man with a bad knee to walk softly past a cell door tonight and tomorrow night and every night until the magistrate's ship sails."

Something moved behind Dree's face—calculation, caution, and underneath both, faintly, something almost like relief, the relief of a man who has waited for something to happen and finally knows what shape it will take. "You're asking me to carry a message. To the thief."

"She's not a thief."

"I don't care what she is. I care what Hale is, and Hale is the reason that cell has a new lock on it, and Hale is the reason I check that lock twice a shift and write my name saying so." He crossed his arms. "If I'm caught carrying words to that woman—"

"You won't be caught. That's what the pearls are for. They're not payment for courage, guard. They're payment for craft." Mara took a step closer, and lowered the hood, and let Dree see their face fully—young, tired, ordinary, the face of every indentured worker he'd shoved through a gate a hundred times. "And I'll tell you why you'll do it, and it isn't the pearls. The pearls are so you can tell yourself it was the pearls. But you'll do it because you've walked past that cell three nights now and heard her coughing, and she's sixty years old, and whatever Hale says she stole, you know what she actually is. She's the woman who pulled two of your fellow dockhands out of the channel ice seven winters back. Everyone on this quay knows it. You know it."

Dree's jaw worked. The moon slid behind cloud, and the salt sheds became shapes, and the harbor became sound only—the slap of water, the creak of moored hulls, the enormous slow breathing of the tide.

"Hale hanged a man in Corren's Crossing for less than a message," Dree said at last, and his voice had gone quieter, which Mara understood to mean the negotiation had entered its second chamber.

"Hale hanged a man in Corren's Crossing because no one stood up while he did it." Mara folded the pearls back into the canvas, wound it, held it out. "I'm standing up. You can stand next to me or you can walk away, and either way, come morning, the magistrate on the Morning Tide will have testimony that Halloway's founders sank forty divers for profit. Forty. Their names are written down. My father's name is among them." They let the sentence land, and watched it land—watched the way men's faces always changed at the number forty, because everyone in Saltmere had lost someone to that storm-that-wasn't-a-storm, and everyone had been taught to call it weather, and weather was grief you couldn't argue with. This was not weather anymore. This was a name on a page.

"The old diver," Dree said. "Petrel." He said it the way you say a name you've known all your life without knowing you knew it. "She dove with the Vane lot. Before."

"Before," Mara agreed, and did not say *my father*, and found that the not-saying was harder than saying would have been.

Dree looked at the canvas roll for a long time. Somewhere beyond the sheds, the drunk had stopped singing, and the silence pressed in like deep water.

"The message," he said finally. "Give me the message."

Mara gave it to him aloud first, making him say it back twice, exactly, because words carried through walls had to survive the journey intact: *"Tell her: hold fast, dawn comes. From the one whose name she covered at the roll-call. Tell her the letter is safe and sailing soon. Tell her she hasn't been forgotten."*

Then Mara pressed the canvas roll into his palm and watched his fingers close around it, and understood that they had just done something irreversible—that twenty pearls, half of everything they had hoarded across eleven years of stolen breath at the bottom of the harbor, had just changed the shape of the world slightly, the way a keystone changes an arch.

"If you sell me to Hale," Mara said, not accusingly, just factually, "you'll get more than twenty pearls. I want you to know I've considered that. I want you to know I'm giving them to you anyway."

Dree stared at them. Something in his kicked-in-door face shifted, some timber settling. "Why?" he asked, and it was not a guard's question. It was a man's.

"Because she held fast for me when it cost her," Mara said. "Now it's my turn on the rope."

They walked away before he could answer, because answers, like pearls, lost value when handled too long in the open air.

* * *

The dormitory again, after midnight, after the watch bell, after lying awake in the salt-dark listening to thirty people breathe.

It occurred to Mara, staring at the black rafters, that they had spent their whole life believing history happened elsewhere—to people with surnames that mattered, to founders and magistrates and men whose names got printed in the shipping pages. That was the philosophy of the invisible, and Mara had worn it like armor for nineteen years: *nothing I do will be recorded, so nothing I do can be blamed on me.*

But tonight they had bought a message with the only currency they had, and the message was traveling now through the dark toward a locked door, carried by a limping man with a kicked-in face, and somewhere on the other side of town an old woman was breathing in a cell because she had lied to a fixer to protect a person whose name wasn't even supposed to matter. That was history. It was small and damp and it smelled of arrack and salt, and it was happening in Marsh Lane, not elsewhere, and it had Mara Vane's fingerprints all over it.

Thorn would have appreciated the entry. *One message, sent. Twenty pearls, expended. Balance outstanding: everything.*

Mara smiled at the rafters, a small crooked smile with no joy in it and a strange fierce tenderness underneath, the tenderness you feel toward people who spend themselves for you—Petrel with her lie, Sable with her broken face, even Dree with his bad knee, all of them paying out line into the dark so that Mara could stay down a little longer and come up with the truth in their teeth.

*Hold fast,* they thought at the cell wall across the town, at the woman inside it, at all the drowned and all the indentured, the whole long rope of them stretching back through the years into the green cold. *Hold fast. Dawn comes.*

Sleep came finally, shallow as a diver's rest, one ear always on the surface.

They woke before first light to the sound of running feet in the lane, and a knocking that was not the watch bell, and a voice shouting the thing Mara had been dreading and expecting in equal measure:

"The Morning Tide's cleared customs early—she sails on this morning's tide!"

Mara sat up in the dark, heart slamming, and reached for the boot with the remaining pearls, and the seed pod sewn into the coat lining, and the deed pressed flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

Not dawn, then. Dawn was now.
