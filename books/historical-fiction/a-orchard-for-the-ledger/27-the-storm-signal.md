## 27. The Storm Signal

The flags went up an hour before dawn.

Mara saw them from the sorting shed's high window—three black pennants snapping on the harbor mast, and beneath them the storm cone, tarred canvas swollen like a lung about to burst. The whole quay seemed to flinch at once. Dockhands stopped mid-haul. A boy selling hot eels dropped his basket and did not pick it up.

*The Morning Tide will not sail today.*

Mara stood very still among the trays of pearls, their fingers buried in cold nacre, and felt the news move through their body like cold water through cloth. Not panic—not yet—but something worse: a recalculation. Every hour they had counted on was now subtracted from them, and what remained had to be spent differently, more carefully, in smaller denominations of time.

The storm had been building for two days out past the shoals. Old Petrel had smelled it Tuesday morning, standing at the harbor wall with his ruined ear turned to the sea, and said, *There's weather sitting on the horizon like a creditor.* Everyone laughed. Petrel did not laugh at his own jokes; he made them the way he made knots, functional and unadorned. Now the creditor had come to collect, and it would take the tide with it.

The Morning Tide would not sail until tomorrow's morning tide. Twenty-four hours. Perhaps more, if the swell ran long.

Twenty-four hours in which Corvus Hale could search again. In which the checkpoints could thicken. In which Sable—who had come back to Mara two nights ago with a face like a dropped plate, holding himself together by will alone—had to stay hidden, stay quiet, stay alive long enough to make his second run to the harbormaster across the strait. That packet was already gone, or it wasn't. Sable had sworn it was away on a mackerel boat before the gates closed last night. But Sable had also once sworn loyalty over stolen rum, and Mara had learned that oaths, like pearls, were graded by flaws you couldn't see until you held them to the light.

*The letter is memorized,* Mara reminded themselves, as they had reminded themselves every hour since the burning. *It cannot be taken because it is nowhere. It is only in me.*

But memory, they were learning, was its own kind of weight. It pressed on the ribs. It kept a person awake listening for boots on stairs.

* * *

By mid-morning the harbor had pulled everything in that could be pulled. Boats came up the channel stacked bow to stern, fishermen hauling their punts onto the shingle rather than trusting moorings, and the water inside the mole churned green-gray, slapping at the pilings with a sound like slow applause. The gulls had gone inland, which Petrel always said was the truest forecast there was—*birds don't gamble.*

Mara worked the sheds because stopping would be noticeable, and being noticeable was death. They sorted pearls with hands that remembered how, while their mind ran the route again and again like a tongue finding a broken tooth.

The harbor gate: Sable's fish cart had carried the decoy through once, but the real crossing—the one that mattered—was still ahead, if it was needed at all. The customs house: a stamped manifest, a forged entry in a hand Thorn had taught so well that even Mara sometimes forgot which entries in the company's books were lies written by clerks and which were truths written by thieves. And the gangway of the Morning Tide, where guards checked every worker boarding to load cargo, patting down coats, tapping boot heels, looking into faces.

And the testimony itself—Thorn's words, forty names, Joren Vane among them—lived now only behind Mara's eyes, sewn into them more securely than the deed and the seed pod were sewn into the lining of their coat.

Tomorrow's morning tide. One chance. If it slipped—if the storm stretched, if the magistrate's patience wore out, if the ship sailed without them—then all of it collapsed back into silence. Thorn would have died for nothing. Petrel would rot in the company cells for nothing. Sable's split lips and burned hands would mean nothing.

Mara looked down at the tray in front of them and realized they had been holding the same pearl for a full minute, turning it, turning it.

"Vane." The shed mistress's voice, sharp as a shell edge. "You're dreaming."

"No, ma'am. Sorting."

"Then sort faster. Halloway wants double lots packed before the weather shuts us in. Storm means ships can't leave, and ships that can't leave means ledgers that must balance." She moved off down the rows, and Mara understood her perfectly: when time stopped, the company simply demanded more of everyone else's.

That was Hale's arithmetic too. Mara could feel it coming the way you feel a change in pressure behind the ears.

* * *

It came at noon, in the form of a bell.

Not the storm bell—that had been ringing since dawn, slow and booming from the harbor mast. This was the company bell, three floors up in the counting house, rung hard and fast, the summons that meant every indentured worker on the quay was to drop what they held and line up.

They lined up in the rain that had begun to fall sideways, two hundred bodies in oilskins and sacking, while the wind tore at the washing lines above the drying yards and the whole gray world leaned eastward. Corvus Hale stood on the loading platform under an umbrella he didn't need, because the rain refused to touch him—it seemed to bend around his shoulders the way everything else bent—and beside him stood four constables in oilskin capes, and beside them, a man Mara had never seen before with a ledger open against his chest, protected by a sheet of oilcloth.

"Company business," Hale said, and his voice carried the way voices carry in storms, flat and clear over the noise, as though the weather were cooperating with him. "Until the Tide sails, no worker leaves the compound. No worker enters it. Roll will be called twice daily, morning and evening. Your papers—" he let the word sit in the rain a moment, "—will be inspected at the gate. Both directions."

A murmur went down the lines. Papers. Indentured workers carried chits, not papers—little lead-stamped tags that said who owned their years. Inspecting them at the gate meant searching every body that passed through, turning out bundles, patting down coats.

Coats. Mara's coat, with its sewn seams, its flat hidden pocket riding against their left ribs where the orchard deed lay folded small as a hymnal and the seed pod rattled faintly when the wind shook them.

Hale's eyes traveled the rows. He was smiling, of course. He was always smiling, the way knives are always sharp—just a condition of the object, nothing personal. His gaze came down the line of pearl divers, unhurried, and Mara did what nineteen years of invisibility had taught them: they became furniture. They softened their face into the dull patience of someone thinking about supper and nothing else. They let their shoulders slump. They became a person Hale's eyes could slide off like rain off oilcloth.

His gaze passed over them. Paused. Came back half a heartbeat, no more.

Mara's heart did not stop, because stopping would be a luxury. It simply beat harder, quietly, like fists knocking on a door from the inside.

"Storm work," Hale continued. "Double shifts. The Tide loads at first light tomorrow whether the sky has finished crying or not, and she'll carry a full hold if we break our backs doing it." A pause, and then, almost gently, the way a man lays a trap gently: "Anyone with information regarding missing company property may approach me privately. There is gratitude in this company for honesty. Real gratitude. The kind that comes with freedom papers attached."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the line, a man coughed.

"Dismissed."

* * *

The compound locked at dusk, earlier than it had ever locked, and the guards at the gate were doubled exactly as promised—four instead of two, with lanterns, with the constable's ledger man standing between them checking names against names. Mara watched from the dormitory window as the last workers were funneled through, each one stopped, each bundle turned out, each coat patted along the seams.

Along the seams.

They sat on their bunk in the dark and put their hand flat against their ribs and breathed. The deed was paper, thin, folded to the size of a playing card. The seeds were dry husks in a pod no longer than a thumb. Against a searching hand, paper was a whisper. Husks were crumbs. It could be survived, maybe. Or maybe a clever hand—a patient hand, a hand like Hale's—would feel the wrongness of a seam that bulged a hair too much, and then it wouldn't matter how small the things were, only that they existed, and that they belonged to a diver's child who had no right to inherit anything but debt.

*Tomorrow,* Mara thought. *Tomorrow I walk the gangway with these against my ribs, past whatever guard Hale stations there, and if he doubles the gangway too—*

They caught themselves spiraling and did what Thorn had taught them, in that other life, copying columns by candlelight: break it into entries. One problem per line.

Entry one: the deed and the seeds could not go through a search. Therefore they must either not be searched, or not be carried. Burning them was unthinkable—no. Not unthinkable. Unbearable. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. The seed pod was the only thing on earth that had touched their father's trees. The deed was the only document that had ever promised the Vane family anything. To burn them would be to burn the past twice.

Entry two: the testimony was safe. Memory could not be patted down.

Entry three: Sable. Where was Sable? The compound gates were shut, but Sable had never once been inside them after dark in his life; he lived in the wet creases of the harbor like a crab lives in rock. The question was whether the mackerel boat had gotten out before the harbor closed, whether the harbormaster's deposition was already crossing the strait or sitting in some boathouse waiting for weather, and whether Sable himself was lying low or lying bleeding somewhere. Mara had seen him two nights ago, wrecked and resolute, and heard him say: *I gave them nothing, fish-girl. Not my name, not yours, not the route.* And Mara had believed him, mostly, the way you believe a rope bridge mostly holds.

Entry four: Petrel. Still in the cells. Still silent, as far as anyone knew. Dawn comes, Mara had paid to tell him. Dawn was coming—but dawn was now twenty-four hours further away than it had been yesterday, and men in company cells did not always keep.

Outside, the storm arrived in earnest.

* * *

It hit the compound around midnight like a judgment nobody had bothered to read aloud. The wind came off the strait in long shoves that made the dormitory walls groan on their pegs, and rain came not in drops but in sheets, in ropes, in whole torn lengths of sea thrown at the land. The roof tiles chattered. Down on the quay, something heavy broke loose and dragged, shrieking, until it smashed against a bollard, and Mara heard men shouting into the dark, muffled and futile, their voices shredded sideways by the wind.

Nobody slept. The dormitory was full of indentured workers lying rigid in their bunks, listening to the world try to tear itself loose, and every one of them was thinking the same thought in different words: *a storm like this sinks fleets.*

Mara lay with their eyes open and let the thought arrive, because refusing it would only give it power. Forty divers. A rival fleet. A storm that came too fast, too neat, with signal flags that never flew. Their father going down into the green dark with his lungs full of honest air, believing to the last that the weather was weather.

They had believed it too, for ten years. Weather was God's temper; nobody could arrest it.

Now Mara knew better, and the knowledge changed the sound of the storm entirely. Every gust against the shutters sounded like the harbormaster taking bribes. Every wave that broke over the mole sounded like a hull going down with its hatches chained. The storm outside was innocent. The storm eleven years ago had been manufactured, ordered, invoiced—there had probably been an entry for it, Mara thought with a fury that tasted like copper, somewhere in the third-floor ledgers, in a fair clerkly hand: *miscellaneous marine losses, forty souls, write down to salvage.*

Their father had been miscellaneous.

Mara lay in the dark and recited the testimony to themselves, silently, the way other people prayed. *I, Elias Thorn, clerk to the Halloway Trading Company, do set down what I know...* Forty names. They had memorized them as lists are memorized, in rhythm, in groups of five, and now those rhythms lived in their body deeper than fear could reach. *Joren Vane. Diver, aged thirty-one. Boat: the Kestrel's Wing.* Reciting it was like touching a scar to confirm it still hurt, which was a way of confirming the person underneath was still there.

Somewhere in the middle of the night the wind shifted and the rain turned to sleet, ticking against the shutters like small impatient fingers, and Mara finally slept, badly, in fragments, dreaming of a gangway that lengthened as they walked it.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, rinsed clean and trembling. The storm had blown itself hoarse overnight; what remained was a long rolling swell and a sky scoured down to bare bone. The harbor was strewn with wreckage—one punt sunk at its moorings, the drag-mark gouged down the quay boards, nets hung in the rigging of the moored ships like the sea had tried to climb aboard and been beaten back.

But the Morning Tide rode at anchor, black-hulled and patient, her lamps still burning yellow in the gray light. She had held. Ships that mattered to companies always held.

Mara stood in the breakfast queue with a tin bowl of barley mush and watched the harbor wake up, and watched Hale watch it too.

He was on the quay in a dry gray coat, speaking with the harbormaster's replacement and two constables, and even at fifty yards Mara could read the shape of the conversation from his hands. Pointing—at the gate. At the customs house steps. At the gangway, where the Tide's boatswain was already bellowing at stevedores. Hale's finger touched each checkpoint like a man counting coins, and then he raised one hand, spread five fingers, folded four down, and raised another hand.

Doubling them. All three. Exactly as Mara had feared in the dark and hoped was only fear.

The line shuffled forward. Mara stared at their mush and ran the numbers with the cold clarity that terror eventually refines into. Double guards meant double searches at the gate and the customs house. Double at the gangway meant the pearl knife with its hollow handle—the plan they'd built for the final thirty steps—would now pass through four pairs of hands instead of two, four sets of eyes, and hollow things have a sound, a wrongness, when tapped.

And the sailing was at this morning's tide. Hours. The sun was barely up; the tide would turn mid-morning. There was no time left to build new plans, only to bend old ones.

"Mara Vane."

They nearly spilled the bowl. The name had come from behind, quiet, in a voice they knew—Petrel's voice should have been impossible, Petrel was in a cell—and Mara turned with their heart hammering against the hidden seam of their coat.

It was one of the prison trusties, a stooped old sweeper named Cobb, holding his broom like a pilgrim's staff. His eyes were red-rimmed and darting.

"Message," he breathed. "From the old man. Cost me a night's sleep to get it up here, so listen." He glanced toward the quay, toward Hale's gray coat, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "He says—*the harbormaster keeps his own copy.* Says the retired one, across the strait. Says your friend's boat went out before the bars closed." Cobb's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Says the old man's holding fast. Says dawn comes."

The queue moved. The mush went cold in the bowl. Mara stood very still while the words settled into place like ballast, and the relief was so large and so sudden that it frightened them—relief was a loosening, and loosened people made mistakes.

The second proof was crossing the water. The harbormaster's own deposition, independent of anything Mara carried, independent of memory, independent of a hollow knife handle. Two proofs or none, Thorn's draft had said, and now the second proof was out on the gray strait, riding the same swells that had delayed everything else.

Which meant that everything now depended on one thing only: Mara Vane, and the testimony sewn into their mind, walking up a doubled-guard gangway before this morning's tide ran out.

Cobb melted back toward the cells with his broom. Mara tipped the cold mush out onto the cobbles for the gulls, because eating was suddenly impossible, and joined the work detail forming up for the loading.

Across the quay, Corvus Hale turned his head—slowly, deliberately, the way a man turns when he already knows what he'll find—and looked directly at Mara for the first time in days.

And smiled.

"Vane," called the foreman. "You're on the Tide's cargo line. First group across the gangway."

The tide was turning. The guards were doubling. And Mara's name was on the first list through.

They buttoned their coat to the throat, felt the small hard secret of the seed pod press against their ribs like a knuckle, and stepped forward into the wind.
