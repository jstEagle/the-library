## 28. The Customs House Trap

The storm had passed in the night the way storms do in Saltmere—noisily, and then all at once, as if the sea had merely cleared its throat and gone back to sleep. What it left behind was a city rinsed and gleaming, gulls screaming over the fish market, puddles on the customs house steps reflecting a sky the color of unpolished pewter. And what it left behind for Mara Vane was one morning. One tide. One crossing of a marble-floored room where every second was a coin that could be spent only once.

They had dressed carefully. That was the irony of it—the whole art of invisibility reduced to buttons and collars. The promotion order Hale himself had signed hung in a leather folder under their arm, its seal still smelling faintly of wax and something else, something like ambition. *Junior Ledger Clerk, Provisional Appointment.* Four words that had never been meant to protect anyone, least of all the person they were written about. Hale had dangled them like bait; Mara intended to swallow the hook and walk away with the fishing line.

*You are not smuggling anything,* they told themselves on the steps, watching their own boots climb through the reflected sky. *You are a clerk delivering documents. You are the most boring thing in this building.*

The customs house swallowed sound the way deep water did. Voices went in and came out flattened, official, stripped of everything human. The ceiling arched overhead in whitewashed ribs, and morning light fell through tall windows in long dusty columns, so that clerks moved between the light and the shadow like fish passing through kelp. Ink and paper and lamp oil. Beneath it all, faintly, the brine of the harbor seeping up through the very stones—a building standing with one foot in the water that had taken everything from everyone Mara had ever loved.

The manifest desk was third from the left. Mara knew this because they had memorized it, because they had spent two sleepless nights walking the route in their head until the customs house was more familiar in imagination than their own dormitory. Third from the left. A green-shaded lamp. A man named Ovid Brack, fifty years old, ink-stained to the second knuckle, famous among the dock workers for two things: his refusal of bribes and his memory.

It was the memory that frightened Mara now. It should have frightened them before. It hadn't, because fear arrived late these days, always late, knocking politely at the door after the disaster had already let itself in.

Mara joined the short queue of carriers and junior clerks waiting to have manifests stamped. Ahead of them a fish-factor argued cheerfully about tonnage duties; behind them a boy whistled the tune sailors used for raising anchor, off-key, endlessly. Mara counted breaths. In for four, out for six, the way old Petrel had taught them for holding air below—the same trick for holding nerve above it. *Panic spends your air fastest,* Petrel said. *Fear is just breathing done badly.*

When their turn came, Mara set the folder on the desk, opened it, and slid the manifest across the green glass with both hands, steady, unhurried, the gesture of a person who had done this ten thousand times.

"Manifest for the *Morning Tide*, sailing on the morning tide," they said, and almost smiled at the redundancy of it. "Cargo loading begins at eight bells. I'm to deliver the stamped copy to the harbormaster's office before boarding."

Brack took the page. He did not read it immediately—that was the first thing wrong. He looked at Mara instead, over the top of the paper, with the mild, patient attention of a man who had spent thirty years learning to hear the lie inside an honest voice.

"Name?"

"Mara Vane. Junior ledger clerk, Halloway Trading Company." The title sat strangely in their mouth, sweet and rotten at once. "Provisional."

"Vane." He said it without inflection, tasting nothing. Then he bent to the manifest, and Mara watched his eyes move—not skimming, the way most men read a manifest, but walking down it line by line, the way you'd walk a beach after a storm, looking for what the water left.

The manifest itself was clean. Mara had built it clean: pearl-shell cargo, salted fish, general freight, the ordinary bloodless inventory of a company ship. It was not the manifest that would kill them. It was the hand.

Because Thorn had taught them penmanship the way other men taught children prayers—not as skill but as salvation. *A hand is a confession,* Thorn had said once, tapping his own elegant, cramped script. *Every loop tells where you learned, every slant tells who you wanted to be.* And Mara, copying ten thousand entries by candlelight, had absorbed Thorn's hand so thoroughly that their own had dissolved into it—until last night, hunched over the customs form with shaking fingers, when they had made the fatal error of being careful. Too careful. The letters too perfect, the flourishes reproduced rather than lived in. A copy of a copy, and somewhere in the downstroke of the capital M, the ghost of Elias Thorn looking over their shoulder.

Brack reached the bottom of the page. He set it down. He picked it up again.

"You're left-handed," he said.

"No, sir." The answer came out smooth, and then Mara heard what they'd said, and the floor tilted half a degree. Because the question hadn't been *are you*. It had been a trap of the smallest kind—a statement shaped like a question—and they had answered it as if it were real.

Brack's eyebrows rose a fraction. Somewhere behind Mara, the whistling boy stopped.

"I didn't ask," Brack said mildly. "I observed. You smudge from right to left. See here—" he turned the manifest around and tapped the lower corner, and there it was, the heel of their palm dragged through the drying ink of their own signature, a gray smear like a cloud seen through water. "Left-handed writers drag. Right-handed ones don't. So either you wrote this with your wrong hand—" he smiled, thin as a knife laid flat on a table, "—or someone else wrote it, and you've been carrying another person's hand around in yours long enough to forget which one is yours."

The hall went very quiet around Mara, or perhaps it didn't, and it was only their own blood making noise in their ears. The columns of light stood where they had stood. The fish-factor had moved along. The queue shuffled forward. The world continued, indifferent, while Mara stood on a marble floor above a cellar full of drowned names and understood that they had maybe ten seconds in which to decide how to be alive.

*Panic spends your air fastest.*

They did not look at the door. Looking at doors was the first thing guilty people did; Thorn had told them that too, or perhaps they had invented it afterward and given him the credit, the dead were useful that way. Instead Mara did the thing that felt like stepping off a quay into black water: they leaned slightly forward, lowered their voice, and became, visibly, reluctantly, an insider.

"Mr. Brack," they said, "may I be frank, since you've already read my hand better than my own mother could have?"

Brack said nothing, which was permission enough.

"This manifest isn't mine to defend. I copied it under instruction. Three days ago I was a diver with wet feet and no surname worth the ink." They let a small, bitter smile touch their mouth—the smile of a person admitting to ambition, which is the sin every clerk forgives fastest because every clerk has committed it. "Then Mr. Corvus Hale pulled me out of the sorting sheds himself. Promotion order, provisional appointment, signed and sealed. You'll find his name on my letter of authority." They turned the folder around, opened it, and laid the document beside the manifest with two fingers, the way one lays down a card one has been bluffing with all night.

Hale's signature glared up from the page. The man's actual hand, actual wax, actual seal—the instrument of their hunting reforged, overnight, into their armor. *He offered me freedom if I gave him Thorn's papers,* Mara thought, watching Brack read. *So I took the freedom he offered and kept the papers. There's a word for that. I suspect it's the same word he uses for me.*

"Hale," Brack repeated. His voice had changed temperature. Not warmth—nothing so kind—but recognition, the wariness of a man who knows the name of the wolf even when the wolf sends its paperwork ahead. "Corvus Hale is having clerks deliver manifests now."

"He's having clerks deliver everything now," Mara said, and here they took the risk that felt like the whole dive balanced on one breath: they let their eyes flick, briefly, meaningfully, toward the far end of the hall, toward the records annex, and then back. "Since the Thorn business. You'll have heard. A senior clerk dies suddenly, his effects inventoried, his pages pulled for review. Mr. Hale wants every document touching the fleet accounts re-copied, verified, and delivered under personal cover. No loose hands." They paused, and let the pause do its work. "Which is why my hand looks like a dead man's hand, Mr. Brack. I spent three weeks copying beside Elias Thorn. We all did. Half the ledger room writes like him now—it's how we were trained. If you're hunting for irregularity, you'll find it in every entry from that room since midsummer. Including, I'd imagine, in things Mr. Hale has countersigned."

There it was. The blade turned flat, then offered hilt-first: *if you flag my forgery, you flag his office; you invite Corvus Hale's attention into your quiet green-shaded life; you become a name on somebody's list.* Mara watched the arithmetic move behind Brack's eyes and hated themselves for knowing exactly how it worked—for knowing, because they had spent ten years in a company that ran on this exact current, the small fear flowing downhill toward the small people, pooling at the desks where the bribes were refused and the silence was purchased anyway, cheaper than coin, paid in the currency of not-being-bothered.

Brack looked at the manifest. Looked at Hale's order. Looked at Mara's face, which Mara held very still, the way one holds still in shallow water over a stingray—because movement is what draws the barb.

"The storm cost you a day," Brack said finally. "Sailing's tomorrow's tide now."

"Yes, sir. Which is why everything is rushed, and why the rush looks like sloppiness, and why I'd be grateful if we could agree that it's neither."

Another silence. A pigeon clattered somewhere in the rafters. The ink on the smear of Mara's signature had dried to permanence; whatever happened next would dry just as permanently.

Brack picked up the stamp. It was an ugly thing, brass-handled, worn smooth by forty years of other men's decisions. He weighed it once in his hand, and Mara felt the whole world balance on it—the orchard deed sewn against their ribs, the seed pod in its tin, the forty names riding folded in the lining of their coat like passengers too poor for deck passage, and beneath all of it the single sentence they would say if the stamp came down on the other side of the page instead: *Then fetch Mr. Hale, and tell him his provisional clerk is detained at the customs house, and see how long it takes him to arrive.*

But the stamp came down. Thump. Red ink bloomed across the corner of the manifest like a wound in miniature, neat and round and official. APPROVED. Brack pulled the copy, stamped its twin, and slid both across the glass.

"Fleet accounts," he said quietly, not looking up, sliding a blank scrap of paper after them as if absently. "Midsummer onward. If the review finds anything... irregular... it will want corroborating from outside the company. Independent hands." Now he looked up, and his eyes were tired and old and held something that might have been courage, or might only have been the particular recklessness of a man near pension. "The magistrate across the strait keeps afternoon hours. Tell him Ovid Brack remembers the year the flags stayed in their box."

Mara's breath went out of them slowly, controlled, four counts in, six counts out, because breaking apart in front of a customs officer was not on the day's schedule. They took the scrap of paper without letting their fingers tremble—barely—and tucked it into the folder beside Hale's order, two documents from two different worlds lying flat against each other like sleeping strangers.

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me." Brack was already reaching for the next manifest in the tray, already becoming again the bored functionary, the green lamp, the smudge of routine. "Thank whoever taught you that a good lie needs one true thing in it. Yours was the smear." He glanced up once more, and almost smiled. "Walk slower next time. Guilt hurries. Nobody hurries who's only carrying paperwork."

Mara walked slower. It was the hardest walk of their life, harder than the gangway would be, because the body wanted to run and running was death. Out through the columns of light, past the queue, past the whistling boy who had started up again, down the steps into the rinsed bright morning where the gulls screamed and the harbor lay glittering and treacherous as a fresh-minted coin.

Only when they reached the fish market's noise and stink did they allow themselves to stop, press their back against a post crusted with salt, and breathe. Their legs felt boiled. Their shirt was soaked through at the spine. In the folder against their chest, two stamps' worth of red ink and one dead man's memory of handwriting had carried them through the second gate, and there was still the third, and the third had Corvus Hale standing at the foot of it.

They unfolded Brack's scrap of paper with numb fingers. On it, in the clerk's cramped official hand, was a single line—an address across the strait, and beneath it, five words:

*The flags never left the box.*

Mara read it twice and folded it away with the others. Another voice for the testimony. Another name that would need protecting when the inquiry came, because this was the lesson the week kept teaching: every person who helped became a person who could be hurt, and the list of those was growing—Petrel behind bars, Sable vanished, Brack now written into a story he couldn't yet know the ending of.

They pushed off the post and turned toward the quay, toward the *Morning Tide*'s masts rising above the warehouse roofs like bare trees in winter—

And stopped.

At the mouth of Cooper's Lane, half in shadow, leaning on the wall with his weight on one side the way a man does when the other side hurts, stood a figure in a ruined coat. Even at forty paces, even bruised and limping and gray-faced, there was no mistaking the set of those shoulders, the angle of that jaw.

Sable had come back. And behind the pain in their eyes was something else—something urgent, something that made them push off the wall and start toward Mara through the market crowd, mouth already forming words that the gulls and the fishmongers swallowed whole.
