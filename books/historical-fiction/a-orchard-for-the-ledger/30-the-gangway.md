## 30. The Gangway

The storm had passed in the night, and Saltmere woke rinsed and gleaming, the cobbles still dark with rain, gulls wheeling and screaming over the harbor as though the sky owed them something. The *Morning Tide* rode at her berth with her decks crowded and her hatches open, taking on cargo for the crossing. She would sail on this morning's tide—the only tide, now; there would be no other—and somewhere aboard her, in a cabin with a porthole and a locked dispatch box, sat a magistrate with a name Mara had only ever seen stamped on writs.

Mara stood at the mouth of Pier Three with a pearl knife in their coat pocket and their heart going like a diver's pulse in deep water—slow, deliberate, spending itself carefully.

They had sewn the deed into the lining of their coat three nights ago, in the dark of the dormitory, working by touch alone while the other indentured slept around them like sacks of grain. The seed pod was there too, wrapped in oilcloth, flat against their ribs where it rose and fell with every breath. But the letter—the letter could not be sewn anywhere. Hale had learned to search linings. Hale had learned to search everything. So the letter lived now inside the handle of a pearl knife, rolled tight as a mussel's tongue, wedged into the hollow that Joren Vane's people had once bored to balance the blade in a diver's grip. It was an old knife, older than the indenture, one of the few things that had come down to Mara from their father besides a name the ledgers had crossed out. The handle was horn-dark whalebone, worn smooth by twenty years of hands, and when Mara held it, their thumb fit into the groove their father's thumb had worn first. They had never thought much about that groove before. Now they understood: the knife had always been meant to carry something hidden. Perhaps he had carried pearls that way. Perhaps he had carried messages for men who paid in silence. The dead left instructions everywhere if you finally learned how to look.

"Workers for the *Tide* load-out, form up!" A harbormaster's man with a brass whistle and a voice like gravel in a bucket was striding down the quay. "Single line! Papers out! You'll be checked at the gangway and checked again aboard, so don't waste anybody's morning!"

Mara took their place in the line of loaders—forty-some indentured men and women, most of them divers seconded to cargo work because the pearl beds were closed after the storm and Halloway did not believe in paying idle hands. Petrel was not among them; Petrel was still behind bars in the company lockup, and every hour that passed without word was another stone laid on Mara's chest. They had sent the guard his pearls. They had sent the message: *hold fast, dawn comes.* Whether dawn would come for Petrel before it came for Mara was a question they could not afford to answer yet.

The line shuffled forward. Ahead, the gangway rose from the quay to the *Tide*'s rail, a plank span perhaps thirty steps long, flanked on either side by constables with staves and, worse, by two of Halloway's own security men with clipboards—company eyes, checking each worker against the manifest, turning out pockets, running hands along seams. And at the foot of the gangway, where the boards began, stood Corvus Hale.

He had dressed for the weather in a grey wool coat beaded with rain, and he stood with his hands clasped behind him in an attitude of such mild patience that he might have been a man waiting to greet a beloved cousin off the boat. His face was pleasant. His smile, when it appeared, arrived slowly, like something surfacing.

Mara's body performed its old trick: it went quiet. The breath shallowed. The face smoothed into the blank competence of a worker who has nowhere interesting to be. This was the discipline nine years of indenture had built—not courage, nothing so grand, but the practiced erasure of oneself, the art of being furniture. *History happens elsewhere,* Mara had believed once, and had built a life inside that belief the way a limpet builds inside its shell.

But the letter pressed against the inside of the knife handle, and the knife pressed against their hip, and history, they knew now, happened exactly here—on this quay, under these gulls, in the space between their ribs and their spine.

*Thirty steps,* they told themselves. *You only have to own thirty steps.*

The line moved. A loader ahead of Mara—a big Salish man called Brannick who snored like surf—was stopped, turned out, waved through. Another was sent back to the end of the line for missing papers, protesting in a low urgent voice until a constable's stave tapped his shoulder and he went. Each delay stretched time thin as wire.

Then it was Mara's turn.

"Name." The company man with the clipboard didn't look up.

"Mara Vane." Their own name came out steady, which surprised them. Nine years of answering roll-call had made the name automatic, a coin dropped into a slot.

"Vane." The pen scratched. "Manifest says you're assigned hold two, below-deck bales. Knife?"

Mara's blood stopped, started again. "Diving knife, sir. Company issue." A lie, small and smooth. "For cutting bale ties. Harbor office said tools allowed."

The company man held out his hand.

There was no way around it. Refusal was confession. Mara drew the knife from their coat and laid it in the man's palm, and watched—with the peculiar double vision of terror, seeing everything at twice its size and half its speed—as the man turned it over. The blade flashed dull grey. His thumb slid along the whalebone handle, found the groove, rested there a moment.

Mara thought of their father's hands. Thought of forty names. Thought of Elias Thorn coughing blood onto a ledger page and pressing wax-sealed paper into their fingers, whispering *the tide, the tide*, as though the sea itself had been waiting all those years for someone to carry what it kept.

"Balance is good on these," the man said, almost to himself. He hefted it once, twice. The rolled letter sat six inches from his thumb, thinner than a pencil, tight as a secret. "Fine. Board."

He handed it back. Mara took it, and the weight of it was the weight of everything—Thorn's last breath, Joren Vane's last dive, Petrel's cell, Sable's ruined face, the orchard that no longer existed except as ink in a tin. They walked toward the gangway.

Toward Hale.

He was watching them. Of course he was. He had been watching them since the ledger room, since the promotion offer, since the night the dormitory was searched and Mara came back to find their straw pallet gutted and their few possessions strewn like gull-picked bones. He knew there was something. He did not know what, or where, or whether it was worth the price of certainty. That uncertainty was the whole width of the world, and Mara had to walk through it.

"Vane." Hale's voice was warm. That was the horror of Hale—he sounded kind. He sounded like a man who had your interests at heart, who remembered your mother's name, who wanted nothing but to help. "Promoted staff, walking cargo like common labor. That's dedication. Or desperation." He smiled the long smile. "Which do you suppose I'd guess?"

"I'm strong, Mr. Hale," Mara said, eyes lowered, voice flat as ballast stone. "And the beds are closed. Idle hands draw wages for nothing."

"Hm." Hale stepped closer, close enough that Mara could smell the bay rum on him, could see the fine stitching of his coat, the rain-beads caught in the wool of his sleeve. His gaze moved over them with the thoroughness of a customs search—not leering, not cruel, just *inventorying*, the way he'd inventoried Thorn's effects, lifting each item of a man's life and setting it down again slightly less his than before. "You know," he said conversationally, "old Thorn liked you. Spoke well of you, near the end. Said you had a head for figures. Said you could read a page and keep it." The smile widened by a degree. "Funny thing to say about an indentured. Most of them can't read at all."

"The harbor office teaches letters for tally-work, sir."

"It does." Hale let the silence sit between them like a third person. Somewhere behind him a winch groaned; a crate swung overhead on its rope, shadow sliding across the wet boards. "Well. Best get aboard. Tide won't wait, and neither will I." He stepped aside—an unhurried, courteous step—and gestured toward the gangway with one gloved hand. "Mind the gap. Boards are slick after rain. Wouldn't want anything falling into the water. Things fall into the water here all the time, and the water keeps what it's given."

Mara's feet moved. There was no decision in it; the body simply obeyed the plan that had been built plank by plank over two sleepless weeks. First step onto the gangway. The board flexed. Below, between the planks' gaps, the harbor water showed green-black and patient, flecked with foam from the storm.

Second step. Third. A constable at the rail checked their chit against the manifest, nodded, waved them on. Fifth step, sixth—the gangway swayed gently with the swell, and Mara let the sway take them, made their walk the loose, tired walk of a laborer, shoulders rounded, gaze down.

Ninth step. Tenth. Halfway. Behind them they heard Hale's voice again, addressing the next worker, warm as banked coals: "Name? ... Turn out your pockets, friend. Slowly, now."

Fourteenth step. Fifteen. The *Tide*'s deck rose to meet them, smelling of tar and wet hemp and the ghost of the fish meal she'd carried the season before. A bosun's mate pointed Mara aft, toward the hold ladder. Sixteenth step, seventeenth—

A hand closed on their shoulder.

Every sound in the world dropped away except their own heartbeat, enormous, filling the harbor.

It was not Hale. It was the boatswain—Old Cray, gray-whiskered, rope-scarred, the man whose palm had taken Mara's pearls two nights ago in the shadow of the net sheds, who had sworn on his dead wife's name to carry the second packet across the strait to the retired harbormaster. He leaned in close, breath thick with cheap tobacco, and murmured, for all the world like a man giving routine orders:

"Below-deck, hold two. Straight down, no wandering." A pause, one beat too long. His hand tightened, just slightly, on Mara's shoulder—fingers finding the spot where the coat's seam hid the deed, where the ribs hid the seed pod. Then he released them and straightened, barking loud enough for the whole gangway to hear: "Move along! You're holding up my tide!"

Mara moved. Eighteenth step. Nineteenth. The handprint on their shoulder burned like a brand, and they could not tell—if they had been asked, could not have said—which it had been: warning, or claim, or the touch of a man confirming to himself the weight of what he'd sold. Pearls bought loyalty the way bait bought fish. Sometimes the fish came up. Sometimes the hook came up instead.

Twenty-third step. Twenty-fourth. The rail. They stepped off the gangway onto the *Morning Tide*'s deck, and the plank gave one final shudder beneath their boots, and it was done.

They were aboard.

No one seized them. No whistle blew. The gulls went on screaming their ancient grievances, and the winches went on groaning, and the line of workers shuffled on behind them, and Mara stood on free-ish planks in a foreign country of rope and canvas with a letter hidden in a dead man's knife and a living man's handprint cooling on their shoulder. Thirty steps. The longest thirty steps of a life, and no one had even looked up.

* * *

Hold two was dimness and rope-smell and stacked bales lashed under canvas, lit by a single swinging lantern that turned every shadow slowly round like slow thoughts. Mara found their assigned station, took up the loading hook, and worked. That was the genius of the plan's last layer, the part Thorn had never taught them but survival had: hide in plain motion. A worker who works is invisible. A worker standing still is a question.

So they worked, hauling and stacking, sweat rising under their collar where the deed lay sewn, and all the while their mind ran the letter word by word, the way they had run it every waking hour since the burning. *I, Elias Thorn, senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company, being of sound mind and failing body...* Forty names. Joren Vane, fourth from the top, entered in the founders' private schedule as *expendable asset, fleet B.* The bribe to Harbormaster Quill, itemized. The false storm signal, logged and then unlogged. Mara had erased those pages from the company copy with Hale watching over their shoulder, and memorized each one before the ink of the falsification dried. What was written in fire could not be searched out of any lining in Saltmere.

Through the open hatch above, a slice of gray-bright sky. Voices. Boots on deck. Once, distinctly, Hale's voice—aboard already, moving along the rail, speaking to the mate in that warm terrible courtesy—and Mara's hands did not stop, did not falter, though the cold went through them like seawater through cloth.

And once, looking up through the hatch for a ration of air, Mara saw the boatswain at the rail, talking quietly to a man in a constable's oilskin who had come aboard with the pilot. Cray's face wore the expression of a man discussing cargo weights. The constable nodded, wrote nothing down, went back down the gangway.

Or maybe it was nothing. Maybe constables came aboard a dozen times a day. Maybe a hand on a shoulder was only a hand.

The tide bell rang from the point. Lines began coming off the bollards, heavy and wet, slapping the dock like things alive. The *Morning Tide* sighed away from the pier, gathered herself, and turned her bow toward the strait, toward the magistrate, toward whatever waited on the far side of the water.

Mara stood at the rail among the workers, the knife against their hip, the letter against the knife, the words against their heart, and watched Saltmere slide backward—the harbor gate, the customs house, the net sheds, the ledger room's third-floor windows catching the light like closed eyes.

Ahead lay open water. Behind lay everything they had ever been.

At the far rail, half-hidden by the deck cargo, Old Cray turned and looked at Mara—just looked, a long level look across fifty feet of pitching deck—and then touched his cap brim, and smiled.

It was not a kind smile. It was not Hale's smile either. It was the smile of a man counting, and Mara's stomach dropped through the hull into the green-black water, because they understood, all at once and too late, that the tide carried everyone aboard toward something—but not everyone toward the same thing.
