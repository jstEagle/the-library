## 31. Betrayal at the Rail

The Morning Tide rode the gray water like a held breath.

Mara felt her in the soles of their feet before they saw anything—the deep, patient sway of a ship at anchor, different from the dock's dead stillness. She was loaded to her marks: salted fish in the forehold, timber amidships, and aft, under canvas that would not rise until morning tide, a cabin where a magistrate from across the strait sat with his clerk and his seals and his slow, deliberate breakfast. Mara knew this because Petrel had told them, and because Sable had told Petrel, and because in Saltmere every secret traveled by the same roads as the fish—downhill, fast, and smelling of brine.

They boarded with the second cargo shift, six workers deep in a line that moved at the pace of suspicion. The gangway was behind them now. The longest thirty steps of their life were thirty steps behind them, and the pearl knife with its hollow handle sat against their hip like a second heartbeat, and they had walked past Corvus Hale himself without meeting his eyes, and they were alive.

*Alive*, Mara thought, and found the word had changed shape overnight. It used to mean *not yet drowned*. Now it meant something closer to *free*, or at least standing on the deck of a ship that might carry them there.

"Vane." The boatswain's voice, low, from the shadow of the mainmast. "A word."

Daggert. Broad as a capstan, hands like knotted rope, a man who had taken eleven pearls from Mara two nights ago in the dark between the fish sheds and had said, *the packet goes into the magistrate's own hands, or my name isn't Daggert.* His name, it turned out, was not Daggert—or if it was, it was for sale like everything else in Saltmere.

Mara crossed to him. Around them the cargo shift worked on, ropes creaking, barrels rolling on their rims, no one looking, everyone always not-looking; it was the harbor's oldest trade.

"You're supposed to be forward," Daggert said. "Loading the timber. Not standing aft. Not anywhere near the aftercabin."

"I'm where I'm told to be."

"You're where I tell you to be until this ship sails." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and Mara saw then what they should have seen two nights ago, what they would carry to their grave for not seeing: he could not hold their eyes. His gaze skated off them toward the gangway, toward the quay, toward the customs house roof where a man in a gray coat stood very still among the gulls.

Mara's stomach went cold and slow, like water filling a boot.

"How much," Mara said quietly.

Daggert's jaw worked. "Don't."

"How much did he pay you?"

"More than your pearls. More than you'd have earned in—" He stopped himself, which was answer enough, and then, because men like him always needed to make their treachery sound like arithmetic, he finished it: "Twenty years' wages. Vane. Twenty years. You'd have sold me out for less and we both know it."

The strange thing, Mara thought later, was how calm the moment was. No thunderclap. The gulls kept screaming. A barrel thumped down into the hold. Somewhere aft, behind the polished door of the aftercabin, a magistrate was eating eggs.

What Mara felt was not rage. It was the floor dropping—not the way it had dropped when they read Thorn's letter, all vertigo and falling. This was quieter. It was the feeling of having counted on a rope and hearing, one fiber at a time, it begin to sing.

"So Hale knows I'm aboard," Mara said.

"He knows someone's carrying something worth twenty years. He doesn't know what, or where." Daggert's voice dropped further. "That's why I'm telling you and not just letting him come. Get off the ship, Vane. Walk down the gangway, go back to the sheds, and let whatever you've got stay lost. He'll take the ship apart otherwise. He'll take *you* apart. I've seen what he does when he's patient, and he is never more patient than when he's smiling."

"And Petrel?" The word came out of Mara before they could weigh it. "He has Petrel in a cell on a charge of theft that's a lie, and every hour I run, she sits in it longer."

"That's not your rope to hold."

"It's exactly my rope to hold. That's the whole point of it." Mara looked past him at the water, at the far shore lying low and blue beyond the strait, close enough to swim to in a dream and impossibly far in the morning light. "You took my pearls, Daggert. Take one thing more. When the guards come up that gangway—and they will—you'll stand aside. Not help me. Just stand aside, and be slow about whatever he asks you first. Twenty years' wages buys a lot. Let it buy me ten seconds."

Daggert stared at them. Something moved behind his face—shame, maybe, or only the discomfort of a man discovering the goods he'd sold were worth more than he'd charged.

"You're going to stand there," he said, "on this deck, and wait for him."

"I've been invisible my whole life," Mara said. "I'd like to see what happens when I stop."

* * *

Corvus Hale stood on the customs house roof because roofs were where he did his best thinking, and because the wind up here smelled of tar and cold iron and nothing else—no rot, no fish, none of the softness that made people stupid.

Below him the Morning Tide lay at her moorings, fat with cargo, her rails lined with the small bent figures of indentured loaders. One of those figures was the Thorn creature. He was nearly certain of it now. Nearly was a word Hale despised; his whole profession lived in the space between nearly and certainly, and men died in that space, though rarely men as careful as himself.

The boatswain's report had been precise, as purchased things usually were. The Vane child had boarded with the second shift. Had spoken briefly with Daggert. Had not gone forward to the timber. Was standing aft, near the aftercabin, doing nothing—which was itself a confession, because nobody on a working deck did nothing unless they were waiting for something.

Hale turned the problem over the way he turned all problems: by weighing what could be proven.

What could be proven: an old clerk had died of consumption, leaving behind a tidy room and no papers of consequence—Hale had inventoried the room himself, twice, and burned what little he'd found. An indentured diver of no education had been promoted, oddly, and had refused, oddly, to surrender any documents the dead man might have left. A boatswain had been bought. And somewhere between a dying man's desk and this gray morning, a piece of paper existed that could hang four founders of the Halloway Trading Company from the customs house yardarm if it ever reached the wrong desk across the strait.

What could not be proven: that the paper was on this ship. That the Vane child carried it rather than having passed it already—to the smuggler girl, perhaps, whose cart had crossed the harbor gate nine days ago full of fish and, Hale now suspected, something else. The girl had vanished for six days afterward. She had reappeared beaten half to death and had given up nothing under questioning, which meant either she knew nothing or she was tougher than she looked, and Hale had stopped believing in tough a long time ago. People broke. They broke like green wood, suddenly and along the grain of whatever they loved most.

But broken people also lied, and the smuggler girl had lied beautifully, and while she lay bleeding in her loft above the net sheds, some second route had opened that Hale had not found yet. He knew it the way he knew weather coming: the harbormaster, old Rennick, retired to his cottage across the strait, had not answered two polite letters. Someone had reached him first.

So: two proofs, or the beginnings of two. A paper on the ship, maybe. A deposition across the water, probably. And a magistrate aboard the Morning Tide who would sail on the morning tide with whatever fell into his lap.

Hale did not intend to let anything fall into anyone's lap.

"Mr. Quill," he said to the constable shivering beside him—a thin man with a wet cough and a warrant signed by a harbor court justice who owed Halloway more than he owed the law. "How many men can you put on that gangway in five minutes?"

"Six, sir. Eight if I pull them off the quay watch."

"Pull them off the quay watch. Board the ship. Search the aftercabin last—slowly, loudly, so the magistrate knows exactly what we're doing and why. If the Vane child runs, take them quietly. If the child stands there—" Hale smiled, and below him a gull lifted screaming off the rail, "—then we do this politely. Papers, warrants, apologies. Politeness is a search without witnesses, Mr. Quill. Remember that."

"And if the magistrate objects?"

"The magistrate," Hale said, watching the small figure at the Morning Tide's rail turn its face toward the water, "objects to things that happen where he can see them. So we will make sure he sees nothing but courtesy."

* * *

They came up the gangway eight abreast, boots ringing on the planks, and the whole deck changed key.

Mara heard it before they saw it—the loader line faltering, the barrel-rollers straightening, that animal silence that falls over the indentured when authority arrives, older than any of them, learned in the cradle. Then constables' coats, dark blue, damp at the shoulders, and Mr. Quill's narrow face under his hat, and behind them, unhurried, taking the gangway steps like a man boarding his own yacht, Corvus Hale.

He looked better than he had any right to look. Gray coat brushed clean, gloves fresh, that long patient smile already fitted to his face like a tool he'd oiled overnight.

"Good morning," Hale said, to the deck in general, pleasantly. "Routine inspection. Papers and persons, nothing personal. The harbor court's compliments to Captain Reyes—we won't delay the tide more than an hour."

An hour. Mara stood at the rail with their hands loose at their sides and did the arithmetic they'd been doing since dawn: the knife handle against their hip, forty divers' names folded inside their skull, the aftercabin door fifteen feet away with a magistrate behind it who did not yet know why he'd been given a cabin on a cargo ship instead of a berth on the mail packet. Ten seconds, they'd asked Daggert for. Daggert was nowhere in sight.

Quill's men began at the bow. Mara watched them work—quick hands, practiced, lifting sacking, running fingers along seams, tipping a cook's pot of chowder onto the deck boards without apology. The crew watched too, in the sideways way of people memorizing everything and admitting nothing. Old habits. The harbor's oldest trade.

"Mara Vane."

Hale had come to stand beside them at the rail, close enough that Mara could smell the bay rum on him, absurdly civilized, and beneath it the cold stone smell of the customs house that clung to him like a second skin.

"Mr. Hale."

"You look well. Promotion agrees with you." He rested his gloved hand on the rail, an inch from theirs. "I confess I've been worried. A young person alone in a big city, carrying burdens too heavy—it keeps me awake at night. So I've brought friends to help set the burden down."

"I don't carry anything, Mr. Hale. You searched my dormitory twice. You searched me at the gate this morning. I'm an open book."

"Oh, I know." The smile widened by a degree, and Mara understood, with the clarity of a diver seeing a shark's shadow cross the sand below, that Hale wanted them to run. Every word was baited for it. A runner was guilty; a runner could be taken hard and questioned harder, and whatever they carried could be taken off their body on some quiet quay where magistrates' windows didn't reach. "Open books are my favorite kind. But you understand my difficulty. A man dies. A girl gets beaten. A boatswain gets rich. And everywhere, like a bad smell, is you." He turned, leaning his elbows on the rail, gazing out at the water with the contentment of a man watching his own trap close. "Give it to me, and you walk off this ship free. Actual papers, Vane. Your name struck from the company rolls. I have the document in my coat at this moment. All you have to do is hand me a piece of paper, and I hand you your life."

For one heartbeat—one long, swaying heartbeat of the ship against her moorings—Mara let themselves feel it. Freedom, printed and sealed. Their own name on a page that meant *yours*. Nineteen years of being a line item in someone else's ledger, and here was the door, held open by the man who'd built the cage.

Then they thought of Joren Vane, who had been promised an orchard and given the bottom of the strait. Of Elias Thorn, who had spent his last strength pressing a letter into a servant's hands because no one else would carry it. Of Petrel in a cell, silent, holding fast. Of forty names that no ledger had ever bothered to write down properly.

"No," Mara said.

Hale sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor giving his best performance to an audience of gulls. "No?"

"There's nothing to give."

"Then you won't mind if my friends look anyway." He straightened, snapped his fingers once, and Quill came up the deck with two constables, and the three of them closed around Mara like a knot tightening.

And that was when the aftercabin door opened.

* * *

It opened without drama, which was somehow worse. A clerk stepped out first—thin, ink-stained, blinking at the daylight—and behind him came the magistrate himself, a heavy, silver-whiskered man named Aldous Crane whom Mara had seen exactly once before, at a distance, sentencing a smuggler on the quay steps. He held a sheaf of papers in one hand, and he was reading them as he walked, and he did not stop reading them until he reached the rail, and even then he read the last lines standing, his lips moving slightly, before he raised his head and looked at Corvus Hale the way a man looks at something he has stepped in.

"Mr. Hale," said Magistrate Crane.

"Your honor. This is—an unexpected pleasure. Routine inspection, nothing that need concern—"

"Deposition of Ambrose Rennick, harbormaster of Saltmere, retired." Crane tapped the papers against his palm, once, twice, like a man beating dust out of a rug. "Sworn before a justice of the peace across the strait yesterday evening. Brought to me at dawn by a young woman with a split lip who declined to give her name and left by the cabin window before I could thank her." He paused. "She climbed down a rope, Mr. Hale. Onto a moving lighter. In the dark. To bring me this."

Hale's smile stayed exactly where it was, but Mara, watching him sidelong, saw the thing underneath it shift—the first hairline movement, ice beginning to talk.

"A deposition," Hale said smoothly, "by a disgraced official with grievances against his former employers. Your honor, these waters are muddied enough without—"

"Forty names." Crane's voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Forty divers, Mr. Hale, listed by name, with the dates their families were paid off and the sums. Cross-referenced against your company's own fleet manifests—which, I note, arrived in my clerk's hands an hour ago, copied out in a hand that matches no entry in your ledgers, appended to a sworn statement by one Elias Thorn, deceased." He looked at Mara then, for the first time, and his eyes were shrewd and tired and not unkind. "The statement says the copyist memorized the originals before they were altered. Is that you?"

"Yes, your honor," Mara said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Mara Vane. Indentured to the Halloway Company since I was nine years old."

"Then you'll give testimony."

"With pleasure, your honor."

"Mr. Hale." Crane folded the deposition and tucked it inside his coat, and the gesture was small and final, a door closing on a room full of money. "Your constables will disembark. You yourself will remain aboard. This vessel does not sail until I say it sails, and you are detained pending inquiry—voluntarily, I trust, since the alternative involves Mr. Quill arresting you, and I suspect he'd find that awkward, given whose signature is on his warrant."

Quill had gone the color of porridge. His men were already drifting back toward the gangway with the fluid obedience of people abandoning a sinking thing.

Hale stood very still at the rail. And Mara, nineteen years old, invisible no longer, watched the fixer of the Halloway Trading Company discover that the trap had two sets of teeth—and that he had walked into the wrong one. For a moment something moved behind his face that was almost admiration, almost respect, the look of a craftsman studying better workmanship. Then it was gone, filed away, and the smile returned, thinner now, a blade shown rather than worn.

"This isn't over, Vane," he said softly, as the constables retreated down the gangway and the gulls screamed and the tide, at last, began to turn. "Founders fall. Companies endure. Remember that, when they've forgotten your name again."

"They won't," Mara said. "I'm going to make sure of it myself."

Behind them, the aftercabin door stood open, and Magistrate Crane said, "Come inside, both of you. We have forty names to discuss, and the morning tide waits for no one—but today, it can wait for the truth."
