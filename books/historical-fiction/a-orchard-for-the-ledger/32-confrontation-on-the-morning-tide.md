## 32. Confrontation on the Morning Tide

The aftercabin of the *Morning Tide* smelled of tar, cold tea, and old paper—the last of these so thick it seemed to press against the walls like weather. Magistrate Crane had spread the harbormaster's deposition across his folding desk with weights at every corner, and he read it the way Mara imagined men read scripture in a language they were not sure they believed: slowly, twice, his thumb moving under each line as though the words might swim away if he blinked.

Corvus Hale did not sit.

He stood by the stern windows with his hands folded behind him, watching the quay through the salt-hazed glass as if the harbor were a ledger he was auditing and finding short. The constables who had boarded with him waited outside the cabin door, and Mara could hear them shifting their weight on the deck planks, boots creaking, the small restless music of men who wanted to be anywhere else.

"You understand what you've done," Hale said. He did not turn from the window. His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "You've handed a private document to a public officer. Whatever Thorn wrote—and dying men write such strange things—it is hearsay. It is ink. It will not survive the first honest question."

"It survived you," Mara said.

Hale turned then, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him because it never reached further than his mouth. Up close, in the grey morning light, Mara could see how carefully he was assembled—the pressed coat, the oiled boots, the hair combed flat as a page. A man built entirely of surfaces. Ten years in Halloway's ledgers had taught Mara that surfaces were where companies lived.

"Vane." He said the name slowly, tasting it. "Do you know what I admire about you? Nothing. And yet here we are."

"Magistrate," Mara said, and their voice came out smaller than they intended, scraped thin by three days without real sleep. They cleared their throat and tried again. "Magistrate Crane, before anything else is said—I want to say what I have to say to the whole crew. On deck. Where everyone can hear it."

Crane looked up from the deposition. He was an old man, dry as a dock piling, with watery eyes that missed less than they appeared to. "That is unusual procedure."

"So is murder," Mara said. "Forty counts of it."

Hale laughed—a single soft note, like a coin dropped on wood. "There it is. The accusation. Do you hear it, Magistrate? A nineteen-year-old indentured diver, standing in your cabin, accusing the founders of the Halloway Trading Company of mass murder on the strength of a dead clerk's paper. You'll pardon me if I don't tremble. I've heard braver things from drunkards on the quay, and I've buried those too."

"I know," Mara said quietly.

Hale's smile flickered. Just at the edge. Just for a breath.

"I know you've buried them," Mara went on, and now the fear that had been sitting in their chest like ballast all morning began, strangely, to lift—not gone, but changing into something else, something with lift in it, like a body rising through water toward light. "Petrel told me. Old Petrel, who was on the water that night. Who heard them calling from the fleet when it started going down. Who saw that no signal flags ever ran up the harbor mast, though the storm had been visible since noon. Forty divers, Mr. Hale. My father among them. Joren Vane. He dove for your company's pearls and your company drowned him for daring to dive for anyone else's."

"That," Hale said, "is a fairy story."

"Then you won't mind hearing it again." Mara turned to Crane. "On deck. Please. If it's a fairy story, no one will believe it, and nothing is lost but my dignity—which was never mine to begin with. It belonged to the company, same as everything else about me."

The silence stretched. Outside, gulls argued over something floating in the wake. The tide was turning; Mara could feel it in the ship, the slow groan of the hull taking the current, the lines ticking against the pier. The *Morning Tide* would sail within the hour, and whatever happened in this cabin would sail with it or be left on the quay forever.

Crane closed the deposition. He folded his hands on top of it.

"Mr. Hale," he said mildly, "you have been remarkably eager to prevent this young person from speaking. First at the customs house—your men questioned them twice, I'm told. Then at the gangway, where you searched them personally. Now here. In my experience, innocence does not usually chase people up gangways."

"In your experience," Hale said, "magistrates are bought. This one was supposed to be."

Mara felt the whole cabin go still around those words—Hale's own words, offered freely, unguarded, because he had spent thirty years in a company where every man could be priced and had simply assumed this room worked the same way. Crane's face did not change at all. That was somehow more frightening than rage would have been.

"Constables," Crane said, without raising his voice, "will remain on deck. Mr. Hale, you will remain in this cabin until I say otherwise. And the crew—" he rose, joints cracking like ship's rigging, "—the crew will hear what this diver has to say. Because I find I want witnesses. Plural. Many."

* * *

The deck of the *Morning Tide* was crowded with the ordinary machinery of departure—stevedores hauling the last crates of pearl-shell, the bosun bawling about the fore halyard, a boy coiling rope with the absent grace of someone born to it. When Crane stepped out of the aftercabin, the noise died the way noise dies on ships: in patches, outward, until the only sounds were the water against the hull and the wind worrying the flag at the masthead.

Mara walked to the rail.

They had planned this moment for three days, rehearsed it in the dark of the dormitory behind their teeth, and every rehearsal had collapsed the instant their boots touched the deck. Because this was not a plan anymore. Plans were things done in secret, in ledgers, in fish carts at dawn. This was the other thing—the thing history did elsewhere, to people with surnames that mattered. Except that Mara Vane had spent ten years learning that history did not happen elsewhere. It happened in the ledger room, on the third floor, in careful ink, in entries crossed out with unusual care. It had happened to their father at the bottom of the strait while Saltmere slept and the storm flags stayed furled.

They gripped the rail. The wood was wet and cold and utterly indifferent, and that indifference steadied them the way nothing kind could have.

"My name is Mara Vane," they said.

Their voice cracked on the second word. Somewhere in the crowd, Petrel—who should have been locked in the company lockup and was instead standing at the foot of the gangway between two constables who hadn't yet been told to move her—shouted, "Louder, chick! The dead can't hear whispers!"

And Mara understood, all at once, that this was the trick of it. This was what Thorn had died trying to teach them with his unlocked desk and his deliberate errors. Words on paper could be confiscated, forged, burned, switched, sunk. Words in throats, spoken before forty pairs of ears on a ship about to carry a magistrate across the strait—those could not be taken back. Arresting the speaker would only prove the speech worth arresting.

They filled their lungs with harbor air—brine and coal smoke and pearl-shell rot, the smell of their entire life—and began again.

"My name is Mara Vane. My father was Joren Vane, a pearl diver of the Straits Fleet, indentured to the Halloway Trading Company from the year of the Great Storm until the night it killed him. On the night of the ninth of October, eleven years ago, forty-one divers of the rival Straits Fleet put down to the beds off Gull Rock. A storm came up faster than any storm the old divers had ever seen—so fast that it could not have been chance, and was not. Before it came, a man named Aldous Halloway met with the harbormaster of Saltmere and paid him two hundred sovereigns to keep the storm signals furled. The harbormaster's name is Edric Mole. He has written this down himself. He is alive, and he has signed his confession, and it is in the possession of Magistrate Crane aboard this vessel."

A murmur went through the deck like current through water. Mara kept their eyes fixed on the middle distance, on the grey line where the harbor opened into the strait, because looking at faces would break them.

"The fleet went down with its divers below. Those who surfaced begged for help from boats that had been ordered to stand off. The order came from the Halloway counting house. I know because Elias Thorn, senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company, wrote it all down—names, dates, sums, orders—he kept the true ledger, the one that wasn't for sale. He gave it to me the night he coughed blood onto his desk, and he said one word to me as they carried him away: *tide*. He meant this tide. He meant this ship."

"He died in the infirmary the next morning," Mara said, "and Corvus Hale inventoried his effects personally and burned what he didn't carry off. I watched him do it. I copied the false fleet records afterward with my own hand, under Hale's own eye, page by page, erasing the sabotage from the books. And I remember every word I erased."

Now Mara turned, deliberately, and looked at Hale.

He had come out onto the deck at some point—Mara had not seen him move—and he stood near the aftercabin door with his hands clasped behind him, and his face was composed, and his eyes were doing arithmetic. Mara could see it happening behind them: the calculation of witnesses, of distance to the gangway, of whether the constables would follow an order given in front of a magistrate. They watched him arrive at the answer. They watched him decide to gamble anyway.

"This is sedition," Hale said clearly, to the deck at large, in his reasonable voice. "This child has been fed lies by a dying man and a disgraced harbormaster and has swallowed them whole. There is no evidence. There is a paper, possibly forged, and a story. I invite the magistrate—and I invite every man here—to consider what happens to a company like ours, to wages, to this port, to *your families*, if every mad tale from every resentful worker is treated as gospel. I ask you: where is this letter?"

"There is no letter," Mara said.

The lie came out smooth as poured lead, and it surprised even them. But it was true enough—there was no letter. There had been no letter for two days now, only ash in a harbor grate and forty names worn into memory deeper than ink ever sat. Let him search. Let him tear this ship apart plank by plank. The testimony was not in a hollow knife handle or sewn into a coat. It was standing at the rail speaking itself.

"What I have," Mara said, "is a memory. And memories don't burn."

Hale moved then—one step, two, toward the rail, toward them, his composure finally cracking along a hairline—and his hand lifted as if to close over Mara's wrist, and the whole deck held its breath.

"Mr. Hale." Crane's voice cut across the deck like a boathook. "Remove your hand."

"They're inciting—"

"They are testifying. Under the eye of a magistrate of the circuit, aboard a vessel preparing to depart on lawful business, with the harbormaster's signed deposition already in evidence." Crane descended the cabin steps with terrible slowness, each word landing like a crate dropped on a dock. "I have read that deposition, Mr. Hale. I have read the manifest irregularities from the customs house. I have read your own promotion order, which places this diver—this *witness*, let us use the correct term—in circumstances that required your personal attention three separate times in four days. Either you are the most diligent man in Saltmere, or you are the most frightened. I begin to suspect which."

Hale straightened. For one long moment the fixer of the Halloway Trading Company stood on the tilting deck with the morning light full on his face, and Mara watched him perform the final arithmetic and find the sum come out wrong. The constables at the gangway had not moved to him. Petrel stood grinning at them like a gull over a dropped fish. The stevedores had stopped pretending to work and were listening with their mouths open, and every one of them had a father or a brother or a cousin in the water off Gull Rock, or would have, if the water had given them back.

"Detain him," Crane said.

The bosun and two of the largest stevedores did it gently, almost apologetically, which was somehow worse than violence. Hale did not struggle. He let them take his arms, and he turned his head, and he found Mara at the rail across the crowded deck, and he smiled his surface smile one last time.

"Founders fall," he said. "Companies endure. Remember that, when they've forgotten your name again."

"They won't," Mara said. "I'm going to make sure of it myself."

They led him down into the hold, past the coils of rope and the crates of shell, into the dark where the cargo rode, and the hatch cover came down over him like a period at the end of a sentence. The deck exhaled. Someone laughed, high and disbelieving. A stevedore took off his cap, looked at it, and put it back on.

Behind them, the aftercabin door stood open, and Magistrate Crane said, "Come inside, both of you. We have forty names to discuss, and the morning tide waits for no one—but today, it can wait for the truth."

* * *

The cabin was smaller with three people in it, and warmer, though the stove was cold. Crane poured tea from a dented pot into tin cups and handed one to Mara, whose hands shook so badly the surface trembled like the harbor in a blow. They drank anyway. It scalded, and the scald was good. It proved they still had a body.

"You lied to him," Crane said. "On deck. About the letter."

"Yes."

"Why? If you had it, producing it would have finished everything."

"Because I don't have it," Mara said. "Not anymore. Not as paper." They set down the cup and looked at their own palms, at the diver's calluses and the clerk's ink stains, the two trades written into their skin. "It was burned. After I learned it by heart. Every name, every date, every sum. Paper can be stolen—mine nearly was, twice, by people I loved. But I am still holding it." They touched their temple, briefly, awkwardly. "He couldn't take it because it isn't anywhere. It's just me."

Crane studied them for a long time. Outside, faintly, the bosun's whistle sang, and the great hull shivered as the mooring lines came aboard, and the *Morning Tide* leaned away from Saltmere with the whole of the turning tide beneath her.

"Do you understand," Crane said at last, quietly, "what happens next? Depositions. Three days at anchor while I take them. Cross-examination. The company's lawyers will arrive like gulls on a gutted fish. They will call you a liar, a thief, a forger—they already have grounds for the forgery, don't they? The customs house manifest. Don't bother looking surprised; I read everything that crosses my desk." He almost smiled. "It will be ugly, and slow, and there will be nights you wish you'd thrown Thorn's letter into the sea unread. Do you understand that?"

"No," Mara said honestly. "But I understand what happens if I stop. The ledger goes back to being crossed out with unusual care. Petrel stays in a cell. Sable stays beaten. And my father stays a line item marked *paid by death*."

Crane nodded slowly, and took up his pen, and drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself across the scarred desk.

"Then we begin," he said, "with the hardest part. Names. All of them, in order, as Thorn recorded them. Speak slowly. I write quickly, but I have never yet written as fast as grief remembers."

Mara closed their eyes. The cabin rocked. Somewhere above, the sails filled with a wind that smelled of open water, and the strait widened ahead of them, grey and patient, keeping at its bottom the town where their father had drowned and the orchard they would never own.

"Joren Vane," they said, and their voice did not shake at all. "Diver. Age thirty-four. Of the Straits Fleet."

And the pen of a magistrate of the circuit scratched across the page, writing their father back into the world, one name at a time, as the *Morning Tide* ran on toward the far shore where the rest of the truth was waiting to be sworn.
