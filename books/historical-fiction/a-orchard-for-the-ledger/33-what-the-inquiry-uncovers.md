## 33. What the Inquiry Uncovers

The first day at anchor, the *Morning Tide* did not feel like a ship. It felt like a room where everyone was waiting for a door to open.

Saltmere sat across the strait in the haze, its chimneys drawing their gray threads into a sky that could not decide between rain and sun. The magistrate had ordered no one ashore—not Hale, not the crew, not me—and so we all drifted through the decks like fish in a netted cove, touching the same railings, eating from the same galley, avoiding one another's eyes or holding them too long.

I spent most of that first day near the mainmast, because it was there I had spoken the names aloud, and I found I could not be far from them. Thorn's testimony lived in me now the way a song lives in you after you've sung it once too many times—unbidden, complete, rising to my lips whenever my mind went quiet. Forty-one names if you counted my father's twice, which I did, silently, every time the ship rolled and the water slapped the hull like a hand on a door.

The magistrate's clerk set up a table on the quarterdeck under an awning of canvas, with an inkwell weighted against the wind and a stack of paper held down by a brass compass. He was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient face of someone who had learned that truth arrives by appointment, never early. Depositions were taken from dawn until the light failed.

I watched the line form that first morning: crewmen, harbor hands who'd rowed out unasked, a pearl-sorter named Doss who had once shared a shed wall with me and pretended not to know my name. They came to speak about Corvus Hale, about the roll-call, about men taken from the dormitories at night. Their words went onto the paper in the clerk's small careful hand, and each page, when finished, was blotted and stacked and read aloud in a low voice to confirm it, and hearing my secret spoken back to me in other people's mouths was like watching water rise around a stone I thought only I knew was there.

Petrel was brought aboard before noon.

They walked her up the gangway between two constables, and the whole deck went still in that particular way docks go still—every worker finding something urgent to do with their hands. She looked smaller than I remembered, her gray hair loose from its knot, her wrists ringed red where the irons had been. But she walked upright, the way she dove, straight down without flinching, and when she reached the quarterdeck she looked past the constables entirely, found me at the mast, and nodded once, slow as a tide coming in.

*Hold fast,* I had paid a guard to tell her. *Dawn comes.*

Dawn had come. It just took three days and a magistrate.

She gave her deposition standing, refusing the chair twice before the clerk gave up offering it. I was permitted to remain—I was, the magistrate said, "the occasion of these proceedings," which was the strangest title anyone had ever given me—and so I stood six feet away and heard Petrel tell, in front of witnesses and paper and law, what she had told me in whispers among the drying nets.

"The flags," she said. Her voice carried better than you'd expect from a woman her size; thirty years of shouting over water will do that. "Storm signal should have gone up at the harbor mast by noon. Every diver on that fleet knew the look of that sky—we begged the watchman to run the flags up. He wouldn't leave his post. Wouldn't take his eyes off the water." Her hands moved as she spoke, shaping the harbor in the air. "Because he'd been told to watch the water, see. To watch it and report what sank."

The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere behind me a crewman stopped breathing audibly.

"And the divers?" the magistrate asked gently.

"Some got up. The young ones. The water wasn't cold enough yet to kill quick, and they knew the shoals." Petrel's jaw worked. "We heard them calling from the breakwater all night. Company men walked the quay with lanterns and told us to go home, that it was God's weather. But there weren't any home to go to for most of them, and the lanterns—" she stopped, and started again, flatter. "The lanterns weren't looking for swimmers. They were looking for bodies, and counting them against the insurance schedules. I know because I read the schedule later. My husband was number nineteen. Joren Vane was number eleven."

Number eleven. I had known my father was dead since I was nine years old. I had not known he was a number. Something in my chest turned over like a hull taking water, and I gripped the mast rope and made myself stay standing, made myself listen, because Petrel was spending herself again on my account and I would not waste it by fainting like a lady at a funeral.

When she finished, the magistrate asked if there was anything more, and Petrel looked directly at me.

"There's a girl," she said, "who copied pages in the dark for people who couldn't read. Whatever she remembers, it'll be truer than anything Halloway wrote down. You can build on it."

Then they led her below to a cabin—not a cell, the constable assured us, though the door locked from outside—and I went and put my forehead against the cool of the mast and let the ship hold me up for a while.

* * *

The second day, the harbormaster arrived.

He crossed the strait on a hired skiff, an old man wrapped in two coats against the spray, and I watched him come from the rail with a feeling I didn't have a name for. This was the man Thorn's draft confession had named—the second witness, retired now, living beyond the third checkpoint, across the water I had spent my whole life being told was the edge of the world. Sable's second route had gotten his deposition out ahead of Hale's reach, and here he was, delivered by the same tide.

He climbed the ladder slowly. At the top he paused, looked out at Saltmere's skyline—the customs house, the company offices, the harbor mast where the storm flags had hung furled on the wrong morning twenty years ago—and said, to no one in particular, "I used to raise those flags myself. Before they bought me."

His deposition took most of the day.

He spoke of a dinner in the spring of the sinking year, at which two founders of the Halloway Company had proposed, over brandy, that competition in the pearl grounds "required pruning." He spoke of sums—he named figures, and the clerk's pen slowed to keep pace with them. He spoke of the watchman, whose name was Aldous Grebe, who had been given a year's wages and passage to the mainland and whom the harbormaster himself had seen off at the pier, and who had died rich and anonymous in a place where nobody knew why his hands shook.

"I signed the manifest that morning," the old man said. His voice was steady but his hands, folded on the table, were not. "I wrote *lost to sudden squall* in my own hand, forty times, one for each diver. I have written a great many things in my own hand. That is the nature of the office." He looked up at the magistrate. "You want to know how it is done, how a thing like this is done. It is done with handwriting. Nothing else. Just handwriting, and the willingness to make it neat."

I thought of the ledger room on the third floor. Of the entry for VANE, crossed out with unusual care. Of Thorn teaching me a fair hand, teaching me flourishes, teaching me—though neither of us said it—that a person who can write convincingly in another's style can unmake them or remake them at will. The tools of servitude. I had forged a manifest with shaking hands at the customs house and thought myself damned for it. This old man had forged forty deaths and been pensioned for it.

And yet he had kept something. They always keep something, the ones who participate. A copy, a letter, a draft confession hidden under a floorboard—some splinter of the truth worked under the skin where it festered until it had to come out. Thorn had carried his until it killed him. The harbormaster had carried his across a strait.

"Miss Vane." The magistrate's voice broke into my watching. "You've memorized testimony. I'm told you can recite it entire."

"Yes, sir."

"Then tomorrow we will do the hard work. Bring your memory. We'll need it."

* * *

The third day was the ledger day, and I will tell you honestly: I woke before dawn dreading it more than I had ever dreaded the checkpoints, more than Hale's smile, more than the thirty steps down the gangway. Because memory is a strange country, and I had been living in Thorn's letter for weeks—reciting it in the dark of the dormitory, walking its sentences like a night watchman walks his rounds—and I did not know until that morning whether my rounds matched the territory.

They set up the quarterdeck as a kind of court. The magistrate at the center. The clerk with fresh paper. And on the table before him, carried aboard under seal that morning by a customs launch, the Halloway fleet ledgers themselves—the real ones, seized from the company offices at dawn by order of the inquiry, while Hale sat confined in the great cabin under guard and could do nothing but hear the boat arrive and know what it meant.

I recited.

It went like this: the clerk would read an entry aloud from the official ledger—*schooner Merrow's Daughter, lost with all hands, twelve divers, act of weather, no irregularities noted*—and then he would look up at me, and I would close my eyes and give him back what Thorn had written, what Thorn had verified against the true books before the false ones were made, entry by entry, date by date, name by name.

"*Merrow's Daughter*, twelve divers," I said. "Joren Vane, fourth position. The weather entry was inserted after the fact—the ink differs, Thorn noted it, a different batch, browner. The original page recorded a signal flag discrepancy reported by the watchman at midday, and a payment of forty pounds entered under 'harbor services,' initialled E.H. for Elias Halloway. The page was replaced. The original was burned. Thorn kept a copy because burning originals was becoming habit in that office, and habits make men nervous, and nervous men keep copies."

The clerk wrote. The magistrate listened with his chin on his knuckles. Around the quarterdeck, workers and crew stood in a loose ring, and I heard them breathing, and once I heard old Doss from the sorting sheds whisper to his neighbor, "*That's the quiet one from shed four*," with something in his voice I had never once heard directed at me in ten years of being unremarkable.

Entry after entry. Hour after hour. The sun climbed, the tide turned beneath us, and I poured out everything Thorn had pressed into me along with the sealed letter that night he coughed blood into his handkerchief—every figure, every initial, every discrepancy of ink. Some of it I had not consciously known I carried. It came up out of me like water from a well I hadn't known the depth of, and each time I feared the well was empty, there was more.

By late afternoon we reached the entries I dreaded most: the ones where my memory might fail, where Thorn's notes had been dense and hurried and I had been a terrified indentured girl reading by candlestub with a corpse-warm letter in her lap.

The clerk read: "*Pearl fleet losses, aggregate, 1898 season: forty souls, total indemnity paid—*"

"Stop." I closed my eyes. The ship rocked. In the dark behind my lids I saw the candlestub guttering, Thorn's cramped hand, the smudge where his bloody thumbprint marked the margin. "*Total indemnity paid to next of kin: two hundred and sixty pounds, disbursed at four pounds per head, remainder retained by the company under clause eleven.* Four pounds." I opened my eyes. "Four pounds a life. And clause eleven is the same clause they use to dock our wages for broken gear."

Silence. Then the clerk, checking the seized ledger, said quietly: "Confirmed. Clause eleven."

The magistrate wrote a note in his own hand, slowly, and read it back to me: *"Memorized testimony consistent throughout. No discrepancies found."*

No discrepancies found. I stood there on the quarterdeck of the *Morning Tide* with my hands hanging at my sides and felt the words go into me the way light goes into deep water—slowly, in layers, the surface brightening first and then, after a long delay, the depths. For ten years I had believed history happened elsewhere, to people with surnames that mattered. Now history sat on this deck in a stack of seized ledgers, and it had my father's name in it, spelled correctly, restored, and my voice had put it there.

A sailor near the rail was crying and pretending he wasn't. Doss had removed his cap. And somewhere below, behind a locked door, Corvus Hale sat listening to the sound of his employers' world being taken apart entry by entry, and I found—to my own surprise—that I did not hate him in that moment. I pitied him, almost. He had spent his whole life guarding a lie, and lies are heavy cargo, and the tide was going out.

* * *

That evening, the magistrate summoned me to the great cabin.

Hale had been moved out; the room still smelled of his cologne, sharp and expensive, ghosting under the smell of lamp oil and salt. The magistrate sat behind the captain's table with the day's papers squared before him, and for a while he just looked at me, the way you look at a chart to be sure of your position.

"You understand what happens next," he said. "Tomorrow we weigh anchor considerations aside and begin the formal phase. There will be findings. There may be prosecutions, though—" he paused, choosing the words with a surgeon's care— "prosecutions require defendants, and defendants of that rank require arrangements. Companies are large animals. When cornered, they do not always die. Sometimes they shed."

"Shed," I repeated.

"A name here. A founder there. Enough flesh to satisfy the court and preserve the body." He laced his fingers. "There will also be settlements. Claims. Indentures voided, possibly. Compensation for the families of the forty." His eyes came up to mine, level and not unkind. "Including yours, Miss Vane. Including whatever your father was promised and never received."

I thought of the orchard deed sewn into my coat lining, the paper soft as cloth from years against my skin, and the dried seed pod beside it, lighter than a breath. Promised and never received. I knew exactly how much a promise from the Halloway Company weighed. I had been carrying it for a week.

"Thank you, sir," I said, which was not an answer, and he knew it, and let it go.

"One more thing." He drew a single sheet from the stack and turned it toward me. On it, in a clerk's fair hand, was a list of names—the forty, recovered, corrected, ordered as they should have been ordered twenty years ago. My father's name sat eleventh. Someone had ruled a clean line above it, a heading: *THE DIVERS OF THE LOST FLEET, TO BE READ INTO RECORD.*

"They'll ask you to read it," the magistrate said. "At the close of proceedings. All of them. Publicly. Some of these men have daughters and sons in Saltmere who learned today, for the first time, that their fathers were murdered rather than drowned. They will want to hear the names from a voice that means them." He slid the paper toward me across the wood. "Take it tonight. Learn the order. Tomorrow, we settle accounts."

I took the paper. It was nothing—rag pulp and iron-gall ink, the same material as a wage tally, an indenture contract, a forged manifest. Paper had been the shape my whole servitude came in.

But walking out onto the deck, into the last gold light with Saltmere smoking softly across the water, I unfolded it and read my father's name and felt the paper change in my hands, the way water changes when the tide turns—same water, new direction. Tomorrow they would talk of settlements, of shedding, of what the company would give up to save itself. I stood at the rail with the list of the dead against my chest and understood that whatever price they offered, whatever justice arrived and however much of it leaked away between the law and the ledger—

I would be there, awake, to count it.
