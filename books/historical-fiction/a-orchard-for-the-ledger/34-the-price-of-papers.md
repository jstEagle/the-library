## 34. The Price of Papers

The inquiry's third day smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular staleness of a room where men have been arguing about money since dawn.

They had moved the proceedings off the *Morning Tide* by then—too much paper, too many chairs, too many men who required desks to feel authoritative. The customs house's long hearing room, the same one where Mara had once forged a manifest with shaking hands, now held a magistrate's bench, two clerks, a barrister for the Crown, a barrister for the Halloway Company, and a slow accumulation of witnesses who came in wet from the quay and left looking older. Mara sat where the magistrate had told them to sit, on a plain chair to the left of the bench, neither witness nor accused nor counsel—something the law had no clean word for, and so the documents called them *the party of the second testimony*, which was as close to a name as the law was willing to give.

They had learned, over three days, the grammar of such rooms. Learned that justice did not arrive the way a storm arrives, all at once and unmistakable. It arrived the way light arrives through harbor water—slantwise, thickened, taking a long time to reach the bottom, and even then it touched only what it touched.

On the third morning, the barrister for the Crown read aloud the terms.

Mara had expected this part. Thorn had taught them, in the ledger room, that every ruin in Saltmere ended the same way: not with a confession but with a *settlement*, not with truth but with an *arrangement*, the words written in a clerk's best hand and the ink left to dry while everyone went home to dinner. Still, hearing it spoken aloud was different from knowing it. Hearing it was like the difference between knowing the sea is cold and going under.

"The Halloway Trading Company," the barrister read, his voice flat as a ledger line, "in consideration of the Crown's forbearance from criminal proceedings against the company as a corporate body, consents to the following: first, the posthumous disavowal of Augustin Halloway and Edric Mott, founders, whose names shall be struck from the company's rolls, its buildings, and its seals; second, the voiding of all outstanding indentures held by the company, without exception, effective upon the inquiry's close; third, the payment of restitution to the estates of the forty divers lost in the sinking of the *Cormorant* fleet, such restitution to be calculated at the rate of—"

He gave the rate. A figure per drowned man. Mara did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way they did all arithmetic, automatically, in the back of the skull where Thorn's training lived now like a tenant who would never leave. Forty men. The figure multiplied out to less than the company's quarterly profit on a single cargo of copra. Less than the cost of the new crane on the east quay. Forty lives, priced at a rounding error.

"—and fourth," the barrister continued, "the company acknowledges no further liability, the matter being thereby concluded in full."

*In full.* As if a thing like this could ever be full. As if forty drowned men were a cistern that could be filled to the brim and stoppered.

The magistrate, a spare man named Aldous Wren with a voice like a file drawn across soft iron, turned his head. "The party of the second testimony has the floor."

Mara stood. Their knees ached from three days of sitting. They had rehearsed nothing, because they had learned that rehearsed words came out sounding like the company's words, and they would not speak the company's language back to it, not even now, not even in agreement.

"I accept the voiding of the indentures," they said. "I accept the restitution, though I think you should write down what the rate is, so that in fifty years someone can read it and be ashamed." A clerk's pen actually stopped. "I accept the striking of the founders' names, though I note for the record that striking a name from a building is easier than striking it from the water, and the water keeps what it keeps."

"Is there an objection?" Wren asked, in the tone of a man who already knew there was.

"There is," Mara said. "The orchard."

* * *

They had carried the deed sewn into their coat through every checkpoint, through every search, through the whole long terror of the last weeks, and it had come through the way a seed comes through winter—dried down, patient, holding its one idea in the dark. They had taken it out only once since the *Morning Tide* anchored, at night, alone, holding it up to a candle and reading the faded script: *one coastal parcel, four acres, bearing fruit trees, to Joren Vane and his heirs, in consideration of service rendered.* Their mother's tin had held it flat and safe for ten years, and now it lay on the magistrate's bench looking small and ridiculous, a scrap of paper among all this official paper, and yet the only piece of it that had ever been meant to be theirs.

The company's barrister—a smooth man named Ferris who had spent three days saying *my client* the way other men said *my God*—did not even open his casebook. He had it ready. Of course he had it ready. He had probably had it ready since the day the *Morning Tide* raised anchor.

"The parcel in question," Ferris said, "was conveyed by the company to a private purchaser in the year 1891. The conveyance was lawful. The deed held by the Vane family was, at the time of the alleged promise, an unexecuted intention—a promise of a grant, not a grant. The company regrets the misunderstanding. The company does not, however, recognize any outstanding claim."

"Was the purchaser aware," Wren asked, "that the land was promised to the family of a man lost in the fleet?"

"The purchaser," Ferris said, "was the company itself, acting through an intermediary, in a transaction designed to—"

He stopped. Everyone in the room heard him stop. Everyone in the room understood, in the same cold instant, what he had almost said: *in a transaction designed to make sure the Vanes could never hold it.* The company had sold the orchard to itself, through a glove puppet, the moment it became inconvenient. They had drowned the man and then tidied away the promise they'd made him, the way you sweep a floor after a funeral.

"The transaction," Ferris said, recovering, smooth as oil on water, "was lawful."

It was lawful. That was the whole of it, the entire architecture of the thing, and Mara stood there and felt the truth of it go through them like cold water through cloth. The deed was invalid. The land was sold. The law, which had spent three days agreeing that Joren Vane had been murdered by men who signed their names in a ledger room, could not find a single seam in the paper that took his orchard away. The murder was a crime. The theft was a *conveyance*.

"Justice," old Petrel had said once, on the quay, mending a net with fingers like knotted rope, "is what they give you instead of what you're owed. It's a coin they mint themselves. It spends, mind—it spends—but don't ever mistake it for the thing you lost."

Mara had not understood it then. They understood it now, standing in a room full of lawful men, watching lawful paper bury a drowned man's orchard for the second time.

"I have one question," Mara said to Wren. "Not an objection. A question for the record."

"Ask it."

"The land that was sold—the company sold it to itself through an intermediary. That's in the conveyance records, is it not? The intermediary's name, the date, the price?"

Ferris's face did something very small and very fast, the way a man's face does when a floorboard shifts under him. Wren turned to his own clerk. "It will be in the registry," the clerk said. "All conveyances are registered."

"Then I ask that it be read into this inquiry's record," Mara said. "The name. The date. The price the company paid itself for my father's orchard. I don't ask for the land back. I ask that the room hear the number."

And here was the thing Mara had learned from Thorn, from the ledgers, from the long apprenticeship of being invisible in a room full of men who thought no one was listening: numbers were the one language the powerful could not refuse to speak, because they had forgotten how to speak any other. Wren could have refused the request. A kinder man might have. But Wren had spent three days watching Mara recite forty names from memory, watching them reconstruct falsified pages word for word, and he had the particular respect of a legal man for someone who could produce a citation on demand.

"Read it," Wren said.

The clerk read it. The intermediary's name—some clerk of Mott's, long since promoted and retired to a house on the hill. The date—eleven months after the fleet went down, eleven months after Joren Vane stopped breathing in the green dark. And the price: a sum that came to less than the company had paid, that same year, for a shipment of window glass for its counting house.

The room was quiet. Ferris looked at his hands. Somewhere in the gallery behind Mara, a woman began, very quietly, to weep—not loudly, not for attention, just the small exhausted crying of someone who had waited ten years to hear a number said aloud and had needed, more than the land, more than the money, only to hear it *said*.

"Let the record show," Wren said, "the conveyance as read." He looked at Mara over his spectacles. "The deed is ruled invalid. The land stands sold. Is there anything further?"

Mara looked at the deed on the bench—their father's name in faded ink, *to Joren Vane and his heirs*, the promise that had outlived the promiser and been murdered twice, once in the water and once in the registry. They thought about reaching for it. They thought about the whole shape of their life, the nine-year-old pulled from the harbor water, the girl or boy or whatever the company found convenient to call them, the invisible copyist, the smuggler, the voice at the rail of the *Morning Tide* saying forty names into the wind. Every one of those selves had carried this paper toward this room.

"No," Mara said. "Nothing further."

They took the deed back when the session closed. Not because it was worth anything now. Because it was true, and true things should not be left in rooms like that.

* * *

The indentures were voided at noon on the fourth day, in the company yard, in front of everyone.

It was done with a ledger, of course. A company clerk stood at a table under the eaves with the great master roll open before him, and one by one the indentured came forward and the clerk drew a line through each entry and the line was witnessed by a constable and that was all. Ten years of Mara's life—nine years old to nineteen, the dormitory bunks, the pearl-sorting sheds, the ledger room on the third floor, the taste of ink, the weight of the water—closed out with a stroke of a pen that took less than a second and cost the clerk no visible effort at all.

Mara watched the others go up. Old Petrel went up leaning on a younger diver's arm, and when the line came down through Petrel's entry, the old diver did not cheer or weep or even smile—just stood a moment looking at the ink as if checking it for forgery, then nodded once, the way you nod at a tide that has finally turned, and walked away free without appearing to notice any difference in the ground.

Then it was Mara's turn.

"Name," the clerk said, though he knew it. Everyone in the yard knew it.

"Vane," Mara said. "Mara Vane."

The clerk's pen paused. He looked up. For a moment—the length of a held breath—Mara saw it in his face, the whole question: *Vane, like the diver in the inquiry, like the testimony, like the names.* The clerk was perhaps thirty. He would have been twenty when the fleet went down. He might have known. He might have copied the falsified pages himself, or known the man who did, or simply eaten his dinners for ten years on the far side of a wall from all of it. Whatever he knew, he kept it, and drew the line, and said, in the voice of a man reading weather:

"Indenture voided. You're free."

The word went into Mara like a hook going into deep water—no resistance at first, no weight, just the strange sensation of something fastened to you far down in the dark.

*Free.* They walked away from the table and the word walked with them and neither of them knew yet what it could carry.

* * *

That evening they stood at the rail of the anchored *Morning Tide*—they still had leave to come and go aboard; the magistrate's inquiry kept the ship at anchor and Mara's testimony had made them a kind of guest no one quite knew how to host—and watched the light go off the harbor.

The company had already sent men up ladders with chisels. Down on the quay, in the last flat gold of the day, two workmen were picking the founder's name out of the stone above the counting house door. *HALLOWAY*, carved deep in 1861 by some proud mason, was becoming *H——WAY*, letter by letter, the chisel ringing small and bright in the evening air, stone dust drifting down like the world's palest snow. By morning there would be a blank and someone would paint over the blank and in a generation no one would remember there had ever been letters there at all.

Mara watched a long time. It should have been satisfying. It was, a little. But mostly it was like watching the tide erase footprints—the water doing faithfully what water does, and the sand keeping nothing, and the walker gone.

They had struck the name from the building. They had paid out the coin. They had drawn the line through the indenture. And Joren Vane was still exactly as drowned as he had been ten years ago, and the orchard was still exactly as sold, and the forty were still forty, and the sea off the point still came in every evening with the same indifferent shine.

Justice, Mara thought. It had a taste. They turned the taste over. It was like the water in the harbor at slack tide: not sweet, not salt, just *there*, and faintly of everything that had ever been put into it.

* * *

Sable found them at the rail, as they had known Sable would. Sable moved through the world the way rumor moved through a dockside—arriving everywhere slightly ahead of any explanation. The bruising on Sable's face had gone from purple to a greenish yellow, the colors of shallow water over sand. They leaned on the rail beside Mara without asking, the way they had leaned on a hundred rails together, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the harbor was doing its evening work of turning to brass and there were things you did not talk over.

"They gave you papers?" Sable said at last.

"Tomorrow. Signing's tomorrow." Mara turned the word over again. "I've copied ten thousand signatures in my life. I've never once signed my own."

Sable made a sound that was half laugh, half something older. "First thing I ever stole was a name," they said. "Did I ever tell you that? Before the docks, before any of it. I was nobody's child off a nobody's boat, and a harbor master asked me my name for his book, and I gave him one I'd heard on a ship's bell. Been Sable ever since." They looked out at the water. "Names are the only cargo worth stealing, Mara Vane. Everything else you can buy."

"Except orchards," Mara said.

"Except orchards," Sable agreed, and did not say *I'm sorry*, because Sable understood that sorry was a coin from the same mint as the restitution, and Mara did not need any more of that currency. Instead Sable reached into their coat and set something on the rail between them: the dried seed pod, the one from the tin, the one from the trees that no longer existed. Mara had sewn it into their coat lining beside the deed, and had not told anyone, and looked at Sable now with the question rising.

"You sleep like a stone and talk in your sleep like a clerk reading inventory," Sable said. "I saw the shape of it weeks ago when I mended that coat. Thought you knew." A pause. "I didn't open it. Whatever's in it, it's yours. I just—" Sable turned the pod over once in their fingers, careful as a man handling a shell he suspected might still be alive. "You said once your father's trees stood on ground that's gone. Ground that's sold and fenced and not yours." Sable set the pod down again. "A seed don't care whose ground it came from. It only cares where it lands."

Mara picked the pod up. It was lighter than it had any right to be, this small dry boat that had crossed ten years and three checkpoints and a betrayal and a trial, and inside it, if anything inside it still lived, was everything the company's chisels and conveyances and settlements could not reach—the part that was not owed and therefore could not be taken, the part that had to be *made*, the way a free name is made, the way a life is made on ground no one gave you.

Tomorrow they would sign the papers. Tomorrow they would be, on the law's own paper, nobody's. And the law, having given them everything it had to give—a struck name, a voided line, a coin at forty per drowned man—was finished with them, and the orchard was finished with them, and the only thing left was the thing that had never been in any ledger at all.

"Where would you even plant it," Sable said. It was not really a question. It was the question, and they both knew it, and Sable asked it the way you ask a thing you already intend to help with. "Ground costs. Ground around Saltmere costs double."

Mara looked down at the pod in their palm, and then out past the harbor wall, to where the land rose in the last light—the scrub hills above the town, the thin grass going gold, the ground nobody wanted because nothing on it had ever been worth counting.

"I've been counting," Mara said. "I know exactly what's left. And I know a hillside where the price is low because the company's books say nothing grows there." They closed their hand around the seed pod, and felt it hold its one idea in the dark, patient as it had been for ten years, patient as it would need to be a while longer. "The books," Mara said, "have been wrong before."

Below them on the quay, the chisel rang against the last letter of the founder's name, and the sound went out over the water like a small, bright bell—and Mara Vane stood at the rail of the *Morning Tide*, free and unlanded, and began, quietly, to plan a garden.
