## 35. Free Papers

The clerk's office of the magistrate's temporary court smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and it was the smallest room Mara had ever been afraid of.

Not afraid, exactly. Not since the gangway, not since Hale's face going white at the rail while forty names rang out over the deck like a bell being rung against his skull. But the body kept its own ledgers, and Mara's body knew what rooms with desks in them meant. Rooms with desks were where names were entered and never came out again. Rooms with desks were where a boy of nine had pressed an inked thumb to a page and become property.

The magistrate's clerk—a round, mild woman named Ostrey who had the habit of licking her thumb before turning pages, as though every document were slightly salted—set two papers on the scarred oak before Mara and smoothed them flat with both hands, the way one might smooth the feathers of a bird that had been through a storm.

"Indenture void," she said. "Certificate of freedom. Read them if you like. Most don't." She said it without judgment, the way she might have said *most can't*, which was kinder and truer. "The company's man has already signed his part. All that's wanted now is yours."

Mara read them.

It was a discipline learned in the ledger room on the third floor, where Elias Thorn had first tested them with small errors—*can you read more than you admit*—and where they had later copied falsified fleet records line by line, memorizing what they erased. Reading was a weapon. They would not set it down now, not even trembling.

The indenture paper was the old one, pulled from the company's archive, and seeing it was like seeing a photograph of a wound. *Mara Vane, aged nine years, bound to the Halloway Trading Company for a term of—* The term had been struck through in the magistrate's red ink, a single clean stroke, decisive as a gull diving. Beside it, in a smaller hand: *Void by order. Indenture obtained under false pretenses; consideration fraudulent; term unenforceable.* Somewhere behind those dry phrases lay three days of depositions, the harbormaster's confession, Thorn's testimony recited from memory in a voice that only shook once, at the name Joren Vane. The phrases did their work anyway. Dry words, drowned men, red ink. The world was made of such things.

The certificate of freedom was newer stock, heavy cream paper that took the lamplight like shell. At its foot, above a line ruled for the purpose, there was a blank space where a signature belonged.

"Here," said Ostrey, and turned the inkwell toward them.

Mara looked at it for a long moment.

Ten years of documents, and in all ten years they had never once signed anything. They had copied. They had forged. They had reproduced the hands of harbormasters and customs clerks and dead company founders until their wrist could lie in six different voices. But a signature—the signature was the self laid down on paper, offered up as surety, and no one had ever asked Mara Vane for themselves. Only for Vane the copyist, Vane the diver, Vane the number in a roll-call, Vane the crossed-out entry marked *paid by a death*. Their father had signed things. That was how it worked in families with orchards. In indenture families, the thumb went to the ink instead, because a thumbprint could not be argued with and a name could be denied.

"Take your time," Ostrey said, and licked her thumb, and turned to her own pages.

Mara picked up the pen.

It was heavier than it looked, or the hand holding it was lighter than it should have been—that strange buoyancy, that vertigo of a body suddenly relieved of weight it had carried so long the muscles had grown around it. The nib touched the cream paper and left a small dark bloom, and Mara lifted it away too quickly, leaving a fleck like a stray period, like a full stop placed in the wrong decade.

They breathed. The harbor sounds came faintly through the shuttered window—rigging ticking against masts, the chisel still ringing somewhere on the quay where the founder's name was coming off the company facade letter by letter, each stroke carrying across the water like that small bright bell. Petrel was down there somewhere, freed two days ago, walking the boards like a person remembering how legs were meant to be used without permission. Sable was down there too, counting out returned pearls into honest piles, settling accounts the way they'd sworn to do over stolen rum a lifetime ago—or nine days ago; time had gone strange.

Mara set the nib down again and wrote.

*Mara.*

The strokes came out steady, and then, halfway through the second letter, they did not. The *a* wavered, thinned, recovered. It looked, Mara thought, like a diver's silhouette seen from below, wavering at the surface line between one element and another.

*Vane.*

And then it was done, and the strangest thing happened, which was nothing. No thunder. No clerk leaping up to strike them down for presumption. Ostrey turned back, glanced at the page, licked her thumb out of pure habit though there was nothing to turn, and nodded.

"Vane," she read aloud, mildly, the way one reads any word. "That's all, then. You'll want your copy." She blotted the certificate, sanded it, and slid it across the table along with the struck-through indenture. "Keep both. Some people like keeping the old ones. Proof it happened, I suppose."

Mara took the papers, and their hands shook—not from fear now, but from something the body had been saving up for ten years and chose this moment, this small salted-smelling room, to spend. Trembling joy, joy like cold water on sunburnt skin, joy that hurt going down.

"Thank you," Mara said, and heard how strange the words sounded, addressed upward through no chain of command at all, owing nothing, asking nothing. Just thanks, free-floating, given freely.

Outside, the morning had come up hazy gold over Saltmere harbor, and the light lay on the water in long broken columns, and Mara stood on the top step of the magistrate's temporary court with two pieces of paper in a leather folder against their chest and did not know what to do with a body that belonged to itself.

* * *

They walked the long way, along the quay, past the sheds.

The pearl-sorting sheds were running short-handed these days—half the divers had walked off when the verdict came down, some to other ports, some just to sit on the sea-wall and stare at the water as though waiting for it to explain itself. Forty names. The whole harbor was doing arithmetic with them. Mara passed the sorting tables where the loose pearls they'd once dived for lay heaped in wooden trays, and one of the older women, Tamsin, who had taught Mara to hold breath against cold current, looked up and—nodded. Just that. But the nod was different from any nod Mara had received in ten years of nodding exchanges. It had no calculation in it. It cost nothing and protected nothing. It simply said: *I see you, and we are the same kind of thing now.*

At the sea-wall, Petrel sat wrapped in a coat too big for them, feeding bread to gulls with the solemnity of a priest administering sacrament.

"They tell me you signed something," Petrel said, without looking up.

"Two somethings."

"Which one matters?"

Mara considered. "The old one. The one with the red line through it."

Petrel nodded slowly, tore off a piece of bread, and held it out—not to the gulls this time, but to Mara, who took it, because refusing an offering from Petrel was against some law older than any the magistrate administered. The bread was stale and tasted of the sea, and it was the best thing Mara had ever eaten.

"Your father," Petrel said, still watching the water, "once told me he'd sign nothing else after the company papers. Said his name was the last thing he owned and he'd spend it careful." A pause. Gulls wheeled and screamed. "He'd have liked watching you spend yours this morning."

Mara sat down on the wall beside the old diver, and the two of them watched the harbor do its ordinary immortal work—boats going out, boats coming in, water taking everything and giving back only light—and neither of them said the word *orchard*, because the orchard was gone, ruled invalid, sold out from under a dead man's hope, and grief needed no help finding its address.

But after a while Petrel said: "Hillside's still there, though. Hills don't get sold the way land does. Not really. Ask any diver—there's ground under the water nobody holds title to, and things grow there all the same."

Mara looked at the old woman sharply, but Petrel's face gave back nothing but gull-light and patience.

"You've been talking to Sable," Mara said.

"Sable talks to everyone. It's how they know things." Petrel stood, joints cracking like rigging in a swell, and dropped the last of the bread to the gulls at once, ending the ceremony. "Come by the saplings tomorrow. If there are saplings. If there's soil. If there's rain." And they shuffled off down the quay, humming, an old diving song with the mourning verses worn away from use, leaving only the work-song part, the part that keeps time for hands.

* * *

The room at the lodging house—first room ever paid for with money rather than allotted by an overseer—was narrow and smelled of previous tenants' tobacco, and Mara loved it with a fierce, unreasonable love, the way one loves a boat that leaks but floats.

They spread the two papers on the single table beneath the window.

The indenture with its red line. The certificate with its wavering, surfacing-diver signature. Ten years compressed into two leaves of paper, and both of them, finally, finished—closed accounts, settled ledgers, entries that would not be altered again by any company hand.

From inside their coat, sewn deep against the lining where searchers' fingers had learned to pass them by, Mara took out the last of their possessions that mattered: the dried seed pod, brittle as parchment, holding its one idea in the dark; and beside it, folded small, the orchard deed that was no longer a deed, only a memory of ground, a promise made to a drowned man and never honored, invalid now by ruling of law.

Three papers on a table. One of them worthless, one of them finished, one of them barely begun.

Mara sat looking at them for a long time while the light moved across the floorboards and the harbor sounds rose and fell like breathing.

Then they picked up the pen—they owned a pen now, bought that morning with their own coin, a plain steel nib on a plain wood shaft—and on the back of the invalid deed, on the blank side, in the space where no line had been ruled for any purpose because no one had ever imagined Mara Vane would write here, unprompted, unsigned by anyone but themselves—

They wrote:

*Joren Vane. Diver. Orchard-keeper in intention. My father.*

And below it:

*Forty names. To be remembered. To be planted.*

It was not a legal document. It would stand in no court, convince no magistrate, undo nothing. It was the opposite of all the documents Mara had ever handled: it created no obligation, transferred no property, bound no one. It was a piece of paper that existed purely because a free hand had chosen to put true words on it.

Mara looked at it a long moment, then folded it small and sewed it into the coat beside the seed pod and the empty space where the deed used to matter, and felt the coat grow heavier by almost nothing, and lighter by everything.

Down on the hillside above the town—visible from the little window if one leaned out and looked past the chimney smoke—the scrub grass was already going gold in the late light, and the low stone boundary of the plot Mara had bought with the last pearls ran crooked across the slope like a mended seam. Rain was coming; the gulls had gone quiet and the wind had swung around into the west, smelling of green distance. Tomorrow there would be soil to break, and Petrel to help, and a seed pod's patient idea to give to the ground at last.

Tonight, though—tonight Mara Vane sat in a room they paid for, at a table they owned, and practiced signing their own name. Over and over in the fading light, on scrap paper, on the margins of old manifests: *Mara Vane, Mara Vane,* the letters growing steadier each time, the diver's wavering smoothing out into something that no longer hesitated between elements but moved through both, at home in either.

On the last repetition, they stopped.

Because there, at the bottom of the page, under the final confident *Mara Vane*, the pen had rested a moment longer than intended, and the ink had gathered, and fallen—a single dark bloom on the paper, round and perfect as a pearl.

Mara looked at it, and laughed—short, startled, real—and left it there.

Below it, they wrote one line more, small, in the margin, where no clerk would ever rule a line through it:

*Paid in full.*
