## 36. Sable's Debt

The tide was going out when Sable came to settle up, which felt right. Everything that week had been about things flowing away and not coming back—the Morning Tide at anchor with her holds half-empty, the constables gone sour-faced from the quay, the whole city of Saltmere holding its breath like a diver between strokes.

Sable walked the long way around the fish sheds because walking hurt, and the long way was slower, and slow was a kind of honesty too. The bruise along their ribs had gone the color of deep water, green-black shading to yellow at the edges where the blood was finally giving up its argument. Their left eye still swelled shut by mornings and opened by noon, like a shopkeeper who couldn't decide whether to trust the day. Hale's men hadn't been artists. They'd been thorough. There was a difference, and Sable's body had spent four days learning it in a room that smelled of wet rope and old iron.

But they had given up nothing. That was the coin Sable carried now, heavier than any purse.

They found Mara on the quay below the customs house, sitting on a bollard with their coat folded over their knees, watching the water do what water does—nothing, endlessly, beautifully. Nineteen years old and sitting like someone who had already lived through the drowning of a world and come up breathing on the other side of it. Which, Sable supposed, was exactly what had happened.

"You look terrible," Mara said without turning around.

"Good," Sable said. "You should see the other fellow." They lowered themselves onto the next bollard, hissing through their teeth, and Mara's mouth twitched—that almost-smile that was all the smile anyone ever got from them, and somehow worth more than the whole ones other people spent freely. "Actually, no. You shouldn't. He looked fine afterward. That's how you know a man like Hale."

* * *

They sat while the gulls argued overhead and the fishing boats came in with their small silver hauls, and for a while neither of them said anything, because some silences were load-bearing and you didn't knock them out just to fill the air.

It was Mara who broke it, the way Mara always broke things: precisely, once, in the right place.

"I owe you an accounting."

Sable barked a laugh that pulled at their ribs and made them regret it immediately. "An accounting. Listen to you. Four weeks in the ledger room and you talk like Thorn's ghost."

"I mean it." Mara turned then, and their eyes were that gray-green color the harbor went in November, the color of water that has taken things and given none of them back. "I've been keeping accounts my whole life. Other people's debts, other people's payments, entries in books I wasn't allowed to own. I'm done with that. So we're going to do this properly, here, on this quay, before I leave Saltmere. What you're owed. What I'm owed. All of it, out loud, and then we're square, and then—" their voice caught, just barely, a rope going taut and holding— "and then neither of us is carrying the other anymore."

Sable looked at them for a long moment. Underneath the swelling and the bruises, something in their chest did a slow, painful turn over, like a boat coming about.

"You're leaving," Sable said. Not a question. They'd known since the inquiry ended; you could see it in the way Mara stood lately, weight forward, always half-turned toward the horizon like a compass needle that had finally found its north. But knowing a thing and hearing it were different animals, and the second one bit harder.

"Tomorrow. Maybe the day after."

"Where?"

Mara shook their head—not refusing to say, but saying *it doesn't matter yet*, or maybe *I don't know yet, and for the first time in my life that's allowed*. "Free ground," is what they said instead. "That's the only address I have."

* * *

Sable had brought the pearls. Of course they had. They'd brought them every day for six days and turned back every day, because returning them meant the whole thing was finished, and finished things were graves, and Sable had never in their life voluntarily visited a grave.

Now they dug them out of the lining of their coat—a oilcloth packet, knotted twice, warm from riding against their body—and set it on the bollard between them. It made a soft sound, pearls against wood, the sound of a fortune so small it fit in one hand and so large it had bought a boatswain, a message, a chance.

"Count them," Sable said.

"I trust you."

"Count them anyway." Sable's voice came out rougher than intended. "That's the whole point of today, isn't it? No more trusting. Trusting is what got me dragged into a room with wet rope in it. Counting is what keeps people honest. You taught me that—or Thorn did, through you, which amounts to the same thing."

So Mara counted. Untied the knot with careful fingers and laid the pearls out on their open palm, thirty-one of them, each one holding a little captive piece of harbor light. Sable watched them count and understood, suddenly, why Mara had survived everything—why a child indentured at nine, a diver's orphan with no surname that mattered, had grown into a person who could walk past Corvus Hale with a murdered man's testimony hidden in a knife handle and not sweat, not shake, not give themselves away with a single tell.

Because Mara counted. Always. Every pearl, every word, every debt. And now they were counting even their friends, laying them out in the light, making sure nothing was missing, nothing owed.

"Thirty-one," Mara said quietly. "I gave you thirty-five."

"You gave me thirty-five. I used four." Sable held up fingers, ticking them off like a merchant. "Two to the woman who runs the oyster stall at the gate—she saw the constables take me and she kept her mouth shut about which direction they went, which kept them looking east when you'd gone west. One to the boy who watches the Morning Tide's mooring line; he passed your message to Petrel inside a fish, which was my idea and I stand by it. And one—" their voice snagged here, and they pushed through it the way you push through a door swollen shut with damp— "one I pressed into old Petrel's hand through the cell bars the night before they released him. For luck. For the both of us."

Mara closed their hand over the pearls, slowly, and Sable watched them understand exactly what the missing four had bought. Watched them try to speak and fail, and try again.

"That's not a debt," Mara managed finally. "Those are yours. Those four are yours to have spent."

"No." Sable shook their head, and even that motion hurt, and they didn't care. "No, listen. Here's my grudge, since we're doing this properly. My grudge is that you keep trying to make me a martyr. A martyr gets remembered and doesn't get paid, and I am not interested in being remembered. I'm interested in being *even*. So the four are spent, they're entered in the book, closed account, and if you try to argue it I'll start charging interest, and I know for a fact you'll hate owing interest more than you hate anything else in this world."

The almost-smile again. Wider, this time. Nearly a real one.

"Noted," Mara said. "Entered. Closed."

* * *

The tide turned while they talked. Sable felt it rather than saw it—the subtle change in the slap of water against the pilings, the moment the harbor stopped exhaling and began to draw breath. Forty years of docks in their bones. Their mother had worked these quays before them, gutting fish until her hands were more scar than skin, and her mother before that, back to whoever had first looked at this gray water and decided to make a living off what it surrendered.

"And here's yours," Sable said. "Your grudge. Out loud. Fair's fair—I aired mine."

Mara was quiet a long time. A fishing boat puttered past, and the man in it touched his cap to them both—he'd have heard the stories by now, everyone on the waterfront had, the tale of the ledger clerk who spoke forty drowned divers' names aloud on a ship's deck while a company fixer stood there with constables and found his hands tied by the simple, devastating trick of being overheard. Legends grew fast in Saltmere. Faster than oysters, faster than lies.

"My grudge," Mara said at last, slowly, tasting each word like a diver testing air, "is that you took the packet through the gate knowing it might cost you, and you let me believe it wouldn't. You smiled when you took it. You said *easy as rum*, you said *they never search a fish cart*, and you knew—you must have known—that easy things on this waterfront are bait."

"Yes," Sable said simply.

"Why?"

"Because if I'd told you the true odds, you'd have come yourself. And you were the only one who could walk into the customs house and forge that manifest, and you were the one Hale was already watching, and if you'd been taken at the gate with the letter on you, everything ends there. Thorn dead for nothing. Petrel's people waiting for justice that never sails. Your father staying a weather report forever." Sable spread their hands, palms up, the gesture of a person laying cards down. "So I lied to you with a smile, because it was the only way to get you to let me carry it. And I'd do it again. That's my grudge against myself, if we're entering those too: I'd do it again tomorrow, and I don't apologize, and I need you to either hate me for it or forgive me for it, but you can't do the thing you always do, which is file it away unopened and pretend it weighs nothing."

The harbor slapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind them, in the sheds, a knife scraped scales off fish in long rhythmic strokes, the oldest sound on the waterfront.

"I don't hate you," Mara said. "And I don't forgive you, because there's nothing to forgive. You lied to me the way my father lied to my mother every morning he went down—told her it would be fine, told her the water was calm—because the truth would have broken the thing he loved, and he needed it whole." Their voice dropped low. "I've thought about it every night since they found you bleeding on the quay boards. I thought I'd be furious. I waited to be furious. And it never came, and do you know why?" They looked up, and their eyes were dry and bright and terrible in the way of clear water over deep rock. "Because I did the same thing. To you. I let you think we were partners in a small smuggling run, when really I was asking you to stand between me and a company that drowns people. I dressed it as friendship and weighted it like ballast. We both lied. We both paid. And we're both still here."

"Still here," Sable echoed, and found the words sat strangely warm in their chest.

"So we're square," Mara said. "Truly square. Not protector and protected. Not the clever one and the brave one. Two liars who love each other, even."

The word landed between them like a dropped pearl—rolling, ringing, impossible to pick back up.

Sable had never said it. In twelve years of shared stolen rum and shared watch-fires and shared silence on cold quays, neither of them had said it, because words like that were cargo that got ships seized. But Mara had spoken forty dead names into the air of a ship's deck and made arrest impossible; compared to that, one small living word on an empty quay was nothing at all.

"Even," Sable said, and their voice cracked clean through the middle of it, and they let it. "Two liars who love each other. Even."

They shook on it. Hands clasped across the space between two bollards—Mara's grip cool and hard from rope and pearl-shell, Sable's swollen and bandaged—and it was the fairest trade Sable had ever made on any dock in Saltmere.

* * *

They walked together after that, slow, because Sable was slow now and Mara matched their pace without being asked and without comment, which was its own kind of tenderness. Up along the quay, past the sheds, past the harbor gate where a fish cart had once crossed carrying blank pages and a betrayal, past the customs house whose steps Mara had mounted with a forged manifest and a heart like a drum. The whole route of it, end to end. Sable realized Mara was walking it deliberately, touching each checkpoint the way you touch a scar to prove it doesn't hurt anymore. Or to prove it does.

At the top of the quay, where the road split—one way toward the town, the other toward the headland road that climbed out of Saltmere toward whatever free ground waited—they stopped.

"There's one more thing," Mara said. They reached into their coat and brought out the seed pod, dried and split and lighter than it looked, turning it in their fingers. "My father's. From trees that don't exist anymore anywhere but here." They held it out. "Take one."

"Sable doesn't plant things. Sable takes things and moves things and sells things. Planting requires staying."

"I know." Mara's almost-smile. "That's why I'm asking. Someone should hold a piece of this besides me. Not as a debt. As a witness. If mine don't take—if the ground's wrong, if the frost gets them—I want the shape of the tree to survive somewhere, even if it's only in a smuggler's pocket."

Sable took one seed. Small, dark, hard as a secret. They turned it over in their palm and thought about their mother's scarred hands, and the forty names, and a room with wet rope, and a friend who had once been a scrawny nine-year-old on the sorting sheds floor pretending not to exist, and who existed now so completely that a magistrate had rewritten the law's ledgers because of the sound of their voice.

"What will you do," Sable asked, "out there? On your free ground. You can't dive anymore—Hale saw to your certification, and no company will sign a diver who burned their ledgers."

"I'm going to find out what I am when nobody assigns it to me." Mara pocketed the pod. "But first there's somewhere I have to go. The orchard land—the deed was void, sold years ago, but I want to see it. Walk it once. Find out if anything of my father's is still standing in the ground there." They paused, and something moved behind their eyes, some current under still water. "The widow who owns it now. I wrote to her. She wrote back."

"And?"

"She says I should come. She says—" Mara stopped, and Sable watched them choose whether to say the rest, and then say it. "She says there's a tree. One tree still alive. She says it blooms every spring like it's trying to tell somebody something."

The wind came off the strait, smelling of salt and kelp and distance, and Sable stood there with a seed in their fist and a bruise like a map of the last month printed on their body, and understood that they were watching the exact moment Mara Vane stopped being an orphan of the water and became something else—someone with a tree, a grave, a destination. Someone history happened *to*, and who had reached back and made history happen for them.

"Go," Sable said. "Walk your father's ground. And when you plant the rest of these—" they closed their fist around the seed, feeling its small stubborn heart— "plant some on high ground. Above the tide line. Where nothing drowned can reach them."

"I will."

They didn't embrace. That wasn't the grammar of their friendship, and fairness meant not forcing a language on someone who didn't speak it. Mara squeezed Sable's shoulder once, brief and firm, the way you test a mooring line, and turned toward the headland road.

Sable stood and watched them go—small figure, heavy coat, straight spine—climbing out of Saltmere step by step into the wide unbuilt country beyond it. They watched until the road bent and took Mara, and then they stood a while longer, watching the place where Mara had been.

Then they opened their hand, looked at the seed, and tucked it carefully into the innermost pocket of their coat, next to their heart, where the important cargo rode.

Behind them, down on the quay, a harbinger was waiting: a boy in a Halloway Company cap, breathless from running, scanning the crowd—and fixing, at last, on Sable. The company might have sacrificed its founders' names, but Sable had learned in a room with wet rope that companies never sacrifice anything they can sell instead.

"You're Sable?" the boy panted. "There's someone asking for you. Down at the Morning Tide. Says you'll want to hear what they found—in the ledgers they didn't burn."
