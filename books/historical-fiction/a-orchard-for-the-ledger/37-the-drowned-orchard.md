## 37. The Drowned Orchard

The road north out of Saltmere smelled of salt and cut hay, and Mara walked it the way they had once walked the ledger room—quietly, unremarkably, as though they belonged to no one and nothing. But that was a lie they no longer needed to tell. They were free. The papers were folded against their chest where the letter had lived, sewn now into a new coat by Petrel's crooked, careful stitches. Freedom weighed less than paper should have. It shifted when they breathed.

Three days after signing their name—their own name, in their own hand, on a document that was not a copy of anything—Mara had gone to the harbor master's successor and asked, in the flattest voice they could manage, who owned the Vane orchard lands.

The man behind the desk had not even looked up. *Sold,* he said. *Years ago. To a Mrs. Anselm, the widow. North road, past the second mile marker, where the ground starts to rise.* He said it the way one reads an entry aloud from a column: a transaction, a line in a book, a debt marked settled. Mara knew that voice. They had made it themselves for ten years.

*Sold.* As though land could be sold like a pearl, pried loose from its setting and strung on someone else's thread.

Mara walked anyway.

* * *

The orchard did not look drowned. That was the first cruelty of it. Mara had carried the word with them for so long—*the drowned orchard*, their father's trees gone under the same gray water that took him, or so the child's mind had built it—that they had expected to crest a hill and find ruin: blackened stumps, salt-killed rows, something that matched the shape of grief. Instead there was grass. Long, wind-combed grass running up a gentle slope toward a stone wall gone soft with moss, and beyond it, the sea, flat and silvered under a high white sky, so far below it might have been another country.

And trees. Not many. A handful of shapes standing out of the tall grass at intervals too wide to be chance—planted, then abandoned, then forgotten into wildness. Their branches grew sideways, combed for years by the same wind that pushed against Mara's back now, so that each tree leaned away from the sea like a listener turning from bad news.

Mara stood at the wall a long time before climbing it.

They had rehearsed this. On the walk, on the ferry crossing nothing, in the dormitory at night with Petrel snoring two bunks down—they had rehearsed what they would say to whoever owned this ground now. *I only want to walk it once. I won't take anything. My father planted these.* And underneath all of it, the sentence they could not say aloud because it would crack them open in front of a stranger: *This was supposed to be mine.*

But there was no one at the gate to rehearse to. The gate hung open on a hinge rusted to lace. So Mara walked in without permission, which felt, absurdly, like every other thing they had ever taken in their life—small, necessary, and stolen.

The grass reached their knees. Seed heads brushed their palms as they walked, leaving a fine gold dust on their sleeves. Somewhere upslope a lark was going up and up, spending itself on the sky. The soil underfoot was soft, dark, smelling of rain and old leaves—not the harbor smell at all, not tar and brine and fish blood, but something older, sweeter, the smell Mara's body seemed to recognize before their mind caught up. This was the smell of the tin box. Of the dried seed pods their mother had hidden beneath the floorboard like relics. Earth. This was what earth smelled like when it had never been asked to hold anything but growing things.

Their father had come from here. Joren Vane, pearl diver, indentured man, drowned name—he had been born above this slope, or married into it; the deed in Mara's coat said the ground had been promised to him, earned dive by dive, and then quietly sold out from under the promise while he was underwater earning it. Halloway had done that too. Halloway had put its hands on everything: the fleet, the ledgers, the flags that were never raised, the deed, the divers' lungs. There was almost nothing left that company fingers had not touched.

Almost nothing.

Mara stopped at the third tree.

They knew it was the third because they had counted—counting was a habit that would not leave them, counting pearls, counting pages, counting steps down a gangway—and because this tree was different from the others. It stood straighter. Its lean was less pronounced, its bark less torn by wind, and at its base, half-swallowed by grass, lay a ring of stones. Small ones, gathered from the wall maybe, set in a circle around the trunk the way you'd ring a hearth. Someone had tended this tree. Recently, or not long ago.

Someone knelt here sometimes, in the wind, around a circle of stones, and Mara did not know who, and the not-knowing hurt worse than anything the Halloway Company had ever done to them, which was saying something, which was saying nearly everything.

* * *

"You'll be wanting the gate shut, then."

Mara turned so fast the world blurred.

A woman stood at the top of the slope, coming down through the grass with a basket on her hip and no hurry in her at all. Sixty, perhaps. Gray hair pinned up carelessly, escaping in silver threads the wind took pleasure in. Her dress was good wool gone soft at the elbows, mended at the hem by someone who cared about mending. She looked at Mara the way people had begun to look at them since the inquiry—looking *for* something, some mark of the famous witness, the diver's child whose voice had filled a courtroom—and finding nothing remarkable, which was, Mara thought, the oldest joke of their life.

"I'm sorry," Mara said. "The gate was open. I knocked—there was no one—I shouldn't have—"

"Gate's been off its hinge since before I bought the place." The woman came closer, unhurried, her shadow falling long across the grass between them. "Wind took it my first winter and I decided the wind could keep it. You're the Vane one."

Not a question.

Mara's hand went, without permission, to their chest—to the coat, to the papers, the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. "How did you—"

"There's been a magistrate's clerk, and a newspaper man, and a church deacon, all up my road this month asking about the Vane lands. You're smaller than they made you sound." She set down her basket on the grass, deliberately, the way one settles in. "I'm Anselm. Widow Anselm, if you want the whole thing, though I've been a widow twenty-two years and the word fits me worse every season. My husband bought this ground at auction. He thought he was buying an orchard." She looked up at the leaning trees with an expression Mara could not read—something between fondness and grievance. "What he'd actually bought was forty rows of sticks and a debt. Half of them died the first winter. The rest have been arguing with the wind ever since, and losing."

"You kept three," Mara said. Their voice came out strange.

"Four. There's one over the rise you can't see from here." She squinted at Mara. "You counted."

"I count things."

"Aye," she said, "I heard that about you too," and Mara could not tell if she meant the testimony, the memorized pages, the names spoken into the record—all forty, every one, while the room went quiet as deep water—or something else entirely. Something older. Something about a child in a ledger room nobody bothered to see.

* * *

They walked the rows together because the widow walked and Mara's legs simply followed, and because refusing would have required a self Mara had not yet finished building.

"This row was apples," the widow said, waving at a stretch of empty grass where nothing stood but the ghosts of spacing, the regular dark patches where trees had been pulled out, roots and all. "Or so the auction paper claimed. My husband got a crop the first year—windfalls mostly, bruised, but we ate them stewed all winter and thought ourselves rich. Second year, nothing. Third year, the trees started dying from the roots up. Salt in the water table, the fruit man said. Sea's been creeping into the low ground for years." She glanced at Mara sidelong. "Your father's trees, these were. The ones that lived."

"Some of them," Mara said.

"Some of them," she agreed. "The rest are under the pasture past the wall, if the old maps are true. Ground that was orchard when I was young is grazing ground now, and the grazing's poor, and every year the tide comes a little further up the creek. That's why your lot called it drowned, I'd wager, long before anyone was drowned in it. Land goes under slow, here. Slow enough that you can watch it go, year by year, and argue with yourself about whether it's really happening." She stopped walking. "My husband argued with himself eleven years. Then he died, and I stopped arguing."

The wind moved through the grass in a long breath, and for a moment the whole hillside bent together, silver undersides flashing, and Mara understood suddenly that this was what water looked like when it stood up and became land. The same motion. The same light. Their father had lived his whole short life in the border country between the two.

"Can I ask you something?" the widow said.

"You can ask."

"Why now? The inquiry's done. The company's paid its fines, or its reputation has, which is the same coin to men like that. Nobody's contesting the sale—it was legal, whatever else it was. Legal as a knife." She said it without apology and without malice, just the flat truth, the way Petrel told weather. "You could hate me lawful as sunrise. Most in your position would."

Mara looked down at their hands. Pearl-diver's hands still, scarred at the fingertips, nails trimmed short for work even though the work had changed. "I don't hate you," they said, and found it was true, which surprised them. "You didn't sink any ships."

"No."

"You bought what was sold to you. It's the selling I—" They stopped. Started again, lower. "There's a deed in my coat that says this ground was promised to Joren Vane for dives rendered. Promised in writing, by men who knew exactly what they were doing, because a promise to an indentured man is cheaper than wages. He dove for this dirt. Forty years of it, him and his father before him. And when the last payment came due they sold it out from under him and let the paper rot in a drawer until his kid dug it out of a biscuit tin." Mara's voice had gone very steady, which they knew from experience was the dangerous register, the one right before breaking. "So no. I'm not here to hate you. I'm here because I wanted to stand on it once. Before I stop wanting things I can't have."

The widow was silent for a long moment. Out past the wall the sea held its light and gave nothing back.

"That's more honesty than the deacon had," she said finally, "and he was paid by the day to have some." She picked up her basket. "Come on, then. If you're going to stand on it, stand on all of it. There's a tree you need to see."

* * *

It stood alone beyond the rise, in a fold of ground the wind couldn't quite reach—a fold of ground, Mara realized, shaped exactly like a cupped hand, like the hollow where a pearl sits in a palm. Older than the others. Trunk thick as a barrel, bark furrowed deep enough to hide coins in, and heavy with fruit. Late-season plums, dark as bruise, hanging in clusters so numerous the branches bowed under them.

Mara stopped dead.

"That one," the widow said, "was here before your family ever was. Old stock. My husband's auction paper called the whole parcel an apple orchard, which shows how much the paper men know. That's a plum, and a wild one at that, seedling of a seedling going back past anyone's knowing. Your people would have known it, mind. Everyone hereabouts knew this tree. Children used to raid it before I owned the ground and they raid it now. I've given up posting against them. A tree like this doesn't belong to whoever holds the deed." She looked at Mara sharply. "It belongs to whoever climbs it."

Mara couldn't speak.

Because they remembered. Not clearly, not as story—memory didn't work that way for a nine-year-old grief—but as body, as weight and sweetness. Being lifted. Bark rough under small hands. Their father's laugh from somewhere below, telling them *not the green ones, little fish, wait for the dark ones, patience, patience.* Juice running down their wrist and their mother scolding and laughing at once. The taste—God, the taste, dark and honeyed and slightly tart, the exact taste that had been missing from every plum they'd eaten since, every one a lie, every one a copy made by someone who had only read the description.

They had eaten from this tree. Before the harbor. Before the indenture papers, before the ledger room, before the water closed over everything. Some afternoon that no record anywhere had bothered to keep, some ordinary golden hour that history had happened elsewhere from, a child had climbed this tree and their father had stood below laughing, and neither of them had known it was the last time, because no one ever knows, that is the mercy and the horror of ordinary days.

"It's the one," the widow said quietly. Not a question either.

Mara nodded. Their throat had closed around something the size of a plum stone.

"Then pick some," she said. "Go on. It's what it's for."

* * *

Mara climbed the way they had climbed rigging and ladders and the iron rungs of the customs house—without thinking, the body remembering what the mind had spent ten years suppressing. The bark was rough under their palms, exactly right. The branches took their weight with a creak and a sway. Up here the wind was louder and the sea showed itself whole, a vast bright sheet running to the edge of the world, and somewhere down at the bottom of it, under cold green miles of water, lay forty divers and a fleet of lies and a boy's father with his hands full of pearls he'd never sell.

*I brought the names back,* Mara told him silently. *All forty. They're in the record now. Permanent address, like you always said you'd give me. I'm sorry it took so long. I'm sorry about the orchard. I'm sorry about everything except the part where I didn't sign.*

The plums came away warm in their hand, one after another, heavier than they looked. Mara filled their pockets, filled the crook of their arm, ate one where they sat—dark one, patient one—and it tasted exactly like being five years old, and they cried the way the wind cried through the leaves, all at once and without arrangement, grief and sweetness in the same mouthful, and the widow below did not call up to ask if they were well. She sat down in the grass with her basket and waited, the way you wait for weather. For a tide. For a thing that has its own schedule and cannot be hurried or helped, only outlasted.

When Mara came down, their face was wet and their arms were full and something in their chest had shifted—a bone setting, not a wound opening.

"The deed's no good," they said. "I know. The magistrate ruled. The land's yours, fair and legal as a knife."

"It is," the widow agreed.

"But the tree isn't." Mara looked up into the dark clusters, the bowed branches, the old wild stock that had outlived every paper ever written about it. "You said it yourself. It belongs to whoever climbs it."

The widow studied them for a long time. Wind pulled at both of them equally, indifferent to ownership.

"My husband and I had no children," she said at last. "Everyone assumes that's a sorrow. Mostly it's a fact. But a tree like this wants hands on it, and mine are twenty-two years past wanting to climb." She bent, took one plum from Mara's overflowing arm, and turned it in her fingers. "There's a cutting business in Saltmere. Grafting. I've thought about it some years and never had a reason worth the fare." Her eyes came up, sharp and dry and unexpectedly kind. "You'd know someone who'd want cuttings off this tree, I expect. Someone with free ground. Someone likely to live long enough to see them bear."

Mara's hand went again to their chest—to the coat seam, to the small hard shapes sewn there beside the freedom papers. Seeds from a pod their mother had saved from trees that no longer existed. Dried, doubtful, probably dead. Ten years in a tin and a lifetime in hiding, and Mara had carried them north on a hope thinner than thread.

"I might," Mara said.

"Mm." The widow bit the plum, considered it, and handed the rest back. "Well. The season for cutting is late winter, when the sap's down. You come back then, and bring your someone, and we'll see about getting you wood worth grafting. Not a gift," she added quickly, before Mara could speak. "I don't hold with gifts. Call it rent. You'll have climbed my tree, after all. That's worth something. We'll settle accounts like honest folk."

Settle accounts. Like honest folk. Mara thought of Sable on the quay, pearls counted out palm to palm, grudges aired and buried, two people walking away equal. The whole world, it seemed, was trying to teach them the same lesson from different directions: that what mattered was never holding the ground, but standing on it once with your eyes open, and carrying what you could carry, and giving the rest forward.

They walked back down the rows together as the light went long and gold. At the broken gate the widow stopped.

"You'll be planting them up on the hill, then. Where everyone says the ground's no good." It wasn't a question. Nothing today had been a question. "Above the tide line."

"Above the tide line," Mara said.

The widow snorted. "Good. Tired of watching things drown." She turned back up her path, then paused, silhouetted against her leaning, losing, stubborn trees. "Vane," she called back, using the name plainly, without title or apology, as if it were simply a name and not a wreck. "Those plums in your pockets. Eat the dark ones first. They won't keep."

Mara walked south along the north road as the sun went down ahead of them, laying a copper road across the harbor water, and their pockets were heavy with fruit and their chest was light with seeds, and by the time the lights of Saltmere rose out of the dusk they had already, without deciding to, begun composing the letter they would send in the morning—to Petrel, to Sable, to anyone with hands and a spade and a free afternoon:

*Late winter. Bring tools. There's wood worth grafting.*
