## 38. Seed and Soil

The deed to the hillside was smaller than the deed to the orchard had been. That seemed right to Mara. Inheritances, they had learned, were heavy things—paper that pressed on a person like a stone laid over a grave. This was not an inheritance. This was a beginning, and beginnings should fit in a coat pocket.

They climbed the hill above Saltmere in the last grey hour before dawn, when the harbor lamps still burned and the tide was turning below in the dark with a sound like slow breathing. The path was not a path, properly speaking. It was the memory of one, worn into the gorse by goats and by the feet of people who had once come up here to look at the sea and imagine it kinder than it was. Mara's boots found the ruts anyway. Some things the ground remembers even when no one else does.

The parcel of land was barely worth the word *land*. Eleven rods of hillside, sloping north and east, thin soil over old clay, a broken drystone wall along its lower edge where some farmer's grandfather had tried to argue with the wind and lost. The agent at the land office had looked at Mara twice—once at their face, once at the small stack of pearls counted out onto his desk—and said, "You know there's no water up here but what falls from the sky."

"I know," Mara said.

"And the soil—" He had rubbed a pinch of it between his fingers as if tasting it from a distance. "It's orchard soil, I'll grant you. Someone grew things here before. But nothing will grow quick."

"Good," Mara said, and the agent had frowned, because he was a man who dealt in quick things: quick sales, quick profits, quick departures. He did not understand that Mara had spent nineteen years learning patience from water—from the way it wore stone without hurrying, from the way it held pearls in its dark for years before anyone thought to look for them.

Now, standing on the hillside in the cold, Mara took the tin from inside their coat.

Their mother's tin. Dented at one corner where it had been dropped, or thrown, or stepped on—the story lost now, along with so many others. Inside, wrapped in cloth gone soft with age, lay the seed pod: brown, papery, light as an argument won by silence. Their father had carried seeds from the drowned orchard in his kit bag on his last dive. Petrel had told them this, in the low voice Petrel used for true things—that Joren Vane kept a handful of seed pods among his tools, saying that a man who planted nothing owned nothing, whatever the ledgers said.

The ledgers said otherwise. The ledgers had said a great many things otherwise. But the ledgers were ash now, most of them, or corrected, or locked away in a magistrate's strongroom as evidence, which amounted to nearly the same thing.

Mara knelt.

* * *

The ground was harder than it looked. They had brought a mattock borrowed from Petrel, and a knife, and a jar of water carried up from the cistern at the foot of the hill, because the sky above Saltmere gave rain grudgingly and only on its own schedule. The first stroke of the mattock rang against something buried—a root, perhaps, or a stone, or the edge of the old wall reaching further than anyone remembered. Mara struck again. And again.

Digging was honest work in a way copying had never been. When you copied a ledger entry, the ink sat on top of the page and could be crossed out, altered, erased by any man with a stamp and a mind to. But a hole in the ground stayed dug. A seed in the earth stayed planted unless something pulled it out, and if something pulled it out, you could see the wound in the soil and know exactly what had been taken. There were no hidden columns here. No second set of books beneath the first.

Mara thought about that as they worked—the false entries they had copied under Hale's watching eye, page after page of fleet records scrubbed clean of sabotage, every name of every drowned diver reduced to a neat figure in a column marked *attrition*. They had copied those pages with steady hands while rage sat in their chest like swallowed coal. And all the while, they had memorized. Every figure. Every date. Every name that appeared in the falsified record beside the ones that should have been there instead. The magistrate's clerks had compared Mara's recitation against the surviving originals line by line, and line by line the forgery had come apart, and with it the company's careful architecture of lies.

That was three weeks ago. The inquiry was closed. The founders' names hung in the harbor office now on a posted notice, black-bordered, read aloud each morning by whoever passed. Forty names besides. Mara had spoken thirty-nine of them from memory; Petrel had supplied the fortieth, a diver called Anselm Rook whom the company records had never bothered to list at all, as though he had never lived, as though he had simply stopped being one morning between one tide and the next.

Anselm Rook would have a tree. All of them would have trees, if the trees consented to grow.

Mara opened the seed pod with their thumbnail. It split along its seam with a dry whisper, and inside lay the seeds—dark, hard, smaller than pearl-barley, unremarkable in every way. Mara turned a few of them in their palm. These had come off trees that no longer existed, off a coastal orchard promised to a drowning man's family and sold out from under them before the waves finished closing over him. The deed had been ruled invalid. The land belonged to a widow named Corren now, a kind woman with a surviving pear tree and a grief of her own, who had let Mara walk the rows one grey afternoon and stand beneath that last living tree with their hand against its bark, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

But a widow's kindness was not ownership, and ownership had shown itself to be a fragile thing anyway—deeds burned, titles transferred, whole orchards signed away by men who never saw them. What could not be signed away was what a person put into the ground with their own hands. That belonged to the ground. And the ground, unlike the Halloway Trading Company, kept honest accounts.

Mara made the holes. Twelve of them, spaced a forearm apart along the contour of the slope where the soil ran deepest, each one the length of a finger. Into each hole went a seed, pressed down with two fingers the way their father must have done once, somewhere, in some other life—though no one had ever taught Mara this. It came from somewhere older than teaching. The body knew how to bury things carefully. It had practice.

Then water from the jar, poured slowly so it sank rather than ran. Then soil, firmed down with the flat of the hand—not packed, just settled, the way you'd settle a sleeping child under a blanket.

Twelve seeds. Mara had forty names and twelve seeds. The arithmetic didn't work and never would; grief was always larger than what you had on hand to answer it. But twelve trees, if they lived, would drop seed of their own in ten years, and those would be planted, and those would drop more. Someday—Mara was learning to think in decades now, in tides beyond their own tide—there might be enough trees on this hillside to say the name of every diver lost in the strait, and then keep going past the end of the list, into names not yet written, divers not yet born, workers not yet indentured, children who would climb this hill and never know that freedom had ever been a paper thing at all.

* * *

The sun came up while Mara worked, coming through the haze off the water in long pale bars that turned the harbor below into hammered tin. Saltmere woke in stages: first the gulls, quarrelsome and eternal; then the fish carts rattling down toward the quay; then smoke rising from the sheds, and the first bell of the sorting houses, and the deep-throated horn of a coaster standing in past the point. From up here the town looked almost gentle. Almost a place someone would have chosen.

Mara stood, wiped their hands on their trousers, and looked down at it all.

Nineteen years in that town, and for seventeen of them the whole art of living had been to be unremarkable. Keep your eyes down. Keep your name small. History, they had believed, happened elsewhere—to people with surnames that mattered, people whose doings got entered into ledgers under headings grander than *wages paid*. Mara Vane had spent a childhood perfecting invisibility, and it had kept them alive, and it had nearly buried them, and in the end the same skill—the reading, the noticing, the quiet attention of a person nobody troubled to watch—had been the very thing that pulled the truth up out of the dark like a pearl off a drowned bed.

You could not plan that. You could only survive until it was needed, and hope your hands were steady when the moment came.

"Mara Vane."

The voice came from the lower wall, warm as bread. Petrel stood there with a bundle under one arm and the wind pulling at what remained of her hair, her freedman's papers folded in her breast pocket where she'd taken to carrying them like a talisman, touching them the way other people touched saints' medals. She was too old to have climbed that hill easily and had clearly not cared.

"You're supposed to be resting," Mara said. "The magistrate said rest."

"The magistrate," Petrel said, climbing the last stretch with her breath whistling, "said a great many things to a great many people, and half of us are too old to obey." She arrived at the row of fresh-turned earth and stood over it a long moment, saying nothing. Then she unwrapped her bundle: a second mattock, and a sack of something that smelled of lime and crushed shell. "Soil sweetener. Old trick. The orchard men used to burn oyster shell and work it in when the ground went sour." She looked up, eyes bright and wet and fierce all at once. "I helped my father plant trees when I was smaller than you. Thought I'd forgotten how."

"You didn't forget anything," Mara said. "It keeps. The knowing keeps."

"Aye." Petrel knelt at the far end of the row and began working shell-meal into the soil around the first seed with fingers bent like driftwood but still sure of themselves. "Like everything else that matters."

They worked together through the morning. Neither spoke much. There was a language in shared labor that went deeper than speech—the rhythm of two mattocks, the passing of the water jar, the way Petrel hummed an old diving song under her breath, three notes over and over, the one the women used to sing on the boats to call the men up from the bottom. Mara found their own throat humming it before they realized, and neither of them remarked on it, because some inheritances arrive exactly like that: unbidden, unearned, passed hand to hand across a gap where the giver has fallen silent.

By midday the row was finished and mounded, and Mara had set four stones at either end of it—small ones, chosen from the ruins of the wall, each one marked with a single cut of the knife. Not names. Not yet. Names came later, carved deeper, when the trees had earned them. For now the stones only said: *something is planted here. Someone meant it.*

Petrel sat back on her heels and surveyed the hillside, the harbor, the sea beyond shining all the way to the horizon where the Morning Tide had sailed a month ago carrying testimony safely into history.

"Your father would have liked this ground," she said.

"He never saw it."

"Don't matter." She tapped the earth with one knuckle, twice, gently, as though knocking on a door. "Ground's ground. He'd have known it straight off. He was always saying—" Her voice caught, steadied. "He was always saying a man ought to leave more behind than he takes. Said the ledger-keepers had it backwards. Said the only true wealth was what kept growing after you stopped."

Mara knelt and pressed their palm flat against the cool mound of soil. Beneath it, in the dark, twelve small hard things were doing whatever small hard things do in the dark—waiting, testing, deciding. Nothing showed yet. Nothing would show for weeks. But Mara had spent a life reading signs other people missed: the flicker of a clerk's eye, the weight of a crossed-out name, the wrongness in a storm that came too fast. And what Mara read in this unremarkable hillside was the same thing they had finally learned to read in themselves.

Not an ending. A planting.

* * *

They came down the hill in the gold of late afternoon, and Saltmere rose up to meet them changed—or not changed, Mara corrected, but *seen differently*, the way a diver sees the same reef differently on the hundredth descent. The harbor gate stood open, unguarded now except by a bored constable who nodded at Petrel and didn't look twice at Mara. The customs house windows threw back the evening light. On the quay, workers loaded a brig bound south, and nobody checked papers, and nobody counted heads, and a girl of maybe nine years—indenture badge at her collar, eyes down, practicing her own small invisibility—paused in her carrying to stare at the two of them walking free along the boards.

Mara met her eyes. Just for a moment. Held them.

The girl's face did something complicated—fear and question and the very first grain of something else—and then she hurried on with her crate, and the moment closed over like water. But it had happened. Mara had spent a lifetime being unseen by people above them; now they were spending the rest of their life making sure the seeing ran the other way too.

"There's a ship in Thursday," Petrel said at the mouth of the lane where their ways parted. "Coastal packet. Carrying depositions for two families upshore whose people drowned in the same water. Word's gone round. They're sending their papers with the advocate."

"The advocate," Mara repeated. The title still sat strangely on them, like a coat cut for someone slightly taller. *Advocate for indentured workers.* It had begun as a favor asked of the magistrate—help me understand these papers—and become a trade, and was becoming, week by week, a kind of office. People already came to the little room above the chandler's with documents clutched in fists, asking Mara to read them, to explain them, to fight them.

"You'll take their testimonies out safe?" Petrel asked. Not really a question. A handing-over of trust, one more time, easy as breathing now between them.

"On the morning tide," Mara said. "Always on the morning tide."

Petrel laughed, coughed, laughed again, and went off down the lane toward her supper, humming her three notes.

Mara stood a moment longer in the street, feeling the hillside's soil still under their fingernails, feeling the tin—empty now, but kept—in their pocket. Tomorrow there would be papers to read, a widow to visit with cuttings from the surviving pear tree, letters to draft for families who could not write. Tomorrow the work began in earnest, the long patient wearing-away of stone.

But tonight, above the town, in the dark that was already falling, twelve seeds were holding their breath in the earth.

And Mara Vane—who had once believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms with better floors—walked home through Saltmere carrying the whole future in dirt-stained hands, and for the first time in nineteen years, it weighed nothing at all.
