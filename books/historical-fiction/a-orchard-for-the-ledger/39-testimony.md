## 39. Testimony

The inquiry hall had been the customs house's grand chamber once, before Saltmere decided that justice needed less marble and more ledgers. Now the marble was cracked and the ledgers were stacked in towers against every wall, and Mara Vane stood at the front of it on a morning so bright it seemed to have been ordered for the occasion.

They had dressed carefully. Not in company gray—that was over, burned like the letter, gone like the indenture number they no longer answered to—but in a plain coat of deep blue wool, the orchard deed and the seed pod sewn into its lining still, out of habit, out of reverence. Petrel had brushed the salt from its shoulders that morning without being asked, the way an old diver cleans a young one's gear before a deep run. Neither of them said anything about it. Some blessings are only gestures.

The chamber was full. That was the first strangeness: that people had come. Dockhands who could not afford to lose a morning's wage came anyway, standing along the back walls with their caps in their hands. Widows in black that had faded to the color of old storm clouds. Children too young to understand, hoisted onto shoulders so they could see. Sable sat three rows back, arms crossed, watching the doors instead of the bench—a habit of survival that would probably never leave either of them—and gave Mara a single nod when their eyes met, which was worth more than any speech anyone would make today.

At the long table sat the magistrate who had crossed the strait on the *Morning Tide*, the inquiry commissioners, a stenographer with quick fingers and ink-stained cuffs, and, at the far end, two men from the Halloway Company's board who had learned, over three days of depositions, the particular art of looking small in a large room.

Corvus Hale was not among them. Hale sat in a cell across the strait, pending his own examination, and Mara found they could not make themselves think of him for more than a moment at a time. He had become weather again—the kind you sail past once you've survived it.

"State your name for the record," the magistrate said.

And here was the second strangeness, the one that made Mara's throat tighten: that the question was not *what is your name* but *state your name*, as though the name were a thing of substance, weighable, admissible, real.

"Mara Vane," they said. "Child of Joren Vane and Ilse Vane, of Saltmere harbor." They had rehearsed adding the parents' names, decided against rehearsal in the end, let it come on its own. It came easily. It had been waiting a long time.

The stenographer's pen scratched. Somewhere behind Mara, in the crowded benches, someone exhaled—a long, unsteady breath, like a diver surfacing.

* * *

For nine days the inquiry had taken testimony from everyone but the person who carried most of it in their head. The harbormaster's deposition had come first, read aloud in a voice like dry paper: the bribes, the dates, the orders to keep the storm flags furled. Then the ledger clerks, summoned trembling from their desks, confirming the falsifications page by page while the commissioners laid Mara's memorized originals beside the doctored books and watched the discrepancies surface like bodies coming up after a flood. Then Petrel, freed from the company cell two days into proceedings, standing straight despite a limp the guards had given her, telling the chamber about the night the water filled with voices and the shore stayed dark because the lights had been ordered doused.

Mara had listened to all of it from the public benches, hands folded, saying nothing. The commissioners had explained it to them gently: the testimony of the formerly indentured was considered fragile, subject to challenge; better to let the documents lead, better to let the powerful condemn each other first. Mara had understood the strategy. Understanding did not stop them from sitting very still through every hour of it, holding forty names in their chest like breath held underwater.

On the tenth day, the magistrate looked up from his papers and said, "We will now hear from the witness who recovered the primary document," and the room turned toward the benches, and Mara stood, and their legs remembered how to walk forward.

Now, at the front, they were asked to raise a hand and swear, and Mara swore—not on the company's Bible, which was offered, but on "the water and the ground," which the magistrate, after a pause, permitted. Let the record show it, they thought. Let some clerk a hundred years from now wonder what kind of oath that was, and go looking, and find out.

"You hold in memory," the senior commissioner began, "the contents of a letter written by Elias Thorn, late senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company."

"I do."

"The original was destroyed?"

"By my hand. After I had read it entire, four times." Mara met the commissioner's eyes. "I knew searches would come. Paper can be found. What is kept here—" they touched their temple, lightly, "—must be tortured out or buried."

A murmur moved through the benches. The magistrate raised one finger and the room went quiet again, the way a room goes quiet when it understands it is present at something it will later claim to have always known was coming.

"Then recite it," the magistrate said. "As fully as you are able. The record will note that this is a recitation from memory, attested by the witness under oath, and that where it conflicts with the company's books, the commission has already ruled the books suspect." He paused. His voice softened by a degree, only a degree. "Take whatever time you need, Mara Vane. The tide will wait for you. It owes you that much."

* * *

Mara closed their eyes.

This was the part they had not told anyone they dreaded—not the speaking, but the opening of the door inside themselves where they had kept the letter folded up tight for weeks, guarded through roll-calls and searches and the long terrible nights of Hale's kindness. To open it now, in front of all these people, meant letting the words out into air where they could never be taken back and never be held again. It was the last giving-up of the last secret. After this, everything Mara knew would belong to the world.

They opened their eyes instead. Their father had taught them that: don't dive blind, even when the water is clear. See where you're going.

"'To whoever finds this,'" they began, "'written by the hand of Elias Thorn, senior clerk, Halloway Trading Company, in the nineteenth year of my service, being of sound mind and failing body—'"

The words came steadily, unhurried, in Thorn's own cadence, because Mara had heard that cadence in the ledger room for years before they ever heard it on paper—the dry precision, the clerk's habit of putting each fact down and stepping back from it as though it might bite. The chamber listened. The stenographer's pen flew. And Mara spoke the thing that Thorn had confessed: how the founders, Aldous Halloway and his partner Merrick, had grown tired of sharing the pearl beds; how they had paid the harbormaster of that year a sum recorded in a coded column Mara could still see in the mind's eye, third floor, west wall, shelf six; how the rival fleet had been scheduled, flagged, and doomed; how the storm warning flags had been left furled by explicit order while forty divers worked the beds beyond the point, and the storm had come in fast off the open water the way autumn storms do, and there had been nowhere for the divers to run except up, and up was only air, and air was only sky, and no one on shore had been allowed to light a lamp.

"They called it weather," Mara said, and their voice held, though just barely, the way a mast holds. "That is the word the company used. Weather. My mother died believing it. I grew up believing it. I dove those same beds believing it, diving over the place where he drowned, and I thought the cold spot I felt sometimes was only current." They stopped. Breathed. "It was not current."

No one moved. Even the children on the shoulders had gone still, the way animals go still when they sense the ground shifting.

"The letter named the divers lost. Forty-one names, counting my father. Thorn wrote them out himself from the true manifest, the one that was replaced in the company's files by a shorter list. He wrote, and I quote him exactly: 'Let the record be corrected while I am alive to correct it, for I copied the false list with my own hand and I cannot carry both weights.'"

Mara closed their eyes then, and began the names.

* * *

They had done this once before, alone, in the dark of the dormitory after burning the letter—lying awake with the smoke's ghost in their nose, whispering the names into the rafters so they'd stay put in memory. But whispering to rafters and speaking to a chamber full of the living were different waters entirely.

"Joren Vane. My father. Diver, twenty-two seasons."

Petrel made a sound low in her chest, not quite a word, and Mara felt it land between the shoulder blades like a hand steadying them.

"Oskar Bell. Diver, nineteen seasons. His daughter still sorts pearls in shed four."

"Denna Marsh. Dredger."

"Tomas Ivo. Diver, eleven seasons. He was sixteen."

The names went out one by one into the bright air of the chamber, and with each one something happened in the room that Mara had not anticipated and could not have planned: the people in the benches began to answer. Not loudly. Not disruptively. Just a soft, grave chorus of recognition—"here," "aye," "that was my uncle," "my grandmother's brother"—rising and falling as the names passed over them like a slow swell. A woman in the third row wept without sound. An old dredger near the wall repeated each name under his breath after Mara said it, committing it, perhaps, to a memory of his own, a second vault against forgetting.

Mara understood then, with a clarity that felt almost physical, what they had been doing all these weeks. Not smuggling paper. Not even delivering justice, though that was happening, was happening in the two uncomfortable men at the board table and the warrants already drafted. They were doing carpentry. They were building a permanent address for grief. For nineteen years their father's death had lived nowhere—no stone, no record, no plot of ground, a fact without a house, wandering the harbor disguised as weather. Now, stroke by stroke of the stenographer's pen, it was being housed. Entered. Kept. A building made of ink that no fixer could burn and no company could buy.

"...and Rill Osei, who taught half the harbor's divers to hold their breath," Mara finished. "Forty-one. The letter says forty-one. The company's list said twenty-six. Let the difference be entered too, because the difference has a name, and the name is lies."

Silence, whole and enormous, filled the marble-cracked chamber like water filling a hold.

Then the magistrate rose. He was not a young man, and rising took him a moment, and he used the moment, Mara suspected, deliberately—to let everyone watch an officer of the crown stand up for a dead diver's child.

"Let the record reflect," he said, and his voice carried to the far walls without effort, "that the commission accepts this recitation as testimony, that it corroborates the deposition of the retired harbormaster, the confession drafts of Elias Thorn, and the documentary evidence presented on the ninth day of these proceedings. Let it further reflect that the names spoken today shall be transcribed, certified, and entered permanently in the public register of this port, and that copies shall be posted at the harbor gate, the customs house, and the company's own front door." A pause, and something dry and fierce moved through his face. "Where all may read them. Where nothing may erase them."

He looked at Mara. "Is there anything further you wish the record to hold?"

Mara had prepared an answer. Something measured, something about accountability, something worthy of the blue coat and the occasion. They opened their mouth to give it.

What came out instead was quieter, and truer.

"My father kept an orchard once, before the company took the papers. He used to say a man's history should grow somewhere, that a name planted is a name that keeps. The company sold the ground. The trees are gone. One survived—I have seen it." Their hand went, without their willing it, to the lining of the coat, over the seed pod, over the small hard future sleeping there. "So enter this too, if the record can hold such a thing: that Joren Vane planted trees, that his trees were stolen, and that his child means to plant again. On free ground. Where the company holds no deed, because there is no deed for what a person decides to begin."

The stenographer wrote it all. Every word. Mara watched her do it, watched the pen dip and move and dip again, and thought: *this is what copying should feel like.* Nineteen years of copying other people's truths, other people's debts, other people's crossings-out—and now the pen moved for them, and the entry was their own, and it would outlast every ledger on the Halloway shelves.

"Then these proceedings stand adjourned," the magistrate said, "pending final findings, which will be read publicly at week's end. And, Mara Vane—" he gathered his papers slowly, "—when the findings are read, be here. The record is not finished with you. I suspect it never will be, if you have your way."

* * *

Afterward, the chamber emptied slowly, the way a tide goes out—reluctantly, leaving things stranded and gleaming. People stopped beside Mara in the aisle without knowing what to say, and mostly said nothing, and their saying nothing was enough. A dockhand pressed something into their hand: a single pearl, unremarkable, harbor-loose, the kind Mara had spent a childhood diving for. "From the beds," he said gruffly. "Yours earned it back somehow." Then he was gone before thanks could complicate things.

Sable waited by the great doors, leaning against the frame with studied carelessness that fooled no one.

"Well," Sable said. "You said them all. Every name."

"Every one."

"I kept count. In case you faltered." Sable's jaw tightened, the old scar there going white. "You didn't falter."

"You counted anyway."

"Somebody had to guard the door," Sable said simply, "even if the door was only listening." Then, lighter, because Sable could never let gravity sit too long without stealing a coin from under it: "The widow's hillside is waiting, you know. Petrel's been asking me every day when you'll ride out. She talks about your saplings like they're grandchildren she intends to spoil."

"Soon," Mara said. And meant it. There was ground above Saltmere now with Mara's name on the purchase paper—bought with the last pearls, signed with their own hand, the strangest and sweetest signature of their life—and seeds in a coat lining that had waited through fire and search and betrayal, patient as anything that truly intends to live.

But that was for tomorrow, and the day after, and the year after that.

Tonight there was one more thing. Mara walked out of the customs house into the gold end of the afternoon, past the harbor gate where a fresh sheet of paper was already being nailed to the post—forty-one names in official ink, posted where every diver, every clerk, every child passing through would have to read them or step around them—and down the worn steps to the waterline, where the harbor lay calm and bright and full of its kept dead.

Mara crouched at the edge, where the stone was green with old weed, and let the small tide touch their fingers.

"Heard it, Da?" they said softly. "All of it. It's written down now. It's got an address."

The water said nothing, the way water does, and the way water does when it is satisfied.

Behind them, up the hill, Petrel was waving from the gate, and beside her stood a figure in a traveling coat with a satchel of legal papers and a look of purpose that Mara recognized, with a start, as their own reflection—because word had traveled across the strait, and families in three ports were asking for someone who knew the ledgers, someone who had been invisible and wasn't anymore, someone who could speak names into records.

Someone, in short, like Mara Vane.

Mara stood, wiped their hands on the blue coat, and turned uphill toward whatever was being asked of them next.
