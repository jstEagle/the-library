## 40. The Morning Tide

The hillside smelled of turned earth and salt wind, and the saplings stood in their rows like children lined up for inspection—thin, stubborn, green at the tips where the year had written its small victories into them.

Mara Vane walked the rows before dawn, as they did every morning now, though no one required it and no one paid for it. The ground was still soft from the night's rain. Forty trees. Petrel had insisted on forty, one for each diver the water had taken, and Mara had not argued. There were other numbers too—forty-one, if you counted a father who had died believing his daughter would one day stand on ground no one could take from her—but the orchard rows held forty, and the forty-first name lived somewhere else: in the inquiry record, in the magistrate's sealed copy, in the mouth of every diver in Saltmere who now said *Joren Vane* aloud as though the name had never been lost at all.

Mara crouched beside the third sapling in the second row and pressed two fingers into the soil at its base. Damp to the second knuckle. Good. The hillside caught the fog that came off the strait most mornings, and the fog did the watering before the sun could drink it away. Petrel said the trees liked it here because the hill remembered being under water once, long ago, when the sea reached further inland—and trees, Petrel said, trusted ground that had been drowned and come back.

Mara had stopped questioning Petrel's certainties. They had a way of turning out to be a kind of truth that simply hadn't been written down yet.

The eastern sky had gone the color of a pearl held to a lamp—that grey-gold that divers knew better than any priest knew his saints. Mara stood, brushed the soil from their fingers, and looked down the hill toward Saltmere.

The city was waking the way it always woke: smoke from the fish-fires first, then the groan of the crane at the Halloway wharf, then the bells—three of them now, out of sequence, because the customs house had never quite gotten its schedule back in order since the inquiry. The harbor lay flat and bright, and there, riding at anchor in the outer roads with her lamps still lit, was the *Merrow's Debt*.

She was a coastal packet, small and quick, nothing like the great Halloway freighters that used to blacken the horizon. She sailed on the morning tide, and she carried no pearls, no timber, no company freight at all. She carried paper.

Mara had spent the better part of a year learning what that meant—how much weight a sheet of paper could hold, how far it could travel, how many lives it could carry in its folded creases. The *Merrow's Debt* carried depositions. Sworn statements, gathered by Mara's own hand over eleven months, from indentured workers up and down the coast: the girls in the cannery at Greywater Point, the men in the sulfur yards, the shell-mothers who shucked oysters for a wage that was never quite a wage. Forty-two statements this run, sealed in oilcloth, addressed to advocates in the capital who had learned, slowly and at great expense to their own comfort, that Saltmere was worth their attention.

Mara had written none of those statements. Mara had only helped the people write them—had sat in doorways and fish sheds and the back room of the Saltmere chandlery with a borrowed pen and a stub of candle, taking down what people remembered, in their own words, in their own order, without correcting the grammar of their grief. That was the work now. That was the paid work, which still astonished Mara some mornings: that a person could be paid to listen, to record, to carry names.

The bell at the harbor gate rang the half hour. The tide was turning.

Mara took one last look down the rows, then went down the hill to the road, where an old man was already waiting with two mugs of chicory and the patience of someone who had spent sixty years waiting for tides.

* * *

"You're late," Petrel said, handing over a mug. "Ship won't wait."

"The ship will wait. Boatswain owes me."

"Boatswains don't owe. Boatswains collect." Petrel's laugh was a dry rattle, like wind through rigging. He was thinner than he'd been a year ago—Hale's constables had seen to that, in the weeks before the magistrate's order came—but his eyes were the same, pale and quick as a gull's, missing nothing. He wore a coat too big for him now, and in its pocket, Mara knew, was the little tin watering can he refused to let anyone else carry. "You going down to the quay?"

"I'm going down to the quay."

"Going to stand there like a post until she's past the point?"

"I'm going to stand there like a post until she's past the point."

"Good." Petrel drank his chicory and looked out at the water with the expression of a man reading something written there. "I like watching them go out. Never could, before. Before, when a ship left with paper on it, you prayed it sank. Now—" He shrugged. "Now I pray it gets there. It's a better prayer. Lighter on the chest."

They walked down together, the old diver and the advocate, past the terraced gardens that had gone in over the spring on the town's landward side—Mara's doing, though Mara never said so; the workers' association had voted the funds and taken the credit, which was exactly how Mara preferred it. Unremarkable. Some habits kept you alive, and some, kept long enough, turned into virtues.

The harbor gate stood open. It stood open all hours now, and the checkpoint desk inside it was staffed by a customs officer who was paid by the town and answered to the magistrate, and who had, on his first day, taken Mara's hand in both of his and said, *I read the record. All of it. I wanted to see the face that went with it.* Mara had not known what to do with that, then. A year on, they still didn't, entirely. But they had learned to let people look.

The quay was crowded for a packet sailing, which was itself something new. Word had gone round that the *Merrow's Debt* carried the coast's grievances, and half of Saltmere had come to see her off—dockhands off shift, shell-mothers with their shawls, a knot of young divers from the free cooperative that had formed after the indentures were voided, laughing too loudly the way the young do when they have never known the water to take anything from them. Mara moved through the crowd and was stopped four times before reaching the gangway: a woman wanting to know when the advocate could come to Greywater Point; a boy of maybe ten, holding out a folded scrap of paper with both hands, solemn as a priest, saying his mother had written it and would the advocate see it went in the oilcloth. Mara took it, read the first line—*My husband was held past his term at the sulfur yards, three years past*—and folded it into the inner pocket where the important things went.

"I'll see it aboard myself," Mara told the boy. "And I'll write to your mother when there's an answer."

The boy nodded once, twice, three times, and ran.

At the gangway, the boatswain of the *Merrow's Debt*—a broad, scarred woman named Ilse Voss who had taken the berth after the last boatswain's arrest made the position suddenly available—was checking her manifest. She saw Mara and her face did something complicated: respect, wariness, and the particular tiredness of a person who has agreed to carry dangerous cargo and knows exactly what *dangerous* means.

"Forty-two packets," she said. "Sealed. I don't open them, I don't lose them, and if anyone boards me between here and the capital, I burn them before they touch the rail. That's still the order?"

"That's still the order."

"Good." She glanced at the crowd, then lowered her voice. "There's a man at the customs house who watched us load. Plain coat. Not a stevedore, not a clerk. I put two of my crew on the oilcloth hold regardless."

Mara felt the old cold move through their chest—the cold that had lived there since a dying clerk pressed a sealed letter into their hands and said *the tide, the tide*. It came less often now. But it came.

"Hale?" Mara asked. Just the name, no more.

"Couldn't say. Couldn't swear it was him. He's got a face you remember wrong." Voss straightened. "But I'll tell you what I told the magistrate's man: if the Halloway Company thinks this packet is going to vanish, they can send whoever they like. I'll be in the capital by Friday with every name accounted for, and I'll say so loudly, on the record, with my name attached."

*With my name attached.* Mara thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that this had become the currency of the new age—not pearls, not silence, but names, spoken where they could be written down. The whole world had turned on that. Elias Thorn had died for a page of names. Forty divers had drowned for the erasure of theirs. And now a boatswain stood on her own gangway and offered hers like it cost nothing, like it wasn't the most expensive thing a person could spend.

"Thank you, Voss," Mara said.

"Don't thank me. Send me more work." She almost smiled. "A ship that only carries pearls is a poor ship. A ship that carries grievances—" She patted the rail. "Now that's a ship with ballast."

* * *

They cast off on the turn of the tide, as the packet boats did, and Mara stood at the quay's end with Petrel and watched her go.

The crowd had grown quieter as the moment came—not hushed, exactly, but gathered, the way people gather at a graveside or a christening, the two ceremonies that are secretly the same. The *Merrow's Debt* took the tide cleanly, her sails filling with the offshore morning wind, and as she cleared the inner harbor and came abreast of the point, someone in the crowd—Mara never learned who—began to speak the names.

Not sing them. *Speak* them. One at a time, at a walking pace, the way Mara had spoken them into the inquiry record a year ago, the way they had been entered into the magistrate's permanent ledger: *Joren Vane. Olu Ashkem. Tam Brell. Sefa Brell.* The old litany, the forty. Others took it up, the way a fire takes a dry field—not all at once, but in patches, in clusters, dockhands and shell-mothers and the young divers of the cooperative, each voice carrying a few names and letting the rest go, so that the whole quay murmured like water over shingle. Petrel's voice was in it, cracked and steady, giving the names he'd known alive: men he'd drunk with, women who had taught him to read the storm flags, a boy of sixteen who'd been down on his first deep dive the night the fleet went to its engineered grave.

Mara did not speak. Mara stood at the edge of the quay with the wind pulling at their coat—*the* coat, the one with the orchard deed and the seed pod's empty husk still sewn into the lining, because some things you keep even after they're spent—and listened to the harbor say the names back to the sea.

That was the whole of it, Mara thought. That was the ledger, balanced. Not in coin—the company had paid its settlements in coin, grudging and public, and the coin had bought roofs and medicine and one good winter for the widows, and it had not bought back one hour of one drowned life. Not in land—the orchard had gone to the widow Fairweather's heirs, sold twice over before the ink of the inquiry was dry, and no court in the strait would unmake a sale to make a dead man's daughter whole. Mara had walked that ground once, in the spring, and stood under the one surviving tree—a pear, half-wild, still bearing—and the widow had cut a branch for Mara to take, and Mara had taken it, and that was all the inheritance there was, and it had been enough, in the way that a hand on your shoulder from a stranger is enough. Not payment. Witness.

No. The ledger balanced in names. In the sound of forty voices on a quay saying forty names at a departing ship. In a boy's folded paper riding in an advocate's pocket. In a row of thin saplings on a hillside that fog watered every morning, each one planted over a handful of soil brought up from the drowned orchard's fence line, so that the roots of the new trees and the memory of the old ones held the same ground.

The *Merrow's Debt* passed the point. The lighthouse keeper there—an old ally now, appointed after the inquiry—ran up the storm signal flags, but wrong, deliberately wrong, in the old pattern that had *not* flown the night the fleet sank: three flags, hoisted in full morning light, in clear weather, where every ship in the strait could read them. *Danger. Remember. Danger remembered.* The packet dipped her ensign twice as she passed. The crowd on the quay made a sound Mara had no word for—not a cheer, not a sob, something between, the sound of a wound becoming a story.

Petrel put his hand on Mara's shoulder. His grip was light. It was lighter every month now, and they both knew what that meant, and neither of them said it, because some things are carried better unnamed.

"She's past the point," Petrel said. "You can breathe now."

Mara breathed.

* * *

The chandlery's back room smelled of tar and cold tea, and by midmorning it was full.

This was the office, though the sign outside said only *V. — inquiries taken*. The workers' association had wanted to paint ADVOCATE on the door in gold letters, and Mara had refused, and there had been an argument, and Mara had won by pointing out that a gold-lettered door was a door that could be watched. Unremarkable. But the room filled all the same: a shell-mother with a wage dispute and a memory like a ledger; two young divers from the cooperative with a contract the company's remnant office had tried to slip past them, a bond clause buried in the fourth paragraph—*they never stop trying it, do they,* one of them said, and Mara said, *no, and that's why the room exists*—and, near the end of the morning, Sable.

Sable came in the side way, as Sable always did, with the smell of the fish carts still on them and a new scar along one jaw that they wore the way other people wore jewelry: as proof of having survived the price of things. They set a wrapped parcel on the table—smoked fish, the good kind, the kind that said *I'm not here on business, I'm here because you forget to eat*—and dropped into the chair across from Mara with the groan of a body that had been carrying crates since dawn.

"The Voss packet went out clean?" Sable said.

"Clean. Voss is watching for a watcher. Plain coat at the customs house."

"Mm." Sable's face did something small and closed. "I heard about him. I made some inquiries of my own." A pause, the length of a knife being turned. "It wasn't Hale."

Mara looked up. "You're certain?"

"Hale's in the capital. Has been since the spring—clerk's work, of all things, in some shipping office that doesn't ask questions. The magistrate's order stripped his papers, and no company on the strait will touch a man whose name is in that record. He's finished, Mara. Whatever he was, the inquiry made him into a man who watches other people's ships from a distance, and can't even do that without being seen." Sable leaned forward. "The plain coat was a debt collector. For the customs house clerk. He owes money at cards. I checked."

Mara sat with that for a moment. The cold in the chest, the old cold, loosened another degree. A year of learning, and the hardest lesson was still this: that not every shadow was the same shadow. That the world contained ordinary dangers, small and stupid, and that a person could walk past them without it meaning anything.

"You checked," Mara said. "You went and checked."

"I went and checked." Sable shrugged, and the shrug held everything the words didn't—the quay boards, the blood, the weeks of silence, the second route to the harbormaster, the torture that had taken Sable's teeth and given nothing back. They had settled accounts a long time ago, honestly, the way they'd promised: pearls returned, grudges aired, the whole ledger of their friendship read aloud in a fish shed with the door barred. What remained after that was not what it had been before. It was better. It was a thing that had been tested to breaking and had not broken, and both of them knew the difference now between loyalty sworn over stolen rum and loyalty paid for in blood and bone. "Somebody has to. You're too busy being respectable."

"I am not respectable."

"You have a *door*."

"I have a door with no sign on it."

"Respectable," Sable said again, with relish, and pushed the fish across the table. "Eat. You've got the Greywater trip tomorrow and you'll forget."

* * *

Evening found Mara on the hillside again, because the day's work was done and the hill was where the day went to be understood.

Petrel was there, of course, moving down the rows with the tin can, though the fog had already come in and done most of the watering. The saplings stood hip-high now, the oldest of them, and in another two years—Petrel said three, Mara said two, and they argued about it the way other people argued about weather—they would begin to bear. Pears, mostly. The seed pod from the drowned orchard had held eleven seeds, and eleven had been planted, and nine had taken, and the association had bought the rest from the nursery in the capital to make the number forty. Nine trees of Joren Vane's own orchard, growing on free ground above a harbor that had once priced his daughter at nine years old and a ledger line.

Mara stood at the top of the first row and watched the light go.

Below, Saltmere lit its lamps. The harbor gate stood open and would stay open. Somewhere in the town, in doorways and fish sheds and the back rooms of chandleries, people were writing things down—complaints, testimonies, the small brave records of lives that had never before been considered worth recording—and tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, some of those pages would find their way to Mara's door with no sign on it, and Mara would take them down in the people's own words, and seal them in oilcloth, and see them onto a ship.

The tide was coming in. It came in every day, faithful as a debt, and it brought the fog, and the fog watered the trees, and the trees stood in their rows over the ground that had been drowned and come back.

Mara thought of their father sometimes not as a drowned man now but as a planter—one who had put his trees in ground the company took, and his daughter in a world the company ruled, and his name in a daughter's memory the company could never enter. Three plantings. Two had come up. That was a better harvest than most men got.

"You'll want to see this," Petrel said, from the third row. His voice had an odd note in it. "Come look at your stubborn one."

Mara went. At the end of the third row, the smallest sapling—the runt, the one Petrel had sworn would never take and Mara had refused to dig out—had put on a foot of new growth since the spring, and at its crown, where there should have been only leaves, there was something else. A single bud, tight and pale, closed like a fist around a secret. It was months too early. It had no business existing. It stood in the fog-light with the whole hill's wind on it, and it held on.

"Well," Petrel said. "What do you make of that."

Mara looked at it a long time. The harbor bell rang the hour below, and the fog came up the hill, and somewhere out past the point, in the darkening strait, a small ship was running for the capital with forty-two names in her hold, and the names were safe, and the names would arrive.

"I make of it," Mara said, "that some things come up early, when the ground is finally right."

And the bud held, and the tide came in, and the forty trees stood over their ground in the fog, and the light in the harbor below burned on—lamp after lamp after lamp, all the way down to the water, where the morning tide was already gathering itself to come again.
