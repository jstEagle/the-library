## 1. The Counting Season

The fog came in off the water before dawn, as it always did in October, and by the time Bram Sterling had lit the lamps in the Meridian Trading Company's harbor office, the windows had gone the color of old milk. She liked it that way. Fog was a kindness. It blurred the rusted cranes and the tar-stained pilings and the half-sunk hulks rotting at their moorings, and it made the harbor look like something a man might have loved once, before the company got its fingers into it.

She had been head clerk for three years now, which meant she arrived first and left last and knew where every body was buried — figuratively speaking. The ledgers told you everything if you knew how to read them: who drank, who borrowed, who had signed away a season's wages on a woman's name spelled wrong twice. Ten years of indenture had taught her that paper was more honest than people, and less merciful.

The letter had come up from San Francisco on Tuesday's packet, sealed with the company's double-M stamp and addressed to Mr. Aldous Crane, resident agent, in a hand that did not belong to Crane's secretary. Bram had carried it to him unopened, because that was her trade — carrying things unopened — but she had weighed it in her palm first, the way her father used to weigh a crab to know if there was meat inside the shell. Heavy for one page. Government weight.

*Department of Commerce. Census of 1918.*

Crane read it standing at his window with his back to her, and when he turned around his face wore the expression he saved for weather and auditors: mild, gray, immovable.

"A census taker," he said. "Coming to count us."

"Counting is harmless," Bram said. "Names and numbers."

"Nothing is harmless." He set the letter down and squared its corners with the blotter, a small violence of tidiness. "You'll manage her. Give her what the law requires and not a syllable more. You're good at that."

It was not quite a compliment. Bram took it as one anyway, because ten years in this office had taught her to take what was offered and call it enough.

"I'll need the harbor rolls," she said. "The crew manifests. The household lists for the company rows."

"You know where they are." Crane dismissed her with a nod toward the door, then, as her hand found the latch: "Sterling. She's a government woman. That means she asks questions because it's her work to ask them. Don't make it interesting. Interesting is how trouble starts."

He said it the way other men said *don't touch the stove*, and Bram went out into the fog thinking that Crane had never once in ten years asked her a question he didn't already know the answer to, and that perhaps that was why she trusted him, and that perhaps trusting a man like that was its own kind of trouble.

* * *

Odile Voss arrived on Thursday morning on the eleven o'clock launch, and Bram saw her before she saw the office — a figure at the rail in a coat the color of wet slate, hat pinned against the wind, standing apart from the fishermen and crate-men the way a heron stands apart from gulls. Not taller than the others. Just stiller.

Bram met her at the landing with an umbrella she didn't offer and a smile she'd practiced in the office glass.

"Miss Voss? I'm Bram Sterling, head clerk for Meridian Trading. Mr. Crane sends his regards and the use of our front room, such as it is. There's a stove, and the light's better after two o'clock."

"You're the counting house," Odile Voss said. Her voice was low and even, with an accent that had been sanded down by cities — somewhere eastern, maybe, or just far enough away to be deliberate. "That's convenient."

"It's what we're called."

"I know what you're called. I meant that it's convenient — that the place where the records live is also the place where I'll be working." She looked past Bram at the office above the seawall, at its dark windows, and Bram felt the look go through the walls and along the shelves and stop, precisely, at the third cabinet from the door where the older manifests were kept. "Convenient things interest me. Shall we?"

Bram had managed auditors before, insurance men, a customs officer with a fondness for brandy. She knew the choreography: give them the chair nearest the stove, keep the coffee coming, answer every question fully and slightly wrong. But walking up the plank road beside Odile Voss, listening to the fog drip off the wire grass, Bram felt the odd sensation of being counted herself — measured step for step, breath for breath.

In the front room, Odile set out her materials with the economy of someone who had done this in forty towns: ledger book, ink bottle wrapped in flannel, a tin of pencils sharpened to needles. She did not ask about the stove. She did not ask about the light. She opened her book to a fresh page and wrote the date, and the date alone, and looked up.

"Begin wherever you like," she said. "I'll interrupt when I need to."

So Bram gave her the harbor the way she'd give it to a mapmaker. Four hundred and six souls in the company rows, counting children; sixty-one men on the active diving and salvage crews; the cannery women seasonal, mostly gone south by November. Names, ages, trades. She recited them from memory, smooth as tide, and watched Odile write without looking up, pencil moving in small quick strokes, and thought: *this one will be easy. This one only wants the surface.*

For an hour, that held. Then, near noon, with the fog burning off and the light coming hard through the east window, Odile laid her pencil flat across the page and said:

"The crews. You count them by boat?"

"By boat, yes. Each vessel keeps its own manifest."

"And the vessels — do they stay long in your registers? Or do some... end?"

Bram poured coffee. It was a gesture she'd performed a thousand times, and her hands did not shake, because she would not permit them to. "Boats wear out. Storms take some. A few are sold south."

"Which ones were lost?"

"There've been three sinkings since I came to the office. The *Cormorant*, '13, driven on the bar. The *Sable Ann*, '15, boiler failure. And—" she let the pause sit, natural as breathing, "—the *Pearl Lark*. Before my time as clerk. I'd have been a girl."

Something changed in Odile Voss's face. It was very slight — a settling, like water finding its level — and Bram understood suddenly that she had just given the woman something, though she could not yet say what.

"The *Pearl Lark*," Odile repeated. She wrote the name. Underlined it. "Four men, I'm told."

"Who told you?"

"The shipping notices. They run years behind out here, but they run." She picked her pencil back up. "You said before your time. Yet you recited the date of her sinking without opening a book."

Bram heard the trap close with no sound at all. She had given the year — 1911, October, seven years ago almost to the week — and she had given it from memory, from the place where she kept it, which was everywhere and nowhere, which was the shape of her father's shoulders against the water.

"Harbors remember their dead," she said. "It's cheaper than remembering anything else."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a crane groaned under a load of pit props, and the sound came through the wall like something clearing its throat.

"That's well put," Odile said finally. "I'll use it myself someday, if you don't mind." She turned to a new page. "Now. The households. Start with the row nearest the water."

* * *

They worked until four. Bram answered everything, and answered nothing, and marveled a little at her own fluency — how the half-truths rose in her like breath, worn so smooth from use they felt like honesty. She gave Odile the names of living men and the trades of living boats. She gave her the population of the company rows down to the Hendersons' new twins. When Odile asked about wages, Bram quoted the published scale. When Odile asked whether the published scale was paid, Bram said, "The books will show whatever the books show," and smiled, and Odile smiled back, and neither of them believed the other for a moment.

Near the end, packing her pencils, Odile paused over her tally.

"One more thing. Not for the census, strictly. For my own understanding of the district." She capped her ink. "The men lost on the *Pearl Lark*. Did any of them leave family here? The department likes to note dependents. Widows, children."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below the seawall, the tide was turning, and Bram felt it turn in her own chest, slow and enormous.

"No widows," she said. "One daughter, I believe. She was sent to an aunt inland."

"Her name?"

"It wouldn't be in the census," Bram said. "She'd be grown now. Counted elsewhere."

"Everything's somewhere in a record," Odile Voss said, rising, buttoning her slate-colored coat. "That's rather the whole point of my work. Finding where." She gathered her book against her hip and paused at the door, and the last of the daylight caught her face and made it briefly, unbearably open. "Thank you for today, Miss Sterling. You're very good at this."

"At what?"

"At answering." The door stood open on the fog coming back in off the water, patient as ever, covering everything. "Most people aren't. Most people either tell the truth badly or lie clumsily. You do neither. It's almost an art." She stepped out onto the plank road, then turned, half in the gray. "I'll be here a fortnight at least. A harbor this size takes time to count properly. I do hope we'll have the chance to talk again — about the *Pearl Lark*, perhaps. About the daughter."

Then she was gone, a slate figure dissolving into the fog, and Bram stood alone in the front room with the cooling stove and the smell of her own careful words hanging in the air like smoke.

She should have felt victorious. She had managed her, exactly as Crane ordered — every question answered, nothing given away, the surface smooth as poured wax. Instead she felt the way she'd felt at nine years old, treading water past her depth while her father watched from the boat: competent, controlled, and quietly aware of how much ocean was underneath her.

Bram banked the fire, locked the cabinets, and stood a moment with her hand on the third drawer from the door — the one with the older manifests, the one she had not opened in seven years.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would check what the *Pearl Lark*'s manifest actually said. Only to know what the woman knew. Only to be prepared.

She told herself that all the way home through the fog, and did not believe a word of it.
