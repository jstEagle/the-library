## 2. One Question Too Many

Bram had learned the shape of lies the way other girls learned needlework: early, patiently, and under a mother's eye. Her father's death had been her first sampler — *a diving accident, poor man, the water was rough that morning* — stitched in a child's voice until the thread ran smooth. Ten years in Meridian's counting house had turned that stitching into something like art. She could lie with figures now, which was finer work than lying with words, because figures left no tremble in the throat.

She arrived at the harbor office before dawn to do exactly what she had promised herself she would not do: nothing. She sat at her desk with the lamp trimmed low and the third drawer three feet from her knee, and she did not open it. She entered yesterday's receipts instead, in her small upright hand, and let the fog press against the windows like a face trying to look in.

The fog on this coast did not lift so much as thin. By eight it had gone from wool to gauze, and through the gauze came Odile Voss, punctual as tide.

"Good morning, Miss Sterling." She set her canvas satchel on the counter and unwound a gray scarf with the economy of someone who wasted no motion twice. "I've finished the cannery row. Forty-one souls, two of them counted twice by the last census taker, I believe, since neither would admit to being the other."

"The company keeps its own rolls," Bram said. "I can save you the trouble of—"

"That's kind." Odile produced her notebook, opened it flat against the counter, and looked at Bram over the top of it with eyes the color of harbor water. "But the Dominion pays me for my own counting, not for borrowing yours. Where shall we begin today? The workshop men?"

Bram gave her the workshop. She walked her down the rows of bunks and drying racks herself, naming names with the fluency of long practice, watching Odile write each one down in a hand so small and even it looked typeset. The men answered questions about age and birthplace and years of service without looking up from their boots. They knew what census meant here. It meant someone writing things down, and written things belonged, sooner or later, to the company.

It was at the drying loft, among the racks of oilskins breathing their smell of tar and brine, that Odile closed her notebook.

"I have a question that isn't for the rolls," she said.

Bram kept her face where it was. "The Commissioner said you were to ask me anything."

"Not him. You." Odile turned to face her fully. "Four years ago — no. Nine. The dive boat *Pearl Lark* went down off Kestrel Point with four men aboard. I'm told there was an inquiry of a sort, very brief. Who owned the boat that night?"

The question went into Bram like a splinter finding old wood. Nine years. Not four. The woman had done her reading.

"A salvage concern," Bram said. "Independent. The company chartered divers from them sometimes, but the boat wasn't ours."

"What was the concern called?"

"It folded," Bram said. "After the accident. Small operations do, after a loss like that."

"And who owned it before it folded?"

"A man named Corben," Bram said, and heard how easily it came, the name rising out of her like breath. She had rehearsed Corben for nine years; he had never existed, but he was well made. "Hollis Corben. He lost everything and went south. You won't find him."

"Mm." Odile wrote nothing. That was worse than writing. "And the diver who drowned. Sterling. Any relation of the head clerk at the harbor office?"

The fog outside the loft windows had thinned to almost nothing. Bram wished it back with all her heart.

"No relation," she said. "A common enough name on this coast."

"Common enough," Odile agreed mildly, and reopened her notebook, and asked after the ages of two deckhands, and moved on.

* * *

Bram got through the rest of the day the way one gets through deep water: by not thinking about the surface, because thinking about it makes the lungs seize. She balanced accounts. She signed requisitions. She ate her lunch standing at the window, watching the harbor lie flat and dull under a sky the color of pewter.

At half past five the office emptied. Hask had gone up to the mill with the day's reports; the junior clerks had scattered into the fog toward their suppers. And the third drawer sat three feet from her knee, patient as a grave.

She had told herself she wouldn't. She had told herself it was only preparation. Both statements were true in the way most of her statements were true — technically, uselessly.

She unlocked the drawer.

The older manifests smelled of camphor and dust, and beneath that, faintly, of brine that had never quite dried out of the paper. She found 1909 and pulled the bundle. The *Pearl Lark*'s charter was the fourth document down. She read it standing, though her legs wanted otherwise.

Charter party, dated the eleventh of March, 1909. Vessel *Pearl Lark*, engaged for salvage survey, Kestrel Point shoals. Owner of vessel and gear:

Her eye slid past the line once. Then went back.

Not Hollis Corben. The ink, browned now but still legible, said *Meridian Trading Company, per J. Hask, agent.* Owned outright. Chartered to itself. Every word of her fluent testimony about independent concerns and ruined men — she had built that lie brick by brick over nine years, and the whole edifice stood on a page that had been sitting in her own cabinet, in her own keeping, since before she was trusted to keep anything.

Her hand went to the desk edge. The wood was cool and solid and did not help.

Below the ownership line, the manifest listed crew. Four names. R. Aldous, mate. T. Fenn, tender. W. Grieve, second diver. And the last line, written in a hand she knew better than her own — her father's hand, the strong downstrokes, the little hook he put on his g's:

*D. Sterling, master diver.*

And beside it, in the margin, smaller, squeezed in where the column ran narrow, in the same hand, almost playful:

*B. Sterling, passenger (daughter, 12), returned ashore prior to sailing.*

She had been on the books. She had been a line item, twelve years old, logged and filed, and then struck from every story the company ever told. She remembered that day with sudden terrible clarity — her father lifting her onto the dock, saying *not this trip, birdie, the water's too cold for you,* and pressing a peppermint into her palm, and the boat going out through the morning fog with the four of them waving, and only three ever coming back, and none of them coming back right.

There had been no Hollis Corben. There had been no inquiry worth the name. There had been a boat the company owned, carrying four of the company's men, insured by the company's insurers — and a twelve-year-old girl who had been written into the record and then written out of it, except that nobody had thought to strike this one marginal line.

Bram stood in the empty office while the lamp hissed softly and the fog came back in off the water, thickening against the glass, closing the world down to the size of the room.

She had spent ten years learning that survival meant silence. She had believed the silence was hers to choose.

It had never been hers. It had been assigned to her, filed and initialed, the same as any other entry in the ledger.

She put the manifest back precisely where it had lain, squared the bundle, locked the drawer, and pocketed the key with a hand that was nearly steady. Nearly.

Outside, the fog had swallowed the harbor whole. Somewhere out on the black water, a bell buoy tolled — three strokes, pause, three strokes — marking a channel that led, if you trusted it, safely past Kestrel Point.

Bram pulled her collar up and started home through it, and behind her, in the dark office, the third drawer held the truth of her father's death in her own custody — the first thing the company had ever given her that she might choose whether to keep or give away.

She noticed, somewhere in the fog between the office and her door, that she was already planning how to get into the archive room after hours.
