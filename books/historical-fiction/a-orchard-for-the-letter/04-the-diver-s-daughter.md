## 4. The Diver's Daughter

The fog came in off the water the way it always did in October, thick as wool and smelling of kelp and coal smoke, and Bram walked up through it to the cemetery on the bluff with her collar turned up and her hands in her pockets, feeling like a girl again instead of a clerk of thirty-one years with ink stains she could never quite scrub from her cuticles.

She had not meant to come. She had told herself all morning — while she balanced the column for the herring contracts, while she initialed the freight manifests, while she smiled the company smile at a buyer from Astoria who wanted to know if Meridian's divers were "still reliable" — that she would go home to her rooms above the chandlery, eat her bread and cheese standing up, sleep. But her feet had carried her out the harbor road instead, past the cannery with its rusted roof, past the last straggle of houses where the lamplighter was already making his rounds against the early dark, and up the switchback trail where the salal grew rank and the fog dripped from the spruce boughs like slow rain.

Her mother's grave sat in the second row from the seaward fence, where you could hear the water on a clear day. There wasn't much to mark it. A slate headstone with the paint gone soft, MARGARET STERLING, 1871–1912, and beneath the dates a line Bram had paid the stonecutter extra to carve: *She counted what mattered.* It had seemed right at the time. Her mother had kept the household books in a hand so fine it looked like engraving, had kept count of everything — eggs, cordwood, pennies, prayers — and had died counting, some said, though Bram knew better. Her mother had died of the fever, and of waiting, and of knowing things she could do nothing about.

Bram crouched and pulled the fireweed from around the stone's base with numb fingers. The earth was cold and gave off that smell of wet rot and iron that clung to this coast the way salt clung to rope.

"I found something," she said aloud, because there was no one else to say it to. The fog took the words and kept them. "Or it found me."

* * *

Her father had taught her to hold her breath when she was six years old, in the shallows below the point where the Pearl Lark used to moor.

He was a diver then, one of the best on the coast, a man who could work the deep beds off Cape Arago in water that made the younger men quit by noon. He had shoulders like a yoke and hands that were always cold even in summer, and he believed — with a seriousness he applied to nothing else, not church, not politics, not the company men who came to weigh his catch — that a person who worked the water owed the water honesty. You did not lie about depth. You did not lie about your air. You did not lie to yourself about how long you could stay under.

"Again," he'd say, kneeling on the gravel bar in his wool drawers, water to his chest, and little Bram would stand beside him shivering and fill her lungs until her ribs ached. He'd put one broad palm flat on her sternum. "Don't gulp it. Take it slow, like pouring. The panic is what spends your air, not the swimming. Panic is just lying to yourself about how much time you have."

Then he'd press her gently down and count.

She could stay under eleven seconds that first summer. By the time she was nine she could sit on the bottom in eight feet of green light and watch the perch drift over her knees, her father's shape wavering above her like something out of a story, and she learned what he wanted her to learn: that the water tells you everything if you keep quiet long enough. Where the current runs. Where the fish hold. Where the bottom drops away into cold and dark.

"Read it," he'd say after, toweling her hair with a flour sack. "What did it tell you?"

And she'd tell him: the tide was turning, the wind would back to the southeast by evening, there were crabs under the third rock because the sand was fanned in a crescent before it.

"That's my girl," he'd say, and his eyes would crinkle half shut, and for a moment the hard careful man would be someone warm. "Now the other lesson."

The other lesson was quieter. He taught it without teaching it, the way he did everything. She learned it watching him in the Meridian office, hat in his big hands, answering the paymaster's questions in short flat words that gave away nothing. She learned it the night two company men came to their kitchen and spoke low to her father while her mother stood at the stove with her back very straight, and afterward her father said only, "Nothing you need to mind," and her mother said nothing at all, and the silence between them had a shape Bram was too young to read but old enough to remember.

Keep quiet. Keep your air. Don't spend yourself on panic.

It was the summer she was twelve that the Pearl Lark went down with four men aboard — her father among them, and the Okafor brothers who dove the north beds, and young Tom Ellery who was only nineteen and engaged to be married in the spring. The company called it weather. A squall off the bar, a swamped boat, four good men lost to the sea the way men were always being lost to the sea, God rest them, here's your widow's payout, sign here, initial there, next page.

Bram had signed nothing. She'd been twelve. But she had watched her mother sign, watched the pen tremble, watched the paymaster slide the paper away with a smoothness that even then, even at twelve, had looked like a conjurer's trick.

For sixteen years Bram had done what her father taught her. Kept quiet. Held her breath. Counted the seconds until she could surface. Ten years indentured to the company that owned the boat that killed him, balancing its books in her fine clerk's hand, telling herself it was survival, that silence was a kind of honesty because she wasn't actively lying, that a body had to breathe somehow and this was the only air available.

And then Odile Voss had walked into the office with her census book and her precise, unhurried questions, and asked — so mildly, so bureaucratically — whether Bram remembered the Sterling family, the diver's family, and whether the records might show the ownership of a vessel lost in 1902, and Bram had felt her lungs begin to burn.

She had lied to her. Fluently. Beautifully. *I was a child. The ledgers from those years are archived; I couldn't speak to them. So many boats were lost in those seasons.*

And then she had gone down into the archive that night like a diver going down past her depth, and she had found her own name in a ledger she was never meant to read — *Sterling, B., ward of company, board & schooling charged against estate of E. Sterling, deceased* — and understood that the company had not merely taken her father. It had bought her childhood at ten dollars a month and called it charity, and she had spent ten years thanking it with her penmanship.

Bram knelt in the wet grass now, her mother's stone cold under her palm, and let herself feel the whole weight of it at once — which was, she realized, the thing she had never once allowed herself to do. Not at the funeral. Not at the inquiry that never was an inquiry, just a form signed in triplicate. Not once in twenty-six years.

Grief went through her like cold water through a wetsuit, finding every seam.

When it passed, she stayed kneeling, and the grief did something strange: it cooled, and set, and hardened into something with edges. Something she could grip.

"Mama," she said, "he taught me to keep quiet. And it kept me alive. But I don't think—" Her voice caught. She started again, slower. "I don't think it's what he meant."

Because her father hadn't taught silence. He'd taught reading. Read the water. Read the room. Read the men at the door. Silence was just how you held your breath long enough to see clearly — and she had seen clearly now, and holding still any longer wasn't prudence. It was drowning politely.

She stood. Her knees cracked. Down in the harbor the lamps of Meridian's office burned yellow through the fog, and beyond it the workshop lofts, and beyond those the drying sheds where the company's paper lived — twenty-six years of manifests and payouts and insurance filings, every lie in its neat hand, all of it waiting.

* * *

Hask was in the yard when she came down the trail, standing under the eave of the net loft with a lantern, smoking. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a capstan, with a face the weather had worked over for decades and small pale eyes that missed less than people assumed. Foreman of the harbor works for longer than Bram had been alive. He watched her come across the gravel with that unhurried attention of his, and Bram felt the old instinct rise — smooth it over, smile, give the fluent answer —

and put it down deliberately, like setting down a heavy load.

"You've been up the hill," Hask said. Not a question. Smoke curled from his pipe and vanished into the fog.

"To my mother's grave."

"Hm." He knocked the pipe against the post. "Fog'll soak you through. Shouldn't be walking alone at night anyway. Harbor's full of strangers lately. Census people." He said the word the way another man might say *rats*.

"Miss Voss is doing her duty."

"Duty." Hask almost smiled. "That's one name for it." He turned and began walking toward the office, and Bram fell into step beside him because she meant to, because she had decided on the bluff that she was done letting conversations happen to her. Gravel crunched. Somewhere out in the fog a buoy bell tolled, patient as a heartbeat.

"Hask," she said. "The Pearl Lark. Nineteen-oh-two."

He didn't break stride, but something in his stride changed — a half-inch of caution, the way a horse shortens its gait on uncertain ground. "Old business."

"It's in the ledgers. I read it today. The replacement hull was insured for twice what the Lark was worth. The payout went to a signature I can't find anywhere else in twenty-six years of files. And the survey of the wreck was conducted by a man who, according to the shipping register, was in San Francisco that week." She kept her voice level, clerk-flat, the numbers doing the work. "I balance these books, Hask. They don't balance."

They had reached the corner of the office building. Hask stopped and set his lantern on the barrel there, and in its light his face was suddenly close and detailed, every furrow legible, and his pale eyes rested on her with something that was not unkind. That was the worst part. It was not unkind.

"Bram." He said her name the way he'd say it to a daughter, if he'd had one. "You're a good clerk. Best I've seen. You know why? Because you understand accounts." He tapped the lantern glass with one blunt finger. "Every account settles. That's all an account is — a debt waiting to settle. The company has carried a lot of debts a long time, and there's a war on, and wars end. When they do, the charter ends with it, and everything gets settled. All at once. Clean." He picked up the lantern. "You want to be standing clear when that happens. You hear me? Whatever you found, whatever you think you found — paper burns faster than you'd believe. I've seen a whole warehouse go in forty minutes. Nothing left but nails."

The buoy bell tolled. Bram made herself meet his eyes.

"Is that a warning, or a threat?"

"It's arithmetic," Hask said. "Same thing, mostly." He lifted the lantern and started toward his quarters, then paused, his back to her. "Your father was the finest diver I ever worked with. I liked him. Remember that tomorrow, when you decide what kind of clever you're going to be."

He went into the fog, and the light went with him, and Bram stood alone in the dark of the yard with her heart going hard and slow, the way it does underwater.

Everything gets settled. Paper burns faster than you'd believe.

She thought of the archive below the counting house, row upon row of bound volumes, her father's death filed somewhere in them between a shipment of tin and a dispute over cordwood. She thought of Odile Voss's precise questions, and the copy of the manifest page Bram had folded into her bodice that afternoon without fully admitting to herself she was going to take it. She thought of her father's palm on her chest: *the panic spends your air.*

She was not going to panic. She was going to read the water — and the water was saying the current was turning, and the bottom was about to drop away.

Tomorrow, she would find the census taker.
