## 6. The Armistice Rumor

The news came in on the morning boat, folded inside a newspaper three days stale and smelling of bilgewater and someone else's tobacco.

Odile Voss heard it before she read it — heard it in the changed pitch of the harbor office, the way men's voices dropped and then rose again, too quickly, like water finding a new level. She was at her corner desk in the counting house with her coat still buttoned, copying manifest numbers into her field book in a hand she had trained to be small and dull, when the door swung open and let in the fog along with the harbor master's runner.

"Peace terms," the boy announced to the room at large, breathless, important. "They're saying terms might be signed inside the month. Maybe weeks."

For one heartbeat the office was silent. Then it erupted — hats thrown, whistles, a clerk from shipping actually climbing onto his stool to shout at the ceiling as though the ceiling were Europe. Someone produced a bottle of something amber from beneath the floorboards of the freight desk, and the smell of it cut through the ink and coal-smoke like a knife through tallow.

Odile did not celebrate. She watched instead.

She had learned, in eleven years of counting other people's households, that the truth of any room lived in the faces that *didn't* move. And so, over the rim of her ledger, she found them: the two senior officers by the safe, who exchanged not joy but a look — flat, quick, arithmetic. The paymaster, whose smile arrived a half-second late, like a man remembering an appointment. And Hask, the workshop foreman, standing at the back with his hands folded behind him, staring out the fogged window at the drying loft across the yard, his face as smooth and unreadable as a stone at low tide.

*Weeks*, Odile thought. And then, because her mind was a machine that could not be stopped once fed: *What ends in weeks? What is counted down?*

She knew the answer the way one knows a word on the tip of the tongue — felt its shape before its name.

Meridian Trading held its charter under wartime provision. She had read the incorporation papers herself, months ago, when she'd first begun to understand that this harbor's books were less a record than a costume. A wartime charter meant wartime powers: sealed manifests, exempted cargoes, records kept under company seal rather than public registry. And a wartime charter meant that when the war ended — when the armament contracts lapsed and the provisional licenses dissolved — every document Meridian had generated under that seal became what the lawyers called *obsolete holdings*. Obsolete holdings were disposed of. She had seen the phrase before, in the margin of a requisition slip she'd copied without understanding: *to be cleared at dissolution.*

Cleared.

She looked at the safe. Brass-bound, patient, holding ten years of this harbor's dead.

* * *

By afternoon the celebration had curdled into something busier and worse. The yard rang with new urgency — not panic, nothing so honest as panic, but a briskness that had teeth in it. Men who had moved crates all week at a funeral pace now moved them at a market pace. The "inventory audit" Odile had been hearing about for days accelerated into something that sounded, from the counting house window, like demolition.

She went out into the yard under the pretense of verifying headcounts against the labor rolls, and stood in the fog watching the workshop.

It was a long building of blackened timber and corrugated iron, older than the war, older than her assignment here. Divers' gear hung along its outer wall in rows — canvas suits stiff as shed skins, brass helmets with their round glass eyes clouded by salt. The drying loft above smelled even from here of hemp rope and creosote. For ten years this building had been where the Pearl Lark's tackle was mended, where the ledgers' second set of pages were drafted, where men signed receipts for cargo that existed only in ink.

Now men swarmed over it with oilcloth and crowbars, prying loose anything of value. Not to save it, she understood suddenly. To *empty* it. You do not strip a building you intend to keep.

"You're a long way from your desk, Miss Voss."

Hask. He had come up beside her without sound, which was his gift; a big man who moved like weather. He did not look at her. He looked at the workshop, and there was something in his face she might almost have called tenderness, if she hadn't known better what it was men felt for the places they meant to burn.

"Headcounts," she said. "Wartime regulation. Every body accounted for during inventory."

"Mm." He turned a wrench over in his enormous hand, absently, like a man worrying a coin. "You'll have noticed the news."

"I notice most things. It's my trade."

"It is." He said it without menace, which somehow made it more menacing. "Then you'll know the company will be settling its affairs. Tidying up. There's a satisfaction in that, I find. An ending you can plan for." He finally turned his head, and his eyes were pale and mild as skimmed milk. "Some folk dread an ending, Miss Voss. Me, I've always liked knowing the date."

He walked away toward the workshop, and Odile stood in the fog with her heart going hard against her ribs, doing sums.

An ending you can plan for. Knowing the date.

*To be cleared at dissolution.*

* * *

She found Bram at dusk, at the far end of the pier where the tide slapped the pilings and the fog had thickened into something you could almost hold. Bram was supposed to be reconciling freight tallies; instead she stood at the rail with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at nothing, and when Odile's steps sounded on the wet planks she didn't turn.

"They're moving fast now," Bram said, without greeting. "Faster than they should. Hask has the night crew pulling records from the archive annex — not auditing them. *Pulling.* Crates going into the drying loft. I saw the manifests for the incinerator fuel order this afternoon." Her voice was level, which told Odile how much it cost her. "Two hundred gallons. For an office that burns coal."

"Bram—"

"I did the arithmetic before you did, I think." Now she turned, and her face in the dying light was gray as the water. "The charter dissolves when peace is signed. Everything under company seal becomes obsolete. Obsolete holdings get destroyed — it's in the articles, I checked, I went back through the incorporation file twice tonight to be sure I wasn't wrong, and I'm not wrong, Odile, I'm never wrong about the books—" Her voice cracked on its own precision. "The ledgers go. The insurance files go. Every page that says who owned the *Pearl Lark*, who collected on four drowned men — it all goes into the loft, and then the loft—"

She stopped. Looked across the water toward the workshop, where one light burned high in the drying loft, yellow through the fog, steady as an eye that refused to blink.

"—and then the loft goes with them. That's why they're stripping it. That's why Hask smiled at me today and asked after my mother's people, my father's people, as if he were being kind." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "He wanted to know who would mourn me if the fire spread to the offices. That's what kindness is, in that building. Reconnaissance."

Odile stepped closer. The fog had gotten into everything; her collar was damp, her lashes beaded, and Bram's face swam close and pale as a moon in water.

"Then we stop planning around their calendar," Odile said, "and start working against it. We thought we had until spring. We may have until the end of the month. Perhaps less — if they sign early, if the telegraph runs kind to them—"

"If the *telegraph*—" Bram pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Do you know what my father used to say? That the sea doesn't hurry. That a diver learns patience or drowns of impatience." She lowered her hand. Underneath the fear, something else was surfacing now, rising slow and deliberate, like a shape coming up from deep water into the light. "He'd have gone down tomorrow. Into that archive. Straight through the middle of them."

"Then tomorrow we don't sleep much," Odile said. "Tonight we count what we have and what we're missing. The insurance payout signatures — I need the original policy files, not copies. And the Pearl Lark's ownership transfer. If that single page survives, the rest can be rebuilt around it."

"The ownership transfer is in the master ledger." Bram said it flatly, already knowing, already grieving the knowing. "Master ledger stays locked in Hask's office. Day and night. It hasn't left that room since the year my father died."

"Then it leaves that room soon," Odile said, "or it burns in it. One or the other."

Above the harbor, the foghorn sounded — long, low, patient, indifferent to everything men built and burned beneath it. Bram looked at the light burning in the drying loft, and Odile watched her watch it, and understood that something in Bram Sterling had finished deciding itself in that moment, the way ice decides, all at once and silently.

"Two hundred gallons of fuel," Bram said quietly. "They ordered enough to burn the whole waterfront's sins. But they'll want the papers dry before they light it. Paper burns poorly damp — my father taught me that too, the year the cannery went up. So they'll move everything into the loft, and let the stove-dryers run, and wait." She turned from the rail at last. "Which means there's a night between the last crate going up and the match being struck. There's always a night. Books are heavy. Even arson takes scheduling."

"And you'll know when that night is," Odile said, "because you'll be the one keeping their schedules."

Bram almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile — grief wearing competence like a borrowed coat. "I keep everyone's schedules. It's the one thing Meridian ever taught me well."

She walked back up the pier toward the office, toward the lamps and the ledgers and the man with milk-pale eyes, and Odile stayed a moment longer at the rail, listening to the tide work at the pilings, patient as rot.

Behind her, high in the drying loft, the yellow light burned on. Somewhere below it, she knew, men were stacking paper — names, dates, four drowned men, a girl's childhood signature — sorting a decade into piles marked *keep* and piles marked *ash*.

And somewhere across the water, in a capital full of tired diplomats, pens were being dipped into ink that would decide how many nights they had left.

The clock, Odile thought, had stopped being furniture.

It was the antagonist now. And it was running.
