## 7. Hask's Hand

The archive smelled of cold iron and old glue, the way a thing smells when it has been shut up with its own secrets for too long. Bram had learned to breathe shallowly in that room. Ten years of practice. She stood at the shelf where the master ledger lived — a book the weight of a sleeping cat, bound in green baize gone gray at the corners — and told herself she was only straightening its spine.

Outside, the fog had come in so thick it seemed the harbor itself was exhaling. She could hear the winches on the pier, muffled, like men working underwater. Somewhere a gull cried and gave up mid-note.

She had not meant to take anything. That was what she would say later, if later came: *I only looked.* But her hands had already loosened the strap, already tilted the ledger free of its slot, and the lamplight from the corridor fell across the open page before she could decide whether to let it.

*Pearl Lark — hull & fittings — insured £1,400 — surveyor's fee waived per instruction of R. Hask.*

Her father's boat. Her father's name two lines below, written in an ink that had outlasted him: *Sterling, T. — diver, contracted.*

Bram pressed her thumb against the word as though she could feel his pulse through it. The paper was cool. Everything in this room was cool. The company kept its records at a temperature where nothing rotted and nothing forgave.

"You'll want to close that."

Hask's voice arrived before Hask did, the way it always did, low and even, a man who had never once needed to raise it because he owned every silence in the building. He filled the doorway with his coat still beaded with fog, his hat dripping onto the boards, and behind him the corridor lamp threw his shadow long across the archive floor until it touched her shoes.

"I was reshelving," Bram said. The lie came out smooth as oiled rope. Ten years of practice. "The audit lists were misfiled again."

"Mm." He crossed the room without hurry. He took the ledger from her hands the way a man takes back a tool from a child — not angry, merely certain of ownership — and set it on the table, squaring it against the wood grain. Then he sat, which was worse. Hask standing was weather; Hask sitting was judgment. "Sit down, Sterling."

There was one other chair. It had been placed there for her. He had known she would come.

She sat. The chair creaked under her and she hated that it creaked, hated that her body insisted on announcing itself when everything depended on being nothing at all.

"Odile Voss," Hask said, tasting each syllable like a man checking a coin for clipping. "Census woman. You've been managing her."

"Counting houses and counting people. It's all clerking." He almost smiled at that, and Bram understood she had made an error — humor was a door left open. She closed it. "I've given her what the law requires. Names, ages, occupations."

"And after hours?" He folded his hands. His knuckles were enormous, scarred white along the right hand where a winch cable had taken the skin off years ago; Bram had heard the story so many times she no longer heard it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. "After hours you've been doing more than names and ages."

The fog pressed at the high little window. Bram watched a bead of condensation gather on the glass and begin its slow descent, and she thought: *he doesn't know how much I know.* And then, immediately beneath that thought, colder: *it doesn't matter what he knows. He knows enough.*

"She asks questions," Bram said. "I answer them the way the company answers them. Carefully."

"Carefully." He said the word as though weighing freight on a scale that always tipped toward the house. "You're good at careful, Bram. Best clerk I've had since your father drowned himself into my ledgers." A pause, deliberate, the pause of a man laying a card face-up. "That's what we called it, do you remember? Drowned himself. The inquiry wrote it plain. Man went down on a bad line, bad luck, bad sea. Nobody's fault but God's and the water's."

"The line wasn't bad," Bram said quietly. "I've read the chandlery requisitions. The Pearl Lark's air hose was logged condemned in March and reissued in June anyway. Someone signed for it twice."

For a moment the only sound was the fog ticking against the glass. Hask did not look surprised. Surprise, Bram realized, was a luxury this room had never permitted anyone.

"You've been reading things you weren't meant to read," he said, without heat. "And copying things, I'd wager. Little pages in a little hand, carried home in your boot. I don't need to search your rooms, Sterling. I can smell ink on a person. It's like smelling smoke."

Her mouth had gone dry. She thought of Odile's narrow room above the chandler's, the two of them bent over the table with the lamp turned low, the copied lines drying in rows like fish laid out on a dock. She thought of Pell, the junior clerk who had stopped coming to work eleven days ago, whose landlady said he'd gone south for his health, though no one south of here had seen him. She thought of her mother's grave on the hill, and of her father teaching her, in the shallows off the point, to hold her breath until the world went silver at the edges — *the water tells you everything, Brammie, if you keep quiet long enough to listen.*

She kept quiet now. Long enough to listen.

Hask opened the drawer of the table — the archive table, which had drawers she had never once been allowed to touch — and drew out a document. Three pages, fastened with a brass clip. A deposition, printed fair, with blanks where names should go.

"This is for the inquiry," he said. "When it comes. And it will come; the armistice is coming with it, and when the charter dies, every debt this company owes gets called in by lawyers instead of foremen. So we will have our story straight before the lawyers arrive." He slid the pages across the wood toward her. "It says the Pearl Lark was lost to sea conditions. It says the condemned air hose was a clerical error, corrected before sailing. It says Thomas Sterling signed the reissue himself, being eager for the season's wages, against advice."

"My father couldn't write above his own name," Bram said. Her voice sounded far away to her, a voice coming up through water. "He'd have had someone else sign for him."

"Then it says someone signed for him," Hask said, untroubled. "The blank's there. Fill it however suits."

"And if I don't?"

He leaned back. Outside, faint through the stone, the winches groaned on, hauling in whatever the nets had brought, indifferent as tides.

"If you don't," he said, "you walk out of here tomorrow with nothing. No indenture papers — and understand me, girl, those papers are the only proof you have that you exist. No reference. No wages owed, because I'll see wages *unowed*, I've done it before. And a letter follows you to every counting house on this coast, and there are four of them and they all fly Meridian colors. You'll be twenty-eight years old and unable to prove you were ever born." He said it all gently, the way a man describes weather to a farmer. "Or — you sign, the inquiry closes, the company settles its accounts, and you leave here next spring with your papers in order, a character reference under my own hand, and enough put by to start wherever you like. Portland. San Francisco. Anywhere the fog doesn't follow."

"And Odile Voss?"

"What about her?"

"When she keeps asking. When the copies she's making turn up somewhere they shouldn't."

Hask regarded her for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but a kind of recognition, one survivor taking the measure of another.

"The census woman counted herself into this harbor," he said. "She can count herself back out. Whether she does it on the morning boat or at the bottom of it is a matter entirely of how much noise she makes between now and then."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way the deck of the Pearl Lark must have tilted. Bram looked down at the deposition. The blanks waited for her, patient as graves. *Corrected before sailing. Sea conditions. Against advice.* Every phrase a small clean stone laid over a face.

She thought about refusing. She thought about it honestly, the way you think about swimming out past the breakwater in November: knowing exactly what it costs, knowing you might do it anyway. She thought of Odile's voice last night over the lamplight — *we only need the dates and the signatures, Bram, the truth is short, it's the lying that takes pages* — and she thought of the armistice rumor moving up the coast like a change in the wind, weeks away, maybe days, and the workshop full of records that burned beautifully, everyone knew that, paper and pine and paraffin, a fire that would eat ten years of murder in an hour.

Survive, her father had taught her. Keep quiet. Read the water first.

But he had also taught her, laughing, treading water beside her in the summer light: *never let go of the boat, Brammie. Whatever else you drop, don't drop the boat.*

She picked up the pen.

The nib scratched. The sound it made in that silent room was obscene, a small animal dying slowly. She wrote her name in the space marked for it — *Bram Sterling, clerk, Meridian Trading Company* — and her hand, which had copied thousands of pages without a tremor, shook now so badly the letters came out crooked, a child's signature, a liar's signature, and there was no difference anymore, and that was the whole of it, that was the arithmetic of survival done in one crooked line.

"There," Hask said. Not triumphant. Relieved, perhaps. He gathered the pages, clipped them, returned them to the drawer, and locked it with a key from his watch chain. "You've done the sensible thing. Your father would have wanted you safe."

"Do not," Bram said, and her voice cracked on the second word, "say his name."

Hask paused at the door, hat in hand, fog-light behind him making a pale halo of the corridor.

"Go home, Sterling," he said. "Sleep. Sensible women sleep well." And then, softer, almost kind, which was the cruelest register he owned: "Whatever you and the Voss woman think you're writing by lamplight — the master ledger goes into the furnace Friday, with the rest of the audit. After Friday there'll be nothing left to copy. Think on whether that's worth dying for."

His boots receded down the corridor. The outer door boomed. The fog closed over the sound like water over a sinking thing.

Bram sat alone in the archive with the green ledger squared neatly on the table, locked away from her now, and she discovered she was crying without any feeling in it at all — tears running down a face that might have belonged to someone else, some other clerk, some other daughter. She had signed. She had survived, the way she had always survived, by bending. And bending, she understood now, was not the same as keeping the boat. Bending was what the water did to the thing it meant to drown.

Friday. Two days.

She wiped her face with her sleeve, took up the lamp, and went out into the fog to find Odile — and to tell her that whatever they copied, they would have to steal the master itself before the furnace got it, or lose her father twice.
