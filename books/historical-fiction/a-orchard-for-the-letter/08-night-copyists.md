## 8. Night Copyists

**Bram**

The master ledger slept in the iron cabinet in Hask's office the way a sleeping dog does — with one ear open. Bram had learned its habits over ten years: which nights Hask locked it and pocketed the key, which nights he grew careless after his third glass of the company's own rye, which nights he left it to the boy who couldn't be trusted with anything heavier than a mop. Tonight was the third kind. The armistice rumor had come down from San Francisco on the evening packet like weather, and half the office had gone to the Anchor & Fluke to drink to peace or curse it, depending on how much of Meridian's future they were standing in.

Bram stood at her own desk until the last lamp on the row went out. She had become very good at stillness. Her father had taught her that too — not in words; he'd never wasted words — but by example, treading water beside her in the cove below the drying sheds, saying nothing for whole minutes while she learned what her body did when it was afraid. *The sea doesn't take the quiet ones first,* he'd said once, when she finally surfaced gasping. She was eleven. She hadn't understood him yet.

Now she understood everything, and it had taken a census taker with ink-stained cuffs to teach her.

At half past eleven she moved. The corridor smelled of lamp oil and cold paper and the salt that got into everything this close to the water, working into the grain of the desks, the bindings of the books, the lungs of the men who worked here until they coughed like old engines. The cabinet's lock was older than the building's ambitions. Her father's awl — she kept it on her key ring, had kept it ten years without knowing why until last month — found the bolt's shoulder and persuaded it. The ledger came out heavy as a sleeping child. Four hundred pages of manifests, payouts, signatures of men who had never drawn breath outside an actuary's imagination. Her father's death, itemized in a fine copperplate hand: *Pearl Lark — hull loss, total — paid in full.*

She wrapped it in oilcloth and put it under her coat against her ribs, where it beat against her like a second heart.

* * *

**Odile**

The night watch was named Pell. Odile knew this because she had counted him — that was her profession, after all, counting — three evenings running from the shadow of the cooperage across the lane. He made his rounds at quarter to the hour: wharf, drying loft, counting house, workshop, back again. He carried a lantern and a rifle he held the way men hold things they've been given but never chosen. He was maybe nineteen. He hummed when he thought no one could hear, tunelessly, something with a bugle in it.

She intercepted him at the wharf rail with a story she had rehearsed until it wore smooth as river stone: a light burning late in the counting house, she'd seen it from her room at Mrs. Adey's, and wasn't that against the wartime regulations, and oughtn't someone check it directly, tonight, before the inspector came? She let her voice do what voices did best — carry worry. She was good at worry. She had been carrying enough of it lately to lend some out.

Pell frowned toward the dark shape of the counting house. "Nobody works this late."

"Someone does," Odile said. "I saw the flame move behind the glass. Flame moves when there's a person breathing near it." This was true. It was Bram's lamp, banked low, waiting. A lie built out of true planks holds weight better than any other kind.

He went. She watched his lantern bob away along the boards, and she stood at the rail in the fog with the harbor breathing beneath her — black water, blacker boats, the masts of the herring fleet leaning like patients in a ward — and she thought about how far she had walked from the road that brought her here with a leather satchel and a government form and a belief that counting people was simple arithmetic. Every soul in this harbor was a sum with something hidden in the remainder.

A door opened somewhere behind her, soft as a page turning.

* * *

**Bram**

They met in the copying room as arranged, though arrangement felt like too calm a word for what they were doing. Odile came in smelling of fog and lantern smoke, cheeks pink with the wind off the water, and looked at the lump under Bram's coat and then at Bram's face.

"You have it," she said.

"I have it."

"Then we work."

They worked. The copying table stood under the one window that faced the water, and they shuttered it except for a finger's width, so their lamplight wouldn't show from the lane. Bram turned pages. Odile wrote — she had the faster hand, a clerk's hand, narrow and tireless — transcribing dates, sums, names, the architecture of the fraud laid bare line by line: the Pearl Lark insured twice through two houses under two descriptions; the payout signed by a *T. Merriweather, harbormaster* who had died in '09; a second signature in Hask's unmistakable fist, pressed hard enough to emboss three pages down.

"Read me the next entry," Odile said, pen moving.

"*October fourteenth, 1908. Salvage refused. Wreck declared unsound.*" Bram's voice stayed level. She had practiced level. "*Note: diver Sterling's effects to be sold against debt.*"

Odile's pen stopped. "His effects."

"A knife. A diving watch. Letters, if he had any." Bram heard her own voice go somewhere flat and far away. "They sold my father's letters, Odile. For eleven dollars."

The pen resumed, slower now. Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters like something listening. Somewhere across the harbor a bell buoy counted out the swell, patient, indifferent, the same rhythm her father's chest had made when she lay against it as a child — and Bram made herself keep turning pages, because grief was a current and you did not fight a current, you read it, you angled across it, you got where you were going or you drowned, and she was not going to drown tonight with the evidence of it warm under her hands.

By two o'clock they had forty pages. By three, sixty-three. The candle stubs burned down in their dish like small hours of their lives being measured out, which is more or less what they were.

Then Odile lifted her head. "Do you smell that?"

* * *

**Odile**

Smoke has a grammar. Odile had learned it during the Portland fire of '11, when she'd been boarding near the waterfront and woken at three in the morning knowing before she knew — the sweetness of it first, then the bitterness, then the roar underneath like a train arriving forever. What came under the copying-room door now was the sweet part. Fresh-cut wood, pitch, something green burning that should not have been green.

"The drying loft," Bram said, already standing. Above them. Directly above them, because the loft sat over this wing like a lid over a pot, full of lath racks and turpentine and ten years of the company's paper residue, every scrap of it dried to tinder by the stove pipes.

They went up the stair two abreast because the stair was narrow and neither would go first alone and neither would go second. At the top the heat met them like a wall swung open. Fire had taken the north racks — a clean orange animal, eating fast, and above it the tar of the roof beginning to blister and stink. There was no telling how it started. That was the thing about fire, Odile thought with a strange floating clarity even as the heat shoved her back: fire kept no ledgers. Set or accidental, spilled lamp or struck match or Hask's own hand with the armistice coming — it would burn the same, and it would burn everything, and the inquiry would never sort intention from accident because both would be ash.

"The ledger," Odile shouted. "Is it dry?"

"It's oilcloth—"

"Then it lives. Go!"

But Bram didn't go. Bram was looking at the fire with an expression Odile would remember for the rest of her life — not terror, though terror was in it, but recognition, the way one recognizes a face across twenty years. Whatever the company had done to her father, it meant to end the same way: consumed, unrecorded, *unsound*, written off. And Bram Sterling stood in the doorway of the thing that had eaten her childhood and refused, with her whole stubborn body, to be eaten with it.

"We go out the loading door," Bram said. "The stairs will go before the roof does."

They went down through smoke that tasted of the century itself, all its burned paper and boiled glue, Odile clutching the copied pages inside her coat, feeling them crackle against her ribs like live things. Behind them the loft roared and something structural gave way with a sound like the harbor ice breaking up. The loading door stuck; Bram hit it with her shoulder once, twice, and it gave onto the night and the fog took them in like cold water taking divers.

* * *

**Bram**

They ran. Down the lane, past the cooperage, past the Anchor & Fluke where the lights were coming on window by window as the town woke to the orange glow climbing the sky — and behind them the workshop burned with a thoroughness that no accidental lamp spill could account for, fire leaping the gap to the counting house roof as if it had been shown the way, and Bram understood, running, that it had been. Someone had walked that fire from building to building with intent. Someone had decided tonight was settlement night, armistice or no armistice, because a company that kept its survival in its paperwork does not wait for peace to destroy the evidence.

On the shingle below the high-tide line they stopped, both of them coughing, fog condensing on their scorched faces. The whole harbor front was lit now, red on black water, and the townsfolk were coming down to watch with the helpless festival air of people watching a thing that cannot be stopped. Nobody looked at two women standing in the tideline wrack.

"Sixty-three pages," Odile said at last. She pulled them out and checked them by the firelight, hands shaking only a little. "Every entry. Names, sums, signatures. Enough."

Bram reached under her coat. The oilcloth was warm but whole. The master ledger — four hundred pages, the original, the only original in the world — sat against her heart, intact.

"They think it burned," she said. The words came out steady, and she marveled at them, at herself, at the ten years of silence that had finally taught her something worth using. "They'll stand there tomorrow and count the ashes and believe it's finished. Hask will sleep."

"And?"

Bram looked at the fire, at the workshop where she had signed her life away in ink at fifteen years old, at the roof falling in over the iron cabinet with its empty shelf. Somewhere in that collapse her indenture papers were curling to nothing, and the thought did not frighten her. It freed something in her chest that had been bound since before she knew it was bound.

"And tomorrow," Bram said, "there's an inquiry."
