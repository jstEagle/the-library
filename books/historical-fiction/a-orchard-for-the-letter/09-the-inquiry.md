## 9. The Inquiry

The harbor office had been scrubbed for the occasion the way a body is washed before burial — with care, with cold water, and without love.

Bram noticed it all from the doorway: the floors mopped until the old pitch stains showed through like bruises beneath a bandage, the windows thrown open to a fog that refused to come in and be useful, the long trestle table draped in bunting that had been stored since the coronation and smelled of camphor and mouse. Someone had set out a carafe of water and six glasses, as though what was about to happen were a dinner party rather than an accounting of drowned men.

She stood in the doorway because her legs had made their own decision to stop, and she let them.

*You can still walk away.* The thought arrived in Hask's voice, which was the cruelest thing about it. Ten years of indenture had taught her that voice by heart — reasonable, low, patient as tide. *Sign nothing more. Say nothing. Sit in the back row with your hands folded and be furniture. Furniture survives.*

But her hands were not folded. They held a leather portfolio against her ribs like a child carried out of a fire, and inside it were forty-one pages copied in two hands — hers and Odile's, alternating through those lamplit weeks, Odile's script tall and severe as fence posts, Bram's small and slanted, learned at her father's knee from the pages of his diving log. Forty-one pages, and under her coat, folded into quarters and soft as cloth from handling, the master ledger's most damning leaf, which she had cut free the night the workshop burned rather than leave it for the flames.

The room filled around her. Company men, every one — she knew them by the way they took off their hats and hung them on the same rack, by the way they greeted each other with first names and old jokes, by the particular looseness of men who have never once doubted they will be believed. Meridian's charter officers in their good wool. The insurance assessor, Mr. Pell, whose signature appeared on the Pearl Lark payout between the name of a man who had died in '09 and a man who had never been born at all. And near the front, alone on a bench built for three, sat Hask, who did not look at her, which was its own kind of message.

Odile Voss stood at the far end of the room beside the inquiry's clerk, and when Bram's eyes found her, Odile gave no nod, no signal, nothing but a long level look — the same look she'd given over the census ledger that first morning, the look that had started all of it, precise as a surveyor's line. *I am here,* the look said. *Count yourself.*

"Bram Sterling." The magistrate — a mainland man named Corrigan with a voice like gravel poured slow — read her name off his list and glanced up. "Step forward, please."

Her legs remembered how to work.

* * *

They swore her in on a Bible so worn its cover had gone the color of driftwood, and she put her palm flat on it and felt the heat of everyone else's hands still living in the paper.

"State your position."

"Head clerk, Meridian Trading Company. Harbor office." Her voice came out steadier than she had budgeted for. "Ten years' service."

"You've provided a deposition already, Miss Sterling." Corrigan turned a page. "Signed the eleventh of last month, witnessed by Foreman Hask. In it you state you found no irregularities in the company's manifests pertaining to the vessel Pearl Lark."

"I did state that." She heard herself say it plainly, without flinching, and something in the room leaned forward. "It was a false statement."

The word *false* went through the company men like a dropped glass. Somewhere behind her a bench creaked; someone whispered another man's name. Hask had gone very still at the front, stillness like a held breath underwater, and Bram thought — absurdly, terribly — of her father's first lesson, down at the tideline when she was seven, his big hand flat on her sternum.

*"Don't fight the water, little bird. Fighting spends your air. Go loose. Go quiet. The bottom's not your enemy — panic is. Panic is the only thing down there that can kill you."*

She went loose. She went quiet. She opened the portfolio.

"May I approach?"

Corrigan nodded once, and she crossed the floor with the pages held out before her, and the walk was the longest of her life — longer than the walk to her father's funeral, where she had been nine years old and had not been permitted to cry because her mother needed both her hands. She laid the copies on the table before the magistrate, each page squared to the last, because order was the one language she spoke fluently, and then she stepped back and began.

"The Pearl Lark left this harbor on the night of March second, 1908, with four men aboard. My father, Elias Sterling, was contracted as lead diver. The others were Tom Quill and the Ferro brothers, all of them on Meridian's books. The vessel was insured for four hundred pounds — nearly triple her surveyed value, the survey having been performed by an assessor named Grady who does not appear anywhere in the company's employment records before or after that date, because there was no such assessor. There is no such man."

She turned to the second page. Her own handwriting looked up at her, small and slanted, transcribed from the master ledger by lamplight while the fog pressed at the windows.

"The boat did not founder. She was scuttled. The hull was bored from inside below the waterline — the salvage report notes the borings angled upward, which happens when a hole is drilled from within a settling hull, and not otherwise. My father was found with his weight belt buckled and his air line severed clean, not torn. Clean, Magistrate. Lines tear. They do not cut themselves square." Her voice caught on that — one hairline fracture — and she let it, because hiding it would have cost more breath than showing it. "He taught me everything about water there was to teach. He would never have gone down with his belt unbuckled, and he could not have cut his own line square while drowning."

"And who," Corrigan said quietly, "gave the order?"

"Charter Officer Ambrose Wren signed the insurance claim. Charter Officer Silas Mowbray countersigned the payout and the manifest amendments. But the order itself—" she turned to the third page, the leaf she had cut from the master ledger and now laid down with fingers that trembled only a little, "—is here. Dated February the twenty-eighth, 1908, three days before the sinking. *Pearl Lark to be written off; arrangement confirmed; dividend to follow.* It is initialed A.W., and beneath it, in the margin, in Hask's hand, the word *handled.*"

She did not turn to look at Hask. That was the hardest discipline of all. She kept her eyes on Corrigan and on the page, and she heard, behind her, the sound of a man rising halfway off a bench and being pressed back down by the weight of witnesses.

"Miss Sterling." Pell, the insurance assessor, had found his feet, smoothing his waistcoat, wearing the smile men wear when they step onto ice they believe will hold. "You'll forgive the observation, but you are an indentured clerk. Bound to the company whose officers you're accusing. Under considerable— pressure, shall we say, given recent events. A fire, I understand. A theft from the archives. Might a young woman in your circumstances not say whatever seemed safest? Whatever some— outside party—"

"Mr. Pell." Corrigan's gravel again, but directed now downhill. "The witness will answer questions. You will wait to be asked."

Pell sat. The room settled. And Bram understood, standing there in the camphor smell and the gray light, that she had just been offered the door — that a cleverer coward than Pell would have walked through it, wept a little about coercion, withdrawn everything, and been forgiven by Friday. The company men were waiting for exactly that. She could feel their patience like warmth from a stove. *Say it was the Voss woman. Say you were frightened. We'll take care of you, little bird. We always have.*

Ten years they had taken care of her. Ten years of silence bought at a penny a word, and her mother dying with the truth locked in her throat, and her father ten years at the bottom of the harbor mud while the men who put him there drank sherry at Christmas and asked after her health.

"My deposition was false," she said again, louder, "because Foreman Hask told me that if I did not sign it, my indenture papers would be voided and I would be put ashore with nothing, and I believed him, because he has done it to others. But my father did not sign anything false in his life, and I am done signing things false in mine. Everything in these pages I copied myself, from the company's own ledgers, in the company's own ink. Check the hand. Check the entries. Check the grave markers against the payroll — you will find that two of the men listed as receiving dividends after the Pearl Lark sank were buried years beforehand, which is a difficult trick even for a trading company."

A sound went through the room — not laughter, not quite, but its grim cousin.

Corrigan drew the cut ledger leaf toward himself and read it twice, slowly, his finger moving under the initials. When he looked up, he looked past her, at the row of wool coats and good hats.

"Foreman Hask," he said. "You'll remain. Officers Wren and Mowbray — you'll remain also. Everyone else, out. We'll reconvene at two o'clock, and I want the harbor master's original wreck report on this table, and I want it untrimmed."

* * *

Outside, the fog had begun to lift, tearing into rags along the mastheads, and the light came down the harbor in long gray shafts like something searching. Bram stood on the office steps with her empty portfolio under her arm and discovered that her knees had opinions now that the work was done. Odile came out beside her and said nothing, which was the correct thing; they stood shoulder to shoulder in the thinning white while somewhere below a gull screamed at the water.

"They'll appeal," Odile said at last. "Or scatter, or burn whatever's left. Men like that don't end, Bram. They just change harbors."

"I know." Bram watched a steamer nosing in through the fog-gap, its wake unrolling behind it like a page being turned. "But they'll end *here.* In the record. That's not nothing. That's the whole point of records — you can outrun a constable, but you can't outrun ink."

Odile almost smiled. Then she reached into her coat and brought out an envelope, soft-edged, yellowed, stamped with a mail-room mark ten years faded, and held it out.

"This came to the district office yesterday. Forwarded from Meridian's mail room — they're clearing everything out ahead of the dissolution. It's marked *Elias Sterling, hold for collection.*" Her voice gentled. "There's a note on the back in the clerk's hand. *Undeliverable — addressee deceased. Return to sender unknown.* It's addressed to you, Bram. It's been sitting in that room since 1908."

Bram took the envelope. Her name was on it in her father's hand — *For my Bram, if I'm late home* — and the paper weighed nothing at all and more than the ledger ever had, and the harbor light lay across it like a blessing she had stopped expecting.

She broke the seal with her thumb, right there on the steps, in front of God and the gulls and the lifting fog.

Inside, folded twice, was a single page — and at the top of it, in her father's careful diver's hand, words that changed everything she had testified to that morning:

*Bram, if you are reading this, then Wren moved faster than I thought he would, and there is something you must find before they do — I hid it where Mother used to say the letter O lived. Trust the tree, little bird. Trust the tree.*
