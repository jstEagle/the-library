## 1. The Blank Page

The mortar knew the weight of my hand better than I did.

I had been grinding lapis for an hour before I understood that the blue spreading through the binder was not the blue Master Fen had specified. His notes called for a sky at the moment just after rain — *the hour when the world is rinsed*, he had written in the margin of the coast plate, in his cramped and slanting hand — and what I was making was too deep, too much like evening, like the color of a room after the candles go out. I scraped it back into the mortar and ground harder. The pestle made its small grinding complaint against stone, and outside the wind worried the shutters of the workshop the way a dog worries a bone it cannot crack.

Vellamere in the last week of autumn was a city of gray water and grayer stone, and from the high south window of Master Aldous Fen's workshop you could see all of it that mattered to a mapmaker: the river bending its patient elbow around the garrison hill, the roofs of the tanners' quarter steaming their slow stink into the cold, the masts of the grain barges standing in the harbor like winter reeds. Beyond the walls, faint as pencil ghosting, the orchards climbed the southern slope — rows of bare trees in their winter orderliness, black branches ranked like regiments.

*Like regiments.* I caught myself at the thought and put it down the way one puts down a tool that has grown hot in the hand. Everything in this city could be made to resemble the Regiment if you let your mind wander toward it. That was, I suspected, rather the point of them.

I did not let my mind wander. Wandering minds ruined coastlines.

The Vellamere Atlas lay open on the great table under its linen dust sheet, forty-two finished plates clipped to their board, each one a country or a coast or a city street drawn true as prayer. Forty-two plates, and room on the binding frame for fifty. Eight remained: three inland surveys, four city plans, and the final page, which had no subject written against it in Master Fen's ledger. The final page stood blank. It had stood blank for two years, and when I asked him about it once — only once, because his look stopped me — he said, *A map isn't finished until something has happened worth mapping,* and turned back to his inks as though he had said nothing at all.

That was in spring. In summer he went out to survey the marsh road and did not come home, and in autumn they told me the river took him, which rivers do, and there was nothing to be done about it, which there wasn't.

So I ground my pigments. I traced my coastlines. I kept the workshop warm enough that the vellum would not buckle, and I kept the schedule he had kept, because the Atlas had a patron waiting — though lately the patron's letters had begun to smell of wax seals heavier than courtesy required — and because work is the only grief that can be measured. You cannot measure a master's death. You can measure the width of the Serevan delta to the span of a hair, and I did, twice daily, and found it unchanged both times, and this was somehow a comfort.

History happens elsewhere. That was the creed I had built for myself out of mortar dust and tracing paper, and I maintained it the way one maintains a roof: daily, without thinking, until the leak comes.

* * *

The workshop woke in stages, as old buildings do. First the light came gray through the horn panes and lay along the floorboards like spilled milk. Then the stove ticked and breathed. Then the smells assembled themselves in their usual order — linseed oil first, sharp as a struck match; then hide glue, sweetish and animal; then the pigment drawer's whole quiet library, ochre and verdigris and lampblack and the lapis that cost more per ounce than a soldier earned in a year, which was one of the small ironies of my trade that I permitted myself.

I worked the morning plates by lamplight even after the daylight came, because lamplight does not flatter. A coastline drawn in flattering light will lie to you; drawn in honest shadow, it confesses. Master Fen taught me that in my first month, standing behind me with his hands clasped at his back so he would not be tempted to correct my grip. *You are not drawing what you wish were there,* he said. *You are drawing what is. If those frighten you apart, take up another trade.*

He had said it kindly. He said most things kindly, except about the Regiment, about which he said nothing at all, and his silence on that subject was the least kind thing in him.

By midmorning I had the Serevan delta confessed and drying, pinned flat under its weights, and I moved to the fourth remaining city plan: Vellamere itself, the atlas coming home at last to its own streets. This was the plate I liked best and dreaded most. Every line of it was memory. Here the bridge where the fishwives shouted their prices before dawn; here the guildhall with its leaded windows; here the lane where I had lodged my first winter above a chandler's shop and gone to sleep smelling of tallow and feeling rich about it. And here — I paused, pen lifted, breath held out of habit rather than need — here the garrison hill, rising over everything, its walls drawn in the strong clean strokes the Regiment's own surveyors had supplied us, because they supplied everyone, because a city that maps itself must first ask permission of the men who hold it.

Their survey was accurate. Their survey was always accurate. It was the only thing about them I had ever been able to admire, and I admired it the way one admires a knife.

I inked the hill. I did not embellish it. I did not shade it darker than truth, though my hand considered it.

At noon the wind changed and came off the river, and the shutters began their low conversation — tap, tap, tap — that I had heard every working day for nine years. Nine years. I had arrived at this workshop at fourteen with a portfolio of drawings so earnest and so bad that Master Fen had laughed, actually laughed, before sitting down beside me and showing me how a line could be humble. He had fed me. He had taught me. He had never once asked about my family, which in Vellamere was either tact or wisdom, and I had loved him for never knowing which.

And now the stool across the table stood empty, and his coat still hung on its peg by the door because I could not make myself move it, and I measured everything else in the room — the temperature, the humidity, the angle of the light on the drying racks — and did not measure that.

There was a ledger trick he used to play on new apprentices. He would set a page before them covered in figures and ask what was missing, and they would hunt the columns for an error and find none, and he would say: *What's missing is the thing you didn't count.* I thought of it now, unbidden, standing over the Vellamere plate with the garrison hill drying black under my pen. I counted my pigments. I counted my days to deadline. I counted the plates remaining, eight, and the hours in each, and the cost of the gold leaf for the cartouche, and I did not count the empty stool, and I did not count why the Regiment's letters had begun arriving sealed in two places instead of one, and I did not count the way the neighbors had stopped greeting me in the lane since midsummer, nodding instead, quickly, the way people nod at houses where someone is ill.

What's missing is the thing you didn't count. I ground more lapis instead.

* * *

Afternoon came down the river in bands of copper and slate. I trimmed the dried delta plate free of its waste margins, and the knife whispered, and outside a patrol went past on the quay below — six men, boots striking cobbles in the rhythm I could have set to music, red sashes dark in the failing light. I did not look up from my work until they had passed. This too was diligence. This too was a kind of measuring: I measured the distance between my window and their eyes, and the thickness of the glass, and the seconds of their footsteps, and I called the counting prudence and got back to my coastlines.

Because here was the thing I told myself, the creed under the creed, the one I recited while the stove ticked and the glue pot breathed and the atlas waited under its sheet like a sleeping animal: history happens elsewhere. Happens in the capital, happens on frontiers, happens to other people, to families with names the Regiment reads aloud, to masters who walk marsh roads and don't come home — no. Not that. To *other* other people. To people whose doors get knocked on. My door was a door where ink dried and schedules held and a dead man's coat hung quietly on its peg, and no army in the empire had ever troubled itself over a door like mine.

I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold. Not as knowledge. As habit.

The light failed early, the way it does in the last week of autumn, and I lit the lamps one by one, moving through the workshop's familiar dusk — past the drying racks with their sheets of tested paper hanging like laundry, past the locked cabinet where the expensive blues slept in their drawers, past the door at the back of the storeroom that led down to the cellar where Master Fen had kept his stock of vellum, which I had entered perhaps five times in nine years because the stairs smelled of earth and old rain and something older than either.

On the great table, under the sheet, the forty-two plates held their breath. Somewhere among them, hidden the way a coin hides in flour, was the portrait folio — a loose gathering of engraved faces Master Fen had never explained and I had never asked about, defaced nearly all, scratched through with hard deliberate lines, faces ruined beyond reading. I had found it years ago while reorganizing his drawers and had put it back exactly as I found it, because there are things in a dead man's workshop that are not yours, and some part of me had understood even then that the folio was not decoration. It was evidence. Of what, I had not wanted to know.

I did not think of the folio now. I want to be honest about that: I did not think of it. There is a discipline to not-thinking, and I had practiced it for a season, and that afternoon I was good at it. I thought about gold leaf. I thought about the deadline, eleven weeks hence. I thought about whether to order more gum arabic from the apothecary on Copper Lane, whose prices were criminal but whose stock was pure, and I decided yes, tomorrow, and wrote it on the slate, and the chalk squeaked, and the sound of it was the sound of an ordinary life continuing.

The final blank page waited at the end of the binding frame. Fifty leaves, forty-two filled, seven subjects assigned, and the fiftieth — the last — carrying nothing in the ledger but a dash, a single horizontal stroke of Master Fen's pen, which might have meant *nothing* or might have meant *not yet*, and which I had looked at so many times over two years that I no longer saw it, the way you stop seeing a stain you have decided to live with.

I covered the table. I banked the stove. I checked the shutters against the wind, which was rising now, coming down off the hills with a raw edge to it, the first real cold of the season, and I remember thinking — standing there with my hand on the cold latch, the lamps guttering behind me — that tomorrow I would start the Vellamere city plate in earnest, that I would give this atlas its own streets at last, that the winter would be long and the work would be long and I would survive both by the simple arithmetic of diligence.

Below, on the quay, the watch called the hour. Six bells. The patrol had passed an hour ago. The city settled into its evening of smoke and lamplight, and the river ran dark past the walls, carrying whatever it carried.

I was blowing out the last lamp when I heard it.

At first I took it for the wind — the same shutter-tap, the same dog-worrying-the-bone sound the building made on every cold night of its long life. But the wind taps. This knocked. Three times, spaced, deliberate, soft as a child's knuckles would be soft, at the workshop door, at the bottom of the steps, in the last blue minute between day and night.

No one came to this door after dark. No one came to this door at all, except the Regiment's couriers, and they did not knock softly, and they did not come alone.

The knock came again. Tap. Tap. Tap. Patient. Small.

I stood in the dark of my master's workshop with my hand still on the latch of the window, and every ordinary arithmetic I owned — the deadlines, the pigments, the creed that history happens elsewhere — fell suddenly silent, the way a clock falls silent in the second before it strikes.

The knocking did not stop.
