## 2. A Child at the Door

Dawn came to Vellamere the way it always did in the cold months — not arriving so much as leaking, a grey seepage through the workshop's east window that turned the dust motes above my desk into a slow, drifting snowfall of their own. I had been awake since before it. Sleep had become a country I visited briefly and without enthusiasm, and I had learned to do my best work in the hours when the city below was still deciding whether to exist.

The workshop of Master Aldous Fen smelled, as it had for twenty years, of linseed oil and lampblack and the particular mineral sweetness of ground lapis — though the lapis was nearly gone now, hoarded in a stoppered jar like a relic, spent only on the atlas's most important waters. I sat at the long drafting table with my sleeves pinned back and my breath fogging faintly in the unheated room, tracing the coastline of the Serpent Isles onto a fresh sheet of hot-pressed paper. The nib whispered. Outside, a cart rattled over cobbles somewhere near the fish market, and a gull complained about it.

This was the whole of my world, and I had arranged it that way deliberately. History happened elsewhere — in the garrison squares where the Regiment drilled its grey columns, in the magistrate's court with its new banners, in the mouths of men who spoke of the Reconciliation as though it had been a wedding rather than a wound. Here there was only ink. Here, if a thing was true, you could hold it flat under your hand and measure it, and measured things could not hurt you.

I had been telling myself this for eleven months, ever since they carried Master Fen home with his skull broken on the ice of the Tanner's Canal and called it misfortune. Eleven months of measuring everything except the shape of the hole he'd left. I knew to the hair's breadth the width of his chair's seat, worn pale at both arms. I knew how many steps it was from his stool to the pigment cabinet — nine, though he had made it seven by taking them long. I knew the exact weight of the unfinished atlas: four stone, six pounds, three ounces, because I had weighed it once at three in the morning when I could not sleep, as though its mass might explain why I could not set it down.

Forty-one plates finished. Nine remaining. One page at the end left blank, which Master Fen had never explained, and which I had come to regard the way one regards an empty chair.

I was working the third pen-stroke along the Isles' eastern shore when I heard the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. A sound like a knock that had been attempted by someone who did not believe knocking would work — a small, arrhythmic tapping, low down on the door, as though it came from a person of reduced stature or diminished hope. The gulls went quiet. The cart had passed. There was only that tapping, patient and small, and then a pause, and then it began again.

I set down my pen. Ink dries quickly in cold rooms; I capped the well out of habit, because Master Fen had beaten that habit into me across six years — *the work survives us, Rosalind, only if we tend it* — and crossed the floorboards, which announced me in their usual creaking dialect.

Through the peephole I saw nothing but cloak. That was my first impression: a fold of grey-green wool, military issue by the cut of the shoulder seam, far too large for whatever stood inside it. It hung to the ground. It pooled. And then it shifted, and a face tilted up into the thin light beneath the transom — a boy's face, six years old perhaps, seven at most, smudged with soot and cold, with a crusted cut above his left eyebrow and eyes the colour of wet slate that looked at the door as though it were a wall between him and the last warm place in the world.

I did not open it. Every instinct I owned — and I owned them all, carefully maintained, like a collection of knives — told me not to open it. Children did not arrive at mapmakers' workshops at dawn wrapped in soldiers' cloaks. Children arrived at foundling houses, at parish doors, at the Regiment's own intake offices in the Cittadel quarter, where they were weighed, named, numbered, and absorbed. This child had been delivered to the wrong door, or to exactly the right one, and either possibility was worse than the other.

"Go around to the parish," I said through the wood. My voice sounded older than I felt. "St. Verity's. Two streets down. They'll feed you."

The tapping stopped. Then, in a voice scraped thin by crying and cold: "She said wait."

"Who said?"

"She said *wait for the light*. She said the man who draws maps would open." A pause. "Are you the man who draws maps?"

I am not, I almost said. I am the apprentice who finishes maps. But the correction died somewhere in my chest, because through the gap beneath the door I could see his feet — bare, blue-white with cold, standing in a puddle of last night's frost-melt — and because the word *she* hung in the air with a weight I did not yet understand and already dreaded.

I opened the door.

The cold came in first, as cold does, shouldering past me into the warm room like a creditor. The boy swayed. The cloak — a sergeant's greatcoat by the braid, I noted with some distant professional part of my mind, Regiment grey-green, Third Cohort — slipped off one shoulder, and beneath it he wore nothing but a nightshirt and a string of dried mud. He held a folded paper in one fist so tightly that his knuckles had gone the colour of candle wax.

"You're freezing," I said, which was the stupidest possible observation, and stepped aside. "Come in. Quickly."

He came in the way small animals enter unfamiliar territory: low, fast, and ready to bolt. He made it two steps past the threshold and then stopped dead, struck — I realized a moment later — by the walls. Every wall of the workshop was hung with proofs and plates: coastlines, mountain hachures, the great quartered plan of Vellamere itself with its orchard district rendered in fine green stipple. He turned in a slow circle, mouth open, frost still melting on his lashes.

"It's all *drawn*," he said. "Everything's drawn."

"Most things can be," I said, and shut the door on the morning.

* * *

I put him in Master Fen's chair by the stove because it was the warmest seat in the room and because I could not bear to sit in it myself, and fed him the end of yesterday's loaf softened in warmed milk, which he ate with the terrible efficiency of a child who has learned that food is conditional. He held the bowl in both hands. He did not spill a drop. When he finished he looked up at me with an expression of such grave politeness that something behind my ribs shifted uncomfortably.

"What's your name?" I asked.

He considered this at length, the way he seemed to consider everything, as though words might have teeth. "Pip," he said finally. Then, with the air of a confession: "That's what she called me. I don't remember the other one."

"The other one?"

"My real one. She said I wasn't to say it. She said—" His brow furrowed, pulling at the crusted cut above his eye, and he winced. "She said forget the face. That's what the paper says. You read it. You're the man who reads."

He thrust the paper at me. It had been crushed and half-un-crushed many times; the creases were soft as cloth. I unfolded it under the lamp.

The handwriting was hurried, slanting, written by someone whose hand had been shaking or whose time had been short, or both. The paper was good stock — garrison stationery, watermarked with the Regiment's tower-and-wheat seal, which raised questions I set aside like unstable pigments. Four words, no signature:

*Forget the face. Please.*

Below that, in smaller script, cramped against the bottom edge as though the writer had been afraid to give even this much: *The mapmaker. Trust the mapmaker.*

I read it twice. Three times. The fire ticked in the stove. The boy watched me with those slate-grey eyes, and I felt the sentence settle over the room like weather: *forget the face.* Forget whose face? Forget it from what? Who writes a note commanding oblivion and then entrusts it to a six-year-old courier?

"Who gave you this?" I asked gently. "The woman. The *she*. What did she look like?"

He thought hard. "Tired," he said at last. "Like she'd been walking a long way in her head." He turned his bowl in his hands. "She cried but she didn't make the noise."

Children say these things with devastating accuracy. I set the note down on the table and found my hands were not entirely steady, and I resented them for it.

"Well, Pip," I said. "You can't go back out there. Not dressed like that, not in this cold. You'll stay until we sort out where you belong."

"I belong here," he said simply. "She said so. She said the map is safe and I'm to stay where the map is."

The milk curdled in my stomach. "What map?"

He shrugged, a small, complete shrug that said *adults ask such obvious questions.* "The big one," he said. "The one that isn't done."

* * *

I washed his brow while the stove worked on the rest of him. The cut was shallow — a fall, most likely, or a blow from a low beam — but it had scabbed badly and the skin around it was bruised in a yellow-green crescent, several days old, healing. Someone had tended it before. Someone had cleaned it and bound it and then, days later, wrapped this child in a soldier's coat and sent him into the dark alone.

He bore the washing stoically, gripping the arm of Master Fen's chair, watching me in the lamplight with that unnerving gravity. And it was there — bent over him, dabbing tincture of arnica along the cut with a cloth — that the first wrongness stirred, a fish turning in deep water.

There was a cast to his features I could not place and could not dismiss. The set of the brow. The particular architecture of the cheekbone, high and flat-planed. The slate of the eyes, yes, but more than the colour — the *shape* of them, slightly downturned at the outer corner, giving even his sternest expression a quality of old sorrow. I had seen that face before. Not the boy himself. The geometry of him. Somewhere in this room, in the last year, I had studied those exact planes and angles with a draftsman's attention, and the knowledge sat just below the surface of my memory like a sunken plate refusing to be netted.

"Hold still," I murmured, mostly to myself.

"You're the man who draws," he said. It was not quite a question. "Can you draw me?"

"If you sit still, I might."

"Draw the map instead. She said the map matters." He recited it like a lesson learned under threat: "*The map matters more than me. But keep me anyway.*"

My hands went still on his brow.

Outside, the city had woken fully now; I could hear the market bell tolling the hour, the ordinary commerce of an ordinary morning, carts and cries and the distant drum of the Regiment's changing of the watch. Inside, a child sat in a dead man's chair wearing a dead cohort's coat, quoting instructions from a woman who cried without noise, and every measured, flattened, holdable truth I had built my eleven quiet months upon began quietly to slide.

I finished binding the cut with a strip of clean linen. I gave him more bread. I told him — and heard how false it sounded even as I said it — that everything would be sorted out, that the parish would be sent for, that he needn't worry his head. He nodded with the polite scepticism of children everywhere, who know perfectly well when adults are lying and file it away for later use.

And while he ate, I crossed the workshop to the folio cabinet, ostensibly to fetch the day's tracing paper, and stood before the drawer where Master Fen kept the plates too dangerous or too delicate to leave lying out. My hand rested on the brass pull. Somewhere in that drawer — I was almost certain now, almost sick with certainty — was a portrait study I had catalogued months ago and barely glanced at: a defaced engraving, faces scored through with a blade, salvaged from some pre-Reconciliation source, filed under *suppressed*, because filing it anywhere else would have meant asking questions Master Fen had taught me not to ask.

*Forget the face,* the note said.

Behind me, the boy hummed tunelessly to himself, swinging his feet in the too-large chair, at home already in a way that frightened me more than any soldier's cloak.

I drew open the drawer.
