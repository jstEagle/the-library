## 3. The Face That Matches

The drawer did not open.

I pulled again, and the brass handle gave me the same cold refusal it had given me for three weeks — since the night they carried Master Aldous Fen home feet-first through the snow and told me he had slipped on the river stairs. The lock was a small thing, older than I was, its keyhole shaped like a keyhole in a child's drawing: a circle and a line, a door drawn by someone who had never had to open one.

Behind me, the boy stopped humming.

"You're pulling too hard," he said. "You'll break it."

"It's meant to be broken open," I said, "by someone who knows how."

"Then you don't know how."

I turned to look at him. He sat where I had put him an hour before — or rather, where he had installed himself, because no one *put* Pip anywhere, I was already learning that — swaddled in the soldier's cloak that smelled of woodsmoke and horse, his bare ankles crossed over the chair rung, watching me with the frank appraisal of a magpie deciding whether I was worth robbing.

"No," I admitted. "I don't know how."

"That's all right." He shrugged, a gesture far too old for his shoulders. "Nobody knows most things."

*Most things.* I thought of Master Fen saying something like that once, standing at the great south window with his hands clasped behind his back while the light went amber on his knuckles. *Rosalind, we are not in the business of knowing. We are in the business of measuring what other people only think they know.*

I had work to do. That was the whole architecture of my grief, if I was honest — and I was trying very hard not to be honest, because honesty at that hour would have laid me out on the workshop floor like a struck deer. The Vellamere Atlas sat open on its trestle table, forty-one plates finished, the forty-second half-inked: the eastern coast, all its little teeth and harbors. A coastline does not grieve. A coastline simply is, and your hand moves along it, and for as long as your hand is moving you do not have to think about anything except the width of your nib and the patience of the paper.

So I inked.

The boy watched me work for a long time. I could feel his attention the way you feel weather coming — a pressure behind the ears. When I finally glanced up, he had gone still in a way children are never still, and he was looking at the atlas.

"What is it?" he asked.

"A map."

"I know a map." Something almost offended in his voice. "What's it *of*?"

"The world."

He considered this. "All of it?"

"All the part worth drawing."

That was a lie, actually. There were pages worth drawing that I would never be permitted to draw, but I didn't know that yet, not properly — I knew it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak, seeing it without understanding. What I knew then was simpler and more terrible: that a six-year-old had been left on my step with a note telling me to forget his face, which meant someone believed there was something in his face worth forgetting, which meant that somewhere out in the grey morning streets of Vellamere there were people who remembered it well enough to fear it.

But first, the blood.

* * *

It was when the light came up fully — that thin, watery light of late autumn, the kind that makes everything look like an unfinished sketch of itself — that I saw the cut above his eyebrow.

It wasn't deep. It was the sort of wound a child collects falling out of trees, off walls, down stairs; the sort that scabs gold at the edges and heals into a story. But it had crusted badly overnight, and the cloak he'd been wrapped in was a soldier's, and soldiers' cloaks carry lice and worse, and Master Fen had drummed one rule into me above all others in nine years of apprenticeship:

*Clean the wound before you wonder about the war.*

So I fetched the basin, the boiled water, the clean linen, the jar of honey-and-salt salve that smelled of summer kitchens, and I sat him on the high stool by the window, and I told him to hold still.

"Hold still," he repeated, in a fair imitation of my own clipped voice, and then, mercifully, he did.

His face tilted up toward mine. And here is the thing I will tell plainly, because everything after it depends upon it being plain: for perhaps ten seconds, nothing happened. I wetted the linen. I pressed it gently to his brow. He winced. I murmured the nonsense adults murmur — *nearly done, brave lad* — words that came out of me sounding exactly like Master Fen, which unsettled me even then, though not nearly enough.

Then the blood lifted away from the skin beneath his eyebrow, and the morning light fell across his face, and I stopped moving.

I have tried since to describe what recognition feels like when it arrives from somewhere below thought. It is not remembering. It is the body remembering for you — a coldness in the wrists, a sudden loudness in your own pulse, the sensation of a floor tilting two degrees. His brow was fine-boned, the brow of some old coin. His eyes were set slightly wide, a pale grey-green like river ice, and under each one ran a faint crescent of darker skin, so subtle you would call it shadow unless you had studied it. His mouth was narrow and decided. His hair, where the hood had pushed it back, was that particular dark gold that is neither brown nor blond but something between, the color of ale held to a candle.

I had seen that face before. Not living. Inked.

"Rosalind," the boy said, using my name with terrible familiarity, "you've gone funny."

"I haven't." My voice came out from a long way off. "Hold still."

I set down the linen. I walked — I remember the strange formality of walking, step after deliberate step, as though my legs had been borrowed from a more sensible person — across the workshop to the folio cabinet against the north wall. Forty-two plates, plus the supplementary leaves Master Fen kept separate, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the third drawer. The plates of persons. Portraits, genealogical charts, the faces of history — the section every atlas carries and no atlas reader looks at twice.

Except that ours was missing most of them.

I unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that did not entirely seem to belong to me. Inside lay eleven portrait plates. Eleven, out of what should have been sixty. And every missing face had been accounted for, officially, in the tidy red-stamped language of the Office of Imperial Correction: *removed for review.* Removed, in the Regiment's vocabulary, meaning scraped. Meaning the copper plate heated and the image burnished away until the metal forgot it had ever held a man's cheekbones. I had helped scrape two of them myself, the winter before last, standing beside Master Fen while he worked the burnisher with steady strokes and said nothing at all, which from Master Fen was a shout.

The plate I took out now was one he had hidden instead of scraping. I knew it was forbidden. I had known for two years and had said nothing, because I understood, in the wordless way apprentices understand their masters, that some disobediences are load-bearing. If I mentioned the plate, it would have to be destroyed, and then something true would be gone from the world, and Master Fen would have to go on standing in this room knowing it.

It was labeled, in Fen's small upright hand, on a slip pasted at the margin: *House of Aurelian — last attested likeness.* And beneath, smaller, in pencil, almost apologetic: *from life, 4th of Thaw, the year before.*

A woman holding a child.

She was rendered in the fine stippled style Fen used for faces he respected — thousands of points of ink building shadow out of silence, so the portrait seemed less drawn than accumulated, arrived at the way truth arrives, slowly and all at once. She sat with her hands folded. The wide-set eyes. The crescents beneath them. The narrow, decided mouth. Around her throat, half-suggested by a few strokes, a chain bearing a sunburst sigil — the rising sun of the Aurelian house, the emblem that thirty years ago had flown over half the province before the Regiment's founding fathers taught everyone, efficiently and permanently, to forget it.

And on her lap, gripping her thumb in one small fist, a boy.

Dark-gold hair. Fine-boned brow. Crescent shadows under eyes too serious for a child's face, eyes already looking directly out of the copper at whoever dared to look in.

The boy on my stool was perhaps five years younger than the child in the engraving. It did not matter. Faces like that do not change; they wait. It was the same face. It was unmistakably, catastrophically the same face, and I stood there in the cold light of the workshop holding a dead man's forbidden copper in both hands while a live child swung his feet behind me and hummed his tuneless song, and I understood — in the way you understand a fire alarm, bodily, before any part of you has agreed to understand it — that the note had not been a riddle.

*Forget the face.* Because the face could not be forgotten. Because the Regiment had spent thirty years and rivers of ink making sure no one would ever see it again, and here it was, sitting in my workshop, wearing a stolen soldier's cloak, asking me what the map was of.

* * *

You must let me go back. You must let me tell you about those last weeks, because I did not know they were the last weeks, and that is the whole cruelty of it — that the ordinary days never announce themselves, that they walk out of your life dressed as every other day, and you only learn their names afterward, when you are left holding the inventory.

Three weeks before the river stairs. Late October, the first hard frost silvering the gutters of Vellamere, and Master Fen behaving strangely even by his standards.

He had always been a precise man — precision was his piety, his prayer, his entire moral system made visible in hairlines and margins. But that autumn his precision turned feverish. He worked past midnight. I would come down in the mornings and find the candles burned to puddles, the atlas open not to the coastlines we were contracted to finish but to the portrait folio, to the genealogical charts, to pages I had never seen him consult. Once I found him copying something onto a loose leaf — quickly, with the guilty speed of a man writing a letter he means to burn — and when he heard my tread on the stair he covered the leaf with his forearm the way a schoolboy hides a love note.

"Good morning, Rosalind," he said, not turning around. "Is the eastern coast dry?"

"It's drying."

"Then leave it. Come here."

He showed me the forty-second plate that day. Not the coast. He flipped past it, past the islands, past the blank final page at the back of the atlas — the page every atlas has, reserved for corrections, for additions, for whatever the future insists upon — and he rested his palm flat on that blankness and looked at me for a long time.

"Why do you suppose they leave it empty?" he asked.

"So it can be corrected."

"Mm." He smiled, and it was not his usual smile, which was dry and sideways. This one was sad, and private, and it frightened me. "Or so it can be *finished*. Those are not always the same operation, apprentice. Remember that. Write it in the margin of your mind."

Another week. He began going out in the evenings, which he never did, returning smelling of cold air and lamp-smoke and once, unmistakably, of the tallow stink of the guild-hall district, where our guild had not met in a month. He locked his desk drawer — the drawer whose key had vanished with him into the river — and I caught him, one afternoon, sliding something flat and wrapped into it, and he looked up and met my eyes and did not pretend otherwise.

"Some drawers," he said mildly, "are load-bearing."

I almost laughed. Then I saw his face, and did not.

The last evening — I know now it was the last, though I learned it the way you learn the shape of a room when the lights go out, by collision — he called me to the window. Snow was starting. The lamps of the Lower Terraces were coming on one by one below us, and beyond them the dark mass of the old orchard on the city's western edge, the ancient trees the Regiment kept fenced and unexplained, the orchard every child in Vellamere was warned away from and every child in Vellamere crept toward.

"Do you know why they never cut it down?" Fen asked.

"They say the roots undermine the foundations."

"They say a great many things." He was quiet for a moment. His breath fogged the glass. "Rosalind. If anything happens to me — and nothing will happen to me, I am a dull old man who draws maps, nothing ever happens to dull old men — but if it did. Finish the atlas. Finish *all* of it. Do you hear me? Not the parts they'll permit. The parts that are true. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole profession."

"Master—"

"And keep the folio drawer oiled," he added, in a completely different voice, brisk and practical, as though he were reminding me about pigment orders. "Locks rust. Rust is honest, at least. It tells you exactly how long a thing has been waiting."

Two days later, they pulled him from the shallows by the river stairs. Slipped, they said. Ice, they said. A dull old man, nothing ever happens to dull old men.

I measured everything else that winter. The depth of his absence in the workshop — four paces from trestle to cabinet, where he used to stand. The rate at which his teacup gathered dust. The exact number of days I managed not to cry, which was nine, and then not anymore.

Everything except my loss. Everything except the one country I had no training to survey.

* * *

"Rosalind."

The boy's voice pulled me back across twenty years and thirty years and one terrible present. I became aware that I was still holding the copper plate, and that my hands had begun to shake, and that the child was watching me with those river-ice eyes — those *inherited* eyes — with an expression no six-year-old should have needed to learn: the careful watchfulness of someone accustomed to being looked at by frightened adults.

"You know them," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Know who?"

"The people on the picture." He slid off the stool and came toward me, and I wanted to tell him to stop, to stay back, to be anyone else, anyone at all — and I also, in the same breath, in the same traitorous heart, wanted him closer, wanted to stand between him and the entire organized forgetting of the Empire of Vellamere. He stopped in front of me and looked up at the plate in my hands. "That's my mama," he said simply. "Before."

"*Before,*" I said. My voice cracked on the word.

"Before the bad men came." He said it the way children say the unbearable — lightly, factually, the way they report that it rained, or that dinner was lentils again. "There was a garden. We had a garden with apple trees, and then the bad men came, and a lady ran with me for a long time in the dark, and then—" He stopped. Some interior door closed; I watched it close. He looked up at me and delivered the sentence that rearranged my entire life with the mild irritation of a customer complaining about the weather:

"You're supposed to know what to do. The note says so."

"The note says to forget the face."

"Well, you won't, though." He shrugged. "Nobody forgets Mama."

I looked down at the copper in my hands. At the woman with her folded hands and her sunburst chain, at the child on her lap staring out of thirty years ago with these exact eyes. At the eleven surviving portraits and the forty-nine scraped ones. At the atlas on the trestle table with its blank final page, waiting, patient as a grave, for whatever the future insisted upon.

Somewhere out in the city a bell rang the hour — seven bells, the garrison changing watch, boots on stone in the distance, always boots on stone. And beneath the bell, beneath the boots, beneath the ordinary morning noise of Vellamere waking up, I felt the ground tilt another degree, the way Fen's floor had seemed to tilt in my memory, and I understood that the past had not merely survived. It had walked to my door in a stolen cloak and sat down in my chair and asked me to fix its eyebrow.

The boy tugged my sleeve.

"There's blood on your picture," he observed.

There was. One small smear, from the linen I'd forgotten I was still holding, across the corner of the copper — across the lower margin, where Master Fen's penciled hand had written something I had never read closely, because I had never let myself look at this plate longer than shame allowed. I wiped the blood away with my thumb.

The pencil note was a single line, in his upright, patient, load-bearing hand:

*If this face comes home, open the drawer.*

I looked at the locked drawer across the workshop. The circle-and-line keyhole. The key that had gone into the river with him, or so everyone assumed — everyone except, perhaps, a dull old man who drew maps, who oiled locks and hid coppers and left messages in margins for whoever inherited.

The boy followed my gaze.

"Is that where you keep the knowing?" he asked.

I did not answer. But I was already crossing the room, and my hand was already closing around the cold brass handle, and this time — this time I was not pulling to open it. I was pulling to break it.
