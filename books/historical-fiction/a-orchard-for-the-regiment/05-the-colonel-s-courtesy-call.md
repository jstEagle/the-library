## 5. The Colonel's Courtesy Call

Colonel Hesper Vance had a theory about workshops.

They were the most honest rooms in any city. A barracks told you what a soldier was ordered to be; a chapel told you what he hoped to be; but a workshop told you what he actually did, hour by hour, with his hands and his patience and his small deceptions. She had learned this in the purge years, when her work had been less about burning things than about reading them — reading ledgers, reading letters, reading the quiet architecture of other people's lives until she could walk into any room in Vellamere and know within a minute what it was hiding.

She stood now on Compass Rose Lane, beneath the leaded windows of the late Master Aldous Fen, letting the rain finish dying out of the sky, and read.

The workshop sat where it had always sat: third building past the cooper's yard, its upper floor jutting a handspan over the lane like a man leaning in to hear a secret. The glass was old, wavy, green at the edges — too expensive to replace and too proud to want to be. Drying racks crossed the light of the upper windows in black lattice. A sign, hand-painted, sun-faded to the color of weak tea: *FEN & APPRENTICE, CARTOGRAPHERS AND ENGRAVERS OF PLATES.*

*And apprentice.* Singular. That was worth knowing. One apprentice meant one pair of hands, one set of eyes, one person who had been alone in this house for however long it took grief to stop being loud and start being furniture.

Vance adjusted her coat collar against the damp and climbed the three steps to the door. The wood was scarred by decades of deliveries; the iron knocker had been polished bright by use in one spot only, low, where a shorter hand would reach. Fen himself had been tall. Which meant either the apprentice was short, or the apprentice had been answering this door long enough to wear the metal to their own height.

She knocked. Politely. Three measured raps, the knock of someone who expected the door to open because doors opened for her.

It took longer than it should have. Long enough that Vance counted it — not rudeness, she decided, but the pause of someone crossing a room while deciding what face to put on. Then footsteps, quick and uneven, a scrape of something heavy being moved — a chest? a stool? — and the door swung inward on warmth and the smell of linseed oil and gum arabic and, beneath all that, the mineral tang of ground pigment, like weather from another country.

The apprentice was younger than she'd expected. That was her first honest note. Younger, and thinner, with ink worked so deep into the knuckles it might have been tattooed, and eyes that did exactly what well-trained eyes should do: nothing at all.

"Colonel Hesper Vance," she said, before the question could form. "Of the Ninth Bureau. I've come about Master Fen." She let the name sit between them like a coin placed on a counter. "I knew him."

*That,* she noted with professional pleasure, *was even true.*

---

The workshop received her the way a mouth receives a thermometer: closed around the intrusion, waiting to see what would be asked of it.

Vance walked slowly, hands clasped behind her back, and let the room talk. It talked willingly. Rooms always did, if you didn't interrupt them.

A long table under the north window, scattered with burnishers and gravers laid out in neat rows — a careful hand, an orderly one. A press of the older kind, its screw freshly greased. Pigment jars ranked along a shelf like a choir: vermilion, Prussian blue, bone black, a jar of ochre with a paper label in two different hands, the second handwriting smaller, more tentative, still learning its letters. Above the cold hearth, a compass hung on a nail — brass, worn silver at the pivot, the kind of instrument a man used for forty years until his thumb wore a shine into it.

There were two chairs at the table. Two cups on the shelf above the stove, both dusty now, but dusted recently enough that the rings showed where they'd stood. Someone had kept setting out the second cup after there was no one to drink from it. Then stopped. The dust told her when: perhaps six weeks. Perhaps seven.

"You're keeping the schedule," Vance said pleasantly, nodding toward the drying racks where sheets of the atlas hung in pale ranks, coastlines and river deltas ghosting through them. "Impressive. Most apprentices let everything slide when—" She caught herself with a small, precise motion, like a fencer withdrawing a touch. "Forgive me. When they're grieving."

"I'm keeping Master Fen's schedule." The voice was steady. Slightly lower when guarded — she filed that away. "He'd have wanted the work finished."

"He was a thorough man." Vance turned a sheet on the rack with one finger, tilting it to the window light. A plate proof of the northern coast, beautifully done, every sound and shoal rendered with a jeweler's fussiness. "Thorough men are rare. The Regiment values thoroughness." She let the word *Regiment* land gently, the way you'd lay a hand on a horse's neck to feel whether it flinched. "Which is why I'm here, actually. Formally, the Ninth Bureau sends its condolences to the guild. Informally—" she smiled, and made sure the smile reached her eyes, because smiles that didn't reach the eyes made people calculate, and calculating people lied worse, "—I promised Aldous I'd look in on his work. He was worried about it. Near the end."

Something moved behind those well-trained eyes. Not fear. Something more interesting. "Worried?"

"He felt time pressing. Don't we all." Vance drifted along the table, trailing one gloved finger an inch above the surface without touching anything — a trick she'd perfected years ago; hovering hands unsettled people far more than touching ones, because touching ones could be watched and hovering ones had to be imagined. "Tell me about the atlas. The Vellamere Atlas — how many plates remain?"

"Fourteen are printed. Six remain." A pause, measured to the width of honesty. "Five, rather. And the final page."

"The final page." Vance turned. "What goes on the final page?"

"I don't know. Master Fen left it blank."

"Blank." She tasted the word. "A blank page in the most ambitious atlas this city has produced in a century. How very like him. He always did believe in leaving room." She laughed softly, as if at a private memory, and watched the apprentice decide whether to ask what the memory was. They didn't ask. Discipline again. "May I see the folio? The master plates. Purely sentimental, I assure you — I'd like to see his hand once more. He had the finest line weight in the guild. Like wire. Like a fence made of silk."

The hesitation lasted two seconds. She counted both of them.

"It's locked away," the apprentice said. "I'm sorry. After— after what happened, I've been keeping things secured."

"Of course you have. Wise. This city eats the careless." Vance waved a hand, gracious, defeated, a guest declining a second helping. "Another day, then. When the grief allows."

But she marked it. Marked the way the apprentice's shoulders had come up half an inch at *folio*, the way the feet had shifted to stand — not quite between her and the cabinet at the room's east wall, but near enough that the geometry was legible to anyone who read rooms for a living. The east cabinet had a new lock. Brass, bright, unscratched. Installed within the last month.

People installed locks for two reasons, Vance had found: to keep thieves out, or to make themselves feel they'd done something. In a workshop full of valuable tools that remained conspicuously unlocked, the new lock on the flat map cabinet was not protection. It was a confession with a keyhole.

---

They drank tea, because refusing tea would have been a statement, and the apprentice understood statements.

Vance held her cup in both hands like a woman warming herself, though the workshop was warm enough, and spoke about Aldous Fen in a low, unhurried voice — his stubbornness about ink formulas, his appalling handwriting in correspondence contrasted with his immaculate hand on copper, the argument they'd once had, years ago, about the true course of the River Sennet, which he'd insisted she look up herself rather than take his word for. All of it true. She hoarded true details the way other officers hoarded promotions; they were currency that never devalued, and she spent them now, deliberately, watching each one strike the apprentice somewhere soft and unguarded.

"That argument about the Sennet," the apprentice said finally, and the voice had changed — warmed, cracked slightly at the seam. "He told me that story. He said a colonel of the Ninth Bureau had called him a pedant."

"He was a pedant. It's why he was great." Vance looked down into her tea. "I liked him enormously. Which is why I'll say plainly what I came to say, and then I'll trouble you no further."

She set down the cup. The porcelain clicked against the saucer with the small clean sound of a decision being made.

"The Regiment has an interest in the atlas. Not a hostile interest — an archival one. The completed work will pass through imperial review before publication, as all major cartographic works do. Standard procedure. You'll be treated fairly; the guild charter guarantees compensation for any revisions requested." She said *revisions* the way a surgeon says *incision*: honestly, and letting the listener supply the blood themselves. "I tell you this now, friend to friend, so it doesn't arrive later as a writ and feel like an ambush. Aldous hated ambushes. He said a map should never surprise the traveler."

For a moment the workshop was silent except for the tick of the cooling stove and, faintly, the drip of last rain off the eaves outside.

"And if the revisions are refused?" the apprentice asked.

Vance smiled again — warmly, sadly, the smile of a reasonable person contemplating an unreasonable world.

"Then the review board refuses to license it, and the atlas cannot legally print, and twenty-six years of the finest work in Vellamere sits in a drawer until the drawer rots." She rose, smoothing her coat. "No one burns anything, apprentice. This is a civilized city. We don't burn books here." A beat, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "We simply decline to publish them, and then, in thirty years, no one remembers there was ever anything to burn. History is tidier that way. Aldous understood that better than most. It's why his maps survived and his opinions didn't."

She was at the door when she paused, one hand on the latch, and delivered the sentence she had actually come to deliver — the rest had been packaging.

"One more thing. A small thing. A boy was seen on this street two mornings ago. Wrapped against the cold, alone, young — six, perhaps seven. The watch makes a note of such children; it's policy, abandoned minors are remanded to the Regimental Foundling House within the week." She turned her head just enough to catch the apprentice's face in profile, and what she saw there was worth the entire visit: not guilt, exactly, but the specific stillness of a person listening to their own heartbeat. "If you see him, the correct thing — the kind thing — is to send him along. The Foundling House is warm. Fed. No child should be sleeping rough in this weather." She opened the door onto the wet, washed street. "You'd tell me, wouldn't you? If you saw him?"

The pause was perfect. Almost perfect. Half a heartbeat too long, and half a heartbeat is a country wide if you've spent your life measuring them.

"If I see him," Rosalind said quietly, "I'll do what's right."

"Yes," Colonel Vance agreed pleasantly. "Everyone always does. That's the tragedy of this city — everyone does what's right according to their own map of it." She stepped out into the thinning afternoon. "My condolences again. Finish the atlas. Make it beautiful. Beauty survives; it's practically the only thing that does."

---

In the carriage, rolling back toward the Ninth Bureau through streets gone gold and grey with evening, Hesper Vance took out her notebook — a plain black one, unmarked by bureau insignia, because its contents were hers alone — and wrote four lines in her small, upright, unsentimental hand:

*Fen's apprentice: composed, grieving, lying. New lock on flat-map cabinet. Second cup retired ~6 weeks. Boy seen on street — apprentice's stillness at the mention confirms shelter. Atlas intact; final page blank per subject.*

She considered the page for a moment. Then, below it, she wrote a fifth line, and the fifth line was the one she would think about later, much later, in a different life:

*Subject did not ask how I knew of the boy.*

She closed the notebook. Outside the window, Vellamere slid past in the last of the light — chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless air, gulls arguing over the fish market, the ordinary gorgeous machinery of a city that believed itself permanent. Somewhere in it, in a drafty room smelling of linseed and gum arabic, a frightened apprentice was probably, at this exact moment, moving something from one hiding place to another.

Let them. Movement was information. Everything a person did with their fear told you where the treasure lay.

Still — and this was the thought she declined to examine, folding it away the way she folded away all inconvenient evidence, neatly, labeled, to be dealt with never — still, she found herself remembering the second cup. The dusted shelf, the ring marks, six weeks of pouring tea for a dead man before the hands finally stopped.

Her own hands, resting on the notebook, were perfectly still.

At the bureau she would file a routine observation report. It would recommend continued monitoring, no action. She would write it herself, carefully, leaving out the lock and the cup and the half-heartbeat, sealing them instead in the black notebook where inconvenient truths went to wait. There was no urgency. The atlas wasn't going anywhere; apprentices always waited too long to run; they always believed finishing the work mattered more than saving themselves.

She was almost certainly right.

She gave it a week.
