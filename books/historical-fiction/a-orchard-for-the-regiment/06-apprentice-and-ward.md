## 6. Apprentice and Ward

The boy would not say where he had come from, and after three days of asking, Rosalind stopped asking.

He had taken to the workshop the way water takes to a crack in plaster — quietly, inevitably, finding every low corner and settling there. He slept on the cot behind the pigment shelves that had been meant for storage, wrapped in the same soldier's cloak he'd arrived in, which smelled of woodsmoke and horse and something else Rosalind could not name and did not want to. He ate what they ate: bread and hard cheese in the morning, soup at night, the soup stretched thinner now because mouths were not budgeted for in Master Fen's ledgers.

*Pip*, he had insisted, when Rosalind asked what to call him. Not a name anyone had given him — he made that clear by the set of his small chin — but a name he had given himself, plucked from nowhere or from somewhere he wasn't telling. *Pip. Like the seed inside an apple.* He had said it with such ferocity that Rosalind had laughed for the first time since the funeral, a startled bark of sound that frightened them both.

So it was Pip. Like the seed inside an apple. Rosalind thought of that often in the days that followed, more than was comfortable. A seed did not ask permission before it rooted.

* * *

The trouble with hiding a child in a mapmaker's workshop was that a mapmaker's workshop had no children in its rhythms. Everything ran to schedule: the drying times of washes, the turning of vellum under weights, the delivery of paper from the mill every fourth day, the guild inspector who came on the first of each month to verify production against the master's registered commissions. The Vellamere Atlas was not merely a work of art. It was a contract, witnessed and sealed, and contracts did not pause for grief or for foundlings.

So Rosalind rose before dawn as always, lit the lamps as always, ground lapis into powder as always — and taught a seven-year-old boy to be furniture.

"Rules," Rosalind said on the fourth morning, crouching to his level beside the cold stove. Pip watched them with the grave attention of a much older creature wearing a child's face. "One: if anyone knocks who isn't the paper man or the milk cart, you go behind the pigment shelves and you don't breathe loud enough to stir dust. Two: you don't touch anything on the long table. Three—" Rosalind hesitated, because the third rule had been forming all night in the shape of a question they didn't want to ask. "Three: if someone comes and I tell you to run, you run out the back and down Tanner's Row and you don't look back. Do you understand?"

"Do you have a back door?" Pip asked.

Rosalind opened their mouth. Closed it. The workshop had one door. One door, and windows facing the street, and a chimney, and that was the whole of it. Master Fen had never needed another way out; masters did not flee their own workshops.

"We'll make one," Rosalind said, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated it.

Pip nodded anyway, with the terrible faith of children, and went to sit on his crate by the window where he liked to watch the carts go by, and Rosalind turned back to the coastlines of the southern provinces and traced them with a hand that was not quite steady.

* * *

The days took on a shape neither of them had planned but both of them began to keep.

Mornings were production. Rosalind worked the great table while Pip sat on his crate and narrated the street below in a whisper — *bread cart, dog, lady with red umbrella, soldier* — and Rosalind's pen paused, just slightly, at every *soldier*. There were more of them lately. Or perhaps there had always been that many and Rosalind had simply never counted before. Grief and fear sharpened the eye in different directions, and between them they left very little unexamined.

Afternoons were harder. A child could not sit still for six hours being furniture; even well-trained furniture fidgeted. Pip's restlessness expressed itself first in questions — *why is the blue more expensive than the green, why do you hold your breath when you draw, is the sea really that color or are you making it up* — and then, when answers only fed the engine, in movement. He began, cautiously, to orbit the edges of the workshop. He learned which floorboards creaked and avoided them with a natural cunning that both impressed and unsettled Rosalind. He memorized the order of things: where the brushes lived, where the blotters dried, which shelf held the finished plates wrapped in linen. Once Rosalind caught him standing before the locked drawer in Fen's old desk — the drawer Rosalind had not yet found the courage or the key to open — simply looking at it, head tilted, as though listening to something inside.

"Don't," Rosalind said, sharper than intended.

Pip flinched. Rosalind knelt, apologized, and found they could not explain why the drawer mattered without explaining everything, and explaining everything was impossible while the everything was still a wound rather than a fact.

It was the compass roses that saved them both.

* * *

Every plate in the atlas bore one — the eight-pointed star, the fleur-de-lis for north, the fine radiating lines that told a reader which way the world went. Rosalind drew dozens a week, and had drawn thousands in nine years of apprenticeship, and had stopped seeing them sometime around year two. But Pip saw them. Pip stood at the edge of the long table — obeying rule two, technically, by touching nothing — and stared at the half-finished compass on the plate of the northern lakes with his whole body, the way other children stared at sweets.

"It's a flower," he said.

"It's a compass rose."

"It's a flower," he insisted. "A flower that knows which way is north." He looked up. "Can flowers do that?"

Rosalind considered the question with more seriousness than it strictly deserved. Sunflowers turned toward the sun, which was a kind of knowing. Moss grew on the shaded side of trees. Rivers remembered mountains. Perhaps everything living knew which way was north, and only people had to draw it.

"No one draws them better than you," Pip said, in the flat certainty of the very young. "Teach me."

And so, on a grey Thursday with rain writing its own script down the windows, Rosalind cleared a corner of the workbench, gave Pip a worn sheet of cartridge paper, a stub of graphite, and the first lesson of a craft neither of them had agreed to begin.

"Start with a circle," Rosalind said. "But not with your wrist. With your whole arm. The circle comes from the shoulder."

Pip's first circle looked like a sheep. His second looked like a sheep that had been startled. His fifth was round enough that Rosalind, who had spent a childhood drawing circles on any surface that would hold still, felt something twist pleasantly under the ribs.

"Now the points. Eight of them. North, south, east, west — those are the cardinal points, the big ones, the ones that matter most. Then the half-winds between. Each line must be true to its neighbor or the whole star lies."

"What happens if the star lies?"

"Ships wreck," Rosalind said. "Armies march the wrong way. People get lost in places that should have been safe." They heard their own voice go strange on the last words and covered it by guiding Pip's hand through the first diagonal. His fingers were ink-stained already, permanently, the way Rosalind's were, the way Fen's had been to the day he died. There was no washing that out. It went all the way down.

Pip worked until the lamp burned low, tongue between his teeth, producing a compass rose that was lopsided and earnest and entirely alive. When he finished he held it up and said, "This one's mine," and Rosalind said, "That one's yours," and meant it in some deeper registry than either of them understood.

They pinned it to the wall above the stove, among the practice sheets of nine years — Rosalind's own early roses, crooked and proud, that Fen had refused to throw away. *Everyone's first star is crooked,* he used to say. *That's how you know a person drew it.* Now there were two hands' worth of crooked stars on the wall, and the workshop felt, for one dangerous hour each evening, like a place where things were kept rather than hidden.

* * *

But warmth, Rosalind learned, has a texture like rope. Every kindness coiled another loop. Every routine built another strand. Teaching the boy to draw was teaching him to stay. Feeding him was feeding a secret. The found-family feeling that bloomed in the lamplight tightened by daylight into something with the diameter of a noose, and Rosalind felt it cinch a little further each morning when the shutters opened onto the street.

Because the street was changing.

It began small. A pair of Regiment troopers walking the Row at hours when the Row had never been walked — past the shuttered dye-works, past the print shop, pausing outside the workshop just long enough for Rosalind, watching through the gap in the shutters, to count the buttons on their coats. Then the milk cart came a day late, and the driver, old Bessam who had gossiped cheerfully over the gate for years, handed over the churns without meeting Rosalind's eyes. Then a notice appeared on the guild board across the square — too far away to read, close enough to matter — and three different neighbors read it, and folded it away, and said nothing to anyone.

On the ninth day, a gentleman in civilian clothes stood across the street for the better part of an hour, examining the workshop's frontage with the unhurried attention of a man measuring a house for curtains. He wrote nothing. He spoke to no one. When he finally moved along, he glanced up at the windows once, and Rosalind — flattened against the wall beside the shutter-gap, heart going like a hammer in wet wool — would have sworn on Fen's grave that the man had looked directly at the spot where Pip's face had been a moment earlier.

Pip had been watching the street from his crate. Of course he had. Children watch streets.

Ros moved him away from the window that evening and invented a game called Cartographer's Siege, in which the entire workshop was enemy territory and the pigment shelves were a fortress wall, and Pip threw himself into the game with delight, unaware — Rosalind prayed he was unaware — that the game was real except for the part where anyone came to storm the walls.

At night Rosalind lay on the pallet beside the great table and listened to the boy breathing behind the shelves, slow and even, seed-deep in sleep, and did arithmetic they did not want to do. Food remaining. Money remaining. Days remaining, though remaining until *what* was a number the equation refused to produce. Somewhere out there was whoever had wrapped a child in a soldier's cloak and written *forget the face* in a hand that shook. Someone had known enough to bring him here — to this workshop, to this door — and knowing that much, they might also have been followed.

And underneath the fear, quieter but persistent as damp, sat the locked drawer, and the portrait plate folded inside the atlas folio, and the face on it that wore itself anew on the sleeping boy every single day. Rosalind had not touched the folio since the night of recognition. Some doors, once you see what's behind them, stay open whether you look or not.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Pip drew a compass rose without help. All eight points true, the fleur-de-lis confident, the circle round as a coin. He carried it to Rosalind at the table and presented it with both hands, formally, the way an apprentice presents a first plate to a master, and Rosalind understood suddenly that this was exactly what was happening — that some inheritance had begun passing between them without either signature required — and had to turn to the window and pretend to check the weather for a moment before trusting their voice.

"It's very good," Rosalind managed. "Master Fen would—"

The knock came then.

Not the paper man's knock, which was three brisk raps and a shout of greeting. Not the milk cart's absence-of-knock. This was two measured strikes, evenly spaced, the knock of a person whose time was worth more than the door it was spent on.

Pip was moving before Rosalind turned — good lad, rule one, already sliding behind the pigment shelves with the cloak gathered around him, vanishing into the shadow between the vermilion and the ochre as if he had practiced it in dreams. Perhaps he had. Rosalind crossed the workshop, put one hand flat on the door's cool plank, and looked through the iron peephole Fen had installed after the riots of '41.

A uniform. Regiment grey, braid at the collar. And above it, unhurried, patient, a face Rosalind knew from the guild functions Fen had dragged them to: Colonel Hesper Vance, Director of Cultural Reclamation, holding her hat in one gloved hand and wearing an expression of perfect, courteous sorrow.

"Master Fen's apprentice, I believe," her voice came through the oak, warm as a banked fire. "I've come to offer my condolences. And to see how the atlas fares in such difficult times."

Behind Rosalind, in the dark between the pigments, a child did not breathe.
