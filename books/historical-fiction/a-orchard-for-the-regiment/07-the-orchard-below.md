## 7. The Orchard Below

Pip woke up on the third morning in the workshop and knew the smell of it before he opened his eyes. Ink. That was the big one — a dark, wet smell that lived everywhere, in the floorboards and the blankets and even, when he licked his lips first thing, faintly on his own skin. Under the ink there was paper smell, dry and dusty like old leaves. Under that there was linseed oil, and under *that*, if the wind came through the shutters right, there was apples.

The apple smell was his favorite. It didn't belong here. Everything else in the workshop had been made by somebody, ground down or pressed flat or boiled in a pot, but the apple smell just drifted up from somewhere below, like the building itself was sitting on top of an orchard nobody could see.

Rosalind was already at the big table when he padded out in his too-big borrowed shirt. They always were. Rosalind worked the way Pip imagined rivers worked — always going, never hurrying, wearing everything smooth.

"Morning, Pip."

"Morning." He climbed onto the stool that was his now. His stool. He'd never had one before. "What's that?"

"Prussian blue." Rosalind tipped the little glass jar so he could see the powder inside, deep and bright, like sky you could hold. "Ground it myself yesterday. Want to see what it does?"

He did. He watched it swirl into water and turn the whole cup into a piece of evening, and he thought, not for the first time, that this place was full of magic that grown-ups pretended was just work.

* * *

After breakfast — bread and honey, real honey, from a crock Rosalind kept high on a shelf — Rosalind went out to deliver proofs to the guild hall, which meant Pip was alone with the workshop for the whole grey-gold length of the morning.

There were rules. Rule one: stay inside. Rule two: don't touch the drying racks without asking. Rule three: if anyone knocks, be quiet as a mouse behind the pigment chest until the knocking stops. Pip was good at rules. Rules were easy compared to the other thing, the thing that lived in his chest sometimes, the remembering-thing that showed him a woman's face and a door slamming and then stopped, like a book with pages torn out. When the remembering-thing started, rules were the only things that held him together.

So he stayed inside. But inside, it turned out, was bigger than he'd thought.

He explored the way he explored everything: low first. Grown-ups looked high, at shelves and windows and faces. Nobody ever looked at knee-height, which was where all the good stuff lived. He found a drawer that stuck and opened anyway (inside: brass pens nib-down in sand, like little sleeping birds). He found a loose floorboard near the stove (inside: nothing, but warm). He found the cat-door in the back wall, long nailed shut, and put his eye to the gap where light came through and watched dust float in the beam like tiny slow snow.

Then he found the hatch.

It was under the drying racks, in the corner where the racks stood thickest, their frames making a kind of forest of legs and crossbars hung with fresh-printed sheets. The sheets swayed when the draft came in, breathing, whispering. Pip crawled into the forest on his hands and knees because that was how you got anywhere interesting.

The hatch was a square of darker wood in the floor, maybe as wide as his arms stretched out, with an iron ring set flat into it like a belly button. There were hinges on one side, gone soft with rust. Around the edges, the wood was worn pale and smooth, smoother than any of the floor around it.

Pip knew what worn-smooth meant. Master Fen — whose name he said to himself sometimes, quietly, like tasting something — Master Fen had had hands, and hands that used a thing over and over left their shine on it. This square of floor had been lifted so many times the wood had given up arguing.

He pulled the ring. It didn't move. He pulled harder, leaning back, heels braced, and the hatch gave a groan like an old man sitting up and swung open onto blackness and cold air and the apple smell, strong now, so strong he could almost taste the skins.

Stairs went down. Stone ones, narrow, curving away past where the light wanted to follow.

Pip sat with his legs dangling in the dark and felt the cold air move against his face like something breathing back at him. Down there, the workshop sounds changed. The tick of the stove got small. The city above — carts, bells, a dog with opinions — got far away and woolly, like sounds heard through a blanket.

And underneath everything, very deep, very steady, was water.

* * *

He didn't go down. Not yet. Rule one said stay inside, and Pip had decided that the stairs were still inside, technically, the same way a mouth is inside a face. But he closed the hatch most of the way — leaving a finger-width gap, because shutting it all the way felt like being unkind to it — and he made himself a promise with the serious kind of promise, the kind you say twice.

That afternoon Rosalind came back smelling of rain and printer's ink and worry, though Pip couldn't have named the worry yet; he just saw it in how they checked the shutters twice before starting supper. Pip kept his secret. Secrets were new to him. He rolled this one around all through supper like a boiled sweet, saving it.

"You're quiet," Rosalind said.

"I'm thinking about blue," Pip said, which was true enough. Everything was blue in his head lately. The stairs would be blue-dark, he decided. Blue like the powder swirling in water.

Rosalind smiled, and some of the worry went out of their shoulders, and Pip learned something important without knowing he'd learned it: that you could give people rest by keeping things back. It was the first tool anyone had ever given him. He held onto it.

* * *

Two days later, a Tuesday, grey and drizzling, Rosalind went out again — to the tannery row for hide glue, they said, back within the hour — and Pip went straight to his forest under the drying racks.

The hatch opened easier this time, like it remembered him. He had brought the stub of candle-end he'd taken from the windowsill and the striker box Rosalind used for the stove, and he'd practiced the striking motion eleven times against his thumb until he got a flame without burning himself. The flame wobbled in the updraft from below and steadied, and the stairs came into view, worn concave in the middle by feet long dead, and Pip went down.

One step. Two. Five. Ten. He counted because counting kept the remembering-thing away. The stone was icy through his socks. At fourteen steps the ceiling dipped and he had to duck, and the air changed — warmer somehow, and greener in his nose, moss and stone and apples and underneath it all the water sound, closer now, a soft black chuckling.

At the bottom was a passage.

His candle showed him walls of fitted stone older than anything he'd ever seen, older than the garrison walls, older than the cathedral towers, stones furred green at the joints with moss that glowed faintly where his flame passed. Roots came through the ceiling in ropes and curtains, thick as his wrist in places, hanging down like the hair of someone asleep overhead. Apple roots. He was sure of it. He touched one and it was cool and alive and slightly damp, and Pip, who had no words for it yet, understood that he was standing inside the tree.

The passage ran two ways. To the left it curved off toward a darkness that smelled of river-mud. To the right it stretched straight, and along its floor lay a line of old barrel staves and broken crates and, every dozen paces or so, a mark chiseled into the wall: a little arrow, and beside it letters worn shallow. P. V. O. He sounded them out. *Pee. Vee. Oh.* They meant nothing to him and everything, the way marks do when you're six and the whole world is still deciding what it will mean.

He walked to the right, careful, candle held out like a little brave sun.

The tunnel breathed. That was the thing he couldn't explain to anybody later, if there ever was a later and an anybody. Tunnels shouldn't breathe, but this one did — slow drafts pushing past him from ahead, warm then cold then warm, as if the passages connected to a hundred chimneys and cellars and mouths all over the old buried city. Once, far off, something metal clinked, and he froze with his heart hammering, and nothing came, and after a while his heart sat back down.

He found a room. Or a widening — the tunnel opened into a roundish chamber where three passages met, and in the middle stood the ghost of an orchard: a ring of great roots coming down through cracks in the vaulted ceiling, and around each root a circle of brickwork like a planter's bed, long empty of soil except a black crumbly loam that smelled impossibly sweet. Somebody had grown things down here once. Somebody had carried dirt down fourteen steps and more, bucket by bucket, because they wanted trees underground, in the dark, where no soldier could see them.

On the wall, someone had carved a picture. Pip raised his candle.

An apple tree. Crude, childlike, cut deep with a knife or nail — trunk, branches, round fruits, and beneath it a stick-figure family holding hands, four figures, five, six. And below that, scratched smaller, almost shy:

*THEY CANNOT BURN WHAT GROWS DOWN.*

Pip read it twice. Reading was new to him too, mostly, and this sentence went into him like a swallowed coal, warm and frightening. He didn't fully understand it. But he understood *burn*. Burn was a word his remembering-thing flinched away from, a word with smoke behind it.

He stood in the buried orchard with his candle and felt, for the first time since the woman's face and the slammed door, like the world had a hidden room in it just his size.

Then he heard the voices.

* * *

They came from the left-hand dark, the river direction — boots first, a scuff and knock of many boots on stone, and then voices, echoing wrong, flattened and multiplied by the tunnel until he couldn't count the men in them. Four? Six? The candle flame bent toward the sound as if leaning to listen.

"—sweep again come spring. Colonel's orders. Every arch, every cellar."

"Every cellar. We did Cellar Row in autumn. Found nothing but rats and a man selling cheese."

"Cheese-man's gone now, isn't he."

A laugh that wasn't much of a laugh. Boots coming closer. Pip pinched his candle out — quick, without thinking, the way you snatch your hand from a kettle — and the dark took him whole and complete, a dark so total it pressed on his eyes like thumbs.

He stood absolutely still among the roots. He had played this game before, the stillness game, back wherever *back* was, in whatever torn-out pages of his life had taught it to him. You became a sack. A post. A shadow with nothing inside it. You breathed small and high in your chest. Your heart was loud as a drum and you hated it and begged it silently to hush.

The boots grew louder. Candlelight — *their* candlelight — began to glow faintly up the left-hand passage, a wobbling orange smear on the far wall, growing.

"—say the mapmaker's got something worth having. That atlas. Captain wants it catalogued before the guild gets sticky about charter rights."

"Fen's atlas? Fen's dead. Dead men's papers are Regiment papers. That's the whole point of dead men."

Another not-laugh. Closer. The orange glow brightened, and Pip could hear now the small sounds inside the boot-sounds: the creak of leather, the clink of a scabbard against stone, a man clearing his throat, another spitting. Real men. Men with throats and spit and orders, walking through the secret under the world, talking about his — about Rosalind's — about the atlas like it was a sack of grain to be counted.

"The kid too?"

"What kid?"

"The one the widow woman says was seen. Small lad, dark-haired. On the mapmaker's step at dawn last week."

Pip stopped breathing entirely.

A pause. Just boots, then, and the drip of water somewhere, and Pip's own blood roaring in his ears like the sea.

"There's no kid," said the first voice finally, flat and tired. "There's no widow woman either. There's a list of rumors as long as my arm and half of them started with people who owe the Regiment money. Sweep your section and mind your own boots."

The glow slid past the mouth of the chamber, throwing the roots' shadows huge and slow across the curved wall — a forest of giants walking — and moved away down the passage toward the right, toward the stairs, toward *his* stairs, the ones that came up into the workshop floor.

Toward home.

Pip stood in the black with his unlit candle gripped so hard the wax bit into his palm, and he thought about what the men had said, turning the pieces over the way you turn over strange stones. *Dead men's papers.* *Fen's dead.* *The one seen on the mapmaker's step.*

Grown-ups lied. He had known this the way all children know it, generally, the way you know weather exists. But this was different. This was lying as a country you could walk into. Rosalind lied — kindly, with worried eyes, checking the shutters twice. The soldiers lied — bored and flat, saying *there's no kid* while standing ten feet from the kid. Even the dead master had lied, maybe, hiding tunnels under his floor and sentences carved in the dark. Everybody Pip had met was standing in front of a door, telling him there was nothing behind it.

But Pip had been behind doors. He knew what was behind doors.

He waited until the boots faded to nothing and the dark stayed dark, and then some more, because the stillness game had rules too and rule one was *wait longer than you want to.* Then he fumbled the striker box out, and on the fourth try got a spark, and the little flame stood up in his hands, shaking as much as he was.

He looked at the carved family under their carved tree, six stick figures holding hands, and he thought about Rosalind upstairs, who would be back soon, who checked the shutters twice, who had taken in a stray boy against every smart bone in their body.

The soldiers were coming back in spring. They were coming to sweep every arch, every cellar. And they were asking questions about a boy on a doorstep.

Pip blew out his candle and climbed the fourteen steps fast and quiet, and pushed the hatch closed over his head just as he heard, far off through the floorboards, the street door of the workshop open below the jingle of keys — Rosalind's step, hurrying, heavier than usual, as if they were carrying something that hadn't been carrying them.

Pip crouched in his forest of drying-rack legs with wax in his fist and apple smell rising through the seams of the world, and made a decision that sat in him like a swallowed stone:

Tonight he would tell Rosalind about the stairs.

Whether they wanted him to or not.
