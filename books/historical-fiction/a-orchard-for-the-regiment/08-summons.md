## 8. Summons

The writ arrived on a Tuesday, which Rosalind would remember afterward as the only strange thing about it. Tuesdays were delivery days — paper stock from the miller on Cordwainer Lane, vellum scraps from the tannery, the occasional crate of gall-nuts that smelled of bitter apples and old grief. A Tuesday was a day for ordinary things. The Regiment's seal, pressed in black wax into the shape of a tower over crossed pikes, had no business arriving among the parcels like any other item of commerce.

Rosalind found it wedged between a bundle of calfskin and a jar of gum arabic, and for a long moment simply stood there in the doorway with the morning cold at their back, unwilling to touch it.

The workshop was quiet behind them. Pip was still asleep in the loft above the drying racks, curled in the soldier's cloak that Rosalind had not yet been able to bring themselves to burn. The cloak was a problem. Everything about the boy was a problem, and yet when Rosalind had climbed down from the loft an hour before dawn to begin grinding pigment — because grief or no grief, the Vellamere Atlas kept its schedule, and Master Fen had taught them that a mapmaker who missed a deadline might as well be drawing fictions — they had paused beneath the loft ladder and listened to the small, even sound of his breathing, and had thought: *I would do it all again.* That was the trouble with problems one would solve twice.

Now they picked up the writ. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, or perhaps that was imagination. The wax seal cracked under their thumbnail with a sound like a small bone breaking.

*To the estate and effects of Aldous Fen, Mapmaker, deceased; and to the apprentice thereof, name entered in the Guild rolls as R. Zhou.*

They read it standing in the doorway, letting the cold numb their fingers, because some part of them understood even then that once they finished reading, the world would have changed shape, and they wanted one more moment of the old world — the world where the atlas was merely late, merely difficult, merely a mountain of copperplate and ink that a dead man had left on their shoulders.

*By order of the Office of Imperial Completion, the work known as the Vellamere Atlas, being unfinished and therefore the property of the Crown by statute of the Ninth Year, is to be surrendered whole and unaltered to the appointed officers of said Office within fourteen days of the date below. Failure to comply will be construed as theft of imperial property and prosecuted accordingly.*

There was more. There was always more, with Regiment documents — the flourishes, the citations of statutes nobody had read since the purge, the signature of some undersecretary whose name meant nothing. Rosalind read it all anyway, twice, the way they read everything now, hunting for the trap stitched into the seams. And there it was, in the fourth paragraph, phrased so gently it might almost have been kindness:

*Said surrender shall include all plates, proofs, drafts, marginalia, and associated papers, that the work may be completed according to imperial standards.*

*Marginalia.* Rosalind's mouth went dry. *Associated papers.* Not the atlas alone. Everything. Every note in Fen's cramped hand, every letter, every proof sheet with its corrections — including, though the writ could not know it, the defaced portrait plate hidden in the folio, and the correspondence in the locked drawer, and the boy asleep upstairs whose face belonged on none of those pages and all of them at once.

They looked up. Cordwainer Lane ran gray and ordinary in the early light, a cart rattling past, a woman shaking ash from her doorstep, two children chasing a dog between the wheelwright's fence posts. Nobody was watching the workshop. Nobody appeared to be watching the workshop. But Rosalind had learned, in the weeks since the boy arrived, that *appeared* was the operative word in Vellamere now. The city had grown eyes. Or perhaps it had always had them, and Rosalind had simply never been worth looking at before.

They closed the door. They bolted it, then un-bolted it, then bolted it again, hating themselves for both decisions.

* * *

Pip woke to the smell of porridge and came down the ladder with his hair sticking up on one side, dragging the cloak, and stood in the middle of the workshop floor surveying the day's territory with the grave air of a general.

"Is it a working day?" he asked. He had learned quickly that some days were working days — days when Rosalind sat bent over the drafting table until the light failed and answered questions only in monosyllables — and some days were other kinds, rarer, when there was bread with honey in it and lessons in compass roses.

"It's a working day," Rosalind said. Then, because the lie sat poorly: "A short one."

Pip nodded solemnly and went to his corner, the corner Rosalind had cleared for him beside the pigment chest, where he kept his treasures: three chalk stubs, a goose feather he insisted was a quill, and a scrap of waste vellum on which he had drawn, over and over, improving daily, the same thing — a circle with eight points radiating out, and a small crooked N at the top. His first compass rose. His first word of letters. Rosalind had watched him labor over the N for the better part of an evening, tongue between his teeth, and had felt something crack open in their chest that they had no time to examine.

They set the writ on the drafting table, face down, and worked.

Work, for Rosalind, had always been a form of thinking. Master Fen used to say that the hand solved problems the mind was too proud to approach — that you could worry a question for a week in your head, but give your hands a burin and a plate of copper and in an hour they'd tell you what you already knew. So they took up the plate for the eastern coastlands, the one with the marshes and the drowned villages, and began cutting the shoreline in fine parallel strokes, and let the question move through them.

*Surrender means destruction.* This was not speculation anymore. The drawer full of Fen's correspondence had settled that. The Regiment did not complete atlases; it amputated them. Somewhere in the Office of Imperial Completion there was a room where maps went to be corrected — villages struck from coastlines, roads rerouted through country that had been emptied of the people who'd named those roads, whole districts redrawn as orchards and grazing land, *an orchard here, pleasant grazing here*, as if nothing had ever stood in those places but trees and sheep. Fen's letters described the process with a mapmaker's terrible precision. *They returned the northern folio to me with forty-one emendations. I counted. Forty-one places that no longer happened.*

If Rosalind surrendered the atlas, the last true map of the thirty years since the purge would go into that room and come out a lie, and Fen's twenty years of work — the surveys taken in bad weather, the interviews with ferrymen and shepherds who remembered what the official charts forgot, the plates cut and recut until the truth of the coastline survived even in miniature — all of it would be inked over, plated out, erased. And the portrait plate, the face that matched the face sleeping ten feet away, would go into the fire with the rest.

And if they refused?

Rosalind set down the burin and looked at their hands. Steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was moving like water finding a new channel.

Refusal meant arrest. The writ was quite clear, and the Regiment was not an institution given to bluffing. Fourteen days, and then soldiers at the door — not the velvet visit of Colonel Vance, with her condolences and her questions, but the other kind, the kind that came with a wagon for the evidence and a wagon for the mapmaker. Rosalind had seen the wagons once, years ago, as a child, from a window. They had not known then what the wagons carried. They suspected the wagons did not sort their cargo.

So: surrender and lose everything slowly, by inches, in a room they'd never see. Refuse and lose everything quickly, loudly, in front of the neighbors. Those were the doors the writ offered, and both of them opened onto the same dark.

Unless.

Rosalind turned the plate toward the window light. The marsh coastline gleamed, wet-looking, alive. Fen had cut the original; Rosalind had deepened the channels after the spring floods, and somewhere in that collaboration — master dead, apprentice continuing — lay the answer the writ couldn't anticipate. *Surrendered whole and unaltered.* The Regiment wanted a thing they could seize. But a thing that did not exist could not be seized. A thing dispersed could not be seized. A thing hidden —

Their eyes went, without permission, to the floor. To the drying racks, and beneath them, to the old flagstones that Fen had never let anyone pry up, not even the guild inspectors, not even that time the chimney flue backed smoke into the workshop and everyone swore the blockage was under the floor. Fen had knelt on those stones the night he died — no. Not died. The night they *found* him, cold at the drafting table with his spectacles folded neatly beside the unfinished final page. Rosalind had spent weeks telling themselves it was his heart, his age, the damp. They had stopped telling themselves that somewhere around the third letter in the locked drawer.

What was under the floor? They didn't know. But Fen had been a man who surveyed everything, who mapped cellars and culverts for the sheer joy of it, whose marginal notes on the oldest city plans spoke of "the orchard ways" with a familiarity that had seemed, at the time, like sentiment. Old Vellamere had been built above an older Vellamere, and the older one had been built around its orchards — the great terraced gardens of the pre-Regiment city, long paved over, their root-cellars and irrigation tunnels becoming rumor, becoming legend, becoming the kind of story children told and adults denied.

Fen had believed in the tunnels. Rosalind was suddenly, coldly certain of it. And Fen had left a blank page at the end of the atlas — a full blank leaf, wasteful, extravagant, in a man who economized vellum like a miser — annotated in the margin in his smallest hand: *for whoever inherits.*

Inherited. The word sat differently now than it had a month ago. Then it had meant the work. Now it might mean the work *and* the danger *and* the way out, all bound together like the leaves of the atlas itself.

"Rosalind?" Pip had come to stand at the table's edge, chalk in hand, watching their face with the unnerving directness of children. "You look like the map's wrong."

Rosalind blinked. "The map's wrong?"

"When you're copying and it comes out wrong, you make that face." He demonstrated — a small furrow, lips pressed — and it was so precisely Rosalind's own expression that they laughed despite everything, a short startled sound that loosened something behind their ribs.

"The map isn't wrong," Rosalind said. "But someone has just told us we have to give it away."

Pip considered this with the seriousness he gave all matters of maps. "Do you have to?"

"That," Rosalind said, "is the entire question." They pulled him up onto the stool beside them, because their arms wanted holding something, and pointed to the plate. "See this line? The marsh channel. Tell me what's wrong with it."

He studied it, brow furrowed, and Rosalind watched him instead of the copper, and thought: *fourteen days.* Fourteen days to finish the remaining plates, to copy the finished ones, to find out what lay beneath the flagstones, to decide what a person owed to a dead man's life work against what a person owed to a living child — and whether those were, in the end, different debts at all.

"There's a village missing," Pip said at last, tapping the empty space where the marsh widened. "You told me. Drowned villages are still villages. You said the water doesn't erase them, it just — covers them up."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "Yes," they managed. "That's exactly right. The water doesn't erase anything. It just covers it up."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the writ lay face-down on the corner of the table like a stone they'd decided to build around rather than move. Twice Rosalind caught themselves calculating aloud in numbers — plates remaining, hours of light, ounces of ink — and twice stopped, because the arithmetic kept resolving into the same sum: *not enough.* Not enough time to finish honestly. Not enough time to hide everything. Not enough hands, not enough dark, not enough luck.

At dusk they sent Pip up to the loft with bread and honey — a non-working-day supper smuggled onto a working day, because the boy had earned it by existing, which was more than the Regiment had ever paid anyone for — and lit the lamp, and finally turned the writ over and read it a third time.

And there, in the fifth paragraph, in the small print beneath the undersecretary's flourish, they found the sentence they had missed twice, or perhaps had not missed at all but had refused, until now, to understand:

*The Office acknowledges the service of the late Aldous Fen to the cartographic arts, and extends to his successor the Crown's expectation of the same loyalty he ultimately displayed.*

*Ultimately displayed.* Rosalind read the phrase four times. It was a courtesy, on its face. It was also a message, and the message was this: the Regiment knew what Fen had done at the end. Knew he had refused. Knew — or suspected — what the refusal had cost him. And they were telling Rosalind, in the polite grammar of empire, that loyalty had a price and that Fen had finally, fatally, paid it.

Or else — and this possibility arrived like cold water down the back of the collar — the phrase was a test. *Ultimately displayed.* If Rosalind signed the receipt, delivered the atlas, smiled at the officers, then the words meant nothing, were nothing, were the harmless rot of bureaucratic language. But if Rosalind hesitated, if the wrong face flickered at the wrong moment before the wrong witness, then the words were a hook already set.

They thought of Colonel Vance sitting in Fen's chair, her gloved hand resting on the arm as if it had always belonged there. *Such a devoted apprentice. He spoke of you, you know.* She had measured them that day, the way one measures a wall before deciding whether to hang something on it or knock it down. And now the writ had come, and it was not a summons to a hearing, not an invitation to negotiate. It was a deadline painted on a door.

Rosalind rose, took the lamp, and crossed to the locked drawer. The key hung where it had hung since the night they'd broken it open — Fen would have hated the broken lock; Fen would have understood. Inside, the letters lay in their careful stack, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the portrait plate: the young man with the high cheekbones and the steady eyes, scratched across the throat with the Regiment's censor-stamp, and above him, in Fen's hand, the single word *Aurelian* — and beneath that, smaller, almost invisible unless you tilted the plate to the light: *the garden line survives.*

The garden line. The orchard line. Rosalind had taken it for poetry, once. Standing in the lamplight with the writ burning a hole in the table behind them, they took it for a surveyor's note.

Fourteen days. They blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the wind work at the shutters and, above, the small rustle of a child turning in his sleep. Then they knelt, laid their palm flat against the cold flagstones beneath the drying racks, and began, finger by finger, to search for the seam.

Under their hand, deep below the floor, something shifted — a hollow sound, faint as a held breath released. The stones were not all stone.

Rosalind stayed kneeling in the dark a long while, heart hammering, and made themselves a promise in the old language of deadlines and drafts: *fourteen days, and not one hour surrendered.*
