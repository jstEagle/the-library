## 10. Vance's Bargain

The rain had been falling on Vellamere for three days, and Colonel Hesper Vance had begun to think of it as a kind of interrogation — patient, indifferent, wearing everything down until it confessed its shape.

She stood at the window of her office in the Regiment's eastern barracks, watching water thread the gutters along Copperlane Street, and turned a sealed letter over in her hands. The wax was the deep garnet of the High Command, stamped with the crossed pikes and laurel that she had spent twenty-two years learning to read the way other people read scripture: every line, every flourish, a sentence in a language of consequence.

She broke it with her thumb and read it twice before allowing herself to sit down.

*Colonel —*

*In recognition of your service during the Reclamation and your continued diligence in matters of archival security, the Command offers consideration of your advancement to Brigadier, pending resolution of the matter of the Fen atlas. The work is to be recovered intact. Intact, Colonel — we will be plain with you, as you have always been plain with us. There are those above who believe the document contains material relevant to ongoing questions of succession. It is not to be burned, copied, or surrendered to any guild authority. It is to come to this office, by your hand alone.*

*You have one fortnight.*

*The Regiment thanks you.*

She set the letter on the blotter and looked at it for a long while, the way she might look at a map of terrain she was about to order men across. *Brigadier.* The word sat on the page like a coin left deliberately in plain sight. At forty-four, with her file full of commendations that were really apologies — *handled the Aurelian question with discretion*, *managed the northern archives transition without incident* — she had long ago understood that the Regiment did not reward excellence. It rewarded usefulness at the moments when usefulness cost something.

This was such a moment.

Outside, a supply column splashed past beneath wet canvas, the mules' ears flat against the rain. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the river ran brown and high toward the delta, carrying whatever the city no longer wanted out to sea. Vance thought, not for the first time, that Vellamere was a city built like a confession it had never finished making: every district named for something it had destroyed. Copperlane, where the coppersmiths had been relocated after their quarter was cleared. Orchardgate, where there had been an orchard once, before the walls took it. She had drawn some of those lines herself, thirty years ago, when she had been a young survey officer with steady hands and a talent for being told things without being told them.

*You have one fortnight.*

She had been to the workshop. Three days ago, under the pretense of condolence — and the pretense had been necessary, because condolence was the only language the guilds would accept from a uniform, and because she had genuinely wanted to look at the apprentice's face when she mentioned the master's name.

Rosalind Zhou. That was the name on the guild rolls. A narrow person, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the particular stillness of someone who has learned to be furniture in their own home. Polite. Careful. Careful in the way that was itself a kind of answer. When Vance had asked about the atlas's progress, the apprentice had spoken of coastlines and pigment ratios and the tedium of hatching, and had said nothing at all about the last page, and the nothing had been so perfectly shaped that it might have been cut with a blade.

And the boy. There had been a boy.

She had noted him only in passing — small, dark-haired, sitting in the corner behind the drying racks with a charcoal stub, drawing what appeared to be a bird — but it was not the boy himself that had lodged in her mind afterward. It was the apprentice's hand. The way Rosalind Zhou had moved, very slightly, between her and the child when she crossed the room. Not obviously. Not rudely. Just a half-step, an adjustment of weight, the way a body arranges itself around a thing it has decided to protect before the mind has admitted the decision.

Vance had made a career of reading bodies. She had never once been thanked for it.

* * *

That evening she dined alone in the officers' mess, which was how she preferred it, at the corner table beneath the portrait of General-Marshal Corvane — the man whose face had been chiseled off three hundred statues in the year of the purge, and whose portrait here survived only because no one could agree on who owned the ladder. She ate the salted river trout without tasting it and drank two glasses of the thin northern wine, and let the memory rise, because it always rose when the word *Aurelian* came near, the way water rises through stone whether or not anyone is watching.

She had been nineteen. A junior survey officer, third-rate academy, no family worth mentioning, which was precisely why they had chosen her — a person with nothing to lose is a person with nothing to refuse. They had put a portfolio in her hands and told her she would be mapping the agricultural reorganization of the southern provinces. It had taken her four days of careful triangulation to understand what she was actually doing: she was drawing the boundaries of erasure. Village by village. Well by well. Every farmstead marked for consolidation, every family plot marked for redistribution, every parish register marked for transfer to the central archive where paper went to die quietly in the dark.

She had been very good at it. That was the part she did not say aloud, ever, to anyone. Not that she had complied — compliance was forgivable; everyone complied; the world was built of compliance — but that she had been *good*. Her purge maps were held up in training as models of clarity. She had once heard a young lieutenant call them beautiful, and she had agreed, and she had slept that night, and the sleeping was the crime she could never make herself confess, because it seemed somehow smaller than murder and yet more damning.

Thirty thousand people, the historians said now, carefully, in the safe phrasing of people testing ice. Thirty thousand relocated, scattered, renamed. The Aurelian line extinguished root and branch. And the maps that made it administratively possible — elegant, precise, *hers* — were still sealed in the archive vaults, because even the Regiment understood that some instruments should not be displayed.

She had told herself the story a thousand times, and the story had worn smooth as a river pebble from handling: *I was young. I did not know. I followed orders. The alternative was ruin, and ruin serves no one.* And the story was true, in the way that all such stories are true, and it had never once, not for a single night in thirty years, kept the cold entirely out.

Because there had been a moment. One moment, early, that she had spent three decades filing under *inexperience*. On her fourth day in the southern provinces, she had surveyed a farmhouse with a peach orchard behind it, and an old woman had come out into the yard and watched her measure the boundary stones, and the old woman had asked, not angrily, merely as though requesting directions: *"Will the trees stay?"*

And Vance — nineteen years old, holding her chain and stylus, knowing exactly what the red hatch-marks on her draft meant for everything inside them — had said, "I don't know, madam."

And the old woman had nodded, satisfied, as though that were the most honest answer anyone had given her in weeks, and gone back inside, and Vance had finished her survey, and the orchard had been pulled up in the spring along with everything else.

*Will the trees stay.* Thirty years, and the question had not aged a single day.

She signaled the steward for a third glass and then didn't drink it. Duty, she reminded herself, was not the absence of doubt. Duty was what remained standing after doubt had done its worst — she had written that herself, years ago, in a memorandum on garrison morale that had been quoted back at her in two separate promotions. She had believed it when she wrote it. She chose, now, in the lamplight, with the rain ticking against the glass, to go on believing it. The alternative was to believe something else, and she was too old, and too far up the slope, to begin believing new things.

The atlas. Recovered intact. Delivered by her hand alone.

It should have been simple. It *was* simple. Fen was dead — officially a fall on the stairs, unofficially anything one liked — and the work had passed to an apprentice with no guild protection worth the name and no family the Regiment need trouble itself about. A fortnight was generous. She would go to the workshop again, politely, patiently, with the velvet gloves on, and if politeness failed she had other instruments, and if the other instruments failed there were men under her command for whom failure was not a word so much as a destination.

Intact. The word bothered her. She turned it over as she walked the corridor to her quarters, boots ringing on stone, lamp swaying. Why would High Command want the atlas intact? For thirty years the policy had been fire. Burn the registers, burn the portraits, burn the genealogies, burn the maps. Erasure was doctrine. Doctrine did not reverse itself.

Unless something had changed. Unless somewhere in the upper rooms of the citadel, someone had become afraid of the past instead of merely contemptuous of it. Fear, Vance knew, was the only force in Vellamere stronger than doctrine. Someone above her had heard a rumor — a surviving face, a living claimant, a page that had not burned — and now the machine that had spent three decades destroying evidence needed that evidence whole, and quickly, and quietly.

Which meant the atlas was not a book anymore. It was a weapon with an owner not yet determined.

She closed her door against the rain and sat at her desk without lighting the second lamp, and let the room go grey around her.

* * *

She dreamed of the orchard. She almost never dreamed of the orchard, or if she did, she woke too fast to keep it.

In the dream she was nineteen again and standing in the yard with her chain and stylus, and the old woman came out and asked her question, and this time — this time the dream gave her the other answer, the impossible one, the one she had never given anyone — this time she said, "Yes. The trees will stay. I'll see to it myself."

And the dream let her believe it for exactly as long as dreams do, and then the regimental horn sounded through the barrack walls and the orchard dissolved into rain and grey morning, and Hesper Vance lay in her narrow bed listening to the garrison wake around her, and performed the operation she had performed ten thousand times: she took the residue of the dream, folded it small, and filed it in the drawer that had no lock because it had never needed one. No one had ever gone looking. No one suspected there was anything in it.

But the drawer was getting heavy. That was the truth she permitted herself, once a year or so, in the dark, before duty came back through the door like a creditor. The drawer was getting heavy, and one day she would have to either reinforce it or open it, and she did not know which frightened her more.

* * *

She spent the morning on routine business — requisitions, a disciplinary review she resolved with unusual leniency and could not have explained, a report on flood levels at the river gate — and at midday she sent for Captain Dree, her intelligence adjutant, a lean man with the complexion of someone raised indoors among papers.

"The Fen workshop," she said. "What do we have?"

Dree laid a folder on her desk without opening it, which was his way of saying the contents were thin. "Guild records confirm the apprenticeship. Rosalind Zhou, seven years under Fen, sole heir to the commission and the premises. Neighbors describe the household as quiet." He paused, and the pause had texture. "There is one irregularity, Colonel."

"Say it."

"A child. Seen by two separate sources on the workshop step at dawn, nine days ago. Wrapped in a soldier's cloak, by the description. The guild watch logged nothing. The parish registered nothing. The child does not officially exist."

"Children arrive on steps in this city every week."

"In ones, yes. But our sources also report that the apprentice has since been observed purchasing food sufficient for two. Milk. Small clothes, from the ragman on Cooper Street." He hesitated. "And the child was seen at the window yesterday afternoon, Colonel. Drawing."

Vance looked at the rain. "Drawing what?"

"That's the thing, ma'am. The source couldn't tell what the subject was — too far away. But the posture." Dree consulted his notes, though he plainly knew it by heart. "Left-handed grip. Head tilted the same way in every sighting. My source used a word I found interesting."

"Which was?"

"'Practiced.' As in — the child draws like someone who has been taught. Recently, intensively, by someone patient." Dree closed the folder. "A six-year-old beggar doesn't draw with practiced posture, Colonel. A six-year-old beggar draws like a six-year-old beggar."

Vance was silent for a moment. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the gutters of Copperlane Street, wearing the city down until it confessed its shape.

"Keep the observation file," she said finally. "Do not act on it. If the child exists, the child is a guild matter, and the guild can manage its own foundlings." She heard herself say it and examined the sentence afterward the way one examines a coin suspected of clipping. It was defensible. It was procedurally correct. It was also, she noticed, the first order she had given in this affair that served no purpose except delay — and she had dressed it in jurisdiction the way one dresses anything, quickly, before it catches cold.

"Yes, Colonel." Dree gathered his folder. At the door he paused. "High Command's letter. May I ask—"

"You may not."

He went. The door clicked. She sat alone with the lamp and the rain and the garnet seal lying on her blotter like a small wound.

Brigadier. One fortnight. Recovered intact.

She thought of the apprentice's half-step, the body arranging itself around a protected thing. She thought of a child drawing at a window with a practiced hand, and wondered — idly, dangerously, in the privacy of her own skull — whose hand it had learned from, and what faces it might grow into, and whether the Regiment's thirty-year project of forgetting had developed, somewhere in its long arithmetic, a remainder it had failed to carry.

She thought of an old woman in a farmyard, asking about trees.

Then Hesper Vance did what she had always done with everything that could unmake her: she opened her ledger, dipped her pen, and wrote, in her beautiful, model-of-clarity hand, *Workshop surveillance to continue. Atlas recovery proceeding on schedule.* And the words were lies only in the sense that all her words had been lies for thirty years — true enough to pass, smooth enough to handle, worn round as river stones.

Below the entry, unbidden, her pen hovered.

She did not write what she was thinking, which was this: *If the child is what I suspect the child is, then the Regiment will want it intact too, and I know what happens to things this Regiment wants intact.*

She blotted the page, locked the ledger, and rang for the steward to bring her carriage. There was, she told herself, a condolence call she owed to finish — a courtesy, nothing more, a colonel checking on a grieving guild household in the rain.

On Copperlane Street, the gutters ran silver toward the river gate, carrying everything the city let go of out to sea.
