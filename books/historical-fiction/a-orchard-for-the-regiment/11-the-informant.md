## 11. The Informant

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Rosalind had learned the grammar of knocks in four years at Fen's workshop — the courier's brisk double-rap, the guild inspector's official triple, the customers' shy single tap that always sounded apologetic even before they apologized. This knock was none of these. It was two fingers, dragged down the door's iron band, slow as a man wiping sweat from his hand before he touched something he did not want to be seen touching.

"Through the back," Rosalind murmured to Pip, who was grinding lapis in the corner with a pestle too big for his grip, blue dust ghosting his knuckles like frost. "The chest. You know which one."

He knew. They had rehearsed it every evening for six days, Pip folding himself into the pigment chest among the cakes of vermilion and lamp-black, pulling the false lid over himself until only a seam of darkness showed. He went now without argument, without even a look back, and that obedience frightened Rosalind more than the knock had. A child who had learned to disappear that quickly was a child who had been taught by necessity.

Rosalind opened the door on Marta Quillon, the letterpress printer three shops down, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate despite the mild morning. She was sixty if she was a day, with ink worked so deep into the creases of her hands that no pumice would ever fetch it out, and she had been Master Fen's friend for longer than Rosalind had been alive. She had spoken exactly eleven words at the funeral. *He was stubborn, and he was right, and now.* Then silence.

"Marta." Rosalind stepped aside. "Come in, it's cold on the step."

"It's cold everywhere," Marta said, which was not agreement, and came in anyway, and stood in the middle of the workshop turning slowly once, the way a woman turns when she is memorizing a room she suspects she will never see again. Her eyes passed over the drying racks hung with proofs, the long table where the Vellamere Atlas lay open to its half-finished map of the eastern coast, the jars of ground malachite catching the grey light. They did not pass over the pigment chest. They rested there for one heartbeat, two, then moved on.

Rosalind felt that rest like a blade laid flat against skin. Not cutting. Weighing.

"You should sit," Rosalind said.

"I shouldn't stay long enough to sit." Marta lowered herself onto the stool by the door regardless, because her knees were sixty years old and loyalty only carries a body so far. She folded her hands. The ink in them looked like old bruises. "I'm going to say something to you, apprentice, and you're going to want to argue, and I'm going to ask you not to waste both our time arguing."

"Then say it."

Marta looked at the door. At the window, where the curtain hung crooked since Tuesday and Rosalind had been meaning to fix it. At the ceiling, as though the Regiment might already have put ears in the rafters, which — Rosalind thought with a fresh chill — was not impossible. The Regiment could afford ears anywhere.

"There's been a report filed with the garrison clerk," Marta said quietly. "Guild channels. Not military ones, not yet — that's why I know about it, because it crossed my husband's desk for transcription before it goes up the chain. A report concerning this address." Her eyes came up, steady and terrible. "Concerning an unauthorized person residing here. A child. Male. Approximately six or seven years of age."

The workshop went very quiet. Outside, the city made its ordinary noises — cart wheels, a fishwife crying her prices, the bell of the Foundry Quarter striking the half-hour — and all of it seemed to belong to some other world, a world where reports were not filed and children were not contraband.

"When," Rosalind said. Their voice came out level, and they were proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud.

"Filed four days ago. It takes time to climb the ladder, that's all the mercy in it. Four days up, maybe five or six more before someone decides it's worth acting on. Someone will decide. Reports don't die of neglect in Vellamere; they ripen." Marta leaned forward. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I'm telling you there is a clock, and it started before you knew clocks were running."

* * *

Afterward Rosalind would try to reconstruct those first minutes and find them missing, the way a page torn from a folio leaves a gap the binding remembers. They remembered standing very still. They remembered the smell of the workshop — linseed oil, chalk dust, the iron tang of gall — suddenly intolerable, as though the air itself were complicit. They remembered thinking, absurdly, of the eastern coastline, how it needed another day of work before the shading would read true, and hating themselves for thinking it, and thinking it again anyway. Grief and fear both flee toward the familiar. The hand wants its work the way the drowning man wants driftwood.

"The informant," Rosalind said finally. "Who?"

"I don't know."

"You transcribed the report. Your husband transcribed it. Names are on reports."

"Not informants' names." Marta shook her head, slow. "That's the design of it, girl. The clerk sees a number, the number sees a seal, the seal sees nothing. Whoever wrote it wrote it knowing the guild's own machinery would hide their face. Clever. Or frightened. Usually both." She paused, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "But whoever it was knew things only someone close could know. That the child sleeps here, not merely visits. That he's been seen at the window. That he—" she hesitated, "—that he helps with the work. Grinding pigment. There was a detail about blue dust on small hands."

Rosalind looked down at their own fingers, stained faintly indigo at the cuticles, and felt the floor tilt beneath the boards.

Blue dust on small hands. Only three people living had seen Pip's hands dusted blue: Rosalind, Pip, and anyone who had stood in this workshop while he worked. And one other category of witness. The dead. Fen had seen those hands, in whatever weeks remained to him — no. No, Fen had died before Pip ever arrived at the door. Which meant the list of the living who had watched a small boy grind lapis in this room was short enough to count without taking off a boot.

Marta watched them do the arithmetic and did not interrupt it. That was a kindness, of its grim kind.

"It isn't me," she said at last. "I know you're wondering. I'd wonder. I'm telling you plainly so you can disbelieve me properly: it wasn't me, and it wasn't Aldous's memory I'd sell, nor yours." She rose from the stool, joints cracking like green kindling. "But I won't tell you who else it might be, because I don't know, and because guessing aloud in this room is how rooms get burned down with people in them. Take the warning. Spend it well."

At the door she stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"Aldous used to say the atlas would outlive everyone who tried to kill it," she said. "I told him he was a romantic fool. I'd give a great deal now to tell him he was right." The pause stretched. "Keep the boy away from the windows, Rosalind Zhou. Curtains are cheap. Children aren't."

Then she was gone, and the street swallowed her slate-colored shape, and Rosalind bolted the door behind her with hands that would not quite obey.

* * *

Pip emerged from the chest on his own schedule, which meant he had heard everything. Rosalind found him sitting on the floor beside it, arms around his knees, chin tucked, watching the door with an expression no six-year-old should own — the flat, waiting look of someone cataloguing exits.

"You heard," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question.

"Blue dust on small hands." Pip repeated it carefully, tasting each word like a foreign coin. His own hands were still blue at the fingertips from that morning. He looked at them, turned them over, studied them as though they belonged to a stranger. "Somebody told on me."

"Somebody told on *me*," Rosalind corrected, and hated the lie even as they said it, because they both knew better. "It's my fault. My house, my work, my—"

"My face." Pip said it simply. No tremor in it. Just fact, the way he'd say *rain today* or *the fire's low*. Whatever had happened to him before he appeared on the workshop step, it had taught him that his face was a thing with consequences attached. "You got me off the street. That part's yours. The face part was always coming."

Rosalind crouched to his level. Up close, the cut above his brow — the one that had started everything, that had shown Rosalind the Aurelian line written in bone — had healed to a thin silver thread. They resisted the urge to touch it.

"Listen to me. Nobody is coming through that door today. Nobody is coming tomorrow. We have days — real ones — and we are going to use every one of them. Do you understand? We are not caught. We are *preparing*."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Preparing what?"

And there it was: the question Rosalind had been circling since Marta's knock, since the writ, since the night they'd found Fen's correspondence and understood that surrender meant destruction and refusal meant arrest. Preparing what? Flight? Flight to where, carrying twenty years of an atlas that weighed more than either of them? Submission? The word tasted of ash.

"I don't know yet," Rosalind admitted. It cost something to say it to a child. It cost less than lying would have. "But I know the first thing. The plates can't stay here."

* * *

The problem was that Rosalind did not know where *here* ended and safety began, and the workshop offered no answer. Every wall was a wall the Regiment had built or taxed or surveyed. Every window faced a street walked daily by men in grey coats. The cellar was a cellar — dug, mapped, recorded in the municipal rolls like everything else in Vellamere, because the Regiment tolerated no unmapped space inside its walls. Rosalind had spent four years learning to see the city as their master saw it, as pure geometry, and now geometry betrayed them: there was nowhere in the drawn city that was not itself a trap.

Which left the undrawn city.

Rosalind was standing at the drying racks, staring unseeing at the proofs, when the memory surfaced — Pip, ten days ago, bursting up from behind the racks with dust in his hair and wonder all over his face, babbling about a hatch, a hole under the floor, voices, tunnels, *the roots smell like apples*. Rosalind had filed it away then as a child's adventure, had even scolded him gently for prying up floorboards. Now they knelt where he had knelt, and pulled at the rack's footboard, and felt the give.

The hatch opened on a breath of air that smelled, unmistakably, of apples. Old apples, cellar-dark apples, the ghost of an orchard pressed into stone. Below, steps descended into blackness.

Rosalind took a lantern down three steps and held it high. The tunnel ran straight as a survey line in both directions, vaulted in old brick gone soft with moss, its walls furred white with mineral bloom. Along the right-hand wall, at shoulder height, someone had carved marks into the mortar at intervals — small, precise, deliberate. A chevron. Three dots. A chevron. An arrow angled steeply down-left.

Rosalind's lantern hand began, very slightly, to shake.

They knew those marks. Every apprentice who trained under Aldous Fen knew them, because Fen used them on his field surveys — a private shorthand for bearing, distance, hazard — and because he had drilled them into Rosalind's skull with the patience of a man teaching scripture: *A map is only honest if its maker can walk the ground twice.* These were survey marks. Cut into the mortar of a tunnel that appeared on no map Rosalind had ever traced, in a hand they would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

Fen had been down here. Fen had measured these tunnels, marked them, *mapped them* — and kept the mapping out of the atlas. Out of the atlas that the Regiment wanted destroyed. Out of reach.

"For whoever inherits," Rosalind whispered, and the tunnel whispered it back in apple-scented dark, and for one long moment they stood on the steps with tears they refused to acknowledge running cold along their jaw, grieving a man twice: once for dying, and once for having known, all along, exactly what was coming — and preparing for it alone, in secret, in the dark, leaving lights lit for a successor he must have feared would never come.

Then they climbed back up, closed the hatch, and went to find Pip.

"We're moving the atlas tonight," they said. "All of it. Plates, folios, the finished sheets — everything. Can you help me carry?"

Pip's eyes went wide and bright. "Down to the apple place?"

"Down to the apple place." Rosalind managed something that was nearly a smile. "You found it first. Seems fitting you should help fill it."

* * *

They worked until dawn, and the work was a strange liturgy.

By lamplight Rosalind lifted each copper plate from its felt-lined drawer and wrapped it in oilcloth, and as they wrapped it they looked at it — really looked, plate by plate, the way you look at faces on a dock you may not sail from. The northern ranges, hatched in Fen's incomparable hand. The river deltas, which Rosalind had finished the winter after his death, working with tears freezing on their lashes because the workshop's coal had run out and pride would not permit buying more until the plate was done. The coastal charts. The cities, drawn ward by ward, street by street — and here Rosalind slowed, because the city maps were the dangerous ones now, weren't they? The ones whose margins Fen had annotated in his crabbed cipher, the ones whose blank final page waited at the atlas's end like a held breath.

Twenty years of a dead man's defiance, weighing perhaps forty pounds in oilcloth bundles.

Pip carried the lighter parcels with fierce concentration, lips pursed, stepping where Rosalind stepped, and between trips he asked questions — *what's this one, why's the river bend like that, who drew this bit* — and Rosalind answered each one fully, patiently, because answering was a way of not screaming. Somewhere past midnight Pip fell asleep on the third step of the tunnel, mid-sentence, a bundle of proofs pillowed under his cheek, and Rosalind sat down beside him and let the lantern burn and did not move him for a full hour, just sat in the apple-smelling dark listening to him breathe, guarding the only thing left worth guarding that wasn't paper.

By grey morning light every plate lay in the tunnel's dry alcove behind the second survey mark, wrapped and stacked and hidden beneath sacking that smelled honestly of stored apples, and the workshop above was stripped of everything that mattered and dressed, carefully, to look as though nothing had changed. Proof sheets still hung on the racks — blanks, misprints, harmless practice work. The great folio of the Vellamere Atlas lay open on the long table to the eastern coast, exactly as it always did, because a searched room should look lived-in and a hidden atlas begins with a visible decoy.

Rosalind climbed up last, sealed the hatch, and stood in the pale washed-out light of the workshop feeling the strangeness settle over everything. The room looked the same. The room was not the same. Trust had gone out of it the way heat leaves a house overnight — invisibly, totally, so that you only notice when your breath fogs.

Every knock was now an enemy. Every friendly face, a possible report. Marta's warning sat in Rosalind's chest like swallowed lead: *someone close.* Close enough to see blue dust on small hands. Close enough to know the child slept here. Close enough that the next visit, the next condolence, the next neighborly loaf of bread might arrive with grey coats waiting politely outside the door.

Rosalind washed their hands, stained blue at the cuticles, and looked at the stain a long time.

Let them come looking for blue dust, they thought. Let them search every shelf and chest and drawer in this room. Everything they find will tell them a careful mapmaker lives here, alone, keeping schedule, grieving a master, troubling no one.

And underneath their feet, beneath the roots and the apple-dark, the truth of thirty years would be lying in the dark with a sleeping boy's warmth still fading from its wrappings — patient as seed, waiting for spring.

Rosalind was banking the fire when they heard it: boots on the lane outside. Many boots. Unhurried, disciplined, and stopping — all together, all at once — directly in front of the workshop door.
