## 12. Blood in the Margins

The knock came in the grey part of morning, before the bread smell from the baker's across the lane had time to climb through the floorboards.

Pip knew all the knocks of the workshop by then. He knew Rosalind's double-tap-tap when they came back from the ink merchant with their arms full and couldn't work the latch. He knew old Master Tam's polite triple rap when he brought paper deliveries, and he knew the milk boy's flat slap of a palm because the milk boy was seven like Pip had maybe been once and didn't care about doors. He even knew the sound of no-knock at all, which was Rosalind slipping out before dawn to buy things they shouldn't be buying, and coming back with their coat smelling of river fog.

This knock was none of those.

This knock was a fist, big as a ham, three times, hard enough that the drying racks shivered and a half-dry map of the northern coast let go one corner with a soft papery sigh.

"Stay here," Rosalind said. They were already moving, already pulling the apron off over their head, smoothing it down like an apron could make everything normal. Their face was doing the thing it did — the still thing, the flat thing, the thing Pip had learned meant *I am frightened but I am not going to show you where I keep it.* "Under the table with your chalks. Don't come out. Don't make a sound. Not for anything you hear."

"Not for anything," Pip whispered, and crawled under the big drafting table where the shadows were thickest, among the dropped eraser crumbs and a dead moth curled like a comma.

Rosalind opened the door.

Pip couldn't see them from under the table, not properly — just boots. Boots filled the doorway the way weather fills a window. Six pairs. Maybe seven. Black leather, polished to a shine so deep it looked wet, each pair planted exactly the same distance apart, like the boots had been grown rather than worn.

"Master Rosalind Zhou." The voice belonged to the tallest boot. It was a voice like a ledger: everything counted, nothing felt. "Colonel Vance sends her compliments and her apologies for the hour. There has been a report of irregularities at this address. We are authorized to conduct an inspection."

"It's hardly an hour worth complimenting," Rosalind said. Their voice came out level. Pip marveled at that, the way he marveled at tightrope walkers. "But you're welcome to look. Mind the wet plates on the left rack; they'll smudge if breathed on wrong, and then we've both wasted a month."

Boots crossed the threshold. Boots spread through the workshop like spilled water, finding every low place.

Pip pressed himself against the table's crossbeam and made his breathing small. That was the first trick, he had learned it hiding from the matron at the foundling house before the cloak and the note and the door step: make your breath small enough to fit inside a walnut shell, and then hide the walnut. He closed his eyes and listened to the workshop being taken apart with terrible politeness.

Drawers slid open on oiled runners. Paper riffled, page by page by page, someone counting the world. A magnifying lens clinked down on glass. Somewhere above him a soldier said, "Coastlines, sir. Northern approaches," and another voice said, "Noted," and wrote something, because there was always someone writing something. The Regiment, Rosalind had told him once, half-joking and half-not, kept more maps than God kept stars, and believed every one of them more than any person.

A boot stopped beside the table leg. Close. So close Pip could see the stitching along the welt, tiny perfect stitches, hundreds of them, and he thought absurdly, *someone sat somewhere and made those stitches and was probably paid almost nothing,* and then he thought, *stop thinking, thinking is loud.*

The boot moved on.

* * *

They went through everything the way frost goes through a window: slowly, completely, leaving nothing untouched.

Pip watched between his own knees, chin tucked to chest, eyes slitted. He saw a soldier hold Rosalind's good brush set up to the light and fan the sable hairs with one thick finger, gentle as a man petting a cat he intended to drown later. He saw another unroll the master sketch of the Vellamere harbor and stand looking at it for a long time — too long — before rolling it back exactly, exactly as it had been. He saw them open the cabinet of finished plates, the ones Rosalind had spent whole nights inking while Pip slept in the loft bed pretending to be asleep, and lift each one out and hold it against the window light, checking for what lived underneath the drawing. Watermarks. Hidden writing. Whatever it was people hid in maps.

And he saw them find the pigment chest.

His chest. Well — not his, really. It was an old campaign trunk, iron-banded, its lid lined with tin so the moisture wouldn't get in, and Rosalind had cleared it out for him the second week, after the night he'd cried without meaning to and pretended it was dust. It held his blanket folded at the bottom, and his chalks in a cracked saucer, and the wooden horse Master Fen had whittled that Pip slept with under his pillow even though six-year-olds — or seven-year-olds, or whatever he was — weren't supposed to need wooden horses. The rest of the space was empty on purpose. Empty so that if anyone ever looked, it would only ever be a chest of pigments and a child's leavings, nothing worth opening twice.

Except he wasn't in the loft bed this morning. He'd been at the low table drawing compass roses when the knock came, and there hadn't been time, and now a soldier stood in front of the chest with his hand on the lid.

Pip stopped breathing altogether.

The soldier was young. Pip could see his jaw from underneath the table edge, and there was a pimple on it, and a razor nick, and that made him suddenly, horribly a person, a big person who was going to open the box and find a boy inside it, and then everyone would know, and Rosalind would be — Pip didn't finish the thought. Thoughts that ended in bad places got you caught, that was another thing he'd learned. You had to keep the ending off the page.

The soldier's hand lifted the lid two inches. Three.

Inside, on top of the folded blanket, lay the saucer of chalks and, because Pip had left it there yesterday in a hurry, the compass rose he'd been practicing — twelve points, drawn wobbly, the north star colored in gold because gold was the best color and north deserved it.

"Hendricks." A different soldier, further off. "The colonel wants the ledgers first. Move."

The lid came down. The latch clicked. The boot walked away, and Pip lay in the dark under the table with his heart slamming so hard he was sure it could be heard in the street, in the next street, in the whole garrison city of Vellamere, and he did the walnut-breathing until the slam became a tap became a whisper, and he was still there, still nothing, still a shadow among shadows under a table where history was being measured inch by inch by people who had never once been hidden inside anything.

* * *

It took them the length of two bells.

Pip knew because the cathedral bell rang nine while they were still going through the map drawers, and ten while they were prying at the locked drawer in Master Fen's old desk — the drawer Rosalind kept locked, whose key Rosalind wore on a cord around their neck, tucked under their shirt, and touched sometimes without seeming to notice they were touching it, the way Pip touched the wooden horse.

"We don't have the key," Rosalind said. "My late master kept his correspondence private. Grief makes people private. I've honored that."

"Break it," said the ledger voice.

There was a crack of splitting wood, and Pip flinched even though he'd known it was coming, because it was Master Fen's desk, and Master Fen was the ghost that lived kindest in this house, the smell of pipe smoke that wasn't there and the humming that Rosalind sometimes did without knowing it was his tune. Something splintered and the drawer rolled open and papers whispered across wood.

Silence. Then pages turning. Then the ledger voice again, quieter, which was worse: "Zhou. These are letters."

"They're personal effects."

"These are letters to my lord Aldous Fen regarding the disposition of certain historical materials. Dated—" a pause, a sound like a tongue clicking behind teeth, "—the month he died."

"My master died of a fall on the ice," Rosalind said. "Everyone knows it. Everyone signed it."

"Everyone signs everything, apprentice. That is rather the point of signatures." Papers folded. A leather folder creaked shut. "You'll understand these must be retained for review."

"You'll understand I'll want a receipt."

Another pause. Pip could hear, in the silence, the exact moment the tall boot's owner decided whether to laugh. He chose not to. "You have spirit, Zhou. Colonel Vance remarked on it. She does admire spirit — right up until she files it away." Boots gathered toward the door, unhurried, savoring. "We're not finished with this address. Consider that a courtesy. The Regiment prefers to extend courtesies before it extends anything else."

The door closed. The bolt — Rosalind's hand, quick on it — shot home.

Pip stayed under the table anyway. He'd been told not to come out, and the telling hadn't been un-told, and rules were the walls he built around himself when the world got big. But his legs were shaking now that the fear had nowhere left to go, shaking like the drying racks, and he hugged his knees and put his forehead down and breathed walnut-small breaths until Rosalind's face appeared upside-down in the gap beneath the tablecloth, pale and smudged with charcoal.

"It's over," Rosalind whispered. "Come out, little wren."

He didn't move. "You said not for anything I heard."

Something crossed Rosalind's face — pain, quick as a bird's shadow. "What did you hear?"

Pip crawled out slowly, joints stiff, and stood in the wrecked light of the workshop. Drawers hung open like pulled teeth. Master Fen's desk had a wound in its side, pale raw wood showing through the dark varnish. His chest sat by the wall, latched, innocent, holding a wobbly compass rose and a wooden horse and a boy's whole secret life.

"I heard them say," Pip said, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted, "that the last Aurelian evidence should burn."

Rosalind went very still. Stillness, Pip understood now, was not calm. He'd thought stillness was calm when he first came here. He knew better. Stillness was what happened when a feeling was too big to move, so it just stood there in you like a bear in a doorway.

"Where," Rosalind said carefully. "Which words exactly?"

"The one with the pimple said the archives burned thirty years ago, everything, every portrait plate and parish roll, and the other one said not everything, and the pimple one said well obviously not everything or why would the colonel send us to a mapmaker's shop, and then they laughed." Pip wrapped his arms around himself. "And then the tall one said the girl-child was never recovered either, and the pimple one said the line dies with the atlas then, and the tall one said no — he said —"

Pip's throat closed. He made himself say it, because Rosalind needed the words whole and he was the only one who'd heard them, and that made him, for the first time in his remembered life, the keeper of something important.

"He said: *burn the shop and the last of them together, when the writ comes due.* And the pimple one laughed and said the colonel likes her fires tidy."

The workshop was very quiet. Outside, the milk boy slapped a door down the lane and someone shouted good-naturedly at him and a dog barked twice, the ordinary world going on with its ordinary noises as though the air hadn't just changed into something else entirely.

Rosalind knelt. They took Pip's face in both hands, thumbs resting light on his cheekbones, and looked at him the way they looked at a map that had just shown them a coastline nobody had charted before.

"Pip," they said. "Listen to me. What they said about burning — about *them*, about the last of a line — that isn't about a family that's gone. Do you understand? They said it standing three feet from the reason it isn't gone."

Pip frowned. He was six, or seven, or whatever he was; big thoughts arrived in him slowly, like furniture being carried up narrow stairs. And then they arrived all at once, and he sat down hard on the floor.

"Me," he said. "The last Aurelian evidence is me."

"Don't say it like that."

"But it's true." He heard his own voice go strange and faraway, the way it did in dreams. "They didn't lose me. They're *looking* for me. All this time I thought — I thought somebody left me because I was bad, or because —" Because he was forgettable, was what he'd thought, in the wordless way children think such things, the way you think about weather. "They wrapped me in a soldier's cloak. Somebody who wore this"—he plucked at the memory of the coarse grey wool, long since cut down and resewn into a jacket he wore every day—"took me past sentries and gates and gave me to a stranger with a note that said *forget the face.* Somebody was trying to save me and they're still trying to catch me and I'm not lost, I'm *hunted.*"

"Yes," Rosalind said, because they were honest in the ways that mattered and lied beautifully only in the ways that kept people alive. "Yes, you are. And do you know what else you are?"

"What?"

"You are sitting in a room full of proof they can't afford to let exist." Rosalind's grip on his shoulders tightened, and their eyes had gone bright and hard and alive in a way Pip had never quite seen before, grief and fury fused into something new. "Thirty years they've burned paper and scratched faces off portraits and renamed villages, and it worked, it mostly worked — except my master spent twenty years hiding the truth in coastlines and survey marks and marginal notes, in a book they think is a book. And now they've come themselves. Now they've told us the deadline. Do you see, Pip? They just handed us the schedule of our own funeral, and funerals can be cancelled."

Pip looked around the ruined workshop — the wounded desk, the rifled drawers, his own chest sitting quiet against the wall with its cargo of chalks and a wooden horse — and something shifted in him, some load-bearing beam of childhood settling into a new position with a groan only he could feel.

He understood, in the way you understand a thing forever once you understand it: adults lie. Not all of them, and not all lies — but the world ran on some of them, big ones, painted-over ones, and the paint was called maps and histories and *everyone signed it.* Master Fen had known. The note-giver had known. Even the soldier with the pimple, laughing about tidy fires — he knew, and laughed, because knowing and laughing was how you survived being the boot instead of the boy under the table.

Pip was never going to be able to unknow it. He felt the unknowing close behind him like a door.

"Then we shouldn't be here when they come back," he said. His voice sounded older to him. He didn't like it and he didn't refuse it. "Should we?"

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "We shouldn't. And there's a place — I think there's a place — under the floor. My master left marks I haven't finished reading." They were already rising, already crossing to the drying racks, already kneeling to press their palm flat against the boards, and Pip saw their hand stop, and press again, and stop.

From below the floorboards — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable — came the sound of boots.

Marching boots. In the tunnels. Under the orchard.

Rosalind's head came up, and their eyes met Pip's, and neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing left to say that the floor wasn't already saying.
