## 13. The Guild Turns

The rain had been falling for three days, a thin grey insistence that turned the cobbles of Inkrow Lane to the color of old pewter and made the whole guild quarter smell of wet paper and tallow. Rosalind walked it slowly, hood up, the way they had learned to walk in the last week — not hurrying, not lingering, a person with an errand and nothing to hide. Pip was at the workshop with Mistress Odile from the dyehouse next door, a woman with hands like walnut shells who had asked no questions when Rosalind requested a favor and had simply arrived with bread and a deck of worn playing cards, as though minding children during a siege were a thing one did between dye lots.

The atlas plates were in the tunnels now. The thought of them down there, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the roots of the old orchard, was both comfort and wound. Every time Rosalind passed the hatch beneath the drying racks they felt the plates' absence in the workshop like a missing tooth — the tongue going to the gap again and again, unable to leave it alone.

But it was not the plates that had brought them out into the rain. It was a name.

Tobias Merrick. Master printer, third stall from the corner, the man who had pressed Fen's proofs for twenty years and who had, three days ago, leaned across his own counter with the smell of ink on his apron and said, in a voice pitched under the clatter of his pressmen: *Someone in the guild told them about the boy. I don't know who. But I know who knows. Come to me when you're ready to hear it, and not before.*

Rosalind had not been ready for three days. They had told themselves it was caution — verify, cross-check, the discipline Fen had drilled into them until it lived in their wrists. *A map is only as honest as its sources, Rosalind. Trust the survey, not the surveyor's smile.* But it had not been caution. It had been dread, plain and simple, because Rosalind had done the arithmetic in the sleepless dark and the list of people who could have known was very short, and every name on it was a name that had sat at Fen's table, had drunk his bad wine, had argued with him about kerning and margins as though the world were made of nothing but letters and lines.

And one of those names was Tobias Merrick himself, which was the cruelest possibility of all, and the reason Rosalind had waited three days — because the printer's warning might be a lure, a kindness turned inside out, and Rosalind could not bear to find that out and still have to walk home through the rain and unlock a door and face a child who trusted them.

Now they stood outside the print shop, watching water bead and run down the window glass, blurring the inverted gilt letters of MERRICK & SONS into a silver smear. Through the blur, the press could be seen moving in its slow iron rhythm, and the pressmen's shadows sliding across the paper like figures in a shadow-play.

Rosalind went in.

* * *

The interior was warm and loud and smelled the way Rosalind imagined heaven might smell, if heaven were staffed by practical men: ink, oil, hot metal, the sweetish rot of old rag paper. Tobias Merrick stood at the stone supervising a proof pull, and he was exactly as Rosalind remembered him from guild feasts — broad, grey-bearded, ink worked so deep into his knuckles that no pumice would ever bring the skin's own color back. He looked up, saw who it was, and something crossed his face that Rosalind had never seen there before. It took a moment to name it, because it did not belong on that broad, untroubled face.

It was shame.

"Back room," Merrick said, not loudly. To the pressman: "Break for supper, all of you. Half hour." And when the last apron had gone out into the rain, he led Rosalind through the hanging sheets of drying broadsides — proclamations, price lists, a hymnal page, the ordinary wallpaper of an ordinary city — into a small room with a stove, two chairs, and a locked cabinet he did not look at, though Rosalind noticed him not looking at it the way one notices a man not looking at a wound.

"Sit," he said. "I'll make tea. Tea helps. My wife says tea doesn't help anything, but she's dead eleven years and I still make it when I'm frightened, so between us we've never settled the question."

He made it badly, hands unsteady, and set the cup in front of Rosalind without ceremony. Then he sat, and put his ruined hands flat on his knees, and looked at the stove instead of at his guest.

"Your master and I were friends for twenty-six years," he said. "I want that said first. Whatever comes after it, I want that said first."

Rosalind's throat had gone tight. "Say it again, then."

"I was the one." The words came out flat and heavy, like type dropped on the stone. "I told them about the boy. Not the Regiment itself — I'd never have gone near the garrison, I'm not that far gone — I told a man who I knew would carry it up the chain, because I was too much of a coward to carry it myself. A broker. He deals in small information the way I deal in small print. I gave him the fact of the child. That there was a child at Fen's old workshop. That the child had not gone to the parish. That you were keeping him."

The stove ticked. Rain worked at the little window. Rosalind found that their hands were perfectly still in their lap, and that this stillness was the most frightening thing about the moment — the body gone quiet the way a workshop goes quiet before the master finds the flaw in the plate.

"Why?" It came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Why would you do that? You warned me. Three days ago you warned me, you put yourself at risk to warn me, after — after you'd already sold us."

"Because I sold you and then I lay awake for two nights listening to my own conscience, which is a poor tenant — pays nothing, makes a terrible noise." Merrick finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "You want the whole of it? You'll get the whole of it. My daughter. My son-in-law. And my granddaughter, who is four years old and who has my late wife's nose and my late wife's temper, God preserve everyone."

He stood, went to the cabinet, unlocked it with a key from around his neck — Rosalind had not even seen the chain — and took out a folder of cheap paper. He laid it on the table between them like a man laying down cards he had already lost with.

"Eighteen months ago, the Regiment opened a file on me. Not for anything I'd done. For something my son-in-law said, drunk, at a wedding — some joke about the Colonel's commission, the kind of joke every man in this city has made and ninety-nine of them got away with. Someone reported him. And the file sat there, and I was summoned — politely, so politely, you know how they are — and a captain with soft hands explained to me that files are like damp in a house. Harmless if you keep on top of it. Terrible if you let it spread. And that a man in my position, with my guild standing, with my access — a man who prints for half the merchants in Vellamere and hears what they say — could be very useful in keeping the damp down."

Rosalind looked at the folder. It was thin. Files of the innocent are always thin; there is so little to write down.

"What did they ask you for?"

"Nothing. That was the trick of it. For eighteen months they asked for nothing. They'd send a note now and then — a name, a question — and I'd answer or I wouldn't, and mostly I wouldn't, and nothing happened, and I began to think I'd imagined the whole arrangement, that it was a leash they'd attached and forgotten." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as paper dust. "And then your master died, and the boy appeared, and the note came. *The child at the Fen workshop. Confirm presence. Report composition of household.* And I sat with that note for a day and a night, Rosalind, and I told myself every lie a man tells. That it meant nothing. That the Regiment knew already. That if I didn't answer they'd simply ask someone else, and my answer or no answer wouldn't change a single thing that happened to that child. And that my granddaughter's nose — my wife's nose — was worth more than a stranger's."

"Is that what you believe now?"

"No." He said it immediately, and with such force that the cup rattled. "No. I believe that I looked at a six-year-old boy and saw a file, and a four-year-old girl, and weighed them, and I will answer for that at whatever court receives me. I believe that Aldous Fen died for what I helped them find, and that if they burn that workshop with you in it, I will have lit the fire. I believe all of it. Belief is cheap. It's the only thing I have left that's cheap."

Rosalind sat with the tea going cold in front of them and tried to feel what they were supposed to feel. Rage was there — they could locate it, the way one locates a burn, a bright specific point under the breastbone. Fen's face floated up, the way it did now in dreams: not the last weeks, when the cough had thinned him, but earlier, whole, laughing over a misprinted proof with Merrick's arm around his shoulders, the two old men wheezing at some joke about a dropped letter, and Rosalind a teenager in the corner pretending not to be charmed. That arm around those shoulders. That laugh, that had been real, that had been twenty-six years real.

And underneath the rage, unwelcome and insistent, something else. Because Rosalind knew the arithmetic Merrick had done. Had done it themselves, in the dark, every night since Pip arrived. *If I keep him, what happens to me? What happens to the atlas? What is one child against the work?* Rosalind had chosen differently — or told themselves they had — but they had run the same sums. The difference between them was not kind of soul. It was only what each of them had been asked to put on the scale.

"Your granddaughter," Rosalind said at last. "What's her name?"

Merrick blinked. Of all the directions the conversation might have gone, this one had clearly not been in his preparations. "Marguerite. We call her Magpie. She steals anything that shines."

"Magpie." Rosalind turned the word over. "There's a boy in my workshop who insists his name is Pip. He chose it himself. I don't know what his mother called him. I don't know if anyone alive knows. And I have spent three weeks understanding that the Regiment's whole power — the purge, the burning, the files, your captain with the soft hands — all of it rests on one thing. That they get to say what people are called. What a line is called. What a village was called, after they burn it off the map. What a file says a man is, instead of what he is." They looked up. "You gave them a fact, Tobias. Facts they can get anywhere. What they cannot get anywhere is what I'm about to give you, and I give it once, so attend."

Merrick went very still.

"I am not going to expose you. I am not going to the guild council, I am not going to the other masters, I am not going to stand in the square and read your file aloud, which is what you deserve by every law of friendship and probably by guild law too. Here is what I am going to do instead. I am going to use you."

The old printer's face worked. "Use me."

"You know every press in this district. You know which masters hate the Regiment quietly, which ones hate it loudly, which ones are bought and which ones are merely frightened, and you know the difference, which is more than the Regiment knows. You know how to move paper and type without the wardens noticing, because you've done it your whole life under their noses. And you owe a debt that money cannot pay, to a dead man and a living child. So you will pay it in the only coin you have. When I come to you — and I will come to you, soon, and ask you for something enormous — you will not hesitate. You will not weigh it against a file. You will do it, and you will bring others, and you will tell them it is for Aldous Fen, and you will not tell them what you did, and you will live with that silence the way I will live with my own silences, because that is what it costs to still be a person inside this city. Do you understand me?"

For a long moment Merrick did not move. The stove ticked. Somewhere out in the shop the rain found a leak and began a small, patient dripping onto wood.

Then the old man bowed his head — not the bow of a subordinate, but the bow of a man at a graveside — and when he lifted it, his eyes were clear in a way they had not been since Rosalind entered.

"I understand you," he said. "Fen taught you that, didn't he. Mercy with a spine in it. He was the same. He caught me skimming paper stock our first year of partnership — skimmed it for my mother's burial, if you want the whole of it — and he never said a word to the council. He just put me on the accounts committee for ten years, where I couldn't skim a thing, and we never spoke of it, and I loved him for it until the day I betrayed him and I love him for it now." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, an ungraceful, complete gesture. "Ask me, then. Whatever it is. Ask me now, while my stomach is still turned and my courage is fresh."

"Not yet." Rosalind stood. The tea was untouched and would remain so; some cups are only ever for holding. "Soon. Within the fortnight, I think, and sooner if the Regiment moves first. When I ask, it will be the largest thing this guild has done in thirty years, and it will need to move fast, and it will need to be deniable, and it will need men who can look a warden in the eye without their hands shaking. You'll want to practice that. You'll want to start tonight."

Merrick almost smiled — a cracked, real thing. "And the boy? The child I sold?"

Rosalind paused at the door, rain-wet air pushing in from the street.

"He draws compass roses now," they said. "He's very good at the north point. He thinks north is the important one because it's where the cold comes from and he respects things that are dangerous." A breath. "When this is done — if we live, if it works — you'll meet him. And you'll tell him whatever you decide a man in your position should tell a child. I won't make that choice for you. I've learned this week that choices made for other people's souls rot fast."

* * *

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist by the time Rosalind came out, and the lane was turning blue with early dusk. They walked home the long way, past the guildhall with its bronze doors and its roll of masters' names going back two hundred years, and found themselves, without quite deciding to, stopping.

Because there was a new plaque. Small, recent, the verdigris barely started. The Regiment had put it up after Fen's death — *In Memory of Aldous Fen, Master of the Cartographers' Guild, Lost to Misfortune in the Winter of His Years* — and Rosalind had avoided it for months the way one avoids a grave one is angry at. Tonight they stood in front of it and read it twice, and what struck them, what stopped them with their hand half-raised to the cold bronze, was not the lie about misfortune.

It was the roll of names on the older plaques above it. Row upon row of masters, apprentices, engravers. And there, third row from the top, in lettering a century and a half old, a name that made the mist seem suddenly much colder:

**Zhou. Master of the Guild, by appointment of the Aurelian Commission.**

Rosalind stood in the blue dusk and read their own family name joined to that extinct word — *Aurelian* — a word they had only learned three weeks ago, from a defaced portrait hidden in a folio, and they felt the ground of their whole life shift the way ground shifts in a flood, quietly and then all at once.

The Regiment had not erased the Aurelians alone.

Someone had drawn the maps for them.

Rosalind turned up their collar and walked home through the mist, faster now, the question walking beside them like a second shadow — and in the workshop window ahead, a candle was already lit, and a small silhouette bent over paper, drawing north.
