## 14. What's in a Name

The candle had burned to a stub by the time Rosalind climbed the stairs, and Pip was asleep with his cheek on the drafting table, one hand still curled around a stub of graphite. Beside his elbow lay a compass rose drawn four times — the first lopsided, the second better, the third and fourth nearly perfect, their petals even as clover. He had signed each one *Pip*, in a careful child's hand, as though signing were the whole point of the exercise.

Rosalind stood in the doorway a long moment, watching him breathe.

*Zhou,* they thought. The name sat differently now. It sat like a stone in a shoe — small, ignorable, impossible to forget once noticed. All evening it had walked home beside them through the mist, and now it followed them up the stairs and into the workshop where the atlas lay open on its stand, twenty years of Master Fen's patience bound in calfskin, waiting for its final page.

They woke Pip gently, walked him down to the cot behind the pigment shelves, and tucked the soldier's cloak around him — the cloak he would not give up, that still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and someone else's life. He murmured something about north and was gone again, deep under, the way children sink when they feel safe. Or when they are too tired to feel anything else.

Rosalind returned to the table and did not light a fresh candle. They worked in the last grey ember of the old one, because some things wanted darkness before they could be looked at squarely.

The archive of the workshop was not an archive. It was an inheritance of clutter: three generations of apprentices' practice sheets, dead men's tracing paper, rolls of surveys too outdated or too embarrassing to destroy. Master Fen kept everything, on the theory — stated once, drunk, at midwinter — that *a mapmaker who throws away a map is lying to himself about what maps do.* Rosalind had always taken this for sentiment. Tonight it sounded like confession.

The oldest chests lived under the eaves, where the roof leaked least and the damp got in anyway. Rosalind hauled down the first one, breathing dust, and began to sort.

* * *

It took until the third chest, past midnight, past the point where the body's tiredness becomes a kind of second sight. They found what they were looking for without knowing, quite, that they had been looking for it — a roll of oilcloth tied with tarred string, heavier than it should have been, labeled in a hand Rosalind knew only from the guild's oldest ledgers:

**ZHOU, WEIRAN — SURVEYS OF THE EASTERN PROVINCES, YEAR 31 OF THE OLD COUNT**

Weiran Zhou. Grandfather. A name spoken in Rosalind's childhood exactly twice: once by their father, wearily, over accounts; once by an aunt, bitterly, after too much plum wine — *he drew for them, you know, that's how we got the house, that's how we got any of it* — and then never again. Rosalind had understood, in the way children understand, that Weiran Zhou was a door the family kept shut, and had built their whole self around not opening it. Mapmaking had felt like a clean trade. Lines and coastlines and honest distances. History happened elsewhere.

They untied the tarred string.

The first sheet inside was a survey of orchards. Rosalind almost laughed — the absurdity of it, the grandfather reaching across fifty years to hand them an orchard — but the laugh died before it started, because the map was not of orchards. It was of villages, marked by their orchards, each grove rendered with loving precision: apple, quince, damson, the almond rows of the lowland holdings. And beside each grove, in Weiran Zhou's small, exact, professional hand, a number.

Not acreage. Rosalind checked — the scales were there, the legend was there, everything a survey should have was there, which is precisely what made it monstrous. It was a perfect map. It was also, unmistakably, if you held it next to the thing Rosalind now carried in their chest alongside their own heartbeat — a census. Households. Hearths. Hands capable of bearing arms, or of being descended from those who had. Each village numbered. Each grove a signature, because families plant orchards and stay with them, and a man who could count trees could count bloodlines.

And in the corner, stamped in violet ink gone brown with age, the seal of the Regiment's founding commission.

Rosalind laid the sheet flat on the drafting table, weighting the corners with the brass objects Master Fen had left behind — the compass, the inkwell, the little lead hand that pointed to whatever page was current. Then they unrolled the rest.

There were eleven more. Eleven years of them. Year 31 through Year 42 of the old count — and then the maps changed. The numbers stopped growing and started shrinking. Villages appeared with their groves drawn beautifully and their household counts struck through, corrected downward year over year, the way a merchant corrects inventory. Damson Hollow: forty-one hearths, thirty-eight, thirty-one, nineteen, six, none. The orchard remained on the map, because orchards outlive their planters. That was the whole horror of it. Weiran Zhou had drawn the trees so carefully that you could walk the empty countryside today and know, from his lines alone, which villages used to have children in them.

On the twelfth sheet — Year 43, the last — the numbers were mostly gone, and in the margin Weiran had written a single line, small enough to be missed, pressed hard enough to be read by fingertip:

*They ask me to stop numbering the living and begin numbering the dead. I have asked to be released from service.*

Below it, in different ink, added later, shakier: *Released.*

Rosalind stood in the cold workshop with their grandfather's confession under their hands and understood that they had been wrong all evening, wrong since the flood-plain shift of the evening before. He hadn't been a monster who drew purge maps. He had been an ordinary man — competent, careful, proud of his fine lines — who told himself, for twelve years, that measuring was not killing. That the map was not the knife. That history happened elsewhere, on the pages where other people wrote the orders.

Rosalind knew the feeling intimately. They had been telling themselves the same story for three weeks, grinding pigments and tracing coastlines while a boy slept behind the pigment shelves with a face worth killing for.

* * *

Pip woke near dawn, as he always did, with the abruptness of a bird. Rosalind heard him before they saw him: the soft thump of feet on the floorboards, the pause at the top of the stairs where he always stopped to check whether the day was safe.

"It's me," Rosalind said. "Come look at this."

He came down wrapped in the cloak, hair flattened on one side, and climbed onto the stool beside theirs without asking — a familiarity that still caught in Rosalind's throat sometimes, how quickly the boy had decided this workshop was his. He looked at the spread of old maps with the frank interest of a child shown anything on a drafting table.

"Old," he pronounced.

"Very old." Rosalind hesitated. There was no way to explain it that wasn't a lie, and they found, testing the lies quickly in their head — *old surveys, nothing important, go draw your roses* — that they didn't want to spend one. "Do you know what a census is?"

Pip shook his head.

"It's counting people. Counting who lives where. Like a map, but of families instead of rivers." Rosalind touched the edge of the Year 41 sheet, the one where Damson Hollow went from thirty-one hearths to nineteen. "This is a census. My grandfather made it."

"Your grandfather." Pip considered this with enormous seriousness. "Is he dead?"

"A long time ago."

"Was he good at drawing?"

Rosalind looked at the almond rows, the precise little crowns of the quince trees, the numbers falling away like leaves. "Yes," they said. "That was the problem."

Pip nodded as if this were a perfectly ordinary answer, which, from a child who had arrived on a doorstep with the words *forget the face* pinned to his cloak, perhaps it was. He studied the map a moment longer, and then — with the terrible accuracy of the very young — put his finger on the struck-through numbers.

"Where did everyone go?"

And Rosalind, who had faced down Colonel Vance over tea, who had lied to soldiers at the door, who was planning a theft of imperial property from inside a burning building — Rosalind could not speak. The silence went on too long. Pip looked up at them, and whatever he saw in their face, he didn't ask again. He just leaned, very slightly, until his shoulder rested against Rosalind's arm, and stayed there.

"We're going to find out," Rosalind said finally. "All of it. Every name. That's what the last page is for."

"For whoever inherits," Pip said solemnly, misquoting Fen's marginal note, which he had seen once over Rosalind's shoulder and adopted as scripture.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "For whoever inherits."

* * *

The guild archive opened at the eighth bell, and Rosalind was at its doors at the seventh, standing in the colonnade with their collar up, watching the mist come off the river in ropes. The archivist, a stooped woman named Mother Cassel who had known Fen for forty years, let them in early without comment. She had been letting mapmakers in early for decades; it was, Rosalind realized with a new chill, the kind of small mercy that institutions run on, the mercy that asks no questions and therefore enables every answer.

"I need the enrollment rolls," Rosalind said. "Survey corps. Old count, years thirty to fifty."

"Family research?" Cassel asked mildly, already leading the way into the stacks, where the smell of vellum and mouse droppings and time was so thick it was almost a taste.

"Something like that."

Cassel pulled the rolls without further questions, set them on a reading desk, and paused, one hand flat on the cover. "Master Fen came here, you know. Near the end. Same request." Her eyes, pale as skimmed milk, settled on Rosalind. "He sat where you're sitting. He read for six hours. When he left, he said—" she recited it with the precision of a woman who had repeated it to herself many times— "*'Every line has a hand behind it, Cassel. I've spent my life pretending mine was clean.'*"

Then she left them alone with the dead.

The rolls were organized by year, and the Z's were at the back, and Rosalind turned to them with hands that were steadier than they deserved to be.

**ZHOU, WEIRAN. Enrolled Year 29. Senior surveyor, Eastern Commission. Seconded to the Founding Census, Year 31. Commendation, Years 33, 35, 37.** Commendations. Medals for the counting. **Reassigned Year 43. Discharged Year 44 at own petition.**

At own petition. Twelve years, and then the courage to say *no more* — a courage that had bought the family a house and a silence and a grandson who grew up thinking mapmaking was a clean trade.

But the roll held one more entry, and this was the one that broke something open in Rosalind's chest, because it wasn't about Weiran at all.

Directly beneath, in the same clerk's hand:

**FEN, ALDOUS. Enrolled Year 61. Apprentice-surveyor, Eastern Commission.**

Year 61. Thirty years ago. The same commission. The same eastern provinces, the emptied villages, the orchards standing in their neat recorded rows. Aldous Fen had begun his career as a young man walking the ground Weiran Zhou had mapped — walking it after, mapping the absence, and somewhere in those years, among the damson trees and the struck-through hearths, he had learned what the numbers meant. And he had spent the rest of his life — twenty years of the atlas, patient as geology — preparing to write the names back in.

*Rosalind,* he had said, near the end, in one of those conversations that seem like weather until afterward, when they reveal themselves as bequests. *A map is a promise. Whoever draws it decides what future readers are allowed to see. Never draw for a man who tells you the seeing doesn't matter.*

Rosalind closed the roll. Sat still in the dust-gilded quiet of the stacks. Somewhere above, water dripped from a gutter onto stone, keeping slow time, like the workshop's metronome, like a pulse.

Shame rose first — the full weight of it, the family shame, two generations deep, the house built on counted hearths. But beneath the shame, unexpected, steady as bedrock under flood silt, something else: responsibility. Not guilt, which looks backward and paralyzes. Responsibility, which faces forward and moves. His grandfather had drawn the erasure. Fen had drawn the record. And the blank page waited for a third hand — theirs — to join them, to finish the sentence that ran from Weiran Zhou's careful numbers through Fen's patient margins and out into the world.

You cannot choose what you inherit. You can only choose what you carry forward.

Rosalind carried the memory of a shoulder leaning against their arm. *Where did everyone go?*

We're going to find out. All of it. Every name.

They stood, returned the roll to Mother Cassel's desk, and asked for one more thing: the parish registers of the eastern provinces, every surviving one, births and marriages and burials, however fragmentary. Names. They needed names.

"There were a great many villages," Cassel said quietly, watching them.

"Then it will take a great many nights," Rosalind said. "I'll copy them all."

* * *

They walked back through the city as the morning markets opened, past stalls heaped with apples and late plums, fruit that had grown in orchards whose names nobody remembered anymore except on twelve rolled sheets under a leaking roof. At the corner of Cordwainer Row, a Regiment crier was posting notices, and a small knot of citizens stood reading. Rosalind slowed, read the nearest placard over a fishmonger's shoulder, and felt the fortnight tighten around their ribs like a band.

By order of Colonel Hesper Vance, garrison commander: the Vellamere Atlas, being property of the Imperial Survey, to be surrendered for completion within the appointed term. Noncompliance to be treated as sedition against the Regiment.

Sedition. The word was printed in handsome black type, the letters perfectly formed, the work of some guild printer paid to make terror look tidy. Rosalind stared at it and thought of their grandfather's commendations, of Fen's six hours in the archive, of a boy asleep on a cot who had asked *where did everyone go* and accepted silence with a child's terrible grace.

Two weeks. Perhaps less; Vance struck Rosalind as a woman who considered deadlines a formality observed toward other people.

They walked the last streets quickly. In the workshop window ahead, the candle was lit again — they had left it burning for Pip, so the room would never be dark when he woke — and through the glass they could see him at the drafting table, bent over his paper, drawing.

Inside, he had made a fifth compass rose. This one he had not signed *Pip*. He had left the space beneath it blank, and when Rosalind hung up their coat he pointed at the empty place with his graphite stub and said, matter-of-factly, "That one's yours. You have to sign it too."

Rosalind picked up the pen. Their hand, the hand of Weiran Zhou's grandchild, the hand that would fill the blank page, hovered over the paper — and signed.
