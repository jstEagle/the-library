## 15. The Fortnight Ends

Colonel Hesper Vance did not sleep on the last night of the fortnight.

She told herself it was the wind. Vellamere in late autumn had a way of pushing through shuttered windows and rattling things that ought to have stayed still — lamp chimneys, loose panes, the small tin soldier her brother had left behind twenty-nine years ago on a windowsill she had never found the heart to clear away. It stood there now, one arm raised in a parade salute no one had ever returned, and at three o'clock in the morning its shadow lay across her desk like a fallen man.

She was not at her desk to work. She had finished the work hours ago: the seizure order, drawn up in her own hand, countersigned, sealed, and set squarely in the center of the blotter where she could not pretend she hadn't seen it. *At first light, take the Fen workshop. Recover the atlas intact. Apprehend the mapmaker's apprentice for questioning.* Clean language. Bureaucratic language. The kind of language that made an atrocity sound like a delivery schedule.

The apprentice. Rosalind Zhou. She turned the name over the way she might turn a coin of uncertain weight.

Vance had met the young mapmaker twice. Once across a guild hall, at distance, among the crowd of mourners who had come to stand uncomfortably near Master Aldous Fen's coffin and then drift away from it, as though grief were a current too strong to swim against for long. And once — eleven days ago — in the workshop itself, under the pretense of condolence that had been neither pretense nor condolence but something colder and more useful than either.

She remembered the smell of the place. Linseed oil and lampblack, wet paper, the mineral sweetness of ground lapis. She remembered the drying racks hung with coastlines like laundry, and the compass above the door with its needle trembling toward a north no Regiment survey acknowledged. She remembered most of all how the apprentice had looked at her — not with fear, which would have been simple, and not with defiance, which would have been flattering, but with *attention*. The look of someone measuring her the way they measured everything else in that room: precisely, patiently, and against some standard she could not see.

*You are afraid of me,* Vance had thought at the time. Now, sitting alone in the blue hour before dawn, she amended it. No. The apprentice was afraid of her the way a good cartographer is afraid of weather — respectfully, professionally, already planning the route around it.

That was what kept her awake. Not guilt. Vance had long ago made a careful inventory of her conscience, the way quartermasters inventory stores, and she knew exactly what was on the shelves and what was not. Guilt over Fen's death was not there. She had not killed him; she had merely failed to prevent the machinery from doing what the machinery did, and there was a difference, and she had built thirty years of career out of such differences.

It was something else. Something smaller and more irritating, like a splinter worked into the pad of a thumb.

*They were finishing it.*

The report from the informant had said so plainly enough. Plates completed nightly. Pigments mixed by lamplight past the second bell. The atlas — the great Vellamere Atlas, the thing the Regiment had spent three decades ensuring existed only as rumor — was not being hidden or sold or burned. It was being *finished*, right up until the hour it was due to be surrendered, as though surrender were simply the next stage of the work.

And why not? Vance thought. Why would the apprentice rush to complete a book they expected to lose? Unless they did not expect to lose it. Unless somewhere in that ink-stained skull lived the belief that a finished thing is harder to kill than an unfinished one. That a map, once whole, becomes a fact, and facts are stubborn animals that bite the hand that tries to put them down.

Or unless — and here the splinter twisted — the apprentice intended never to hand it over at all.

Vance rose from her chair and went to the window. Below, the garrison streets lay pale and empty, the cobbles silvered with frost, the watchfires down to their red coals. Somewhere east, beyond the walls, the old orchard district slept under its bare trees — the orchard that had given the city's founding quarter its name and then been paved over, row by row, until only the name remained, the way a face remains on coins after the person is gone.

Her orders were precise. At dawn — first light, 6:14 by the almanac — two squads would move on the workshop. Quietly. The Regiment had learned the value of quiet; loud seizures bred martyrs, and martyrs bred pamphlets, and pamphlets bred the kind of trouble that took a decade to burn out. She would go herself. Not because protocol required it, but because she had learned long ago that if you wanted a thing done without excess, you stood where you could see the hands.

She had written all of this. She had signed all of this.

So why was she standing at her window doing arithmetic about candle stubs?

* * *

The delay began as nothing.

At half past five she was in the courtyard, checking lanterns, when Corporal Ives came at a trot with the morning dispatch satchel and the particular stiffness of a soldier carrying bad news wrapped in routine. Vance broke the seal on the topmost letter, read it once, and felt the cold of the courtyard move inside her coat.

It was from the Ministry of Completion — that elegant name, that masterpiece of euphemism, the office that existed to finish what the purge had started. The letter thanked her, in language so polished it squeaked, for her diligence in the matter of the Aurelian materials. It noted, with the delicacy of a stiletto entering between ribs, that upon recovery, the atlas was to be conveyed directly to the Ministry's own assessors. Unopened. Unexamined by garrison staff. For reasons of security.

Which meant: do not read it. Which meant: we know what is in it, and we do not want *you* knowing what is in it, and your obedience in this matter will be noted in your file beside your name.

She had served the Regiment for twenty-six years. She knew precisely what it meant when an institution stopped trusting you with information and started trusting you only with instructions. It meant you had become a tool, and tools are maintained, and maintained tools are used, and used tools are eventually — when the edge wears — replaced. Or melted down. The metallurgy of careers.

"You're frowning, Colonel," said Captain Dree, materializing beside her with the two squad rosters. Dree was competent and humorless and entirely trustworthy, which was to say he had never once surprised her, and she sometimes wondered whether that was a virtue in him or a failure of imagination they shared.

"The Ministry wants the atlas sent to them unopened," she said. "Directly."

Dree considered this with the expression of a man watching weather he has already dressed for. "Then we'll box it."

"We'll box it," Vance agreed.

But something in her had begun, quietly, to count. If the Ministry did not want her to see the atlas, then the atlas contained something the Ministry feared she might understand. And if the Ministry feared her understanding, then her understanding was dangerous to them, and dangerous things, in her experience, were usually true.

She had spent thirty years — no, twenty-six; she corrected herself with a small internal wince, thirty years was the length of the purge itself, and she had not earned that number, she had inherited it — she had spent twenty-six years believing that the Regiment's cruelties were the load-bearing cruelties, the ones that held up the peace. Everyone who served long enough arrived at some accommodation with that idea or broke against it. Her accommodation had been tidy: the Aurelians had been a fire risk; fires require burning breaks; she had helped cut the breaks. She had even, in her third year, drawn up the disposition lists for two villages whose names she had since allowed to blur — not forgotten, never that, but filed in the drawer of the mind where soldiers keep the arithmetic of survival.

*Two villages.* She could not recall their names. That was the accommodation working as designed.

"Colonel?" Dree said.

"It's nothing. Form up."

* * *

The column moved at six-twelve. Two minutes early, because Vance had ordered it so — better to arrive ahead of the light than to be caught mid-stride by it, visible, legible, a story waiting to be told by whoever watched from upper windows. The streets of the mapmakers' quarter were still blue-dark, the shop signs hanging motionless, frost smoking faintly off the rooftops where the last night's warmth escaped. Their boots sounded disciplined and wrong against the silence, forty men marching on a room full of paper.

Vance rode at the head. She always rode at the head; it was vanity dressed as leadership, and she permitted herself very few vanities, so she wore this one openly.

They turned onto Inkwell Row at six-twenty. The Fen workshop stood at the end of it, and even in the dark she knew its silhouette — the broad low roof, the tall north window built to catch an even light, the chimney breathing its thin thread of smoke into the grey.

The chimney was smoking.

She reined in. Behind her the column halted with a soft clatter of harness, forty breaths steaming in the cold.

Smoke at this hour meant a lit stove, which meant someone awake, which meant — what? An apprentice keeping vigil over the deadline? Packing? Fleeing? Or simply the ordinary courage of people who refuse to rearrange their lives around the arrival of soldiers?

And yet. She sat in her saddle and looked at that thin grey ribbon rising straight in the windless air, and she found she could not make her hand rise to give the order.

Not could not. Would not, she corrected, with the precision she applied to everything. There was no paralysis in her. There was only a question she had not asked aloud in twenty-six years, asking itself now at the worst possible moment, in the voice of a woman much younger than she was, standing in a village square with a disposition list in her glove:

*What if we are the storm?*

She had answered it then, the way everyone answers it: with procedure. Procedure was the Regiment's great gift to its servants — it let you do terrible things one step at a time, each step defensible, none of them yours. You did not burn villages; you executed directives. You did not erase children; you processed incomplete records. And you did not seize a dead man's life's work while his grieving apprentice watched — you recovered imperial property pursuant to writ.

But procedure required her to begin, and her hand stayed on the reins, and the smoke rose, and somewhere behind her Dree shifted his weight with the patience of a man who assumed his colonel was composing herself rather than failing to compose anything at all.

An hour, she decided. She did not say it aloud, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull where she kept the other unfiled documents. She framed it as tactics. A dawn seizure announced by marching boots gave the occupants every advantage of preparation; better to wait, observe the chimney, confirm the premises' activity, send Ives around to the rear alley to verify the exits. Sound. Methodical. Defensible.

All true. None of it the truth. The truth was simpler and more shameful: the workshop's chimney was smoking, and she wanted, just once, just for an hour, to know what it was like to be the sort of person a lit stove belonged to — someone whose morning began with warmth instead of warrants.

She dismounted. "Hold positions out of sightline," she told Dree. "We wait."

"For how long, sir?"

"I'll say when."

He blinked — the closest thing to surprise he had produced in six years of service — and obeyed. Vance walked forward alone up Inkwell Row, boots ringing on the frozen cobbles, until she stood beneath the workshop's north window, close enough to see the frost melting off the sill in a dark crescent where warmth leaked through.

Through the gap below the shutter, lamplight. And within the lamplight, a shadow moving steadily back and forth, back and forth — the rhythm of someone bent over a table, working. Working, at dawn, on the last morning of the fortnight, as though the deadline were a horizon to sail toward rather than a wall to be shot against.

And another shadow, smaller, quick, darting across the light and back again. A child's shadow. Chasing the first one, laughing — she could hear it, faintly, muffled through glass and wood: a small bright laugh, absurd, indecent in its innocence, the laugh of a boy who did not know that forty soldiers stood in the street outside learning the shape of his happiness.

Vance stood in the cold a long time.

Twenty-six years ago, in a village whose name she had filed away, there had been a child's shoe in a doorway. She had stepped over it. That was all; that was the whole event; history is mostly stepping. She had stepped over a small brown shoe and gone on with the directive, and the directive had gone on with her, and both of them had advanced together ever since.

She looked up at the warm window, and the small laughing shadow crossed it again, and Hesper Vance — Colonel of the Ninth, servant of the Ministry of Completion, author of the seizure order folded in her breast pocket — discovered that she could not remember why the shoe had mattered less than the list.

"One hour," she said aloud, to no one, to the smoke, to whatever was left in her that could still hear itself.

Behind her, out of sightline, forty soldiers waited in the frost. Above her, the lamplight burned. And inside the workshop, unaware, the last hour of the fortnight ticked away over a nearly finished atlas and a child who believed, for a little longer, that dawn was only dawn.
