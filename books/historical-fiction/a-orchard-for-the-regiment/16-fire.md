## 16. Fire

The smoke came before the soldiers.

Rosalind smelled it first as a thread of sweetness on the night air — not woodsmoke, not lamp-oil, but something richer, tarry, deliberate. Pitch. They had brought pitch. You did not bring pitch to a search; you brought pitch to an ending.

They were standing in the orchard tunnels beneath the workshop, Rosalind with a shuttered lantern in one hand and the last of the plates cradled against their chest, Pip pressed close at their side. Above them, through the hatch they had left ajar for exactly one hour — the hour it took to carry everything down — the workshop of Master Aldous Fen was breathing its last.

"Wait," Pip whispered. His small hand found Rosalind's sleeve. "Wait, I want to see."

"You don't want to see."

"I do." His voice had that new hardness in it, the one that had arrived with the soldiers' boots two nights past, the one that made Rosalind's chest ache worse than any grief. "It's ours."

*It was ours,* Rosalind thought. And that was the whole of it, wasn't it — the whole terrible arithmetic of the last fortnight compressed into two small words.

They climbed the ladder together and stood in the dark of the drying room, among the racks where maps had hung for twenty years like laundry, like flags, like the shed skins of a world that had once believed itself knowable. Through the shutters, orange light was already climbing the walls of the courtyard. The Regiment had not bothered with subtlety. Perhaps subtlety was a luxury of the earlier stages of erasure; perhaps, at the end, they always came with fire, because fire was the one instrument that left no map behind.

"They'll have the street watched," Rosalind murmured, mostly to themselves.

"I know," Pip said. "You said. Twice."

Rosalind almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was a sound like a hinge that had not been oiled in years.

* * *

It had begun, as these things did, with a courtesy.

The knock had come at dusk — three measured raps, the knock of a man who expected doors to open for him. Rosalind had been at the bench, burnishing the final plate of the eastern coast, and for one absurd moment they had thought: *a customer.* As though customers still existed. As though the shop had not been a tomb in slow motion for weeks, its windows shuttered, its sign taken down, its only visitor a boy who was legally, officially, a blank in the Regiment's ledger — the one entry they had failed to strike.

Through the door: "Guild business. Open in the name of the Surveyor-General."

Rosalind had blown out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to their own heart behave with more dignity than they felt. The knock came again. Then voices — two, three, low and unhurried — and the creak of a man's weight settling against the doorframe, patient as weather.

They did not open it. The man had not expected them to. After a while the voices moved off, and Rosalind understood that the knocking had never been a request. It had been a rehearsal. A courtesy rendered to the form of things, so that when the form was dispensed with, no one could say the Regiment had not first asked nicely.

They had worked through that night anyway. That was the thing about terror, Rosalind was learning; it did not stop the hands. The hands had been trained by twenty years of dead men's discipline — Fen's discipline, and Fen's master's before him — and the hands did not care that the world was ending. The hands wanted the coastlines finished. The hands wanted the inks ground and the plates wiped clean and stacked in the order Fen had decreed a decade ago, an order Rosalind had once thought fussy and now understood as scripture: *the north first, because the north was burned first.*

Pip had slept under the bench with his thumb in his mouth, a habit he had sworn he'd outgrown, and Rosalind had worked with the lamp shaded and the boy's breathing keeping time like a metronome, and had thought, not for the first time: *this is what he died for. Not the atlas. This. The sound of a child sleeping in a room full of true maps.*

At dawn they had begun carrying everything down.

* * *

The hatch beneath the drying racks had been Fen's secret, though he had never once called it that. He had called it *the cellar*, in the offhand voice he used for things he meant to be underestimated, and for twenty years Rosalind had gone down its ladder to fetch vinegar and stored paper without once wondering why a cellar needed an iron-bound door at its far end, or why that door's lock was older and finer than any lock in the workshop above.

The tunnels ran under the whole of the old city — under the guildhall, under the Regiment barracks themselves, if half the rumors were true — back to the days when Vellamere had been an orchard town, when the fruit had been hauled to the river in carts that traveled underground to escape the tax collectors. Fen's survey marks were cut into the brick at every junction: a small compass rose, a number, an arrow. Rosalind had spent two nights copying them into memory, tracing them with fingers that shook, and had understood at last what the old man had been doing on all those unexplained absences in his final year. He had not been senile. He had been *surveying.* He had been leaving a map for whoever inherited — and Rosalind, without ever being told, had inherited.

Now they stood in the drying room with the last load: the plates, wrapped in felt; the folio with the defaced portrait and Fen's correspondence; the blank final page, which was not blank at all anymore but which Rosalind still thought of that way, the way you keep calling a house by the family that built it.

Below, in the tunnel's mouth, everything else waited in the lantern light. Twenty years of work. Every chart Fen had ever cut, every proof, every corrected state, the great master folio itself bound in its oiled canvas. It had taken three nights to move it all. Rosalind's shoulders would carry the bruise of it for a month, and they found they did not mind. You ought to be bruised, they thought, by what you save.

"Rosalind." Pip tugged the sleeve again. "The light's moving."

It was. The orange glow through the shutters had begun to flicker, to dance, to take on the restless stutter of flame rather than lamplight. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came voices — many voices, boots, the clang of something metal set down on cobbles. The rehearsal was over.

"Down," Rosalind said. "Now."

* * *

They did not go down all the way. That was the choice Rosalind made in the last minute, and they would carry it the rest of their life, and they would make it again, they knew, every time they were asked by whatever tribunal of memory convened in the small hours: *you could have gone down, you could have been safe in the dark with your plates, and instead you stood in the drying room and watched.*

Because someone should watch. That was the whole of the argument, and it was not a reasonable argument, and Rosalind made it anyway. Someone should stand witness to what the Regiment did, so that it could never be said afterward that the burning had happened in the dark, unwitnessed, unweighed. Fen had spent his life making the world visible. The least his apprentice could do was look.

Through the gap in the shutters, Rosalind saw them come into the courtyard: six soldiers and a fire officer in Regiment grey, moving with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times in many cities. They did not smash the door. They did not need to. One of them produced a key — *a key,* Rosalind thought, and something cold turned over in their chest, because a key meant a lock meant someone inside the guild had provided it, meant the informant's information had traveled all the way to a locksmith's bench — and the workshop of Aldous Fen stood open to the night for the first time in a month.

They went in with lanterns. Rosalind counted them by their lights, the way Fen had taught them to count everything: *measure, measure, measure.* Five lights moving through the front room. Then the light of the fire officer's torch, unlit, carried like a scepter.

"They're taking things out," Pip whispered. He had found a knothole in the shutter, the one he used for watching the milk-cart, and his eye was pressed to it. "The big frame. And the— the chest with the brass corners. Why are they taking things out if they're going to—"

"Because the theft comes first," Rosalind said quietly. "The burning is only to hide the theft. That's how it's done, Pip. That's how it's *always* done. First they take the true thing, then they burn the place so no one can prove what was taken." They heard their own voice and were startled by it: level, instructive. Fen's voice. Grief had apparently decided, at the last, to put on the master's coat.

The soldiers emerged with the good instruments — the brass theodolite, the framed engraving of the city that had hung over Fen's bench, the chest of rare inks. Loot for someone's study, trophies for someone's wall. Then the fire officer raised his torch, and Rosalind watched the small black bud of flame at its tip with the strange calm of a person watching a wave they have already decided not to outrun.

"Rosalind." Pip's hand found theirs. His fingers were very small and very warm and they gripped with a strength that seemed impossible for their size. "Don't watch. Please don't watch."

"You don't have to."

"I'm watching *you,*" he said, with the terrible precision of children. "Not it. You."

The torch went through the doorway. For a moment — two, three — nothing. The workshop held its breath. And then the pitch caught, and the night turned orange, and twenty years of Aldous Fen's life began to roar.

* * *

Rosalind had imagined, in the fortnight of hiding, in the nights of carrying plates down a ladder while their back screamed, that this moment would feel like dying. They had rehearsed it: the loss like a blade, the grief like a flood. They had braced for the wave.

It was not a wave. It was a *release.*

That was the word that came, unbidden, unforgivable, as the light through the shutters went from orange to white and the heat pressed against the boards and the smell of burning paper — burning *maps* — filled the drying room. Release. Like a knot cut. Like a fever breaking. Because the workshop had been, for a month now, a tomb and a trap both; because every hour in it had been an hour of listening for boots; because the atlas, the true atlas, was already safe below in the dark, wrapped in felt, breathing its paper-breath in the cold of the tunnel. The Regiment was burning a shell. They were burning the room where the truth had been made, and mistaking the room for the truth, and there was a fierce, terrible comedy in that which Rosalind knew they would laugh at later, alone, and probably weep while laughing.

Fen would have understood. Fen, who had once watched a flood take his first studio in the river district and had stood on the far bank making notes on the water's behavior. *The work is not the paper,* he had said, wringing out his sleeves. *The paper is only where the work sleeps. The work is in the hands. The work is in the head. Burn every map in Vellamere and the coastlines will still be true.*

"Come on," Rosalind said, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it crack, because the boy was watching *them,* not it, and he deserved to see that his guardian was made of something that could break and still stand.

They went down the ladder together, Pip first because he was quick and Rosalind's arms were full, and Rosalind pulled the hatch shut above them and shot the bolt — Fen's bolt, Fen's iron, good for perhaps a quarter hour against a crowbar, which would be a quarter hour more than the fire would need to explain everything.

* * *

The tunnel took them in with its cold and its dark, and Rosalind stood a moment at the ladder's foot with the lantern shuttered to a thread, listening. Above: the roar, muffled now to a heartbeat. Around: the drip of water, the smell of old apples and older stone, the vast patient silence of a city's buried bones.

Pip's hand slid into theirs.

"It's loud," he said. "Up there."

"Yes."

"Is all of it burning? The drawing table? The stool with the wobbly leg?"

"All of it."

"The window where the cat sits?"

Rosalind's throat closed. The cat — a half-wild grey thing that had appeared that winter and been fed in secret against all of Rosalind's stated principles — had not been seen in three days. Cats, at least, knew when to leave.

"All of it," they managed. "Every wobbly-legged stool and every window. That's what fire does, Pip. It doesn't choose."

He was quiet a moment. In the thread of lantern light Rosalind saw him working his jaw the way he did when a question was too large for his mouth and he was trying to fit it anyway.

"Did the maps burn?" he asked finally. "The true ones?"

"No." Rosalind lifted the lantern a fraction, and the light found the stacks: folio, plates, proofs, the whole sleeping archive of twenty years, packed in felt like a cargo of eggs. "The true ones are here. Every one."

He looked at them for a long moment — that face, that impossible face, the face of a line the Regiment had spent thirty years striking from every portrait and parish record and stone inscription in the empire, looking up at Rosalind out of the dark with Fen's own expression, the expression of a man checking a measurement twice.

"Then they burned nothing," Pip said.

Rosalind stared at him. Six years old. Seven, perhaps; no one knew. A child who had walked out of nowhere with a note in his cloak reading *forget the face,* and who had just, standing in a tunnel under a burning building, said the truest sentence Rosalind had heard in thirty years.

"No," Rosalind said, and knelt, and pulled him in, and held him against the felt-wrapped plates and the cold tunnel air and the muffled roar of everything they had lost. "They burned nothing at all. Remember that. Whatever they take from us after this — remember that they burned *nothing.*"

He held on. He was good at holding on; he had been practicing since the night he arrived on the step. And when they finally separated, Rosalind wiped their face with a sleeve — no point in dignity, there was no one down there but the dead master's maps, and Fen had seen them cry before — and took up the lantern and the heaviest of the bundles.

"North first," they said. "Because the north was burned first. Fen's rule. We follow the compass roses to the river gate, and at the river gate there will be a boat, because a man who surveys tunnels for a year does not leave the last door unaccounted for." They heard the faith in their own voice and marveled at it. Faith had seemed like one more thing the Regiment had burned. Apparently it had been in the tunnel all along, waiting in the dark with everything else they'd saved.

Pip picked up a bundle half his size and staggered gamely under it.

"Which way is north?" he asked.

Rosalind raised the lantern, and the light found it on the brick ahead: a small compass rose cut into the mortar, Fen's chisel-marks crisp as the day he'd made them, its arrow pointing true.

"That way," Rosalind said.

Above them, the workshop collapsed inward with a sound like the city clearing its throat, and ash began to sift down through the old brick seams, fine and grey, settling on their hair and shoulders as they walked — the dust of twenty years, the dust of the only home either of them had, falling gently through the dark like the first snow of a world that had not yet been mapped.

They went north. The compass rose said so. And somewhere ahead, past the junctions and the old fruit-carts' ruts and the roots of an orchard that had been paved over before either of them was born, the river waited — and on the river's far bank, though neither of them could know it yet, a printing press was already being carried down to a cellar by hands that had decided, tonight of all nights, which side of the fire they stood on.
