## 17. Under the Roots

*Rosalind*

The dark down here was not the dark of night. Night had edges — a window's grey, a lamp's halo, the shape of a sleeping child in a chair. This dark was total, a darkness that pressed on the eyes like wool, and Rosalind learned quickly that it could not be fought. It had to be negotiated with.

One hand flat against the tunnel wall — brick older than the Regiment, older than the guilds, brick laid by hands that had grown the apples that gave this city its name before anyone thought to pave an orchard and call it progress. One hand gripping Pip's small, cold fingers. One satchel of copper plates riding their hip like a second spine. And somewhere behind them, muffled by forty feet of earth and the collapse they'd heard but not seen, the workshop burning.

They did not think about it. They made a discipline of not thinking about it, the way Master Fen had made a discipline of sharpening his own pencils: same hour, same motion, every day, until the act became a wall the mind could lean against.

*Left at the third junction,* Fen's notes had said. *Follow the marks.*

But Fen's notes were written for Fen, a man who had walked these tunnels by lantern-light with a surveyor's chain and a boy's appetite for secrets, thirty years ago, when the marks were fresh. The marks now were thirty years stale, and the water table had risen, and the city above had driven pilings through half the old routes, and Rosalind was not a surveyor. Rosalind was a mapmaker's apprentice who had spent seven years learning to render the world accurately and had never once needed to trust a map with their life.

"Rosalind." Pip's voice came out very small in all that blackness. "I can't see you."

"I'm here." They squeezed his hand. "I'm always going to be here. Say it back."

"I can't see you, but you're here."

"That's it. That's the whole trick of it."

They stood a moment in the wet breathing silence of the tunnel, listening to water move somewhere below them — the river, or a cousin of the river, seeping through the old foundations. The air tasted of stone and rot-sweetness, apples gone to cider gone to vinegar gone to nothing, a ghost of orchard so faint it might have been memory.

Then Rosalind struck flint to the stub of candle they'd carried from the workshop — the last thing taken from the last shelf, chosen without thinking, and now they understood why some part of them had grabbed it. Light bloomed, guttered, held. A circle of yellow perhaps four feet wide, and beyond it, the dark resumed its patient work.

On the wall inside that circle: a mark.

Not much of one. A chiseled arrow no longer than a thumb, its point softened by decades of damp, and beneath it three short scratches, the middle one crossed. Rosalind knew the system without knowing they knew it — had catalogued it once, years ago, among Fen's field notebooks, a cipher he'd used for private surveys he never entered into any guild ledger. Three scratches, middle crossed: *safe route, low water.* An uncrossed set would mean flood risk. A circle meant dead end.

Fen had walked here. Fen, young and alive and already keeping secrets from the Regiment, had stood in this exact blackness with his lantern and his chain and had cut this arrow into the brick so that someday — and Rosalind felt the full weight of that word land like a hand on the shoulder — *someday*, someone would need to follow it home.

"He left us a trail," Rosalind said aloud, and was surprised by how their voice shook.

Pip leaned close to look. His face in the candlelight was smudged with soot, the cut above his brow scabbed over, his eyes enormous. He studied the arrow with the ferocious concentration he brought to everything, as if the world were a problem that could be solved if only you looked hard enough.

"It's pointing wrong," Pip said.

"What?"

"The arrow. It says left. But the water sound is coming from the left too, and you said—" his voice dropped, reciting Rosalind's own words back to them with a child's merciless accuracy— "you said we shouldn't walk toward water we can hear, because the river gate has stairs going *down* to it, and if the water's rising we'd get stuck."

Rosalind stared at him. Then at the arrow. Then at the tunnel ahead, where the darkness swallowed everything past the reach of their little flame.

The child was right. Or Fen was right. And both things could not be true.

Unless—

"Unless there are two ways," Rosalind murmured. "A dry way and a wet way. He'd have marked both." They raised the candle higher, turning slowly, and there — high on the wall, near the vault of the ceiling, nearly erased by a crack running through the brick — a second arrow, smaller, angled up and to the right. Beneath it: two scratches, uncrossed. *Safe route. High ground.*

"Oh," Rosalind said, and something loosened in their chest that had been tightening since the first smoke. "Oh, you clever, stubborn old man."

"Pip was right," Pip said.

"Pip was right." Rosalind crouched to his level. In the candlelight their own shadow loomed huge on the far wall, and Pip's shadow stood inside it like a smaller figure sheltering in a doorway. "Listen to me. Down there, I'm the mapmaker and you're the apprentice. But in these tunnels, tonight, you're the one who remembered what I said when I was too frightened to remember it myself. So we're partners. Both directions. Understood?"

Pip nodded solemnly, and then ruined it entirely by yawning, jaw cracking, eyes watering — a child of six or seven who had watched his only known world burn an hour ago and had been walking ever since, because nobody had told him he was allowed to stop.

"We'll rest soon," Rosalind said. "At the next junction. We'll eat, and sleep in shifts, and I'll keep watch."

"I'm not tired."

"You are enormously tired."

"I'm *not*—"

Another yawn swallowed the protest whole. Pip glared at Rosalind with watery indignation, and Rosalind laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and disbelieving sound that echoed down the tunnel and came back changed — because grief and terror and exhaustion had apparently boiled themselves down to this: a soot-faced orphan insisting he wasn't tired while his body betrayed him at volume.

They turned up the high passage, following the second arrow, and the dark closed behind them like water.

* * *

*Pip*

The dark was a big animal. Pip had decided this about an hour ago and saw no reason to change his mind. It breathed. You could hear it breathing, slow and wet, in all the side passages where the candle didn't reach. Sometimes it moved — a drip, a settling of stones, a sound like something shifting its weight — and Rosalind's hand would tighten around his and they'd both stop, standing still as painted people, until the animal settled again.

Pip wasn't scared. Well. He was scared, but it was a useful scared, like the scared he imagined soldiers had to have, the kind that made your ears work better. Rosalind was a different kind of scared, the bad kind, the kind that made you forget things. So somebody had to keep the remembering, and Pip had decided it would be him.

He kept the list in his head, walking:

One. Follow the arrows. Two. Don't drink the water that tastes like pennies. Three. If Rosalind goes quiet for too long, say something, anything, even just counting, because quiet-Rosalind was worse than scared-Rosalind. Four. The satchel mattered more than either of them, which Pip understood perfectly well even though nobody said it out loud. The satchel was why the fire happened. The satchel was what the soldiers wanted. The satchel was heavy and clanked and Rosalind carried it anyway, every step, and never once suggested leaving it, and Pip loved them for that in a way he didn't have words for yet.

Five. Master Fen was still helping them.

This was Pip's own discovery and his favorite item on the list. The arrows were Master Fen's handwriting — well, his *chisel*-writing, but Pip had seen the man's actual handwriting enough times, looped and slanted across the margins of maps, and the arrows had the same feel, the same tilt, like the letters were leaning forward to hurry you along. Which meant Master Fen had walked down here before he died, cutting messages into walls for people he hadn't met yet. People like Pip.

*He knew I was coming,* Pip thought, and the thought was warm, like holding your hands near a stove. Maybe not *him* specifically. But somebody. Somebody small and hunted and needing a way out. Master Fen had spent his last years carving exits into the dark for a stranger, and Pip had decided that when they got out — when, not if; Rosalind always said *when* — he would learn to do that too. Cut arrows. Leave trails. Be the person whose handwriting helped strangers a hundred years from now.

"Junction ahead," Rosalind whispered.

It didn't look like anything to Pip — just more dark — but the candle showed a widening of the passage, a pillar of brick in the middle where two tunnels split like the fork of a tree root, and Rosalind called a halt the way they'd promised.

They ate hard bread and a wedge of cheese gone sweaty in its cloth, sitting with their backs against the pillar. Pip saved half his bread. Rosalind pretended not to notice him hiding it in his pocket. The candle burned between them, and its light made a little room in the dark, a room with exactly two people in it, and Pip found he liked the room.

"Tell me about the river gate," Pip said, because item six on his list was *know things before you need them.*

Rosalind tore a piece of bread and didn't eat it, just held it. "It's an old lock on the north wall. From when the city traded by water. There's a stair down from these tunnels — Fen marked it — and a gate at the bottom that opens onto the river. It's been sealed since the Regiment took the customs houses. Officially."

"But?"

"But Fen's notes say otherwise, and Fen's notes have been right all night." Rosalind finally ate the bread, chewing slowly. "There'll be a bar, probably rusted into its brackets. We may have to break it, and I don't know how we break iron with what we're carrying. And there may be soldiers. The Regiment isn't stupid; they know the old tunnels exist. They just haven't cared enough to search them properly. Yet."

Pip considered this. "You're doing the forgetting thing."

"What forgetting thing?"

"When you talk about the bad parts too fast, like they're just words. Your master died today. Our house burned down. Soldiers want to catch us. And you said all that like you were reading a shopping list."

Rosalind went very still. In the candlelight Pip could see something move across their face, quick as a fish under ice — hurt, maybe, or surprise, or the particular naked feeling of being seen by someone small.

"You're allowed to cry," Pip added helpfully. "I cried earlier. When the roof fell. Nobody saw, so you can't tell anyone, but it helped a lot."

And Rosalind — careful, measured Rosalind, who measured coastlines and ink ratios and the exact angle of every letter they drew — put their face in their hands and cried. Not loudly. Quietly, the way rain starts, a few drops and then more, shoulders shaking, the candle flame trembling in the small wind of their breath. Pip sat next to them and did the only correct thing, which was to lean his whole body against their arm and stay there, solid as a doorstop, until it passed.

It passed. Rain always does. Rosalind wiped their face on their sleeve, blew their nose with tremendous dignity, and said, "That was item four, I suppose. On your list."

"You have a list too?"

"Everyone has a list. Mine just doesn't work as well as yours." Rosalind looked at him then, really looked, and whatever they saw made them smile — tired and crooked and real. "Master Fen used to say a map is just a promise that the world makes sense if you measure it honestly. I've been measuring everything except the true things. You keep me honest, Pip. Do you understand? Out of everyone in this city, the Regiment sent their hunters after the wrong person's apprentice. You're the best instrument I've ever worked with."

Pip didn't entirely follow, but he understood the important part, which was *you matter, keep going.* He nodded and finished his cheese and let Rosalind wrap him in the soldier's cloak he'd arrived in — the only thing left of his old life besides the note that said *forget the face* — and slept, for a while, in the little room the candle made, with the dark animal breathing all around and Rosalind's hand resting on his head like a paperweight keeping him from blowing away.

* * *

*Rosalind*

Pip slept. Rosalind kept watch and lost the argument with despair.

It came the way it always came, in the hours when the mind had nothing to measure: in fragments. The sound of the roof beam going. The orange bloom through the hatch. Twenty years of Fen's life — twenty years of *their* life, apprenticeship folded into devotion folded into love they had never named aloud — curling into ash above them while they ran. The atlas plates were safe in the satchel. The atlas itself, the bound vellum, the great gathered body of it — had they brought it? Yes. Yes, they had, they'd wrapped it in oilcloth and strapped it inside the satchel between the plates, they remembered the weight of it, they had *both*. Everything that mattered was here in the dark with them.

Everything else was smoke.

And that was the arithmetic that broke the heart open: everything else was smoke, and the everything-else was nearly everything. The grinding slab worn into a hollow by Fen's elbow. The drying racks that smelled of linseed and dust. The drawer full of failed compass roses, each one drawn by a younger Rosalind, each one slightly crooked, which Fen had kept — kept, all those years, never thrown away, and Rosalind had never asked why and now never could. The chair by the south window where the old man sat every evening at dusk and said nothing, absolutely nothing, watching the light go, and Rosalind had learned to sit in the other chair and say nothing too, and that shared silence had been the truest conversation of their life.

Gone. All of it. And the Regiment would call it an accident in their reports, the same word they'd used for Fen's death, *misfortune*, and there was no one left to write the truth except a fugitive with a candle and a sleeping child and a satchel full of proof.

Rosalind looked at the candle. Half gone. They had perhaps two hours of light left, and no way to make more down here, and the river gate was — how far? Fen's notes gave distances in paces, but paces assumed a man's stride and a straight line and neither applied. They could be an hour away. They could be five. They could be flooded.

The despair said: *sit down. Sleep. Let the dark have you. You were always going to lose; the Regiment always wins; thirty years of erased names prove it. What is one more name?*

The despair had a reasonable voice. That was the horror of it. It sounded almost like sense.

And then Pip stirred in his sleep, and mumbled something — a word, half a word, "*...left...at the third...*" — following the arrows even in dreams, and Rosalind felt the despair recede like a tide pulled back by something too stubborn to drown.

Because that was the answer. Not courage. Not hope, that expensive word. Just this: a six-year-old had watched his world burn and had responded by memorizing the escape plan. The Regiment had thirty years, an empire, fire, and iron, and their counterweight was a child who kept lists and a grieving apprentice who had no business being brave and was going to be brave anyway, because someone small was depending on it.

Rosalind stood. Took up the candle. Looked at the two arrows on the junction pillar — the low road and the high road, the wet way and the dry — and chose the high one, and woke Pip gently, and said, "Up, partner. We've got a river to argue with."

* * *

*Pip*

The stairs were the worst part.

They went down forever, stone steps worn into shallow bowls by feet a hundred years dead, and at the bottom the air changed — colder, moving, tasting of actual river now instead of river's ghost — and the candle flame stretched sideways like it wanted to run. Pip counted steps to keep the scared useful. Forty-one. Forty-two. His legs ached. Rosalind's breathing had gone ragged above him, and twice they'd stopped to press a hand against the wall and stand very still, and Pip had pretended not to notice, because pretending-not-to-notice was also on the list.

Forty-nine. Fifty. And then the stairs ended and the candlelight touched iron.

The gate was bigger than a door, taller than Rosalind with their arm stretched up, a grid of bars green-black with age, and beyond it — visible through the gaps, silver-grey in the pre-dawn — the river. Moving. Alive. The first living thing Pip had seen in hours that wasn't himself or Rosalind, and he pressed his face to the bars and breathed it in, mud and cold and distance, and felt his heart bang against his ribs like it was trying to get out and swim.

"Is it locked?" he whispered.

Rosalind ran their hands along the frame, testing, and Pip heard metal shift — a groan, a grind, and then, impossibly, a sliding scrape, as a crossbar older than the Regiment gave up its grip and moved an inch. Then another inch.

"Fen," Rosalind breathed. "He oiled it. He *oiled the bar.* Thirty years ago or thirty days ago, I don't care — it moves, Pip. It moves!"

They wrestled it together, Rosalind's arms shaking, Pip pushing with his whole small weight, and the bar slid free with a sound like a bone setting, and the gate swung inward on a whisper of hinges, and there it was: the river, wide and slow and grey-gold with the first light coming up behind the eastern hills, and tied at a rotted jetty fifty yards downstream, a flat-bottomed wherry with its oars shipped and a lantern burning at its bow.

A lantern. Lit. At dawn. For no reason the river usually required.

Rosalind stopped. Pip felt them stop, felt the sudden stillness, the fear arriving before the thought did. A lit lantern could be rescue. A lit lantern could be a trap. The Regiment knew the tunnels existed; the Regiment had burned the workshop; the Regiment could be standing in the dark of that boat right now, patient as spiders, waiting for two fugitives to walk down the jetty into their hands.

"Rosalind?" Pip whispered. "What do we do?"

Rosalind didn't answer. Because at that moment the lantern lifted — a hand raising it, a figure stepping to the wherry's rail, a shape resolving out of the grey — broad-shouldered, aproned, ink-stained to the elbows, and even at fifty yards Pip recognized the stoop of him, the neighbor from the print shop, the man who brought them misprinted posters for the backs of drawings and complained about the price of rags.

The printer cupped his hands around his mouth, and his voice came across the water, low and rough and carrying like a shout kept carefully quiet:

"They told me to report a child. I'm reporting a boat instead. Get down here — both of you — before the patrol rounds the mill, we've got minutes, maybe less —"

He paused, and the lantern light caught his face, and he added, softer, almost gentle across all that water:

"Aldous would have come to my door. Let me come to yours."

Pip looked up at Rosalind. Rosalind looked down at Pip. Somewhere behind them, back in the dark of the tunnels, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on stone — many boots, descending the stairs they had just climbed, closing the way they had come.

There was nowhere to go but the water, and the water had a friend in it.

Rosalind took Pip's hand. "Hold your breath, partner," they said. "We're going to make a splash."
