## 19. Vance's Choice

The order arrived at the fourth hour of the morning, carried by a lieutenant whose gloves were too new and whose salute was too crisp, as though he had practiced it in a mirror on the way over. Colonel Hesper Vance read it standing at her window, where the light came grey and thin through the frost-ferned glass, and she read it twice, because the first reading had seemed impossible and the second merely confirmed that the world had not changed — only her place in it had.

*By authority of the Provisional Command of Vellamere: Colonel Hesper Vance is hereby detached from garrison administration and assigned to the recovery of the fugitive atlas and apprehension of its custodian. She will report directly to General Marrow. Failure to recover the material within one fortnight will be considered dereliction.*

One fortnight. The same span they had given Rosalind Zhou to surrender it. The Regiment loved its fortnights the way a miser loved coins — counted them, hoarded them, spent them only when something was about to be taken.

Vance set the paper down on her desk beside the inkwell, beside the small brass compass she kept there for no reason she had ever admitted aloud, and looked at it for a long time. Outside, the parade ground lay white with rime, and the morning drill was beginning without her, five hundred boots striking frost in imperfect unison, a sound like slow applause for no one.

Twenty years ago she would have felt nothing but the clean cold pleasure of purpose. Twenty years ago she had stood in a village called Sere Hollow with a list of names in her hand and watched men who were very good at their work make sure none of those names remained anywhere but on paper, and she had told herself — she had been twenty-six, and she had believed it with the whole ferocious sincerity of the young — that she was doing the arithmetic of peace. That some numbers must be subtracted so that the sum could hold. She had signed the confirmation at the bottom of the page, and her signature had been beautiful then, unhesitating, each letter placed like a stone in a wall.

She did not sign things like that anymore. She had noticed this about herself perhaps three years ago, the way one notices a scar has stopped aching: not with relief, exactly, but with a strange bereavement, as though even the pain had abandoned her to her own company.

* * *

The tracking report was waiting on her desk when she returned from the briefing — the real one, the one that mattered, delivered by a man from the Survey Office whose eyes never quite settled on anything, the way water does not settle on glass. He laid out the papers in a fan, and Vance understood immediately what she was looking at, because she had helped design the system herself, years ago, when she still believed systems could be innocent.

The Regiment's couriers left traces. Ink purchases, wax seals, the registration marks pressed into every sheet of official paper by the guild presses — a pattern of pinpricks invisible to the eye, unique to each press, legible to anyone holding the key. And someone, somewhere in the lower city, had begun buying paper in quantities that made no sense for a shop of that size. The report named the printer. Aldric Voss. A shop off Tanner's Row, wedged between a tallow merchant and a house that had been empty since the second year of the purge.

And beneath that, in the surveyor's careful hand, the detail that stopped her breath for half a second — long enough that she was glad no one was watching: *sightings of two figures near the river gate at dusk, an adult and a child, the child wearing the adult's coat.*

An adult and a child.

She thought of the workshop. Of the smell of linseed and lampblack, of the apprentice who had stood too straight and answered too evenly while the boy's small boots dripped meltwater onto the floorboards behind the screen, and how Vance had known — had *known*, with the certainty of thirty years of reading rooms — and had walked out anyway, because walking out had been easier than knowing what to do with knowing.

She had told herself then that it was procedure. That the writ would come, that the machinery would turn, that her part was to be precise and let precision do the cruelty so she would not have to feel it happen. That had been the bargain of her entire life, hadn't it. Not to be cruel. Only to be exact.

Now the machinery wanted her hands on it personally.

* * *

General Marrow received her in the afternoon, in a room so warm it felt like being swallowed. He was a heavy man gone soft at the edges, with the complexion of someone who had not been outdoors in a decade, and he spoke of the fugitives the way other men spoke of weather — a nuisance, a front moving in from the coast, something to be managed before it spoiled the season.

"The girl has the atlas," he said. "Or pieces of it. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that it doesn't circulate." He turned a document toward her with one finger. "You knew Fen. You inspected his workshop. You are the officer most familiar with the material."

"I inspected it under pretense," Vance said. "He was never cooperative. His apprentice is likely worse."

"Then you understand why we want you." Marrow smiled, and it was meant to be warm. "Recovery, Colonel. Intact. The General Staff has a particular interest in the final plate."

"The final plate is blank," Vance said. "I have seen the folio inventory myself. Two hundred and eleven plates, and the last is virgin stock."

Marrow's smile did not waver, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it speaks. "Then it will remain blank," he said, "or whoever filled it will fill a grave alongside it. You have your orders, Colonel. Fortnight. Report directly to me."

She saluted. Her boots struck the floor in the old rhythm. In the corridor outside, the warmth fell away from her like a coat, and she stood a moment in the cold draft from the windows, breathing, letting the cold find the places inside her that the warmth had softened.

*Directly to me.* Which meant that every report she filed passed through one pair of soft, clever hands, and that whatever she wrote would become true simply because Marrow said it was. She had spent her career inside that truth-making machine. She knew its tolerances the way a clockmaker knows a movement — where it ran fast, where it stuck, where a single grain of grit, properly placed, could silence an entire gear.

* * *

That night she sat in her quarters with the tracking report spread before her and a candle burning down between her and the window, and she did not move for a long time. The frost had climbed high on the glass. Beyond it, the lower city lay in its sprawl of dark roofs and thinner dark streets, and somewhere down there — in a cellar or a back room smelling of ink and tallow — a mapmaker's apprentice was teaching a child to hold a pen, or running through the night with plates wrapped in oilcloth, or dead already; she could not know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She thought about what the report required of her. Troop dispositions along Tanner's Row. Seals on the river gate. A cordon drawn tight enough that a woman with a child and a satchel of copper plates could not slip through — unless the cordon was drawn a day late, or a street wide, or in the wrong direction entirely.

Misfile it, said a voice in her that she did not recognize, or rather recognized with horror, because it sounded like her own voice at twenty-six, the one that had once believed arithmetic could be innocent — except reversed now, run backward, subtracting nothing but time. Misfile it. Buy them a week.

She took up her pen. The nib scratched. She wrote the summary in her own hand, the beautiful hand, each letter placed like a stone: *Tracking inconclusive. Press-registration trace suggests activity in the Weavers' District, north quarter. Recommend widened search pattern pending corroboration.*

Every word of it was false. The Weavers' District lay a mile and a half from Tanner's Row, on the wrong side of the canal, and widening the search there would scatter her own patrols thin as smoke. She dated it correctly. She sealed it correctly. She initialed the corner, and her initials looked exactly as they had looked above the confirmation at Sere Hollow, and she understood, sitting alone in the candlelight with frost flowering up the window like white lichen, that a signature does not change. Only the hand that writes it changes, and the weight it carries, and the ground it falls upon.

She misfiled it into the pending tray — the tray where reports went to age like wine until they were either confirmed or forgotten — and she blew out the candle, and in the dark she laughed once, briefly, a sound with no humor in it at all.

*Too late,* she thought. *Thirty years too late. One village, one list, one signature, and everything after was just the echo of it rolling downhill.* She had not saved Fen. She had stood in his workshop and smelled the fear under the linseed and walked out. Whatever this was — this single grain of grit, this week purchased with a lie — it was not redemption. Redemption was a word for people who still had time to earn it. This was only a person, surfacing late, gasping, in water she had helped pour.

But the water was what it was. And she was in it.

* * *

The days that followed had the quality of a held breath. Vance moved through her duties with the flawless surface she had worn for decades — inspections, requisitions, the small theater of command — and beneath the surface she conducted a private campaign of small failures. A courier roster amended so that the Tanner's Row beat went to a corporal who was fond of drink and slow to rise. An intelligence request routed through a clerk notorious for losing things. When Marrow asked for an update on the press-trace, she presented the Weavers' District theory with such thoroughness, such documented diligence, that he nodded and thanked her, and she walked out of his office with her spine straight and her hands cold as coins.

She caught herself, on the third day, standing in the records annex, pulling the old purge files. Sere Hollow. The villages adjacent. The master lists with the names struck through in red — struck through, all of them, and beneath the strikes, in the column marked *disposition of minor dependents*, entries in a younger version of her own handwriting: *transferred to foundling intake.* She had written those words thirty years ago and had never once wondered, until now, what became of the children after intake. Foundling houses. Registries. A boy of six or seven, wrapped in a soldier's cloak, with a face the Regiment had spent a generation trying to erase.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. Someone had written that note. Someone had carried a child across a city in the dark and set him on a mapmaker's step, and that someone had been inside the machine, the way she was inside it, and had chosen — late, badly, with trembling hands — to be a person instead of an instrument.

It occurred to her that she might find out who. It occurred to her, in the same breath, that she did not want to know, because wanting to know was the first step of hunting, and she had decided — had decided in the candlelit dark with a false report drying under her seal — that she was done hunting this particular quarry.

* * *

On the fifth day, the grain of grit worked loose.

A patrol sergeant, conscientious and ambitious, filed an independent sighting: movement near Tanner's Row, two figures, dusk. It landed in the general queue, and by the time it reached the duty desk Vance was already there, and she lifted it from the stack with steady hands and read it twice, and then she did the thing she had spent five days rehearsing in the privacy of her skull.

She attached it to the Weavers' file.

*Corroborates pattern of activity, north quarter,* she wrote in the margin. *Cross-reference pending.*

It was not a lie, exactly. It was worse than a lie — it was truth bent until it pointed the wrong way, which was the Regiment's own native language, spoken fluently, finally, against itself. She initialled it. Filed it. Walked back up the corridor past the maps of Vellamere hanging in their long ranks, the city rendered in the old official colors, orchards green, walls grey, rivers blue, everything tidy, everything wrong.

At the end of the corridor, the window overlooked the river gate. She stopped there, as she sometimes did, and looked down at the small archway and the water sliding past it, brown and patient, going wherever water goes when it leaves a city behind. Somewhere below, sooner or later, a woman with ink-stained fingers would come through that arch with a child and a satchel, and there would be guards at the gate, because there were always guards at the gate, and Vance could not misfile every pair of eyes in Vellamere.

She had bought them a week. Perhaps less now. The sergeant's report would not stay buried forever; ambition was patient, and Marrow's patience was shorter than most.

Vance stood at the window a moment longer, her breath ghosting the cold glass, and made herself look at the thing she had been refusing to look at since the order arrived: that she would be ordered to the gate herself, in the end. That Marrow would want his most familiar officer there when the trap closed, and that she would stand at the river with soldiers at her back and a child in front of her, and that the record — the great ledger she had served all her life, the arithmetic of peace — would demand a final entry in her hand.

Unless, said the voice that sounded like her at twenty-six, run backward — unless when that hour comes, you put down the pen.

Below, the river slid on. In her breast pocket, folded small, was a copy of the tracking report she should have destroyed — the true one, naming Tanner's Row — and she did not yet know why she had kept it, only that some instinct older than her service had told her that a day was coming when the truth would need to change hands quickly, and that she might be the last officer in Vellamere still carrying it.

The summons came at dusk, as she had known it would. *Colonel Vance to report to the river gate at first light. The fugitives have been located.*

She reached for her cloak, and her hand did not shake, and that frightened her more than anything else.
