## 20. The Printing of Truths

The press began its breathing an hour after midnight.

Rosalind had always thought of printing as a loud trade — the slap of type, the groan of the screw, the wet hiss of paper taking ink — but Tam Voss's press breathed rather than bellowed, a long exhalation and a soft percussion, over and over, like a sleeper who could not quite be woken. They stood in the cellar beneath the print shop on Cordwainer Lane with the shutters above battened and the lamps shaded down to coals, and they listened to that breathing, and it sounded to Rosalind like a heart deciding to keep going.

"One more," Tam said, and fed another sheet.

They were printing the atlas.

Not the whole atlas — not yet, not tonight — but the first gathering of plates, folded and nested: the coastlines, the river roads, the old city walls that no longer appeared on any Regiment survey. Fen's lines. Rosalind had cut new blocks from Fen's drawings by lamplight over six nights, carving pearwood until their fingers cracked at the knuckles, because the original copper plates were too heavy to move and too dangerous to copy in metal. Wood would do. Wood had always been the honest material. You could see every stroke of the carver's hand in it, every hesitation, every correction. There was no pretending wood was anything but work.

The sheet came off the press damp and mirror-dark, and Tam lifted it to the lamp with two fingers the way a priest lifts a relic, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"There," Tam said finally. "That's the one."

It was the estuary plate. The Vellamere delta as it had been before the Regiment redrew it — before the villages of the south bank were struck through and renamed, before the ferry crossings were closed, before the whole southern shore became a blankness labeled, in the official surveys, *imperial reserve*. On this sheet the villages had their names again. Marrowfield. Sallow Cross. Little Aurel. Rosalind had carved each letter twice as deep as it needed to be, so that when the Regiment's censors eventually got their hands on a copy — and they would — the names would still be legible beneath whatever blade tried to take them out.

*Let them scrape,* Fen had written once, in the margin of an early proof. *Ink goes deeper than knives.*

"How many can we do before dawn?" Rosalind asked.

Tam did the arithmetic the way printers do it, with a glance at the stack of paper and a shrug that meant *enough*. "Two hundred, if the type holds. Three hundred if I don't sleep and you don't ask me questions." He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with ink permanently mapped into the creases of his hands, and he had spent thirty years printing proclamations for the Regiment — levy notices, execution notices, the tidy announcements by which power introduced itself. His face while he looked at the estuary plate was the face of a man reading his own confession.

"You understand what happens if they find us," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question either. It was the last door left open, held politely ajar.

"I've understood since the night I sent word about the boy." Tam set the sheet carefully among its finished sisters. "I told myself I was buying my family's safety. And then I lay awake listening to my own house settle, and I thought: *this is what safety costs now. This is the price. Other people.*" He wiped his hands on his apron, which only spread the ink further. "My father printed pamphlets against the last king. He used to say a press is just a bell you can ring quietly. Well." He put his palm flat on the wooden frame of the machine. "Ring it, then."

So they rang it.

* * *

The work organized itself the way water finds a channel.

By the second night there were eight of them in the cellar. Tam ran the press. His daughter Maren, seventeen and quicker than anyone Rosalind had ever seen with a folding bone, broke down the sheets and gathered them into quires, her fingers flying through the folds in patterns too fast to follow. Two of Tam's journeymen worked the ink slab, and an old woman named Bettrice — who turned out to have been Fen's color-grinder forty years ago, back when his workshop was a single room above a fishmonger — sat by the lamp examining every finished sheet against the master proofs, holding them up to the light, turning them, hunting flaws.

"This corner's light," she'd say, or "the third line of the legend's doubled," and the sheet would go to the spoil pile, and Rosalind would feel each rejected copy like a small amputation. Two hundred copies. Every one a voice. Let none of them stammer.

Pip was not supposed to be there.

Rosalind had given the instruction with all the gravity available to them — *you stay at the safehouse, you mind Marta, this is not a game* — and Pip had nodded the grave nod of a seven-year-old absorbing orders he fully intended to interpret rather than obey. He arrived on the third night through the coal chute, gray with dust, carrying a satchel, and before Rosalind could speak he opened the satchel and produced, with tremendous ceremony, a loaf of bread, a twist of salt, and three candle stubs.

"For the workers," he said. "Marta says night people eat."

And that was the end of the argument, because Bettrice laughed for the first time in anyone's memory, and Tam said the boy had better earn his bread, and Pip was installed at the far end of the cellar with the task of stacking finished quires — a task he performed with the fierce concentration of a mason building something that would outlast empires. He had stopped flinching at sudden noises. Mostly. When the press exhaled he didn't startle anymore; instead his lips moved slightly, counting, keeping time with the breathing of the machine as though it were a large animal he had made peace with.

At some point past two, Rosalind found him asleep among the quires, curled like a comma, one stack still clutched against his chest as though the papers might wander off without him. Rosalind stood over him a long moment. In the lamplight the Aurelian jaw was less visible than it had been at the beginning — or perhaps Rosalind had simply stopped seeing the bloodline and started seeing the child, which was, they suspected, the entire point of everything.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. As if faces could be forgotten. As if the Regiment hadn't spent thirty years learning that they couldn't.

Rosalind tucked the soldier's cloak — his first cloak, the one he'd arrived in, kept now for no reason except that throwing it away felt like lying — more closely around him, and went back to the press.

* * *

The patrols were the sea they all worked beneath.

They came around on a rhythm Rosalind had learned to read by ear: boots on Cordwainer Lane at roughly the half hour, four men, lanterns swinging, the particular drag in the step of soldiers walking a beat they had walked a hundred times and no longer believed contained anything. They passed the print shop's shuttered front. They paused sometimes outside — a scrape of boot leather, a low exchange — and in those pauses the whole cellar went still as a held breath. Maren froze mid-fold. The journeymen lifted their brayers clear of the slab. Even the press seemed to quiet, though of course it didn't; Tam simply eased the screw so the next pull made no sound, and they waited, everyone watching the ceiling as though the plaster might thin enough to let them see out.

Then the boots moved on, and the breath went out of the room, and Tam pulled the lever, and the press exhaled, and another truth came into the world.

"They're not looking hard," Maren whispered during one of these intervals, on the fourth night. She was nineteen now in Rosalind's private count; she aged a year a night, it seemed. "Why aren't they looking hard?"

"Because they don't know what they're looking for yet," Tam said. "A rumor moves slower than a man on foot. Give it a week." He said it calmly, but Rosalind saw him glance at the coal chute, calculating exits, and saw him look away from the calculation quickly, as if ashamed of it.

Rosalind knew the rumor's speed precisely, because Rosalind was feeding it. Each dawn, finished gatherings went out of the cellar sewn inside bundles of ordinary job work — handbills for a tannery, price lists for a chandler, the dull paper commerce that no sergeant would trouble to open. The guild's own carriers moved them, unwitting or otherwise, to three separate cellars across the city where sympathetic hands would assemble and bind them. By now perhaps sixty complete copies existed. Perhaps eighty. It was impossible to know exactly, and Rosalind had decided that not knowing was a kind of strength: you cannot burn what you cannot find, and no one, not even Rosalind, knew where all of them were.

But the fifth night brought the sound that changed the arithmetic.

It was nearly three. The half-hour patrol had come and gone. The press was running sweetly, the type holding, two hundred sheets stacked and folded and most of them already gone out the chute in Marta's brother's coal cart — and then boots again, off-rhythm, many boots, and under the boots a sound Rosalind knew from a different life, from the night the Regiment had come for Master Fen's records: the iron knock of a ram being carried, not used, merely carried. Carried the way you carry a thing you expect to need before morning.

Everyone in the cellar heard it. Nobody moved.

Tam raised one hand, palm out, and held it there through the whole passage of the sound — up Cordwainer Lane, past the shop, pausing — *pausing* — at the door above their heads. The knock, when it came, was perfunctory. Three raps. A voice, bored: "Print office! Open in the name of the garrison!"

Silence. Tam's hand stayed raised. In the corner, Pip had woken, and Rosalind watched him fold himself smaller behind the quire stacks without being told, without a sound, and hated the Regiment with a completeness that felt almost peaceful, the way deep water is calm.

Another voice above — higher, irritated, official. A lieutenant's voice. Something about *the wrong district*, something about *the writ says the river ward*, and then a rustle of paper, a muttered correction, and the boots moved off eastward, toward the river ward, where there was no print shop at all.

Nobody breathed fully for a full minute after the sound faded.

"Writ," Tam said at last, very quietly. "There's a writ with our street on it somewhere. Just the wrong house number." He looked at Rosalind. "That's not safety. That's a clerk's error. Clerk's errors get corrected."

"Then we finish tonight," Rosalind said. "All of it. Everything we can pull before dawn goes into the ground or out of the city. We don't run a sixth night."

Bettrice, at her lamp, spoke without looking up from her inspection. "The last gathering isn't carved yet."

"I know."

"The final plate. The one your master left blank." She turned then, and her eyes were old and steady and merciless in the kindest way. "Two hundred copies of an atlas missing its last page is a book with a hole where its heart should be, apprentice. What will you put there?"

Rosalind had known this question was coming for days. Had carried it the way you carry a stone in the shoe — aware of it with every step, unable to set it down. The blank page. Fen's last page, left deliberately empty, annotated in his cramped hand in the margin of the folio: *for whoever inherits.* Rosalind had filled the original — the true map of the purge, the villages restored, the numbers of the taken written village by village in Fen's own recovered notes — but filling a page is not the same as cutting a block, and cutting a block means committing the truth to wood, to repetition, to two hundred mouths speaking at once.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Tonight. Before the press stops."

* * *

They carved by turns, and the cellar smelled of pearwood shavings and lamp oil and fear, which Rosalind was beginning to think was simply the smell of history being made by amateurs.

Tam took the first shift at the graver because he had the steadiest wrist. Then Maren, who turned out to carve like she folded — impossibly fast, impossibly clean. Rosalind took the letters themselves, because the letters mattered most: the dedication, the one Rosalind had composed in the safehouse by candlelight and rewritten eleven times, the list of names — the officers who had refused the order thirty years ago, culled from Fen's correspondence, from parish books, from a widow's memory in Sallow Cross. Seventeen names. Some had died obscure. Some had descendants living in Vellamere right now, carrying other names, not knowing what their grandmothers had done.

*For the seventeen who refused,* the dedication read. *May the record grow.*

While Tam carved, Rosalind pulled proofs of the earlier plates and collated them into the master sequence — and it was during this, kneeling among the paper with the lamp low, that Rosalind noticed the patrol rhythm had changed.

Boots at the quarter hour now. Not the half. And lighter steps — two men, not four, moving quick and quiet, no lantern swing, the walk of men who had been told something specific. Twice they passed. Twice they paused. The second pause lasted longer, and ended with one of them trying the print shop's door handle above their heads, gently, once, like a man testing whether a fruit was ripe.

"They're counting our silences," Rosalind murmured to Tam between pulls. "They're learning the rhythm of the press by its absence."

"Then we give them nothing to learn." Tam wiped the type face, broke down the forme with swift economy — the clatter of type distributed back into the case sounding obscenely loud in the hush — and stood rolling his shoulders. "Dawn's in ninety minutes. Tell me what we have."

What they had: two hundred and twelve copies of the coastal gathering. Two hundred and five of the city plans. One hundred and ninety-eight of the estuary. All of it bundled, wrapped, and moving out through the chute in relays — Marta's brother, then the tanner's boy, then, incredibly, the widow Ostrey from the chandlery, seventy years old and built like a barrel, hauling paper under her shawl as though it were candles. The whole lane was awake in the way a field is awake underground: nothing showing, everything moving.

What they didn't have: the last plate. The block was carved — Tam had finished the borders and Maren the cartouche — but unprinted, and there was no longer time to print it here.

"Then we print it where they aren't," Rosalind said, and heard the words come out of their own mouth with a certainty they didn't entirely feel, the way you hear yourself say something true only after you've said it. "The guild has a common press. The exhibition press, the one they use for the fair demonstrations — it sits in the guildhall under lock, and the lock's key hangs in Aldous Fen's old drawer, because he was Warden of the Guild for nine years and never gave the key back. I've seen it. I've held it." They had, in fact, held it the night they found the correspondence — turned it over, wondered at it, put it back. The master's habit of keeping keys to rooms he no longer entered. As if he'd known Rosalind would someday need the room.

"The guildhall," Tam repeated. "Where the writ was filed. Where the clerk works who wrote the wrong house number on tonight's order." A slow, dangerous grin moved across his inked face. "You want to print the last page of your forbidden atlas on the Regiment's own demonstration press, in the building where they're organizing the search for it."

"I want the last page printed somewhere it can't be burned with us," Rosalind said. "And I want—" they stopped, surprised by the truth arriving late even to themselves, "—I want them to be able to trace it, afterward, to their own machine. Let them stand in their guildhall and look at the press that told on them."

Maren made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer.

It was agreed in the way things were agreed in that cellar now — quickly, quietly, with everyone already reaching for their coats. Bundles went down the chute. Bettrice kissed Rosalind on both cheeks, an act that undid Rosalind more than the burning of the workshop had, and pressed into their hand a small tin of ground vermilion. "For the compass rose on the last page," she said. "Your master always signed his roses in red. Sign yours."

Pip was shaking Rosalind awake from where he'd slept through the final hour, and his eyes were wide and completely awake, and he said, before anyone could tell him anything:

"I'm coming. I know the coal tunnels to the guild quarter. Marta showed me. I'm small and I don't get tired and nobody looks at children carrying bread." He lifted the empty satchel. "We can hide the block in the bread basket under real loaves. Nobody searches bread."

Rosalind opened their mouth to refuse, and looked at the boy — at the child the Regiment wanted erased standing in a cellar full of the evidence that erasure fails — and closed it again.

"Dawn's in eighty minutes," Rosalind said. "Get the loaves."

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell rang once — the garrison's changing-of-watch, ordinary, meaningless — and somewhere between that bell and the next, the last page of the Vellamere Atlas went out into the streets in a bread basket, headed for the enemy's own press.
