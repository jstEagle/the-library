## 21. An Orchard for the Regiment

The river gate at dawn smelled of iron and wet stone.

Rosalind had imagined this moment a hundred times in the tunnels — the last hundred paces of their flight rehearsed like a plate traced over and over until the lines wore grooves into the copper. In every imagining, the gate stood open. In every imagining, they slipped through before the watch changed, two shadows among the market carts that came up from the fishing villages with the tide. In none of them did Colonel Hesper Vance stand beneath the arch with her hands clasped behind her back and the mist beading on her braid like frost on wire.

"Apprentice Zhou." Her voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. That was the horror of it — a voice that belonged in a parlor, offering tea, while its owner signed orders that emptied villages. "You've been busy."

Pip's small hand tightened in Rosalind's. They had come up out of the orchard tunnels an hour past midnight, through the hatch behind the ruined press-house, and crossed the sleeping city by streets Rosalind knew from drawing them — every alley, every culvert, every place the atlas said the stones were uneven because Fen had walked them himself and refused to trust anyone else's survey. The satchel held the finished plates wrapped in oilcloth. The atlas itself — the true one, the one with the final page filled — lay against Rosalind's chest under their coat, bound tight as a second heartbeat. The printer's cart was waiting beyond the gate, hired under a fishmonger's name, bound downriver to a port where three presses waited and a guild of quiet conspirators had promised paper, ink, and silence.

It had all gone exactly as planned. Which was how Rosalind should have known it wouldn't.

"You're out early, Colonel," Rosalind said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Somewhere below the fear, they noticed, there was a strange calm — the calm of the drafting table, when a line must be drawn and there is no use trembling over it.

"I'm always out early." Vance stepped forward, her boots ringing on the wet flagstones. Two soldiers flanked the gate behind her, halberds grounded, faces carefully blank. "I walk the walls when I can't sleep. It's remarkable what the city tells you at this hour. The river especially. It remembers everything, the river. Carries it all down to the sea eventually." She stopped ten feet away. Her eyes went to Pip — lingered there, just long enough — then returned to Rosalind's face. "And you're out early too. With a heavy bag."

"The atlas," Rosalind said. "You want it."

"I want it *surrendered*. There's a difference. Surrender is orderly. It has paperwork." Vance smiled without warmth. "I've spent thirty years making sure things in Vellamere happen with paperwork, Apprentice. It's how you keep a purge from becoming a massacre. Or rather—" the smile thinned, "—it's how you make sure no one can prove which one it was."

* * *

The mist came off the river in slow coils, wrapping the archway, softening the soldiers into gray shapes. Pip pressed closer against Rosalind's side, and Rosalind could feel the boy's heart going fast as a bird's through the thin cloak he still wore — the soldier's cloak, the first thing he'd ever been wrapped in, the only inheritance the Regiment had given him before Rosalind gave him another.

They had slept three hours in the safehouse above the print-shop, and before sleep, Rosalind had done one more thing to the atlas. They had written the dedication page. It had taken most of the night, and their hand had not shaken once, which surprised them; grief and fury, it turned out, made for a very steady line.

Every officer who had refused the order. Every name Fen had gathered in those last secret weeks, tucked into correspondence like seeds into a winter cellar — the quartermaster who burned his own requisitions rather than feed the purge columns, the surveyor who mislaid the village maps so they could never be found again, the young lieutenant who wrote *this is not a war, it is an erasure* and was court-martialed for handwriting that matched. And at the end, in fresh ink, the newest name: the officer who had delayed the seizure by an hour, who had let a report go missing, whose career was now a candle burning down in a locked room somewhere in the Citadel.

Hesper Vance. Refused. The record would say it whether she wanted it to or not.

"Colonel," Rosalind said, and unslung the satchel.

Vance's hand moved — not to her sword, but toward the bag, quick and hungry, and Rosalind saw in that half-second everything the woman would never say: thirty years of service measured out in paperwork, and at the end of it, a promotion dangled like bait on a hook, recover the atlas intact and be made General, be made anything, be made permanent. Power loved permanence. That was the whole disease of it.

But Rosalind did not open the satchel. Instead they drew the atlas itself from under their coat — the true one, the finished one, Fen's life and Rosalind's and now Pip's inheritance bound in calfskin gone soft at the corners — and held it in both hands between them.

Vance went very still.

"If you take it," Rosalind said, "you'll have to burn it. You know that. You'll stand in some courtyard and watch the pages curl, and you'll write your report, and the report will say the work was incomplete, unsound, seditious. And then in twenty years someone will dig in the ashes of the archive and find nothing, because there will be nothing, because you made sure of it." They turned the book over slowly, showing the blank spine, the worn boards. "Or you can read it first. Just once. What's one hour, Colonel? You've already given me one."

Something moved behind Vance's eyes. Not softness — Rosalind doubted there was much softness left in her, and did not need there to be. It was something colder and more useful: recognition. The look of a woman seeing a ledger opened and finding her own name in it.

"What are you talking about?" Vance asked, and her voice, for the first time, lost its parlor smoothness.

"The dedication page." Rosalind opened the atlas to the front matter, careful not to expose the plates, and turned it around. "Read it."

* * *

The mist thickened. One of the soldiers shifted his weight, and the sound of it was enormous in the hush. Pip had gone silent in the way he'd learned hiding in the pigment chest — that terrible learned stillness, breath shallow, eyes wide — and Rosalind ached at it, ached at what survival had taught a seven-year-old, and kept their hands steady anyway. Steady hands. Fen's first lesson. *The map does not tremble even when the world does.*

Vance read.

She read for a long time. Longer than the page required, unless she was reading every name twice. The mist collected in the creases at the corners of her mouth. Once her jaw tightened; once her thumb pressed flat against the paper as if to hold a word still. The river ran dark and patient beneath the arch, carrying whatever it carried down to the sea.

"These names," she said finally. Her voice had dropped. "Where did you get these?"

"Master Fen gathered them. For years. He didn't collect them to accuse anyone." Rosalind heard the truth of it as they said it, felt it settle into place like a coastline finally surveyed. "He collected them because a map that shows only what the powerful want shown isn't a map. It's a wall with a picture painted on it. He wanted the record to be whole. That's all. Wholeness. He died for wholeness."

"And me." Vance closed the atlas gently — gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom. "Why am I in it? I burned your workshop. I hunted you across half the city. I've spent a fortnight trying to put this book in a fire."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "And you were ordered to seize it at dawn and you waited an hour. You were handed a tracking report and you filed it wrong. Don't ask how I know. The same way you knew about my bag — the city tells people things at this hour." They took a breath. "The record doesn't measure intentions, Colonel. It measures acts. Your act bought us the river gate. So it goes in the book. That's the whole of it. That's all a record is — the sum of what we actually did, kept against the day we start lying about it."

"And if I burn it now?" Vance's hand rested on the cover, light as a woman testing a door latch. "One book. One copy. My career saved, my promotion secured, thirty years of tidy paperwork preserved. Who would ever know?"

"The river," Rosalind said quietly. "The river would know. And so would you. Every morning for the rest of your life, when you walked the walls to keep from sleeping."

Behind them, far off across the sleeping district, a bell began to ring — the Citadel's morning bell, or perhaps an alarm, impossible to tell at this distance, its tone smeared by fog. One of the soldiers turned his head. Vance did not.

"You understand," she said, "that if I let you pass, I am finished. There's no coming back from this. They'll break me. Quietly — they're good at quiet — but thoroughly. Thirty years, and I'll end my days keeping records in some garrison at the edge of the salt marshes, and everyone will pretend I was never anything else." She looked down at Pip then, really looked, and her expression did something complicated and almost human. "He has the eyes. You know that. Everyone old enough to remember knows it, even if no one says it."

"He has his own name," Rosalind said. "That's what matters. We gave him one. He'll choose what he carries."

For a moment — one long turn of the mist, one pull of the river — Rosalind thought it would go the other way. Thought they would see the order come down over Vance's face like a visor, thought they would hear the pleasant voice say *take them*, and the halberds would lift, and the atlas would end here at the water's edge, and the last page would stay blank forever after all.

Then Hesper Vance did something no one in Vellamere would ever quite believe, least of all herself.

She straightened the atlas in Rosalind's arms, squared its edges with two precise taps of her finger — the gesture of a woman who had spent thirty years aligning paperwork — and stepped aside.

"Gate detail," she said, without turning to the soldiers, her voice clipped back into parade-ground economy. "Open the arch. Fish cart's expected. Move."

The soldiers obeyed. Of course they did; obedience was the whole architecture of the Regiment, and it never occurred to men built inside that architecture to question why the colonel wanted a fish cart admitted at first light. The great timber doors groaned back along their channel, and beyond them the quay opened gray and gold, gulls wheeling, the hired cart standing where it should with the fishmonger's boy dozing at the reins.

Vance looked at Rosalind one last time.

"When it prints," she said, "spell my name correctly. Hesper. People get it wrong."

"It's spelled correctly," Rosalind said. "I checked twice. Master Fen taught me — check everything twice, especially the things you'd rather not see."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her — short, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from a rafter. Then her face closed, and she was the Colonel again, and she walked away down the quay into the mist without looking back, her boots ringing on stone, a small straight figure growing smaller, carrying thirty years of paperwork and one hour of conscience out along the river she had once said remembered everything.

* * *

They did not run for the cart. Running would draw the eye; Fen had taught that too. They walked, unhurried, an apprentice and a child going about the morning's dull business, and the fishmonger's boy woke, yawned, and flicked the reins, and the cart rolled down onto the ferry road with its cargo of oilcloth-wrapped plates hidden under herring barrels, and Vellamere fell away behind them wall by wall, tower by tower, its smoke rising straight into the windless dawn.

Pip spoke only once, when the city had shrunk to a smudge on the eastern shore.

"Was she in the book because she was good?"

Rosalind considered it for a long mile, the river sliding beside them, wide and brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.

"She was in the book because of what she did," Rosalind said at last. "Good is a harder question. Ask me again in a year."

Pip nodded gravely, the way he nodded at everything Rosalind said lately, storing it the way he stored compass roses — carefully, in rows — and then he leaned his head against Rosalind's arm and slept.

Downriver, past the last bend, three presses waited in a cellar that smelled of ink and lamp oil. By tomorrow night the first sheets would be pulling, and the day after, the atlas would begin to move through the city like water finding its level — into taverns and vestries and guildhalls, into the hands of clerks and midwives and soldiers, everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, unburnable now, unstoppable, a truth distributed beyond any colonel's reach.

Rosalind sat with the atlas open on their knees, the wind riffling the pages, and watched Vellamere's towers sink into haze, and felt — for the first time since the night a child had appeared on the workshop step carrying a note that said *forget the face* — that they might, at last, set down the weight.

The presses were waiting. And somewhere behind them in the waking city, a woman was walking the walls, unable to sleep, waiting to learn how history would spell her name.
