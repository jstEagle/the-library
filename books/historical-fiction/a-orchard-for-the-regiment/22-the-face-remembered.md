## 22. The Face Remembered

A year, Rosalind thought, was a strange unit of measurement. They had spent their whole apprenticeship learning to measure everything — coastlines in furlongs, rivers in chains, the span of a city wall down to the width of a thumbnail — and no instrument they owned could measure what a year did to a person. It stretched. It folded. It carried you across distances you never agreed to walk.

One year since the workshop burned.

They stood in the doorway of the new workshop on Tanners' Row, morning light falling through glass that was still slightly wavy from the glazier's kiln, and watched the street wake up. A milk cart rattled past. Two women argued cheerfully over a bolt of dyed wool. A child chased a hoop with a stick, and no one looked at that child twice, and no one looked at Rosalind twice either, and this — this ordinariness, this blessed invisibility — still felt like a luxury they had not entirely earned.

The atlas had made them famous, which was another thing they hadn't earned and couldn't return. *A Orchard for the Regiment* sat in shop windows now, its spine unhidden, its title printed in honest black ink. Booksellers stacked it beside almanacs and romances as though it were any other book, which was, Rosalind supposed, exactly the point. Master Fen would have appreciated that. He had believed all his life that a map should sit quietly on a shelf until someone needed it, and then it should be there, complete and true, asking nothing for itself.

Copies circulated openly now. That was the phrase people used — *openly*, said with a small lift of wonder, like a word in a language recently learned. In the coffee houses along the river, merchants traced the old purge routes with their fingers and went quiet. Schoolmasters pointed to the restored villages — Ashcombe, Ferrowdale, the little settlements along the Aurelian marches whose names had been scraped from every official chart for thirty years — and told their pupils, *these places existed. These people existed.* Some of the names on Rosalind's final plate belonged to people who had come to find Rosalind: an old woman who wept over her grandmother's farm rendered in fine ink; a man who stood in the doorway of the workshop for a long time without speaking, because his family's village was on the map and he had spent his whole life being told it never was.

Rosalind kept a ledger of these visits now, though Fen would have laughed at the sentimentality of it. But it seemed important — necessary, even — to record what a map did after it left your hands. Maps were not finished when they were printed. Maps finished themselves in other people, one reader at a time, the way rivers finished themselves in the sea.

The Regiment had not fallen. Rosalind had no illusions about that. Colonel Vance's misfiled report had bought a week, and the week had bought the printing, and the printing had bought something larger than any of them — but the Regiment itself still sat in its fortress above the city like a stone in a riverbed, and the water simply flowed around it now instead of being stopped by it. There had been resignations. There had been tribunals, quiet ones, for the officers named in the dedication page. There had been a new charter for the Cartographers' Guild, ratified in autumn, requiring that all surveys be archived publicly. Small things. Slow things. The kind of change that happened the way erosion happened — invisibly, and then all at once, and then you looked up and the cliff had become a valley.

And once — only once — a letter had arrived with no signature, on paper too fine for any ordinary correspondent. It contained a single line: *The orchard was a good choice.* Rosalind had burned it in the stove, but they had read it four times first, and they had not decided, even now, whether it had been a threat or a blessing or an apology, and they suspected they never would. Some maps you never finished surveying.

Inside the workshop, the smell of linseed oil and fresh-cut paper was nearly the same as the old place. Nearly. There were differences Rosalind had chosen deliberately: wider windows, so the light fell honestly on every plate; a second workbench at the far end, low, built for someone small; a lock on the front door that was sturdy but not fortified, because Rosalind had decided a year ago that they would not live behind barricades, that living behind barricades was how the Regiment won without firing a shot.

And above the door — inside, where the morning sun would catch it, not outside where weather could take it — hung Master Aldous Fen's compass.

It had been found in the ashes. That fact still undid Rosalind a little, whenever they let themselves think about it directly, which was rarely. Of everything in the workshop — twenty years of plates, the pigment chest, the drying racks, the great oak table scarred by three generations of apprentices' knives — the fire had taken nearly all of it, and had spared a brass compass no larger than a plum. It had been in Fen's coat pocket, presumably, hanging on its hook by the door the night the soldiers came, and the coat had burned and the compass had dropped into the cellar ash and lain there through the winter until Rosalind went back — against everyone's advice, including their own better judgment — to sift the ruins.

It still worked. That was the miracle of good instruments; they were honest past all reason. Rosalind had cleaned the soot from its face, oiled the pivot with a single drop taken on the tip of a needle, and set it on the table, and it had swung north as if nothing had ever happened, as if fire and death and a year of grief were merely weather it declined to notice.

Fen had bought it, Rosalind knew from the earliest ledgers, with his first wages as a journeyman. Fifty years he had carried it. And now it hung above a new door, catching light, pointing at nothing and everything, and every apprentice who walked beneath it would pass under the same needle that had steadied the hand that drew the atlas.

Inheritance, Rosalind had come to understand, was not what the Regiment had always claimed it was. The Regiment believed inheritance was blood — that a name carried forward like a flame passed hand to hand, and that some flames must be smothered so others could burn alone. Thirty years of erasure rested on that belief. But Rosalind had held the compass, and taught the boy, and filled the blank page, and they knew now that inheritance was not blood at all. It was choice. It was what you chose to carry, and what you chose to set down, and who you chose to carry it for. A compass was not inherited because it was brass. It was inherited because someone hung it above a door.

Which brought Rosalind, as everything eventually did, to Pip.

* * *

He came down the stairs two at a time, the way he did everything now, all elbows and momentum, seven going on eight and convinced he was really ten. His hair had been shaggy a year ago, soldier's-cloak shabby; now it was cut badly by Rosalind's own impatient shears and stuck up at the crown like a rooster's crest, and his brow bore only the faintest silver thread of a scar where the cut had been, the cut that had started everything, the cut over the eye that matched a portrait painted before he was born.

"Rosalind." He skidded to a stop on the workshop floor, breathless with news that was clearly enormous. "Master Quillon says the third printing sold out. All of it. He says they're asking for it in Corvale. *Corvale,* Rosalind. That's across the mountains."

"I heard," Rosalind said. "He told me yesterday."

"Well he told me *today,*" Pip said, with the gravity of a boy who understood precisely how information worked and intended to be on the right side of it. "Also there's ink on your nose."

"There is frequently ink on my nose."

"Yeah." He nodded solemnly. "It's kind of your whole thing."

Rosalind laughed — freely, easily, in a way that had taken most of the year to relearn. For the first months after the fire, laughter had felt like something borrowed, something that belonged to a person who had not stood in an orchard tunnel listening to boots overhead. But children were relentless restorers. Pip had rebuilt Rosalind the way he'd learned to build everything: without permission, without plan, out of whatever materials lay at hand.

They worked together through the morning, the way they always did. Rosalind drew the coastline of a lake district for a merchant client while Pip, at his low bench, practiced hachures — those fine tick-marks that showed the steepness of slopes — tongue between his teeth, destroying three sheets and improving visibly on the fourth. He had a gift. Rosalind tried not to say it aloud too often, because praise made him careless and carelessness made his lines wobble, but the truth was plain to anyone with eyes: the boy saw the world in contours. He noticed where the land rose and fell. He noticed things.

At midday they ate bread and hard cheese and apples from the market — real apples, bought with money, though Pip still refused to eat any apple that wasn't from the old orchard district, a superstition he defended with theological intensity. And it was there, with crumbs on the great table and sunlight moving slowly across the floor, that the conversation came — the one they had been circling for months, approached and retreated from like surveyors mapping a border both sides pretended not to see.

"The lawyer came again," Pip said suddenly, staring very hard at his apple core. "To the printer's. Quillon told me."

"Yes."

"He says I can have the name back. If I want." Pip turned the core in his fingers. "Aurelian. He says it's legal now, since the atlas and everything. He says there are documents."

"There are documents," Rosalind confirmed gently.

"He says a person should know who they are." Pip said this in a flattened voice, quoting, and Rosalind heard in it the whole machinery of officialdom that had spent thirty years deciding who people were — the writs and seals and tribunals, the same hands that had signed destruction orders now offering restoration orders, as though identity were a drawer in a bureau that could be emptied and refilled by clerks.

Rosalind wiped their hands and gave the question the silence it deserved. Then: "What do you think?"

Pip was quiet for a long time. Outside, a bell rang the hour. The compass above the door caught the light and threw a small bright coin of reflection onto the floorboards, and Pip looked at it.

"Pip is who you found," he said finally. "On the step. Pip is—" He frowned, hunting for it the way he hunted for the right contour line, testing and discarding. "Pip is the one who hid in the pigment chest. Pip is the one who found the hatch under the racks. Pip is the one who walked the tunnels." He looked up, and his eyes were steady in a way no seven-year-old's eyes should have to be, and yet were. "Aurelian is who they killed. I didn't meet him. I don't remember him. I'm sorry he's dead. But I'm not him."

"No," Rosalind said softly. "You're who survived him."

"And I choose Pip." He said *choose* carefully, tasting it, a word he had clearly learned from the lawyers and repurposed for his own uses. "Can I have both, though? Can the documents say Pip? Both names?"

"They can say anything you tell them to say."

"Then Pip Aurelian." He nodded once, decisively, a magistrate closing a case. "Aurelian at the back where nobody sees it. Like the last plate."

Like the last plate. Rosalind's throat tightened unexpectedly — grief and pride tangled together, indistinguishable, the way they had been for a year. The boy had understood, without anyone teaching him, the whole secret architecture of the atlas: the true map bound at the end of the book, present but unadvertised, carried rather than displayed. He had been carrying his own name the same way all along. He had simply been waiting for permission to file it properly.

"Pip Aurelian," Rosalind agreed. "We'll go Thursday."

"Good." And then, because he was eight next month and grief could only hold him for so long: "Can we go to the orchard after?"

"We can always go to the orchard after."

* * *

They went in the late afternoon, when the light came slantwise through the trees and turned everything the color of weak tea and honey. The old orchard district sat on the hill above the river, half-wild now, the regimental barracks that had briefly occupied it abandoned and stripped for timber. Apple trees gone untended grew crooked and abundant, dropping fruit into grass that no one mowed. Bees had moved into the walls of the ruined press-house. In spring the whole hillside bloomed white and reckless, a monument no one had planned.

Beneath it ran the tunnels. The hatch was still there, behind the ruin of the old drying shed — Rosalind had never sealed it, had never wanted to. Once a month or so, they walked it together, lamp in hand, Pip ahead on the narrow stairs with the fearlessness of the converted. Fen's coded survey marks still flecked the brickwork: a chiseled arrow here, a number there, the dead master's handwriting in stone, leading always toward the river gate. On the worst nights of the past year — and there had been some, nights when the fire dreams came — Rosalind had thought about walking that route again, following the marks, feeling the way each one seemed to say *this way, keep on, almost there.* The dead leave directions. It is the living who must decide whether to follow them.

Today they only walked to the mouth of the tunnel where it opened under the roots of the oldest tree, a gnarled apple said to be older than the city itself, its trunk hollowed and its roots grown thick as beams over the hidden entrance. Pip scrambled up into the roots and settled himself in the hollow of the trunk, which fit him perfectly, as though the tree had been waiting a century for exactly one small boy.

"Do you think he knew?" Pip asked, from inside the tree. His voice came out rounded, wooden, oracular. "Master Fen. Do you think he knew about the tunnels when he drew the marks?"

"He surveyed the old city when he was young. Before the Regiment. So yes — he knew."

"But did he know we'd need them? Us specifically?"

Rosalind leaned against the trunk and considered the question seriously, because Pip deserved serious answers; he had paid for them. "I think he drew the atlas for whoever inherited it. That's what he wrote on the last page — *for whoever inherits.* I don't think he knew it would be us. I think he just... left the door open. Left the page blank. Left the marks on the walls. He did his part, and then he trusted the future to do its part."

"That's a lot of trusting," Pip said. "For a dead person."

"It's the only kind there is." Rosalind reached up and pulled an apple from a low branch, buffed it on their sleeve. "Everything anyone makes is a message to someone they'll never meet. A map. A tunnel. A name. You just have to decide what yours will say."

Pip was quiet, turning this over. Above them the leaves moved, and the light came down through them in shifting coins, and somewhere below their feet the tunnels ran dark and patient toward the river, empty now, their work done, keeping their secret out of habit.

"When I'm a mapmaker," Pip said at last — not *if,* never *if,* and Rosalind had stopped correcting the presumption months ago — "I'm going to draw the orchard. Properly. Every tree. With numbers. Nobody's ever drawn the orchard."

"No," Rosalind said. "Nobody has."

"Then it'll be mine to draw." He slid down out of the roots, apple in hand, already halfway to the next thing, the way children are — carriers of the future who don't yet know they're carrying it. "Come on, Rosalind. It's getting dark, and you always get lost when it gets dark."

"I do not get lost. I take scenic routes."

"You get lost," Pip said comfortably, and took Rosalind's hand anyway, because some inheritances ran both directions, and together they climbed the hill through the long gold light, under the branches, toward home.

Behind them the old tree stood over the hidden door, blossoming and fruiting and dropping its seeds into the grass without any need for permission — keeping, in the only way living things keep anything, exactly what it chose to carry, and letting the rest go where the wind took it.
