## 1. The Weight of Glass

The gale came in from the southwest an hour after sunset, the way winter gales do on Carrick Island — without announcement, without apology, as though it had been standing offshore all day waiting for permission.

Xanthe Thackeray felt it arrive through her boots before she heard it. A tremor in the iron floor of the lantern room, faint as a pulse. Then the sound: a long low moan building under the wind, the tower itself singing its one note, and she was already moving, cup of tea abandoned on the desk to go cold as it always went cold on nights like this.

"Right," she said to nobody, because there was nobody to say it to, and because saying it steadied her voice for the hours ahead. "Right, then."

She climbed the ninety-one steps to the lantern. She had counted them once, early in her widowhood, when counting things was the only prayer she knew, and now the number lived in her the way a hymn lives in a churchgoer — available at any hour, requiring no thought. Ninety-one. Elias had never counted them. He had simply climbed them for nineteen years until his heart stopped somewhere around step sixty, and they'd carried him down past the landing where the paint was worn to bare metal in two boot-shaped patches, his and hers, side by side.

She did not think about that now. Thinking about it was a luxury for calm nights, and this was not going to be a calm night.

In the lantern room the wind hit the glass like a living thing throwing itself against a door. The barometer — Elias's barometer, brass-cased, its face gone amber with age — stood at 28.6 and falling, which meant the glass would take everything the sea could give it and be asked for more by morning. Xanthe checked the lamp first, always the lamp before anything else, because the lamp was the reason the tower existed and the reason she existed too, if she was honest, which she tried not to be more than necessary.

The flame burned steady in its mantle. Good. She checked the fuel reservoir, the clockwork that turned the lens, the spare wicks in their tin. All good. Then she took up the chamois leather from its hook and began to polish.

This was the part no manual mentioned. The Trinity House manuals spoke of cleaning the glass as maintenance, as duty, as a line item. They did not speak of what it meant to stand inside a cage of storm-tossed light at nine o'clock at night with a square of soft leather in your hand, wiping salt haze off panes your dead husband had wiped ten thousand times before you, in weather exactly like this, humming exactly the same tuneless hum she caught herself humming now.

She polished pane by pane, working counterclockwise the way he had worked, starting always at the seaward face because that was the face that suffered most. Each pane weighed nothing and everything. That was the thing about glass that she had learned in four years of keeping this light alone: it looked fragile, it sounded fragile when the gale tested it, but it held. It held because someone had fitted it true, bedded it in putty mixed properly, seated it against the glazing bars with patience. Elias had been a patient man with glass. Impatient with everything else — with paperwork, with visitors, with his own family, whose letters he stopped opening sometime around the year she met him — but patient with glass, patient with the slow work of keeping a flame alive behind a curved wall of it so that some stranger's hull wouldn't open on the Carrick rocks in the dark.

She finished the seaward pane last, pressed her palm flat against it, felt the cold coming through like a message from the water.

"Hold," she told it.

It held. It always held. But you told it anyway, because telling it was part of the ritual, and rituals were what kept a person upright in a tower on a rock in the second year of a war that showed no sign of ending.

* * *

At half past ten she went down to the living room — the round room, Elias had always called it, the way other men said drawing room — to write the log.

The typewriter sat on the table by the window, a black Underwood with a hairline crack across one key cover, bought secondhand in Penzance the year they married because Elias said a keeper's hand should write as steady as his light. His hands had written on it for nineteen years. Hers had written on it for four. Between them the keys had worn into their own particular geography, the E shiny as a coin, the space bar dipped in the middle like a worn threshold.

Xanthe wound a fresh sheet into the platen. The paper was government issue, thin and gray, rationed now like nearly everything, and she made each sheet do double duty where she could — log copy on the front, her own notes on the back in pencil, small enough to be erased if anyone official ever asked what else she wrote in the margins of her nights.

She typed:

**CARRICK LIGHT — NIGHT LOG**
**14 January 1917. Keeper X. Thackeray.**

**22:00. Wind SW freshening, by 2230 full gale. Barometer 28.6 falling. Sea high, breaking over low rock. Lamp trimmed and lit 17:15, burning well. Glass cleaned, all panes. Revolutions regular.**

She paused, fingers resting on the keys, and listened. Above her the wind worked at the lantern like a burglar working a lock — patiently, methodically, trying every seam. Below her the sea detonated against the island's western shoulder, a concussion she felt in her ribs rather than heard.

Elias had typed his logs in short declaratives. Wind. Sea. Lamp. He had believed — he'd said it often, usually with his chin tipped toward the tower above them — that a log should read like the light itself: plain, repeated, unanswerable. *No poetry in a logbook,* he said. *Poetry is for people whose mistakes don't drown anybody.*

But she had found, sorting his effects, that his logs were poetry anyway. Not in the words — the words were plain as bread. In the cadence. In the fact that every night for nineteen years, in gales and calms and the long fogs of spring, the man had sat down at this machine and testified: *the light burned, I kept it burning, here is the proof.* Nineteen years of the same sentence wearing different weather. If that wasn't poetry, Xanthe didn't know what was, and she'd read Tennyson cover to cover twice during her first lonely winter here.

She continued:

**23:30. Gale increasing. Spray over lantern. Glass holding.**

Then, because the official log was done and her own night was not, she turned the sheet's back toward herself and penciled smaller, in her own shorthand, the entries that belonged to no archive:

*Milk tin empty. Ask Tom Wrenn to bring up two tins Thursday, not one. The boy forgets.*

*Second coat of stove black needed on the kitchen range door.*

*Letter to Mrs. Pascoe re: flour. She will pretend she hasn't got any and then produce it out of kindness and embarrassment both. Bring something to trade — the marmalade? No. Keep the marmalade. Trade the candles.*

That was how Xanthe measured her life now, in portable things. Two tins of milk. A tin of stove black. Three candles against a pot of marmalade. When Elias died she had discovered that grief, properly examined, was mostly inventory — the sudden accounting of everything a person had carried that now had nowhere to be put. His coat still hung on its hook because she could not carry it and could not burn it and could not give it away, so it stayed, a portable thing gone immovable. His razor. His half-finished letter to his father, begun and abandoned three times, the third attempt folded into a book of tidal tables where she pretended not to know it lived.

Everything else she had learned to carry. You learned or you didn't, and the ones who didn't were the ones who let the light go dark, and she was not going to be one of those.

* * *

At midnight the gale reached its first fury and the tower earned its keep.

She went back up the ninety-one steps with the oil can and the spare fuse wire, moving in the rhythm the stairs demanded — hand on rail, hip clearing the turn at fifty-three, head ducked under the beam at eighty-eight. The lantern room shuddered around her. Rain came sideways past the glass in silver ropes, and beyond the ropes the sea was a chaos of white, the Carrick rocks showing and drowning and showing again like the backs of enormous animals.

The lamp burned on. The lens turned on its bath of mercury, gathering the flame into its great curved ribs and flinging it seaward every seven seconds — flash, and the dark, and flash again. Seven seconds. She had lived inside those seven seconds so long that her heartbeat had adjusted to them; some nights, lying awake, she was sure her heart flashed on the interval whether the lamp was lit or not.

She checked the mercury bath — levels good — and the clockwork — winding due at one — and then, because the wind had thrown something against the gallery rail with a clang she didn't like, she cracked the service door and leaned out into the roar to look.

Salt spray hit her face like a slap. Her oilskin snapped around her legs. On the gallery, half-lit by the rotating beam, lay the thing that had struck: a piece of hatch cover, splintered oak, torn free from some deck somewhere and thrown forty feet up the tower by a sea with an arm like a crane.

Her stomach dropped. Somewhere out in that chaos was a ship close enough to lose parts of itself against Carrick.

Back inside, she rang down the telephone line to the coastguard station at Porthmellin on the mainland — the line crackling, the wind screaming through the wire itself, making the whole conversation sound conducted underwater. Yes, they knew. A coastal steamer, the *Merrow*, out of Falmouth for Bristol, reported overdue. Yes, they'd warned her. No, nothing yet. Keep the light burning, Mrs. Thackeray. As if there were any possibility of anything else.

She typed the addition to the log at ten minutes past one:

**01:10. Wreckage sighted on gallery rail — hatch cover, oak, painted green. Coastguard informed. SS Merrow overdue, Falmouth–Bristol. Light shown at full power.**

Then she sat with her hands flat on the table and waited, and the waiting was the worst of it, worse than any gale, because waiting was the one thing a keeper could not fix with competence. She could trim a wick, wind a clockwork, replace a broken pane by lamplight in forty minutes flat — she had done it twice, once in a gale, and her hands had not shaken either time. But she could not steer the *Merrow*. She could only throw her seven-second sentence at the dark and trust that somebody aboard was awake to read it.

Flash. Dark. Flash.

Somewhere out there, a man at a wheel or a woman at a chart table, counting flashes the way she counted stairs.

* * *

The *Merrow* came clear of the rocks at twenty past two, by God's grace and her own engines and whatever the mate saw when he looked south and caught the Carrick light right where it ought to be. Xanthe watched her running lights crawl northward through the rain until the dark took them, and then she typed:

**02:20. Vessel answering description of SS Merrow sighted standing north, clear of Carrick. Appears undamaged.**

And underneath, in pencil, on the back, where only she would ever read it:

*One ship. One night. That is the whole job and it is enough. Remember this on the bad days. There will be bad days.*

There had already been bad days. Four years' worth. The day the telegram came. The day of the funeral, when men she barely knew had lowered him into mainland earth because Carrick had no graveyard and the sea that killed him couldn't have him either. The first night alone, when she had climbed the ninety-one steps and stood in the lantern room with her hand on the lamp and understood that the entire apparatus of her life — the tower, the tide tables, the typewriter, the marriage — now rested on her two hands, and that the Board in London could withdraw her appointment with a paragraph if they decided a woman couldn't hold a station alone, and that therefore every single night had to be perfect, because imperfection was the excuse they were waiting for.

So: perfect. Every night. Four hundred and thirty-odd nights of perfect, logged and dated in Elias's cadence, in Elias's chair, at Elias's machine.

She did not resent the machine. That was the strange thing she'd never told anyone, because there was no one to tell. Some widows kept their husbands' coats; she kept his keyboard. When her fingers found the home row they found grooves his fingers had cut, and typing felt less like writing and more like putting her hands where his hands had been, which was as close to holding him as physics allowed anymore.

* * *

Toward dawn the gale spent itself the way they do — not dying but withdrawing, sulkily, in stages, leaving the air scoured clean and the horizon hard-edged as cut tin. At six she climbed up and cleaned the glass again, because the spray had left its crust even through a closed lantern, and the light deserved clean glass at sunrise the same as at any hour. Pane by pane, counterclockwise, seaward last. Palm flat against the cold. "Hold," she told it, and it had held, and she said thank you anyway.

Downstairs, she typed the final entries:

**06:00. Wind moderating, SW backing W. Barometer rising, 29.2. Sea decreasing. Lamp extinguished 06:40, civil twilight.**

**Glass cleaned. All well.**

All well. The oldest lie and the truest sentence in the English language, depending on the hour. She signed it, blotted it, laid the sheet in the tray with its brothers — four years of them, tied in bundles by month, the complete testimony of one woman's stubbornness on one rock.

The relief boat was due Thursday, weather permitting, which after last night meant probably Friday. Tom Wrenn would come up the path with his milk tins and his endless questions, eighteen years old and hungry for the world, asking her again — he asked every week, as if repetition might wear her down where logic hadn't — to tell him what it was really like, the keeping, whether he could bear it, whether she'd put in a word with the district superintendent. And she would answer as she always answered, that the keeping was mostly glass and grease and waiting, and that he should finish his schooling first, and he would look at her with those eager dog's eyes and she would feel the peculiar ache of being, at thirty-four, the closest thing to an elder that some people had.

Thursday, then. Milk tins, stove black, the post.

She thought of the post the way she thought of most things now: briefly, practically. A newspaper a week late. An occasional note from the Board. Mrs. Pascoe's accounts, if she sent them. Nothing else came to Carrick; nothing had come to Carrick for four years except bills and bulletins. Whatever the world wanted from her, it wanted by wire or not at all.

Outside, the morning came up hard and blue behind the dissolving gale. The sea lay down in long exhausted swells. Gulls worked the aftermath along the tideline, screaming their grievances at nothing.

Xanthe Thackeray boiled water for tea, set two cups out of habit, put one back, and carried the other to the window to watch the light of day replace the light she'd made — her own private changing of the guard, performed every morning, witnessed by no one, recorded nowhere but in the log.

Ninety-one steps. Seven seconds. Two tins of milk. One cup.

She drank her tea and waited for Thursday, having no way of knowing that Thursday's post would contain an envelope sealed twice — once by a solicitor, and once, years earlier, by a dead man's crest — and that everything she had learned to carry was about to be weighed against something she could not carry at all.
