## 3. What Can Be Carried

The gale had gone out with the tide, and what remained was the silence after — that particular hush a lighthouse knows better than any house on land, because a lighthouse is never silent long enough to forget what noise is. I stood in the lamp room with my hands still warm from the brass and listened to the island breathing.

Two days to the fair passage. Two days to decide what a life weighed.

I had always measured my life in portable things. It was not a philosophy I chose; it was one the sea taught me. When Elias brought me out here as a bride of twenty-three, everything I owned fit in two trunks and a hatbox, and I remember standing in the tower's cold round parlor thinking: this is all there will ever be, so let it be enough. Eleven years later, when they carried Elias down the stairs under a flag, everything I owned fit in the same two trunks, minus the hatbox, which I had given to the wind off the gallery rail the week after the funeral, along with a good deal of screaming the gulls still haven't forgiven me for.

Portable things. You learn their grammar. A thing is portable if you can carry it past your own grief without setting it down.

I took a pencil and the back of an old supply manifest and began at the top of the tower, because that is how Elias taught me to do everything — from the top down, against gravity, against sense.

*One lens, first order,* I wrote. *Not portable.*

The great lens turned above me in its bath of mercury, slow as a planet, throwing its beam out over water the color of gunmetal. One hundred and eighty-eight separate pieces of glass, ground in a French workshop by men whose names were stamped on a plate no visitor would ever read. It was the largest thing Elias loved and the only thing he loved that could not love him back, and I had spent eleven years learning its moods the way other wives learn a husband's. Which prism caught the dawn and flung it red across the ceiling. Which bearing sang flat in a northwesterly. Where the dust settled first, and why it always settled there, and what Elias would say — *dust is just the world arriving late, Xanthe, be patient with it* — if I complained.

Not portable. And yet.

Below the lens, in the service room, the inventory grew more honest. *Spare prism, six.* Portable, barely, wrapped one to a crate like infants. *Mercury trough, drain fittings, wick trimmers, three sets.* Portable. *Chronometer, gimbal-mounted.* Portable, and precious; a chronometer keeps time for ships that are alive, and I wound it every Sunday at noon whether it needed winding or not, because Elias had, because his father had, because somewhere behind that habit stood a whole line of Thackerays winding time for strangers.

Thackeray. I wrote the word and then looked at it a long moment, sitting there in my own handwriting on a government form, as if it had been waiting all these years for me to notice it.

The letter slept in its tin box in the parlor. Sealed twice — once by the solicitor in Penzance, once, beneath, by an envelope bearing a crest I had seen perhaps four times in my life and never touched: the apple tree split by lightning, the motto underneath in Latin I'd had to look up. *Ex umbra in lucem.* Out of shadow into light. A family of lighthouse men who put a joke about darkness on their crest and then spent three generations not speaking to each other.

I had not burned it. I had not opened it again either, though I had sat with it through one entire night watch, turning it over like a stone. I had told no one, which on an island is the same as telling everyone nothing while they invent everything. Mrs. Trevanion at the supply boat had looked at me Tuesday with an expression I did not care for. Grief gives you privacy; suspicion takes it away.

I went down the stairs, counting as I went. Forty-one steps. Elias had counted them differently — forty-two, because he insisted the top step belonged half to the tower and half to the man climbing it. Sentimental nonsense. I missed it every day.

* * *

The parlor held the rest of the arithmetic.

The typewriter sat on its table under the window, the Remington Elias had bought secondhand from a marine insurance office in Falmouth the year we married, paying for it out of the money meant for a honeymoon. We had never taken the honeymoon. Some nights I typed the log while he read the shipping lists aloud, and we called that our travels — Genoa, Odessa, Port Said, all of them arriving as paper, all of them safer than going.

I typed the way some people pray, which is to say badly at first and then with a terrible fluency. The log books stacked beside the machine ran back eleven years in Elias's cadence and mine, though anyone reading them straight through would struggle to find the seam where his hand left off and mine began, because grief does not change your syntax so much as it confirms it. *Wind SW freshening. Barometer unsteady. Lamp trimmed and lit at 17:42. All well.* All well. The two most lied-about words in the English language, and I had written them nine hundred times.

What else. His coat, hanging on the back of the door where it had hung since March of '16. A pea coat, navy wool, salt-stiffened at the cuffs, too big for me in the shoulders and too short everywhere else, which did not stop me from wearing it on the coldest watches for the first month until Tom Wrenn arrived and I caught him looking at me in it with an expression between pity and awe, and after that I kept it on the hook where it belonged. Coats hold their shape better than people do. That is the whole secret of coats.

His razor. His tobacco tin, empty, which I kept anyway because the hinge made a small sound like a bird when you opened it. The photograph from St. Ives, 1909, both of us squinting into sun, his arm around me with the confidence of a man who believed the sea would give him fifty years. The sea gave him thirty-one. I do not recommend negotiating with it.

I wrote the list until the pencil wore blunt. Then I sat back and looked at what I had done, which was measure out my entire existence in things that would fit in two crates and a duffel bag, and found that the exercise, which had comforted me for years, now felt like something else. Like packing a coffin in advance. Like agreeing in writing that a life could be made small enough to carry.

It couldn't. That was the discovery of that morning, standing in my own parlor with a pencil stub and a list. Everything that mattered about this tower — the sound the mercury makes at 3 a.m., the smell of brass polish and paraffin, the way the whole structure hums in a storm like a struck bell — none of it was portable, and none of it would survive my leaving, and I had known that when I decided to go, and I was going anyway, because a dead man's orchard had sent for me and dead men's families do not send twice.

There was a knock at the door. On an island, a knock at the lighthouse door means weather, or government, or trouble, and often all three.

* * *

Tom Wrenn came in carrying a crate of paraffin he had clearly invented as an excuse, because the boat that brought supplies wasn't due until Thursday and the crate said Thursday on it in Mrs. Trevanion's hand.

He was nineteen that winter, all wrists and opinions, with hair the color of wet rope and a way of standing in doorways as if the doorway might ask him to leave. The Board had sent him out in June as relief keeper — young men being scarce and old men being dead, what with the war — and I had expected to resent him and instead had done something worse, which was grow accustomed to him. He learned the clockwork fast. He learned to keep his mouth shut during a lamp change faster. By October he was anticipating my orders before I gave them, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like being understood, a sensation so foreign I nearly reported it as a malfunction.

"You're leaving," he said.

"I'm going to the mainland. There's a passage Friday."

"For how long?"

"Indefinite."

He set the crate down with exaggerated care, the way people do when they're buying time to think. "Who keeps the light?"

"The Board will send someone."

"They'll send someone." He laughed, one note, no humor in it. "They'll send Mr. Coad, probably. Mr. Coad fell asleep on watch twice at Round Island and they only moved him because he snored loud enough to hear in the fog signal."

"That's the Board's affair."

"It'll be your affair when a steamer puts her bow through the Wolf Rock because Mr. Coad was dreaming about his dinner." He had gone pink. Tom went pink easily; it was one of the hundred things about him that reminded me of no one, which is to say it reminded me of himself, which is a rarer thing than you'd think. "Mrs. Thackeray. Xanthe." Nobody called me Xanthe. Elias had, and hearing it in Tom's mouth was like hearing a key turn in a lock I'd thought rusted shut. "Take me with you."

"No."

"You haven't thought about it."

"I don't need to think about it. This is a station, not a school. And whatever you think you're proposing, it isn't proper, and it isn't sensible, and the answer is no."

"What I'm proposing," he said, "is that you can't carry all that yourself." He nodded at the crates I hadn't started packing yet, because he had apparently deduced their existence from the way I was standing near them, which should have alarmed me more than it did. "The typewriter alone. And you'll want someone on the mainland who knows how a light works, if it comes to that. If whatever's in that letter—"

My face must have done something, because he stopped.

"There is a letter," he said quietly.

"There are many letters in the world, Tom."

"Mrs. Trevanion says you've had one from a solicitor. She says it came inside another envelope. She says—" He faltered under my look. "She says the old envelope had a crest on it."

Mrs. Trevanion. I added her name to a mental ledger that was getting crowded. "Gossip travels faster than mail out here. It's the only cargo that doesn't pay freight."

"I'm not gossiping. I'm asking."

"And I'm declining to answer. Which is also free."

He didn't move. That was the thing about Tom Wrenn that winter; he had a stubbornness with no architecture under it, no reason, no doctrine, just a plain animal refusal to be dismissed. I had seen him hold a lamp change through a rising gale with his fingers bleeding because I told him the job needed finishing, and I had privately thought: whoever gets this boy in their life has bought more than they know.

"Where are you even going?" he asked.

"Orchard country. Inland."

"Inland," he repeated, as if I'd said the moon. Tom was island-born; I suspected he regarded the interior of Cornwall as a kind of mythological realm, full of trees and other hazards. "Doing what?"

"Seeing about property."

"Whose property?"

"Mine, apparently." I said it before I could stop myself, and immediately regretted it, because his whole face changed — the pink drained, the eyes went wide, and I watched nineteen years of orphanhood and half-pay and borrowed bunks reorganize themselves around a single fact: she has a place. She has land. She has a somewhere that isn't a rock.

"That's yours?" he said. "Land? Actual land?"

"According to a piece of paper I may yet burn."

"You won't burn it." He said it with total certainty, and I hated him a little, because he was right. "You keep things. That's — that's what you do. You've kept this light running for two years with half the glass cracked and no budget and a relief keeper who talks too much. You're not going to burn the one letter anybody ever wrote you on purpose."

The room was very quiet. Down below us, the sea worked at the rocks with its patient hatred, and above us the lens turned, and between those two motions I stood in my dead husband's coatless parlor and understood that the conversation had left my control entirely.

"I'll say it once more," I said. "No."

"Yes, ma'am." He picked up the crate. At the door he paused, and did not turn around, which was somehow worse. "Friday's boat leaves at seven. I'll be on the quay at half six. If you sail without me, I'll walk inland and find you. I'm told you can't get lost in orchard country. Everything smells the same for miles."

He was gone before I could construct the sentence that would end it properly. I stood holding my pencil list, this inventory of a portable life, and read it again top to bottom, and understood that I had left something off it.

I had been counting things. I had not been counting people, because people were not portable — that had been my rule since Elias died, since before, maybe, since the day I married into a family that treated its members like freight to be shipped away. People sink. People desert. People inform, and betray, and stop writing, and die in boats. Things at least stay where you put them.

But the boy would stand on that quay at half six. I knew it the way I knew the barometer's lies. And I knew — with the same cold, clear, infuriating clarity the letter had given me, the clarity that had started all this — that I would not sail at seven.

I would sail at five minutes past, so it wouldn't look like I'd waited.

* * *

That evening I typed the log in Elias's cadence, because the log deserved the truth and I could only tell the truth in his rhythm:

*Wind backing NW, moderating. Sea decreasing. Lamp extinguished 07:14. Keeper preparing for transfer of station. Relief: uncertain.*

I stopped. Looked at the page. Typed:

*Relief: Tom Wrenn, provisional.*

Then I sat for a long while with my hands in my lap, listening to the tower settle around me, forty-one steps of iron and stone that had been my whole country for eleven years, and admitted the thing the inventory had been circling all day: I was not leaving because the letter summoned me.

I was leaving because for the first time since the flag came down the stairwell, someone had knocked on my door wanting in, and I had discovered that solitude, which I had worn like Elias's coat — too big, wrong shape, kept anyway — had begun to chafe.

Out of shadow into light, said the crest on the envelope sleeping in the tin.

I blew out the lamp, and went down to pack the heavy things first.
