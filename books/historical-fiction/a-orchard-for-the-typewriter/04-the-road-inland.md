## 4. The Road Inland

Tom Wrenn had never been off the island before, not properly. There had been the supply boat to Penzance twice a year, a few hours of crates and coal and back again, and once, when he was eleven, a trip to the mainland doctor that he mostly remembered as the smell of carbolic and his mother's hand too tight on his wrist. But this was different. This was leaving with intent, with luggage, with a woman who had packed her dead husband's typewriter in a fish crate padded with wool socks.

He carried the crate. It was heavier than it looked, or maybe it wasn't, maybe the weight was something else entirely, something Tom had no word for yet at nineteen years old. Mrs. Thackeray walked ahead of him down the cliff path with Elias's coat folded over her arm like a sleeping thing, and she did not look back at the tower even once, which Tom thought was either very brave or very terrible, and he couldn't decide which.

The relief keeper's gig met them at the landing slip — old Mr. Curnow, who smelled of tar and peppermints, and who asked no questions because asking questions was not what Cornish boatmen did. The sea was flat as poured pewter, which Tom privately considered an insult after three years of gales. You wanted weather for a departure. You wanted the island to fight you a little, so leaving felt earned. Instead it just let you go, quietly, the way a door lets go of a hand.

Mrs. Thackeray stood at the stern the whole crossing and did not speak. Tom counted things to keep himself steady: eleven gulls, four buoys, one church spire rising out of Penzance like a needle through cloth. When the mainland came close enough to smell — coal smoke and horse dung and salt rot — she finally turned away from the water and looked inland, and her face did something Tom had never seen it do.

It went young.

Not soft, exactly. Younger. Like someone had taken ten years off it without asking permission. She looked at the green country rolling up from the harbor and Tom realized, with a small shock, that he had never once thought of her as having been anywhere else. She was the lighthouse. She was the lamp room and the log book and the kettle always just boiled. He had been on the island two years and in all that time he had never imagined her standing in a field.

Now here she was, about to stand in several.

* * *

The train from Penzance went east along the coast first, then turned inland where the sea gave up on Cornwall and the land started doing whatever it liked. Tom had the window seat because Mrs. Thackeray said she preferred the aisle, though he suspected she preferred watching him watch everything, which was its own kind of kindness.

The typewriter crate rode on the rack above their heads like a passenger. Every time the train took a curve, Tom flinched up at it, and every time, Mrs. Thackeray said, without looking, "It's fine, Tom."

"How do you know?"

"Because I packed it."

That was all. That was the whole conversation, and somehow it settled him more than a longer one would have. She was a woman who knew how things were packed. He'd learned that much in two winters — that she measured the world in what could be carried, that she kept lists, that nothing in the lighthouse was ever lost because nothing in the lighthouse was ever unaccounted for. Spare lenses' worth of brass and glass, catalogued. Elias's coat, brushed and folded. A tin of tea, weighed to the ounce. If the world ended tomorrow, Mrs. Thackeray could tell you exactly how much of it she'd have gotten into the boat.

The carriage emptied as they went. By Truro there were only three other people: a farmer asleep against his own hat, two women with baskets of something that smelled of yeast, and a boy in uniform who got off without waking anyone, which seemed to Tom like a trick the war taught people — how to leave a place without disturbing it.

At every station, the name boards went by. Tom read them because reading them was free and he was hungry for names: **CHACEWATER. TRURO. FALMOUTH BRANCH. PROBUS. GRAMPOUND ROAD.**

And then, somewhere past Grampound, a sign swung past the window and Mrs. Thackeray flinched.

It was a small thing. Her shoulders came up half an inch and came down again, quick, like a bird startled off a wire. Tom wouldn't have caught it if he hadn't spent two years learning to read her moods by lamplight, the way you learn to read weather off water. He looked out the window to see what had done it.

**THACKERAY LANE**, said a painted fingerpost at a crossroads, already going grey with weather.

Just a lane. Some farmer's name on some road, probably no relation at all. Half of Cornwall was related to the other half; names got scattered across signposts like seed. But she flinched, and then at the next station she flinched again — **THACKERAY HALT**, a platform so small it had a bench and a bucket and nothing else — and by the third time, Tom understood that this was not coincidence. The name was following them inland. Or rather: they were traveling into the country where the name had come from, the way a river travels back toward its spring.

"You knew," he said. It came out wrong, too blunt, the way his words always came out wrong when he'd been holding them too long. "You knew it was near here. The estate."

"I knew the county." She was looking straight ahead, hands folded on Elias's coat. "I hadn't let myself know more than that."

"What does that mean?"

"It means there's a difference between knowing a thing and letting yourself know it." She glanced at him then, and her eyes had the lamp-room look, the one that meant she was measuring something. "You'll understand when you're older, Tom. Or you won't, and that's fine too."

He was nineteen and this was intolerable. But he let it be, because the train was slowing, and because the conductor was calling out a station name that made both of them sit forward:

**THACKERAY ORCHARD. HALT.**

There was actually a halt named for them. For her. Tom watched her hear it, watched the word *Thackeray* land on her like a wave she'd been counting the sets for, and she didn't flinch this time. She went still instead, completely still, the way the sea goes still before it decides what it's going to do.

"That's us," she said, and stood, and reached up for the typewriter crate before Tom could get to it.

* * *

The halt was a plank platform in the middle of orchard country, and the air hit Tom like a wall of sweetness. He'd lived his whole life in salt and gull-cry and the iron smell of the lamp room, and he had not known that air could taste like this — apples gone over, fermenting on the ground somewhere out of sight, and underneath it cut grass and woodsmoke and something floral he had no name for. It was almost too much. It was like being shouted at by a smell.

"Is it always like this?" he asked.

"In harvest, yes." Mrs. Thackeray set the crate down and looked around herself slowly, taking inventory the way she took inventory of everything. "Elias talked about it. He grew up here, you know. Before the light."

Tom hadn't known. Nobody on the island talked about before. Before was a country everyone had emigrated from and nobody sent letters back to.

"He said the trees were so old they'd stopped being trees." She was walking now, down the platform steps onto a rutted lane, and Tom followed with the crate. "He said they were more like buildings. Rooms. That you could get lost in the orchard the way you'd get lost in a house, and that his grandfather had hung lanterns in the biggest trees so the pickers could work after dark."

"Did they?"

"Work after dark?" She considered. "He never said. Elias left out as much as he put in. I used to think that was modesty." Something crossed her face then, quick as the flinch at the signposts, but deeper. "Now I think it might have been something else."

The lane climbed between hedgerows higher than Tom's head, knitted tight with blackthorn and briar, and the light came down through it green and dappled, moving on the ground like water. Twice they passed gates, and twice Mrs. Thackeray stopped and read the nameplate on the pillar, and twice the nameplate said THACKERAY, and twice her jaw tightened in a way she clearly believed Tom couldn't see.

He could see. He said nothing. He was learning that from her too.

The third gate was iron, and bigger, and the pillars were topped with stone apples, each one furred with moss so they looked like fruit gone over for good. The gate itself stood open — not broken open, not sagging, but deliberately, carefully ajar, which was somehow worse. A gate left open is a message. Tom just couldn't read it yet.

Beyond it, the drive ran uphill under an avenue of trees so large and so old that Tom stopped walking. He had seen oaks before, from trains, from postcards. He had not seen trees like these — apple trees grown to the size of churchyard yews, trunks furrowed and split, limbs propped on crutches of rotten timber where some long-dead gardener had tried to hold the weight of the world up off the ground. And everywhere, everywhere, apples. Fallen apples in drifts deep enough to wade through, brown and gold and wasp-drunk, sweetening the air until it was thick enough to chew.

Nobody had picked any of it. Years of it. Maybe decades.

"It's gone wild," Tom said, stupidly, because someone had to say something.

"Yes." Mrs. Thackeray stood in the middle of the drive with the coat over her arm and the wind moving her hair loose from its pins, and she looked up into those enormous trees the way she used to look up into the lamp — checking, assessing, finding what needed tending. Then her gaze came down and traveled along the drive to the house, and Tom watched the assessment change.

Because the house was ruined.

Not collapsed — not quite — but slumped, the way a tired man slumps in a chair. Slates missing in rows like teeth pulled. One wing darkened with ivy that had gotten ambitious. Windows either boarded or blank. And smoke — that was the thing that stopped them both — a thin grey thread of smoke rising from behind the house, from a chimney that had no business smoking, because nobody was supposed to be living here.

Mrs. Thackeray went very quiet. Tom knew that quiet. It was the quiet she kept during gales, the one that came right before she gave an order.

"Someone's home," he said.

"Someone is." She shifted Elias's coat to her other arm. "Well. It's my house, apparently. Let's go and meet my household."

She said it lightly, but Tom heard what was under it, the same way he'd heard the flinch under the silence at every signpost since Grampound: that the name on the gate and the name on the halt and the name on the deed were all the same name, and none of them were hers by choice, and every mile inland had cost her something he couldn't count and she refused to itemize.

They went up the drive together, Tom carrying the typewriter, Mrs. Thackeray carrying the coat, and the fallen apples crunching under their boots loud as breaking glass. Halfway up, she stopped once more and looked back — not at the house, but down the avenue, back toward the gate and the lane and the rail line beyond, back the way they'd come.

Checking the route out, Tom thought. Counting what can be carried. Measuring the door.

Then she turned around and walked on, and the smoke-thread rose thin and grey against the sky, and somewhere behind the ruined house a dog began to bark — or something that was almost a dog, low and irregular, more warning than welcome — and a figure detached itself from the shadow of the great trees and stood waiting for them among the drifted apples, a small bent shape with a hoe in one hand, planted in the middle of the drive like something that had grown there and intended to go on growing whether anyone approved or not.

Mrs. Thackeray slowed. Beside her, Tom felt her draw one long breath, the breath she took before weather.

"Well," she said, mostly to herself. "So the inheritance has opinions."

The figure raised the hoe an inch, not quite a greeting, not quite anything else, and waited.
