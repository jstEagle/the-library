## 5. An Orchard Gone Wild

The gate was the first thing that told me the truth.

Solicitors' letters can say *estate* and *grounds* and *historic orchard* all they like; a gate doesn't lie. This one hung off its lower hinge like a man leaning on a bar at closing time, and beyond it the drive had gone to grass so tall it hissed against the axles of Tom's borrowed cart. I had imagined — I will admit it now, since I admitted nothing then — gravel raked smooth, walls whitewashed, rows of trees standing to attention the way Elias had stood. What I got instead was a green riot, a country gone feral, apple trees grown into each other until you couldn't tell where one ended and the next began.

"Blimey," Tom said, from the cart seat beside me. He said it the way other men swear.

I didn't answer. I was doing what I do, which is measuring. The wall: eight feet once, now shouldered down in places by ivy thick as my wrist. The house ahead, half-hidden behind a screen of self-sown sycamores: two storeys, granite, a slate roof holding maybe sixty percent of its slates. Chimney standing. That counted for something. On an island you learn to count what stands.

The typewriter case sat between my boots on the floor of the cart, wrapped in oilcloth, and I put one hand on it without deciding to. It had made the whole journey from the light that way — through the ferry crossing where the swell ran high enough to make the crew laugh nervously, up the coast road, through towns whose signposts kept saying THACKERAY LANE and THACKERAY HILL as if the name were a common weed here instead of a thing I'd only ever known as a word on a marriage certificate and a headstone.

Tom had watched me flinch at every one of those signs. He was kind enough not to watch me now.

We came around the sycamores and the house opened out in front of us, and that was when I saw we were not alone.

There were washing lines strung between two apple trees — actual laundry, shirts and a pair of enormous men's trousers and small clothes no bigger than my spread hand. There was a goat tethered to a broken plough. There was smoke rising from a chimney I'd have sworn was cold from the road, which meant there was a fire lit inside a house that belonged, according to a letter sealed twice over, to me.

And there was a boy.

He stood at the edge of the drive with a bucket in each hand, water slopping over the rims, staring at us with the fixed, unblinking attention of a heron. Eight years old, perhaps nine. Barefoot. His hair cut badly, in the way hair gets cut by someone who is not a barber and does not love scissors. He did not run. He did not call out. He set down both buckets with great deliberation, never taking his eyes off the cart, and then he turned and ran — toward the house, silent as a bird going up.

"That'll bring the others," Tom said. "Should I—"

"Leave the horse," I said. "Bring the typewriter."

It sounds absurd, written down. A woman walking into a disputed house carrying a typewriter like a sidearm. But I had spent four years learning that the machine was the steadiest thing in any room, including rooms with storms in them, and I wanted it near my hands.

* * *

They came out to meet us, or to meet us off, which is not the same thing.

The old woman came first, and she came like weather. She was small and square and dressed in a man's coat cut down badly, and she walked across her own ruined garden — because whoever she was, it was hers in every way but paper — at a pace that dared us to keep up. Her face was brown and seamed and set in an expression I recognized from church windows: righteous displeasure, awaiting its subject.

"You'll be the Thackeray woman," she said. Not a question. She stopped six feet away and looked me over the way I'd looked over the gate. "Took you long enough."

"I only learned last week that the place existed."

"Aye." Something moved behind her eyes — too quick to name. "Letters are like that in this family. Slow, or burned."

"I'm Xanthe Thackeray. My husband was Elias Thackeray. His father was—"

"I know who his father was." She said it flatly, and then, as if remembering manners were a crop she'd let go fallow: "Prudence Coad. Prue. Forty years in service to this house, and I'm still in service to it, whatever the papers say. You'll be wanting to turn us out."

It was not a question either. I was beginning to understand that questions, in this place, were luxuries.

"I'll be wanting," I said, "to see the estate I've inherited. And to know who's living on it."

"Then look." Prue turned and swept one arm at the whole wild acreage, the fallen walls, the trees grown together like conspirators. "Look well, Mrs. Thackeray, because nobody else has in seven years. Look at what your family left."

Behind her, half in the shadow of the doorway, stood a man I hadn't seen come out. Young — younger than me, though he carried himself older, shoulders drawn in like a man perpetually making himself smaller. Dark hair, unshaved, hands that wouldn't stay still. His eyes went from me to Tom to the cart and back with a quickness that wasn't curiosity. It was arithmetic. I knew that look. Keepers learn it: the look of a man calculating distances and exits.

"Fen," Prue said, without turning. "Stop skulking and help with the cart."

"I'm not skulking."

"You're standing like a man about to leg it, which is worse."

He flushed dark and came forward, and took the handles of the trunk Tom was lifting, and for a moment the three of us were arranged around my belongings like a tableau: the widow, the keeper's boy, the stranger with the restless hands. Fen's grip on the trunk handle was white-knuckled. Not from weight. From something else.

"The boy," I said. "Whose is he?"

Nobody answered fast enough, and the silence itself answered.

"He's Ollie," Prue said finally. "He's ours. That's all the answer there is today."

* * *

Inside, the house was worse than outside and better than outside, both at once.

Worse: the ballroom — someone had built this house with a ballroom, in some fat century — stood open to the sky through a collapsed corner of roof, and grass grew in the parquet, ankle-high, silvered with seed heads. Rain had gotten into the library and swelled the shelves until books burst their spines like overripe fruit. Plaster hung from ceilings in sheets, curled like birch bark.

Better: the kitchen was alive. Scrubbed table, blackened range breathing heat, bundles of herbs drying from a beam, a row of jam jars on the sill with labels in a careful old-fashioned hand. Someone had fought for this one room the way I fought for the lamp room — chosen a defensible position and held it. I felt an unwilling kinship with that kitchen before I'd been in it five minutes.

Ollie sat at the far end of the table, watching me over the top of a bowl of bread and milk. When I looked directly at him he looked away, but not quickly enough to hide that he'd been studying me — my coat, my hands, the typewriter case I'd set down within reach of my chair.

"Does he talk?" I asked Prue.

"He talks plenty," she said. "Just not out loud." She said it with a finality that closed the subject like a door.

Fen had taken the trunk to the foot of the stairs and then, oddly, hadn't left. He hovered near the range, feeding it splits of wood, and every few minutes his eyes came back to me the way a tongue comes back to a broken tooth. Finally he said, to the fire rather than to me:

"They said in the village there'd be a new mistress coming. Soldiery at the inn had a bet on whether you'd sell straight off."

"And what did you bet?"

He almost smiled. It transformed his face and then vanished. "That you'd want to see the ledgers first."

The word went through me like a fishhook. Ledgers. I had not mentioned ledgers to anyone. I had barely thought the word myself — and yet here was a squatter in a ruined house telling me my own instincts back.

"Why would I want to see ledgers?"

"Because you're a keeper's wife." He shrugged, elaborate and careless. "Keepers write everything down. Everyone knows that about you lot."

It was true, and it was also a deflection, and I let it pass the way you let a suspicious vessel pass in fog — logged, marked, tracked.

* * *

I spent the afternoon walking the grounds alone, because there are things you cannot assess with an audience.

The orchard had been, once, magnificent. You could read it under the ruin the way you read a wreck under water: rows, spacing, the ghost of order. Two hundred trees, maybe more, most of them now locked in mutual strangulation with ivy and elder. Half bore fruit anyway — small, hard, windfall-scattered apples that the wasps had claimed and the birds had argued over. Some had died standing, gray and armless. One enormous specimen near the walled garden had split down the middle of its trunk and both halves had gone on living, growing away from each other, which seemed less like botany than like a message.

At the far boundary I found the cove path.

It dropped steeply through gorse to a shingle beach, small, cupped in cliffs, invisible from the sea until you were nearly on top of it. A boat would need local knowledge to find it after dark. Standing there with the wind pushing at my back, I understood the geography of the place in my body before my mind caught up: this was a door. Whatever the Thackerays had been besides farmers — and a letter sealed twice, and a solicitor's careful language, and forty years of a servant's bitterness all suggested they had been something else — this cove was how it came and went.

There was rust on the rocks below. Old iron, orange-brown, half-buried. I climbed down far enough to touch it: a ring-bolt, set deep in the rock, worn bright on its inner curve by rope. Worn bright meant used often. Used often meant not one season's smuggling but a habit, a trade, a generation of it.

I stood on that shingle with the tide coming in and felt the shape of the inheritance change under my feet. An hour ago it had been a ruin and a legal nuisance. Now it was a question, and questions are heavier than ruins.

When I climbed back up, Ollie was sitting on the wall above the path, watching me. He must have followed me the whole way. In his lap he held a windfall apple, polished bright against his shirt, and as I passed him he held it up — not offering it exactly. Showing it to me. As if to say: *this is what grows here. This is what survives.*

I took it. His face did something complicated and then went blank again, and he slid off the wall and was gone through the trees, soundless.

* * *

Supper was bread, cheese from a cloth-wrapped wedge that tasted of the goat's opinion of us all, stewed apples, tea. Four of us and the boy around the scrubbed table, and the candle between us making everyone's face into someone else's.

"We'll need terms," I said, when the plates were cleared. "If I don't turn you out — and I haven't decided that I won't — there will be terms. Rent, work, accounts. I'll want the estate office opened and everything in it catalogued."

Prue's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Catalogued," she repeated.

"Inventoried. Recorded. It's how I keep anything — a lighthouse, an orchard, a household. Everything entered, dated, signed." I heard my own voice take on the cadence I used for log entries and didn't fight it. "No one loses a home while I hold the pen. But no one hides from it either."

The silence had texture. Fen was looking at the table. Prue was looking at me with an expression I couldn't file — anger wearing grief's clothing, or grief wearing anger's.

"Seven years," Prue said at last, quietly, "I've kept what I could keep. Trees pruned where I could reach. The boy fed. The roof patched over the rooms that mattered. And every year on the eleventh of October I've picked the earliest apples off the tree by the south wall and laid them on the step of the potting shed, and no one has ever asked me why, and I have never told." She set the cup down with a click. "You come here with a typewriter and talk of terms, Mrs. Thackeray. Your family buried my whole life in that office and locked the door, and you want to *catalogue*."

"Then show me what's worth keeping," I said. "You've held this place for seven years. That makes you the only expert in it. I'd rather have your knowledge than your obedience."

Something flickered across her face — the undertow beneath the hostility, the thing I'd been sensing all day without being able to name it. Recognition, maybe. Two women who had each kept a light burning in a structure that everyone else had abandoned, waiting for relief that came late or not at all.

She didn't soften. But she refilled my cup, which among her kind is a treaty.

It was Fen who broke the evening open. He'd been quiet so long I'd nearly stopped tracking him, and then he said, carefully, each word placed like a foot on ice:

"The office. Is it — has it been opened? Since the old master died?"

"Not by me. Why?"

"No reason." Too fast. "Only rats. Old houses, rats get everywhere. Be a shame if—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Be a shame if things were spoiled."

I looked at him across the candle and he looked back, and for one naked moment neither of us pretended. He was afraid of that room. Specifically, physically, visibly afraid of it — a man who had done arithmetic on our arrival now doing different arithmetic, and getting a number he didn't like.

And underneath my own caution, answering it like a second current under a first, was the thing I had been refusing to feel since the gate: that these three — the bitter keeper, the frightened drifter, the silent boy with the polished apple — were not strangers squatting in my inheritance. They were its garrison. Seven years, Prue had held it. However wrong the arrangement, however much suspicion sat between us like a third person at the table, they had kept something alive here that the world had written off.

I knew what that cost. I had paid it in glass and solitude for four years.

"I'll sleep in the east room tonight," I said, standing. "Tomorrow I'll walk every row of the orchard with you, Prue, and you'll tell me what each tree is. And tomorrow night—" I put my hand on the typewriter case, "—I'll begin the record."

Prue snorted. Fen flinched. Ollie, from the shadows by the dresser, watched me with those heron's eyes, and just before I reached the door he did a strange thing: he tapped the tabletop twice, softly, deliberately, like a signal, like a knock from inside a locked room.

Behind me, I heard Fen whisper, urgent and low: "Not the office, Prue. Whatever she says — not the office."

And Prue answered him, weary as winter: "Boy, she's a Thackeray. Sooner or later she finds everything."
