## 6. Terms of Tenancy

Forty years I'd kept that kitchen, and in all that time I'd learned one thing worth passing on: you can tell everything about a family by what they keep in the drawers nearest the stove. The Thackerays kept string, a broken barometer, and forty years of grudges, and I dusted around every one of them without comment.

The widow came down at seven, washed and buttoned to the throat like Sunday even though it was Thursday, and stood in the middle of my kitchen as if she were waiting for someone to tell her where she was allowed to stand.

"You'll want tea," I said. It wasn't a question. Nobody wants tea at that hour; everybody needs it.

"Thank you." She watched me work the range. "You've been here a long time."

"Longer than the apples."

She didn't smile at that. I'd have liked her better if she had. There's a kind of person who can't take a joke from a servant, and there's another kind who can't hear anything true from one either, and I hadn't yet worked out which she was.

"I found your name in the household book," she said. "Prudence Curnow. Entered 1877."

"Prue," I said. "Nobody's called me Prudence since Mrs. Thackeray the elder died, and she only did it when she wanted something."

"What did she want?"

I poured the tea. Set it down in front of her hard enough to slop. "Everything," I said. "Same as all of them."

* * *

She drank her tea like a woman taking medicine, which I approved of, and then she said, "Mrs. Curnow — Prue — I need to understand what happened here. The solicitor's letter named me heir. But this house—" She looked around at my kitchen, at the damp bloom on the ceiling above the window, at the garden gate hanging off its hinge since 1913 because nobody with money left to fix it. "This house has been let go for years. If I was always the heir, why did no one write? Why did no one *tell* me?"

There it was. The question I'd been waiting three days for her to ask, and now that she'd asked it I wished she hadn't, because the answer was a wound I'd been keeping clean under bandages of routine for six years.

"Sit down proper," I said, and pulled out the chair opposite hers, the servants' side of the table, and sat myself, which I hadn't done across from a mistress of this house since before the war. "And I'll tell you about letters."

* * *

Old Mr. Thackeray — that's Xanthe's father-in-law, Charles, though everyone called him the Squire behind his back and worse in front of it — was a man who governed his house by post. He wrote to his sons instead of speaking to them. He wrote to his brother Jago, who ran the boats out of the cove. He wrote to his wife when she was in the next room. I used to say if he could've written himself into heaven instead of dying his way there, he'd have done it.

Two sons. Elias, the younger, who went off to keep lighthouses because he couldn't stomach what went on in the orchard cellars — and don't look surprised, missus, we'll come to the cellars. And the elder, Martyn, who stayed, and who was everything his father wanted and less than his father needed.

When the Squire took ill — the winter of '11, it was — he had me fetch pen and paper, and he wrote a letter, and he sealed it, and he gave it to me with his own two hands, shaking like the last apple on a November tree.

"This is the succession," he said. "It names who keeps the orchard after me. When I'm gone, Prue, you put it in the office drawer, and whoever comes asking gets it unopened."

"And who came asking?" Xanthe said. Her voice had gone quiet the way the sea goes quiet before it decides.

"Martyn came first. Within the hour, near enough. He'd been listening at the study door, which he'd done since he was six and never got better at. He held out his hand for it."

"And?"

"And I gave it to him. Unopened, as instructed." I looked her full in the face, because this part deserved looking-at. "And then I watched him carry it to the study fire and burn it. Never broke the seal. Never read a word. Fed it to the flames whole, and stood there while it burned, and then he turned round and said, *'That's settled, then,'* and rang for brandy."

The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs Fen moved a chair, careful as a thief.

"Why?" she asked. "Why would he burn it unread?"

"Because he knew," I said. "Whatever his father wrote, Martyn knew it wasn't him. A man only burns a letter unread when he already knows what's inside and can't bear to have it witnessed."

* * *

She set her cup down. Her hands were steady, but I noticed she'd wrapped them around the cup first, holding the heat, the way people do when they're borrowing courage from crockery.

"So Martyn kept the estate," she said.

"Martyn kept the estate and ran it into the ground and into the sea, and when he drowned—"

"Drowned? I thought—"

"He went out with Jago's boat, the last run before the war made the cove too hot. That boat never came back. All hands. The Squire's brother among them, and Martyn, and six good men whose names are on nothing anywhere except in my head." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the way it does. "After that the solicitors circled like gulls over a gutted fish, and nobody could produce any paper saying who inherited what, because the paper was ash, and Martyn's papers were 'lost' — lost being the word men use for destroyed on purpose. And the estate sat. And I stayed. Because somebody had to feed the boy."

"Ollie," she said. "Whose child is he?"

"That," I said, "is the second letter."

* * *

I got up and fetched it. I'd sworn I wouldn't — I'd told Fen we'd say nothing, show nothing, until we knew whether she was a Thackeray or just married to one — but sitting across from her, watching her hold that teacup like it might bolt, I remembered the night the Squire died, and how I'd promised him, God forgive me, promises I'd spent six years regretting.

The second letter was in the tin where I kept the Christmas candles, under the floorboard behind the flour bin, wrapped in oilcloth. I put it on the table between us.

It was sealed twice. Once with wax, once with a strip of brown paper pasted over, and on the brown paper, in the Squire's crabbed hand: *To be opened only by the one the orchard chooses.*

Xanthe stared at it. "What does that mean?"

"It means an old man playing riddles with other people's lives," I said. "He wrote it the same night as the first one. Two letters. One naming the heir plain, which Martyn burned. And this one, which he told me — his dying words to me, near enough — was the *true* one. He said the first letter was for the lawyers and the second was for the orchard. He said, *'Prue, when the right one comes, you'll know, because they'll ask about the trees.'*"

Her head came up sharp. "The trees?"

"Aye. Did you ask about the trees?"

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "On the road, coming up from the station. I asked the carrier what varieties were still standing. Cornish Aromatic. Pig's Nose. Whatever survived."

I sat back. Forty years in that kitchen, and my knees cracked doing it, and I let them.

"The Squire loved those old apples more than he loved either of his sons," I said. "That's not a secret, that's just a fact. So he built his riddle out of them. And I've sat here six years with that letter under the flour bin, waiting for somebody to ask about the trees, telling myself every stranger who came up the drive was a bailiff or a spy or Halloway's man sent to dig it out of me." I pushed it toward her. "Well. Here's the thing, missus. I don't know yet if you're the right one. I know what you asked. I don't know what you'll do."

"Do about what?"

"About *this.*" I tapped the letter. "Because that seal isn't just wax. It's everything that happened in the cellars, and the boats, and the men who didn't come back, and the reason the shipping ledgers have pages cut out of them like teeth knocked from a mouth. You open that, you inherit all of it. Not the land. The *truth*. And truth, in this county, is the one crop that gets you shot."

* * *

She didn't touch it. I'll give her that — she didn't snatch at it the way Martyn would have, or weep over it the way Elias would have, or weigh it in his hand calculating the price the way the Squire himself would have done. She just looked at it, and then she looked at me, and her eyes were her dead husband's eyes, and that nearly finished me.

"Elias," I said, before I could stop myself. "Was he — at the end —"

"He kept the light," she said simply. "Right up to the end. He said a light doesn't choose what it shows. It just shows it."

I laughed. First time in months, I expect, and it came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's coat. "Aye. That's Elias. His father wrote letters because he was afraid of being seen. Elias climbed a hundred feet every night to be seen by everybody." I wiped my eyes with the heel of my hand. "You see now why I waited. The last letter naming an heir was burned unread, and the man who burned it is at the bottom of the cove, and I'm an old woman with two secrets in her kitchen and a deserter sleeping over her head. Loyalty's a funny crop, missus. It grows toward whoever waters it."

"And who have you watered?"

"The ones who needed it," I said. "Every soul this orchard ever sheltered. That's my tenancy. Forty years and no paper for it, same as Ollie's mother had no paper, same as the drowned men have no graves with names. Paper's what the Thackerays used against people. I'm asking you, before you break that seal, to tell me you're not here for the paper."

The fire ticked. Upstairs, silence — the deliberate kind, the listening kind.

Xanthe reached out. Not for the letter. For my hand, lying on the table beside the flour tin, rough as sacking. She held it the way you hold something you're afraid of breaking, and she said:

"I'm here for the record, Prue. Whatever's true. All of it. Even if it hangs someone I share blood with — even if it hangs me."

And Lord help me, that was exactly the wrong answer and exactly the right answer both, and I knew it the way I know weather.

I slid the letter the last inch across the table to her. "Then open it," I said. "But not in my kitchen. Take it to the study, where the Squire wrote it, and read it standing up, because what's in it will want you on your feet."

She rose, letter in hand, and paused at the door — and it was Fen's voice that stopped us both, from the top of the stairs, quiet and ragged:

"Don't read it aloud," he said. "Whatever it says. Some words, once they're loose in a house, you can't put back."
