## 7. The Locked Potting Shed

The rain stopped sometime before dawn, and by the time I came down the back stairs the garden was steaming. Mist stood in the orchard rows like a congregation that had been waiting all night for someone to open the church.

I had not slept well. The letter was still in my coat pocket, and I had taken to carrying it the way Elias used to carry his watch — not to consult it, but to feel its weight and be reassured that some things kept their own time regardless of my attention. A solicitor's envelope inside a dead man's envelope, sealed twice over, naming me heir to ground I had never seen until three days ago. I had told no one. Not Tom, who was still on the island minding the light and writing me letters full of weather reports disguised as small talk. Not Prue, whose eyes missed nothing and who had already asked me twice, with terrible gentleness, whether I meant to stay through the winter.

I meant to find out what I had inherited. That was different from staying. I was trying very hard to keep those two things separate in my mind, the way you keep a lamp's oil separate from its flame — necessary together, fatal mixed.

I walked out into the wet grass without boots. It was a habit from the island, where the distinction between indoors and outdoors had always seemed to me a formality. The mist took me at the third row of trees. The apples hung wild and small, russeted, many of them split from the frost, and the wasps had gotten at the fallen ones so that the whole orchard smelled of cider fermenting where no one had pressed it. Forty years of harvests, Prue said, left to rot because there were no hands. Or because there were no hands willing. I was beginning to understand these might be two different sentences.

The potting shed stood at the orchard's far corner, where the ground began to fall away toward a hedge and, beyond the hedge, according to Prue's terse geography, the top of the cove path. It was a long low building of stone, roofed in slate gone green with lichen, its windows boarded — not curtained, not broken, but *boarded*, nailed from the outside with fresh pine that had not yet silvered. Everything else on this estate had been left to rot honestly. The shed alone had been maintained. Someone had spent money and trouble keeping people out of it, and had done so recently enough that the nails still shone.

I should have turned back then. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole of what I am trying to practice now. There was a moment — I can mark it precisely, the way I could once mark the exact second a light went dark on the horizon — when I stood six feet from the door and felt the wrongness of the thing come off it like cold off stone. Boarded windows. A new padlock. And behind the boards, when the wind moved, a sound I did not like: something shifting, settling, with the faintest metallic complaint, as though the shed were full of things that resented being still.

But I have spent eleven years alone with a machine that requires me to write down what is true every night, whether or not the truth is convenient, whether or not anyone will ever read it. A log does not permit you to look away. You record the ship that founders; you do not decide she is too far off to concern you. So I did not turn back. I noted the wrongness and continued toward it, which is either courage or a defect of character. Elias used to say it was both.

* * *

The padlock was the first puzzle. It was a good lock, heavy brass, the kind a farmer buys once and buries his sons with. But it was the hasp that interested me, because the hasp had been replaced too, and the staple set into the doorframe had been mounted over an older one. I put my fingers to the wood and found, beneath the newer iron, the ghost of the original fitting — and beneath that, worn into the grain, a keyhole.

Not a keyhole for the padlock. A keyhole cut directly into the door itself, old, filled with putty and painted over so many times it was nearly invisible, but unmistakably a keyhole, and shaped wrong. I mean that literally. It was not the figure-eight of a standard lever lock nor the simple slit of a rim lock. It was narrow and deep, with a lateral notch cut high on the left side, and I stood looking at it for a long moment before the recognition arrived, unwelcome, from a part of my mind I had thought was occupied with other things.

I knew that shape. Every keeper knows that shape. It was the profile of a wick-adjustment key — the long flat instrument we use to raise and trim the burner in a lens assembly, the tool Elias kept oiled and wrapped in flannel and treated with more tenderness than he ever showed his own hands. No carpenter cuts a door keyhole in the shape of a lamp key. No carpenter does it by accident.

Someone had wanted that door opened only by a person who would recognize the shape — a person of the light. My father-in-law, perhaps. Or someone who trusted him. Or someone who trusted no one else at all.

I tried my ring of keys against the padlock anyway, because method is a comfort even when it is futile. Nothing fit. The padlock was modern; my keys were mostly Elias's, and Elias's keys belonged to an island, not to this place. I stood back and considered the boards over the windows. Fresh pine, but nailed by someone in a hurry or someone who had never swung a hammer for a living — the heads sat proud, half-driven at angles, rust already bleeding streaks down the pale wood. I worked my knife under the nearest board and it came away with less argument than I expected, taking a curl of soft pine with it and leaving a gap the width of two fingers.

I put my eye to the gap.

Darkness first. Then, as my pupil adjusted, shapes. The shed was deeper than it looked from outside, running back into the hillside, and it was full — not of pots and loam and the paraphernalia of gardening, though there were some of those pushed carelessly against one wall, but of crates. Wooden crates, stacked in ranks, maybe forty of them, stenciled on their sides in faded ink. I could read only fragments through the gap and the gloom: *H.M.* on several, and what might have been a port name, and numbers. Between the crate rows, leaning against the far wall where a window's worth of light fell through a knot hole, stood objects I could not resolve — tall things, curved things, catching the grey morning light along edges that gleamed.

Metal. Brass, by the color of the gleam. A great deal of brass, standing upright, patient as a congregation of its own.

And on the nearest crate, close enough to the gap that I could see it clearly, a shipping label, pasted on and then partly torn away, deliberately, by someone who had wanted the destination unread. What remained showed the tail end of a printed line — *...to be delivered upon cessation of hostilities* — and below it, in a different hand, in pencil, in a script I did not know but which made the skin of my arms tighten anyway:

*Hold for E.T.*

E.T. Elias Thackeray. My husband, who had died two years ago on an island four hours' sail from this orchard, and who had never once, in eleven years of marriage and thousands of pages of logbook, mentioned that he held property here. Hold for E.T. Written in a hand steady enough to belong to a man who wrote such instructions often.

I stepped back from the gap. My heart was doing something I recognized from storms — that quick shallow sprint that means the body has understood danger before the mind consents to. I looked at the padlock, at the putty-filled keyhole shaped like a lamp key, at the board I had pried loose and could nail loosely back if I chose to pretend I had seen nothing.

I could choose ignorance. People did it daily. The vicar would bless them for it; the magistrate, whoever he was in these parts, would leave them in peace; Prue would go on sweeping her step and telling me nothing, and I would take my inheritance, whatever portion of it proved portable, and return to the island, and type my logs, and die as respectable as a closed book.

But the letter in my pocket had named me heir. Sole heir. Which meant that whatever was held in trust in this shed — held, apparently, for a Thackeray — was now, by paper and by law, held for me. And I had learned one thing about records in eleven years of keeping them: a thing written down waits for you. A thing locked away grows teeth while it waits.

* * *

I spent the middle of the day not thinking about the shed, which is to say I thought about nothing else. I inventoried the kitchen stores with Prue, who watched me count tins of condensed milk with an expression suggesting she could count the thoughts behind my eyes just as easily. Fen appeared at noon, as he always did, materializing near wherever work was being done with his sleeves rolled and his face arranged into the studied blankness of a man practicing at being unremarkable. He offered to clear the fallen apples from the north rows. I said yes. He went. Ollie trailed him at his usual distance of ten feet, the mute boy's silent orbit, pausing once to look back at me — and Ollie's look lingered a beat longer than usual, I thought, on the direction of the orchard's far corner.

Everyone knew about the shed. That was the day's second discovery, arriving quietly somewhere between the milk tins and the apple rows: everyone in this household knew exactly what stood locked at the orchard's edge, and everyone had agreed, without any meeting I had witnessed, that Xanthe Thackeray would not be told. Whether out of loyalty, fear, or the settled conviction that some doors are better carried shut than opened, I could not say. But I had lived alone long enough to know the temperature of a silence, and this one had weight and intention.

Fine. I had kept a light burning alone through eleven winters. I could keep my own counsel too.

Night came early, as it does in October, and with it a hard clearing sky — the wind swinging north, scrubbing the clouds away, dragging the stars out sharp. On the island I would have called it a night for the lamp. Here there was no lamp to keep, and the absence of my duty left me restless, pacing the house like a cat in a strange room. Around ten o'clock I took the storm lantern off its hook in the back passage, lit it, and let myself out.

The moon was three days off full and strong enough to throw shadows. Frost was already forming on the grass, and my breath went up in flags. The orchard at night was a different country — the trees black and gnarled, the frozen apples glowing faintly underfoot like scattered lamps, the mist gone and replaced by a clarity that felt almost accusatory. I walked the rows fast, not giving myself permission to slow down, and came out at the corner where the ground dropped toward the cove, and there was the shed, silvered with moonlight, its padlock throwing back a small bright point of reflection like a star observed through glass.

I had brought tools. I want that recorded plainly: I had planned this. In the estate office that afternoon, while sorting papers I was supposedly cataloguing, I had found a pry bar and a cold chisel, and I had put them in my coat, and I had told myself I was merely tidying. Eleven years of typing nightly logs teaches you a certain discipline about lying, chiefly that you get worse at it, not better, because you spend all day practicing precision.

I set the lantern on an upturned barrel. Its light made a yellow room of the air around the door. Then I set the chisel against the hasp screws — not the lock, I noticed; the lock I could defeat later, but the hasp plate was held by four screws, and screws turn, whereas locks must be broken, and breaking leaves evidence, and evidence invites questions. I turned the first screw with the chisel's edge and it came grudgingly, shrieking a little, loud in the frozen quiet. I stopped. Listened. The house was distant; the cove was distant; the only sound was the sea, far below, working at the rocks with its endless patience.

The second screw. The third. My breath smoked in the lantern light and my fingers were going stiff, and I thought, absurdly, of Elias trimming a wick at midnight, of how he'd taught me that the trick to any delicate work in the cold is to warm the hands first and then hurry, because haste after warmth beats patience in the cold. I hurried. The fourth screw resisted, stripped, and gave all at once so that I staggered and caught myself against the door, and the door — unfastened now except for the dangling padlock and hasp — swung inward an inch under my weight.

Warm air came out. That was the strangest thing, the thing I still think about: the shed breathed at me, dry and faintly sweet, smelling of oil and apples and old paper, as though the interior had been keeping its own season, a summer hoarded against the years.

I lifted the padlock free of the hasp and set it on the barrel beside the lantern. Then I took up the lantern, and I went in.

* * *

The crates were as I had seen them, rank upon rank, stenciled H.M., labels razored or soaked away, and between them the tall brass things I had glimpsed stood in their dozens against the walls — and now, in the lantern light, I could see what they were, and understanding arrived in stages, each one colder than the last.

Ship's fittings. Bells, binnacles, a wheel with its spokes wrapped in sacking, nameboards pried from sterns, and — I counted without wanting to — seven ship's bells, hanging from nails along the back wall like a carillon nobody rang. Each bell bore a name engraved across its crown. I read them by raising the lantern to each in turn, and I wrote them down later, and I will not set them all here, because names are heavier than lead and I was carrying enough. But the last bell I examined was smaller than the others, polished brighter than the others, maintained — someone had been keeping it clean, in secret, the way you keep a grave. And its name was one I knew from the ledgers I had begun sorting in the estate office, from the gaps in those ledgers, from the missing years Prue would not discuss and Fen pretended not to wonder about.

It was the name of the boat lost with all hands. The boat from the story Prue told in fragments and then refused to finish. The boat that had gone out of the cove one stormy night and had never come back, and whose loss had ended everything — the smuggling, the rescues, whatever this family had truly been — with a single stroke.

Below the bells, in the corner where the lamplight barely reached, stood a desk. An actual desk, oak, with a chair, and on the desk a lamp (unlit), an inkwell, a ledger bound in green cloth, and a stack of letters tied in bundles with string, dozens of bundles, hundreds of letters, addressed in a dozen hands to a dozen names — and none of them, as far as I could see in that first stunned survey, ever delivered. Undelivered post, warehoused in the dark like contraband, which in a sense it was: contraband correspondence, words seized at the border between what happened and what was allowed to be known.

And on the desk's blotter, centered, squared to the desk's edges with a precision I recognized down in my bones — the precision of a man who trimmed wicks and typed logs and believed that order was a form of love — lay a single envelope. Sealed. Unstamped. Addressed in Elias's handwriting.

To me.

I stood in that cold sweet-smelling room with the lantern shaking slightly in my hand, and I understood that I had crossed a line I could not uncross. Whatever was in these crates, whoever had hidden these bells, whatever grief or guilt or scheme had built this archive of the unsaid — it was mine now, by inheritance and by trespass both, and there would be no returning to the woman who had stood six feet from this door that morning and weighed ignorance and found it heavy.

Behind me, out in the moonlight, a floorboard creaked under a weight that was not the wind.

I turned, lantern raised, and through the doorway — silhouetted against the frost-bright grass, small and still as a carved figure on a church wall — stood Ollie, the mute boy, looking at me with an expression I had never seen on a child's face before.

He was not surprised to see me. He was waiting for me.
