## 8. Ledgers and Lies

Fen had been in the estate office three days before he understood what it was for.

Not for farming. Not for accounts, not really. It was a room built to remember things on behalf of people who preferred not to. Every surface held paper — ledgers with cracked spines, bundles tied in butcher's string, drawers that stuck and then gave with a sigh like an old man lowering himself into a chair. The air smelled of dust and tallow and, underneath it, apples gone soft in some forgotten corner years ago.

He'd volunteered for the work because volunteering was what a man did when he wanted to look useful and harmless. A deserter who kept his hands busy was a man nobody watched. Prue had raised an eyebrow at him — *you? sorting papers?* — but Xanthe Thackeray had simply said yes, thank you, start with the east wall, and that was that. She treated him the way she seemed to treat everything: as a problem that could be solved if you approached it steadily enough.

She frightened him a little, if he was honest. Not her manner — she was quiet, watchful, kind in a clipped way that cost her something each time she spent it. What frightened him was the typewriter. He'd heard it through the floor of the night he arrived, that dry insect rattle going on past midnight, and he'd lain awake thinking: *she is writing everything down.* Everything said in this house went into that machine eventually, or so it felt. And Fen's whole existence now depended on things *not* being written down.

His name wasn't Fen. His name was Corporal Edmund Fennimore, 2nd Battalion, Duke of Cornwall's Light Infantry, absent without leave since March, and if that ever reached the right desk in Bodmin he'd be shot against a wall somewhere with a chaplain reading over him and his mother getting a printed card. So he answered to Fen, and he hauled brambles, and he fixed the pump, and he sorted paper, and every morning he woke at five with his heart going like a drum in a parade and lay still until it slowed.

The office work suited him better than he expected. There was a soldier's satisfaction in it — inventory, order, things squared away. And there was this: nobody asked questions in an archive. People assumed that a man elbow-deep in old receipts had no secrets worth having.

* * *

Xanthe worked opposite him at the big desk by the window, the one with the green leather top worn pale where elbows had rested for two generations. She'd brought up a second lamp from the house and set them both burning even in daylight, because the windows were furred with grime and the November light came through grey as dishwater.

"Read me the column," she said, not looking up. "Eighteen ninety-one. Receipts."

Fen turned the ledger toward himself. The handwriting changed hand to hand down the decades — a fine copperplate early on, then a cramped business scrawl, then something looser, almost careless, near the end. He read out figures: barrels, cider pressed, wages paid, hay bought in. Numbers were safe. Numbers couldn't hang anyone.

"Slower," she said. "I'm typing."

The keys clattered. He liked watching her work, actually — the way she fed the page, the way her fingers moved without her looking, the way she'd stop mid-line when something snagged her attention and sit very still, like a heron deciding whether the water held anything.

It was on the third day, in the shipping ledgers, that the water held something.

These were different books from the farm accounts. Bigger, broader, bound in blue cloth gone grey at the corners. *Thackeray Orchard — Shipping & Consignment,* stamped on the spines, though half the entries inside had nothing to do with apples. Boat hire. Wharfage fees at ports Fen didn't know orchard fruit ever travelled to. Payments marked only with initials. He noted it the way you note weather — filed it, kept working — because a man hiding a crime learns to notice other people's without staring.

Then he came to 1893.

There was a gap. Not a torn-out page — he knew torn pages, he'd seen enough service records doctored to know how a page leaves a binding, the stub left behind, the ghost of glue. This was cleaner than that. Someone had taken a blade to the gutter of the book, low down near the spine, and lifted four pages out whole. The cut was so fine you'd miss it unless your thumb caught the edge, which his thumb did, and once it caught he couldn't stop feeling it — that whisper of steel-work under the paper, smooth as a scar.

"Fen." Xanthe's voice. "You've stopped reading."

"There's something here," he said, and heard how carefully he said it, and hated how carefully he said it, because careful was the voice of a man with something to lose.

She came around the desk. She took the book from him gently, the way you take something from a stranger you don't yet trust, and ran her own thumb along the gutter. Her face did nothing at all. That was the frightening thing about her — grief or suspicion or fear, it all went somewhere behind her eyes and stayed there, and you were left looking at a calm surface and wondering what moved underneath.

"Razored," she said. "Not torn. Torn is anger. This is patience."

"How many?"

They counted together, and the counting took the rest of the afternoon. Four pages gone from the 1893 volume. Six from 1896. Eleven from 1899 — eleven, a whole fortnight of entries, vanished. Then smaller cuts, two pages here, three there, running up through the years like a fever chart, and always, always, the missing dates sat in the same months: January, February, the deep winter. Storm months.

Fen stood at the window while she checked the last volume, and looked out at the orchard going wild down the slope, the trees grown leggy and dark, holding their fruit up into the rain like offerings nobody collected. Somewhere beyond the hedge, past the wood, was the cove. He'd seen it his second day here — walked down on a pretext, because a man checking escape routes sleeps better knowing them — and found a stone slipway green with weed, a winch rusted to its axle, and boat timbers bleached white as bone among the rocks.

Boats had come out of that cove. Boats in storm months. And someone had cut the record of them out of these books with a razor and a steady hand.

"You were a clerk, weren't you," Xanthe said behind him. Not a question. "Before. You have a clerk's eye."

"Something like that," Fen said.

Something like a corporal. Something like a man who'd spent two years keeping stores returns for a battalion whose colonel liked his paperwork beautiful, and who had learned that the truth of any unit lived not in its orders but in its wastage — what was drawn and never accounted for, what was signed for and never seen. He knew what falsified books looked like. He was standing in a room full of them.

"The storms," she said. She'd laid the volumes out on the desk in a row, open to the cut gutters, and she touched each one in turn like a woman counting beads. "Every gap falls in a storm month. Whoever did this wasn't hiding a bad harvest. They were hiding weather."

"Hiding what happened in weather," Fen said.

She looked at him then, fully, and he felt the look go through him like wire through cheese. *What do you know,* the look said. *What are you carrying.* And he made his face into the face he'd learned to make at inspections — blank, co-operative, slightly dull — and the look passed on, satisfied or pretending to be.

"We'll need the earlier volumes," she said. "The cellar, probably. Prue will know."

* * *

Prue knew. Prue knew where everything was, and what she didn't know she remembered anyway, out of pure contrariness. But when Xanthe asked her about the cellar and the old shipping books, the old woman's face closed like a door.

"Those'll be damp," was all she said, and went off to the kitchen, and the conversation was over.

That evening Fen carried lamps down to the cellar anyway — Xanthe asked, and he'd stopped being able to refuse her anything, which worried him more than Halloway's men would have, if he'd known about Halloway's men yet. The cellar ran the whole length of the house, stone-floored, racked with empty bottles and old casks, and at the far end, behind a partition of rough planking, they found the crates. Paper, packed in straw. Ledgers going back to the 1860s. A child's copybook filled with practice figures. A bundle of letters tied with ribbon, addressed in a hand Fen recognised from the recent volumes — the same careless scrawl — and sealed, every one of them, unopened.

Xanthe held that bundle a long time under the lamp. She didn't open it. She set it down on the crate beside her with the care you'd give a sleeping animal, and Fen understood that whatever was in those envelopes, she wasn't ready to know it, and that she was a person who decided carefully what she was ready to know.

It was Fen who found the last thing, because Fen was the one kneeling, because kneeling was where deserters ended up sooner or later. Under the bottom crate, flat against the cold stone, was a slim oilskin packet, the kind fishermen kept matches in. Inside was a single page of ledger paper, cut cleanly at the gutter — one of the razored pages, kept, not destroyed — folded twice.

He should have given it straight to her. He told himself that later, many times. Instead he opened it, because his hands opened things before his mind could vote, and he read it by lamplight in a cold cellar while a woman he owed nothing to and was beginning to owe everything to stood three feet away.

It was a consignment list. Dates in January 1899. Boat names. Weights — not of apples; the tonnage was wrong for fruit, too heavy, too regular. Initials receiving payment. And at the foot, in that same careless scrawl, a line that had nothing to do with shipping at all:

*Men landed safe — 4. One hurt. Payment doubled.*

Men. Landed. In storm months, at a ruined slipway, from boats whose records had been cut out of the family books with a surgeon's patience.

Fen's mouth had gone dry. Because he knew what men landed secretly on a Cornish coast in winter meant, and it wasn't brandy. Brandi didn't bleed. He thought of the war office notices he'd memorised and burned, the reward offered for information on men avoiding service, the word *harbouring* and what it cost the harbourer. He thought: *this family moved deserters. This family lost someone doing it, maybe. This family has been razoring its own history for twenty years, and I am standing in the middle of it with my own desertion folded up small inside my coat like a letter I daren't post.*

And Xanthe was watching him. He felt it before he saw it — that heron-stillness of hers had shifted, sharpened. He held out the oilskin packet without letting his face move, and she took it, and read it, and her breath went out slow through her nose.

"So it wasn't smuggling," she said quietly. "Or it wasn't only smuggling."

"No," Fen said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that and ashamed of it in equal measure. "Looks like it wasn't."

She refolded the page along its old creases, precisely, and put it inside her jacket, against her body, where the typewritten copies of everything else in her life seemed to live. Then she looked at him again, and this time the look stayed.

"You knew what this was," she said, "before I did."

"I know paper," Fen said. "I told you. Clerk's eye."

"It's more than that." She said it without accusation, which was worse. "You looked at that list like a man reading his own mail."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead a door moved in the wind, and Ollie's footsteps crossed the kitchen floor above them, quick and light, the boy who didn't speak. Fen stood in the cold with his secret pressing against his ribs like a splint, and understood that the woman in front of him was collecting truths the way other people collected debts — patiently, completely, with interest — and that sooner or later hers and his would come due in the same room.

"Mrs Thackeray," he said carefully, "whatever your family was moving through that cove — whoever they were saving, whoever they were paid by — somebody cut twenty years of it out of these books. Somebody close. Razors don't get into a locked office by accident."

She nodded slowly. Behind her calm, he could see the arithmetic running — the same arithmetic he'd run on his own officers, once, working out who in a company could be trusted with a map.

"Then we find out who held the razor," Xanthe said, "and we find out why the church and the county both want my family's papers so badly they'd burn a building for them."

She picked up her lamp and started toward the stairs, and paused with her hand on the rail, not turning round.

"And Fen — tomorrow, when we sort the rest, you'll tell me what a clerk's eye really sees. All of it. I keep a log of everything in this house now." A beat, the lamp-light trembling on the stone. "Everything."

Her boots went up the stairs. Fen stood alone in the cellar with the dead family's paper breathing straw-dust around him, and thought: *she already knows. She's known since the first day. She's just waiting for me to say it out loud where her machine can hear it.*

Above him, faintly, the typewriter began to rattle — patient, insectile, writing down the evening's silence, entry by entry, and leaving a blank space where his name would go.
