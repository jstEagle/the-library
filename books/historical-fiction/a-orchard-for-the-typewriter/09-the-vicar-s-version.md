## 9. The Vicar's Version

He came on a Thursday, when the rain had finally broken and the orchard steamed like a kettle left on the hob. Xanthe saw him from the nursery window first — a black umbrella moving up the ruined carriage drive at a pace too careful for the weather, pausing at each gatepost as though taking their measurements. A man who arrived slowly was a man who had rehearsed.

She met him at the door with her sleeves rolled and her hands still smelling of the ledger's dust, and he bowed over them anyway.

"Mrs. Thackeray. Or ought I say Miss? I'm told the question of your name is a delicate one." His smile was warm as a covered dish. "Reverend Silas Pellowe. St. Brendan's-in-the-Vale. Your father-in-law's parish, and his father's before him."

"Mr. Thackeray didn't keep a parish," Xanthe said. "He kept accounts."

"All the better men do." He stepped inside without waiting to be asked, which told her something, and wiped his boots twice on the mat, which told her something else. Careful men. Men who wanted to be liked by houses they intended to empty.

She led him to the kitchen because she would not lead him to the office. Prue was there, kneading bread with her back deliberately turned; Ollie sat in the corner with his slate, drawing boats. The boy looked up at the vicar with an expression Xanthe hadn't seen on him before — flat and old, older than ten years old had any right to be — and went back to his drawing.

Pellowe took the chair she offered and folded his hands on his knee like a man posing for a portrait that would hang somewhere respectable.

"You'll forgive the intrusion. When word reached me that the Thackeray heir had come home, I felt it a Christian duty." He produced from his satchel a slim volume bound in blue cloth, gilt letters worn to ghosts: *A History of the Thackeray Family of the Vale*, printed 1901. "I helped the late Mr. Thackeray compile this, in small part. He was a great man for the record, as I understand his son's widow is also."

"I keep a log," Xanthe said. "It's not the same thing."

"Isn't it?" He opened the book to its frontispiece — an engraving of the house as it must have been forty years ago, whole-roofed, the orchard in tidy ranks behind it. "A family is a kind of ship, Mrs. Thackeray. Someone must keep her log, or the currents carry her where they will."

"And where have the currents carried this one?"

The vicar's eyes moved — briefly, professionally — toward the corridor that led to the estate office. It was a small movement, but Xanthe had spent eleven years reading men against weather, and she read it plainly. *He knows what's in that room. He may know exactly which pages are missing.*

"The Thackerays were benefactors," Pellowe said smoothly. "Orchardists, philanthropists, loyal sons of Cornwall. Old Mr. Thackeray — your husband's grandfather — endowed the church roof. His brother—"

"His brother drowned," Xanthe said. "In '16. With six other men, in a boat that shouldn't have gone out."

The kitchen went very quiet. Prue's hands stopped in the dough. Pellowe recovered in half a breath, but it was enough of a breath for Xanthe to count.

"A tragedy," the vicar said gently. "One of the war's crueler accidents. Which brings me — delicately — to my purpose."

He drew a second paper from the satchel, this one typed, on letterhead Xanthe recognized from the solicitor's envelope: heavy cream stock, a crest she was beginning to hate. She took it and read it while he watched her with the satisfaction of a man whose document did his arguing for him.

*THE CORNWALL WAR MEMORIAL ARCHIVE. Established under the patronage of the Admiralty and the County Bench, to collect and preserve for posterity the papers, correspondence, and records of families touched by the present conflict.*

"Touched by the conflict," Xanthe repeated. "That's a tender way to put it."

"We are tender with history, Mrs. Thackeray. It is what distinguishes us from savages." He leaned forward, voice dropping into the register men used for offering charity. "Your family's papers are scattered, damp, unsorted — you'll forgive my plainness. Paper rots. Memory rots faster. But placed in the Archive, catalogued, bound, kept in a fireproof room in Truro, they would be safe forever. And your name — the Thackeray name — would stand at the head of the collection. Donor. Preserver. You would be doing for this family in death what no one did for it in life: keeping its account."

*Its account.* Xanthe held the letter very still. Fen had stood in the office two nights ago among the shipping ledgers and shown her, with a clerk's finger, the clean razored stubs where pages had been cut away — 1915, 1916, the storm years, the years of the boat. And here sat a man from an archive funded by the Admiralty and the County Bench, asking her to hand over everything that remained.

"Who funds this archive?" she asked. "Exactly. Names, if you please."

"The subscribers are listed on the reverse."

She turned it over. Among the gentry's names, halfway down, printed in confident serif: *J. Halloway, Esq., J.P.* And beneath the list of patrons, smaller: *Honorary Historian: Mr. Edmund Voss, formerly of the Admiralty Records Office.*

"You know Mr. Halloway," she said.

"The magistrate? Everyone knows Halloway. A pillar." Pellowe smiled. "A great friend to old families in difficulty."

Prue set down the bread knife. It made a small definite sound against the board, and all three of them pretended it hadn't.

"The papers stay here," Xanthe said.

"Of course, of course — I'd never press." He pressed. Gently, the way water presses. "But consider, Mrs. Thackeray — you are alone in a ruin, with squatters—" his glance flicked to Prue's back, to Ollie's slate, and back, each one weighed and filed "—with no experience of estate management, in a county where your family's name has been, forgive me, whispered about since the boat went down. The Archive offers respectability. Protection. A wall between you and every gossip and litigant who smells an unclaimed inheritance. History is a fortress, madam. Best to be inside it when the shelling starts."

"Whose history?"

He spread his hands, wounded. "Why, the truth."

Xanthe rose and fetched the blue cloth volume from where he'd set it down, and opened it to the chapter on the drowning. She had read it the night before by lamplight, twice. Now she read a passage aloud, evenly, the way she entered weather into a log:

"*On the night of the seventeenth of October, 1916, a fishing vessel belonging to the estate was lost with all hands in the great southwesterly gale. The vessel had put out, in an act of tragic misjudgment, against the advice of the harbourmaster. Her crew of seven included Mr. Ambrose Thackeray, youngest son of the house, who perished a hero to his men.*"

She closed the book. "Against the advice of the harbourmaster. Is that how you remember it, Reverend?"

"I remember what was written."

"Was Ambrose Thackeray a hero to his men?"

"He is dead, Mrs. Thackeray, and dead men are whatever we agree they were. That is not cynicism. That is how a nation survives a war." For the first time the warmth thinned and she saw the machinery underneath, oiled and patient. "There will be thousands of these boats, metaphorically speaking. Thousands of drowned men needing to mean something. The Archive exists so that meaning is decided once, properly, by responsible persons — and not left to rumor, to spiteful servants, to deserters hiding in orchards."

The last word dropped into the kitchen like a coin down a well.

Ollie's chalk stopped.

Prue turned around at last, flour to her elbows, and looked at the vicar the way one looks at weather one has been expecting for years. "Forty years I've served this house," she said. "I buried three Thackerays and christened nothing, and I'll thank you not to speak of spite in this kitchen, Reverend Pellowe, where I've fed better men than you off worse plates than yours."

"My dear woman—"

"She's not your dear woman," Xanthe said. Quietly. The lamp-glass steadiness. "And the papers stay here. All of them. Including the ones someone already tried to take out of the ledgers — I've had those gaps measured, Reverend. Millimetre by millimetre. If your Archive wants the Thackeray record, it can apply in writing, and I will decide page by page what leaves this house, and I will keep a copy of everything that does."

Something crossed Pellowe's face then — not anger. Calculation completing itself. He gathered his satchel, his umbrella, his blue cloth book, and stood, and became warm again so quickly it was almost admirable.

"Then I shall pray for you, Mrs. Thackeray. And for the house." At the door he paused, and delivered the sentence she would type out later that night and read a dozen times after:

"One hopes you'll finish sorting those papers before others take an interest less patient than mine. Archives are gentle things. Not everyone who collects history carries an umbrella."

He tipped his hat to Prue — she did not move — and to Ollie, who stared through him at his boats. Then the black umbrella went back down the carriage drive, slower than it came, pausing at the gateposts again.

Xanthe stood in the doorway until the lane swallowed him. The steaming orchard smelled of wet apple and old wood-rot, and somewhere under it, faintly, the sea.

Behind her, Prue said, "He'll be back with worse than a book."

"I know."

"And you'll want to write all this down tonight, won't you. In that machine."

"I already have."

Prue almost smiled. It aged her face kindly for a moment. "Old Mr. Thackeray was the same. Wrote everything. Even wrote down what he burned — date, hour, and reason. There's a line in his hand I never forgot." She wiped her arms. "*Burned unread, by order, at the request of a friend.*"

"Read by whom?"

"That's just it, missus. Nobody living knows. The letter came, and it was burned the same day, and whatever friend ordered it —" Prue looked out at the grey orchard, at the gateposts the vicar had measured with his eyes — "that friend is still alive. Because whoever it was, they've never stopped ordering things since."

That night the typewriter rattled past midnight. The entry ran long. At the bottom of the page, beneath the vicar's parting words transcribed verbatim, Xanthe added a single line in her own hand:

*Note: someone is writing our history ahead of me. Find out who holds the pen — or become it.*

And below that, dated, timed, logged like a bearing taken in fog:

*Tomorrow: the potting shed.*
