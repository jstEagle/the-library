## 10. Ollie Speaks

The boy's name is Ollie. He knows this because Prue says it every morning, into his ear, like a secret she keeps giving back to him.

*Ollie. Time to get up, Ollie.*

He does not say it back. He has not said anything in a long time. Words went away the night the boat went out and did not come home. He thinks of them sometimes, the words, like birds sitting on a wire far off across a field. He can see them. He cannot make them come.

The new lady is here now. The one with the typewriter. She came on a wet day with a young man who carries things for her, and she walked through the orchard grass in boots that were too good for it, and then she stopped walking when she saw the trees all gone wild, and her face did something Ollie could not name but understood anyway. It was the face of someone coming to a grave they did not know was a grave.

Prue calls her *missus*. Fen calls her *ma'am*. Tom, the young one, calls her nothing because he is mostly carrying.

Ollie watches her from places. From the top rail of the pig pen that has no pigs anymore. From behind the dead well. From the crook of the big apple tree by the wall, where the branches grow so thick and sideways that a boy can lie along them like along a floor, hidden, looking down at everything.

She types at night. He hears it through the wall of the estate office where she sleeps now, in the room that used to be Master Charles's. *Tick-tick-tick. Ding.* Like rain on a tin roof, if rain knew how to count.

He likes the sound. It is the only new thing that does not frighten him.

* * *

Mornings are eggs and bread and tea, all of them at the long table in the kitchen because the dining room has a hole in its ceiling and Prue says the rain would want to join us and there isn't enough for six.

There are five of them. Prue. Fen. Tom. The lady. Him.

Fen eats fast and looks at the window while he eats. Ollie knows why. Fen is a runner. You can tell runners by how they sit — never with their backs to a door, always knowing where the hedge gap is. Ollie's da sat like that too, near the end, and Da wasn't even running from soldiers. He was running from a name.

Tom talks with his mouth full and Prue tells him off and he grins and does it again. Tom is easy. Tom is like a dog that got left somewhere once and decided to love whoever opened the next gate.

The lady is none of these things. The lady is quiet the way the sea is quiet an hour before it decides otherwise.

This morning she sets down her cup and looks straight at him. Not past him. At him. People had stopped doing that, after. After, people looked at him the way you look at a chair that someone died in — carefully, and not for long.

"Ollie," she says. "Prue tells me you know every tree in this orchard."

His ears go hot. He looks at his plate.

"She says you showed her which ones still give fruit worth eating. That's a kind of knowledge most grown men don't have." She turns her cup around on its saucer, once. "I'd like to learn it. If you'd teach me. I'm no use with trees. My trade is light and glass."

He risks one glance up. She isn't smiling at him the way adults smile at him, the stretched way, the way that means *poor little thing*. She is just waiting, patient as weather.

He nods. One nod. It costs him more than a shout would cost another boy.

Her face does the unnamed thing again, softer. "Thank you," she says, as if he had given her something real. Maybe he had.

* * *

They walk the orchard rows together, the lady and him, three mornings running. She does not talk much. That is the best thing about her. Other people fill silence like filling a hole, shovelful after shovelful, and the hole just gets bigger.

She carries a little book and writes in it. Tree names. Russet. Costard. A queer old one Prue calls the Widow Maker because it drops limbs without warning. Ollie shows her which boughs are rotten hearted — you knock, and listen, and a live branch answers hollow-bright and a dead one answers dull, like knocking on a coffin lid. He shows her the wasp nests to step wide of. He shows her the tree his father grafted, down by the south wall, the one with two kinds of apple on one arm, half sweet and half sharp.

She stands under that one a long time.

"Your father's work?" she asks.

He nods.

"He was good at it."

He nods again, harder, and his eyes sting, and he hates that, and then her hand comes down slow, the way you'd reach toward a bird, and rests on top of his head for exactly one breath, and lifts away. She doesn't say *there, there*. She doesn't say anything. That is how he knows she understands what happened to words.

On the fourth morning she brings the wrong thing. She brings questions.

Not bad ones. Not the vicar kind, the ones that come dressed nice and have hooks inside. Hers are plain.

"Did you live here all your life?"

Nod.

"Who taught you the trees?"

Shrug. Then, because it is true: a finger pointed at the south wall.

"Your father." She writes it in the book. "What was his name?"

And there it is. The hook inside the plainness. Not mean. Just a hook.

Ollie stops walking. His feet stop before the rest of him agrees to. Down the slope, past the tumbled wall, the ground falls away toward the cove, and even from here, even with the leaves thick on the trees, he can hear it — the water working at the stones, *shush-shush, drag, shush-shush*, patient as anything, like it has all the time in the world to take the whole shore apart grain by grain and carry it out to where the boats went.

The lady waits. She is good at waiting. But the question sits between them like a stone neither of them can lift, and Ollie finds, standing there, that the stone has gotten heavy in a new way. Heavy the way a door is heavy when you have been holding it shut a long time and your arms are tired and nobody knows you're holding it.

Da said hold it shut. Da said, *not a word, Ollie-boy, not a word till I'm back, and I'll be back before the apples turn.*

The apples turned. The apples fell. The apples rotted into the grass and fed the wasps and the blackbirds and the worms, row upon row upon row, and Da did not come back, and Ollie held the door.

His arms are tired.

* * *

That night he cannot sleep. The house makes its noises — beams ticking as they cool, wind finding the broken chimney, Fen turning over downstairs like a man fighting someone in a dream. And under all of it, faint, the tick-tick-tick of the typewriter, and the ding.

He gets up. He is good at getting up without noise; he has had practice. He goes down the stairs that avoid the third step, and along the passage, and out the scullery door into the yard, because sometimes when the words won't stay put inside him he has to be under the sky or they press on his teeth.

The moon is up, nearly round, lying on its back. The orchard is silver and black. The sea is a sound more than a thing.

He walks. He doesn't decide to; his feet do it, the way they do, taking the path his feet have taken a hundred nights, down past the wall, through the gap where the stile broke, down the sheep-cropped slope to the headland above the cove.

From here you can see the whole cove laid out like a bowl. Black water. White teeth of surf on the near rocks. The old slipway, green with weed, running down into the dark like a tongue.

This is where they came. This is what he knows that nobody has asked him yet, and maybe that is why the door is getting heavy — because somebody soon will ask, and he can feel it coming the way you feel weather in an old break.

Boats came to the cove at night. That is the first true thing and he holds it up in his mind and looks at it. Boats with muffled oars, coming in on the making tide, no lights, riding low. Men on the slipway with lanterns shuttered to a slit, and the lantern-light made everyone look like they were made of brass. Crates going up the path on shoulders, and some crates didn't clink like bottles, they were soft, and they breathed, and once one coughed.

Men came up through the orchard. Some stayed a night in the cellars under the house, where Prue took them bread and broth. Some stayed longer. There was a man with a burned face who stayed in the potting shed a whole month and taught Ollie to whistle through his hands, and whistling is the only music Ollie has now, and he got it from a man whose name he never knew.

And men went down to the boats too. That is the other true thing. Some nights the boats brought men in, and some nights they carried men out, and going out was supposed to be the good ending, the happy part, France behind them and somewhere safe ahead. Da called it *the last leg*. He'd stand at this very spot on the headland and watch the boat go and say, *there's another one over the water, Ollie-boy, sleeping warm tonight because of us.*

But the sea kept some.

He counts them sometimes, the ones the sea kept. He is afraid to count them in case he gets the number wrong and it becomes a different number forever. There was the night of the great storm — the night the lighthouse on the island went dark for eleven minutes, everyone said afterward, eleven minutes, how could a light go dark for eleven minutes — and two boats were out, and only one came home, and the one that came home came home empty except for the skipper, who walked up through the orchard soaking wet and gray as ash and never spoke above a whisper again.

Da went out that night. Da always went out on the last legs, to steady the men who'd never been on water, because a deserter panics in a swell and a panicked man swamps a boat.

Da kissed his head at the cellar door. Da said, *before the apples turn.*

The apples turned twice since.

* * *

He hears footsteps on the path behind him and does not startle, because the footsteps are unhurried and land soft, and there is only one person here who walks like that.

The lady comes and stands beside him at the edge of the headland. She doesn't say *you shouldn't be out here*, which is what Prue would say, or *it's cold*, which is what Tom would say. She puts her hands in her coat pockets and looks at the cove the way he is looking at it, and for a while there is just the water saying its one word over and over.

"I couldn't sleep either," she says finally. "The typewriter keeps me up. It keeps me up even when it's silent, if that makes sense. Like a dog wanting to be walked."

He almost smiles. Almost.

"Is this where you come," she says, "when the house gets too small?"

He nods.

"It's a good place for it." She tips her head back at the sky. "On my island, when Elias was alive, we'd climb the tower on nights like this and count ships' lights. He'd name the ports they might be bound for. Lisbon. Bergen. Boston, if they were brave." Her voice goes strange on the name Elias, like a floorboard that gives a little. "I keep his log still. Every night. Even though the Admiralty says the station's closing. Even though soon there'll be nothing to log but weather."

She looks down at him. In the moonlight her face is young and old at once, which he did not know faces could be.

"Ollie," she says. "I'm going to ask you something, and you mustn't answer tonight. You mustn't answer until you want to. Do you understand? The answer will keep. Answers are patient things. It's only the carrying of them that wears a person out."

He looks at her.

"I found pages cut out of the shipping ledgers," she says. "Years gone, razored clean. And a shed I can't open. And a vicar who wants my family's papers buried in a memorial drawer before I've read them. Somebody has been tidying this cove's history the way you'd tidy a room before company comes." The wind moves her hair. "Somebody drowned here, or many somebodies, and someone powerful wants the counting stopped. I intend to count. That's my whole intention, Ollie. To count truly, and write it down, and let no one burn it."

The sea says its word. *Shush-shush. Drag.*

"And if ever," she says, quieter, "you should want to tell me about the boats — I think you know about the boats, anyone who walks here at night knows about the boats — then I will type every word you say, dated and signed, and it will be as true and as permanent as anything in this world gets to be. No one will take it from you. Not the vicar. Not a magistrate. Not me."

She doesn't touch him. She doesn't wait for an answer. She turns and goes back up the path, unhurried, soft-footed, leaving him the whole bowl of the cove and the whole weight of the door.

* * *

He stays a long time. The moon crosses a hand's width of sky. Cold comes up through his boots and he lets it.

He tries the words out silently, the way you test a rope before trusting it. *Boat.* The word holds. *Cove.* Holds. *Da.* That one frays a little in the middle but holds.

Then the hard ones. *Storm.* Holds. *Eleven minutes.* Holds, and heavier than it sounds. *Drowned.* He sets that one down gently and picks it up again and it holds, and his eyes are stinging and this time he lets them, because there is no one to see and because maybe that was the trouble all along — not that the words went away, but that he was holding the door with his face.

Da said not a word till I'm back.

Da is not coming back. Ollie has known it the way you know the sun will rise, without needing proof, for a long time now. And Da was a good man, and Da lied to him kindly, the way you lie to a child, and Ollie understands that too, now, being nearly nine and having seen what he has seen. Da thought he was keeping Ollie safe. But the door was never keeping danger out. The door was keeping Ollie in — in the dark, in the alone, in the whistle-through-your-hands silence where a burned-faced man taught him music and nobody wrote down the man's name, and now nobody ever will, unless somebody counts.

The lady counts.

He thinks about the typewriter. Tick-tick-tick, ding. Rain that knows how to count. Somewhere in the house right now it is silent, waiting, the way she said answers are patient. A machine that takes a thing out of a person's chest and makes it metal and ink and paper, makes it a thing that can be held, copied, hidden, planted like seed, found again in a hundred years by someone who needs it.

He thinks about the man with the burned face, whistling. About the crate that coughed. About the skipper gray as ash. About Da kissing his head, salt-smelling, saying *before the apples turn* like a promise the sea never signed.

The apples turned twice.

It is time.

He climbs the path. He goes in through the scullery, past the kitchen where the banked fire breathes red, up the stairs avoiding the third step, and he stands outside the office door where the lamplight lies in a bright strip on the floorboards, and he listens to his own heart going like a bird against a window.

He raises his hand and knocks. Two knocks, small ones, but knocks.

The typing stops.

"Yes?" Her voice, careful, awake.

He pushes the door open. Lamplight. Paper everywhere, and the typewriter squatting black and gleaming like a beetle, and her face turning toward him, and she sees his face and she does not say *what's wrong, sweetheart, poor lamb* — she goes still, and moves the chair beside hers out from the desk, an invitation, and waits.

Ollie walks in. He climbs onto the chair. His feet don't reach the floor. He folds his hands in his lap like an old man giving evidence, and he opens his mouth, and what comes out is rusty and cracked and small, a voice unused for two years, a voice that scares him coming out, and he makes himself say it anyway, one word, the first word, the door swinging wide:

"Boats."

The lady's breath catches. Her hand moves to the typewriter and rests on the keys, trembling, and she looks at him and says, steady as a keeper lighting a lamp in a gale:

"Tell me everything. I'll write it all down."

And Ollie, nearly nine, feet dangling, begins.
