## 11. Smugglers or Saints

Prue had been waiting for the question since the day the girl walked through the gate with salt in her hair and a Thackeray jaw she didn't know she was wearing.

It came on a Thursday, in the kitchen, with rain ticking at the window and Ollie asleep in the corner chair where he always slept when the weather turned. Xanthe stood by the range with her sleeves rolled, stirring something she'd made from Prue's instructions without being told them twice — that was the thing about her, Prue thought, watching her back. She learned like a keeper learns: once, and for good.

"You've been here forty years," Xanthe said, not turning around. "You knew my husband's father. You knew his brother."

Prue set down the cloth she'd been folding. "I did."

"And the ledgers have pages cut out of them. And there's a shed I can't open. And you look at me some days like you're deciding whether to tell me something or take it to your grave." She turned then, wooden spoon in hand, steam rising off the pot between them. "I'm not asking as an heir, Prue. I'm asking as someone who kept a light alone for two years and knows what it does to a person, keeping things."

The rain ticked. The boy breathed in his sleep, small and even.

"Sit down," Prue said. "This is a long story and it goes better sitting."

* * *

She'd come to Thackeray Orchard in 1877, a girl of sixteen with chilblains and no references worth the paper, and old Ambrose Thackeray had hired her because she could name every apple in his orchard blindfolded by smell alone. Forty years. She'd buried his wife, raised his sons' tempers, and watched the estate pass from a man who planted trees to a man who counted them.

"Ambrose was fruit and soil," she said. "His son Charles — your husband's father — was ledgers and locks. And his brother Nathaniel—" she paused, choosing, "—Nathaniel was neither. Nathaniel was boats."

"Nathaniel," Xanthe repeated. "There's no Nathaniel in the family Bible upstairs."

"No," Prue said. "There wouldn't be. That's rather the point of everything I'm about to tell you."

* * *

It started, she said, the way these things always start — small, practical, and with the best intentions in the world.

The war came in 1914 and took the young men of the parish like a scythe takes wheat, and it left behind the ones who couldn't go and the ones who ran. The first one came to the kitchen door in November of that year, a boy of nineteen with his uniform torn at the shoulder and blood dried black down his sleeve, and he couldn't speak, just held out his hand palm-up like a beggar, and Prue looked at him and looked back at the house where Charles Thackeray sat doing sums, and she made her decision in the space of one breath.

She took him to the cellar.

Not the wine cellar Charles locked. The other one — the long cellar under the north rows, the one that predated the house itself, dug into the hillside with its mouth hidden behind the old pressing room. Smugglers had built it two hundred years before; brandy had come up that passage in the time of Prue's grandmother's grandmother. It had lain dark for generations, and Nathaniel Thackeray, who knew every cove and cave within ten miles of the coast, had shown it to her the first winter of the war with a lantern in his hand and a look on his face she'd never seen on a Thackeray before.

"He said, 'Prue, if anyone ever comes to this door half-dead and afraid of their own army, this is where they sleep.' And I said, 'Mr. Nathaniel, that's treason.' And he laughed — he laughed like his whole body meant it — and he said, 'No, Prue. Treason is letting them be shot in the morning for wanting to go home.'"

* * *

For three years it worked. Boats came to the cove below the orchard on dark-water nights — Nathaniel's boat, mostly, a lugger called *Sweet Promise* that he'd inherited along with a share of the family's older, quieter history — and they carried more than contraband now. Deserters. Wounded men smuggled out of field hospitals by nurses who risked their necks. Boys who'd signed up at fourteen and discovered what signing up meant. The cellars fed them, hid them, moved them inland to farms further east where Quakers and Methodists kept the chain going. Prue cooked for ghosts. She baked bread at midnight for boys whose names she never learned, and she watched them go up the hill path at dawn looking like men who'd been given back to themselves.

"Charles never knew?" Xanthe asked.

"Charles suspected and chose not to know. There's a kind of knowledge that's worse than ignorance for a man who loves his ledgers." Prue's voice went flat. "But Nathaniel — Nathaniel knew everything, because Nathaniel ran everything. He'd come up from the cove smelling of tar and salt, sit in this kitchen, eat whatever I had, and tell me about the night. He loved it, do you understand? Not the danger — the mercy of it. He used to say the orchard finally had a crop worth growing."

* * *

And then, in the spring of 1916, someone informed.

Prue didn't know who for a long time — she still didn't know for certain, though she had her suspicions, suspicions that had curdled into certainty over two years of turning the same stone. But she knew what happened. A convoy was moving wounded men inland on a night in April, and word reached the naval patrol before the boat ever left the cove. Someone had written it all down — dates, tides, numbers of men — and handed it to an officer in Falmouth with a neat hand and a clean conscience.

"They waited for him," Prue said. Her hands were flat on the table now, knuckles white. "They let *Sweet Promise* put out, let her get past the point where she could turn back, and then they came at her with a patrol boat and a searchlight. Eleven souls aboard. Nathaniel, two local men who helped crew, and eight passengers — deserters and wounded, boys, most of them. The patrol fired a warning shot across her bow and she turned into the swell wrong, or maybe they fired again, the accounts differ depending on who was allowed to give one." She stopped. Started again. "She went down inside the bay. Inside sight of the light on the point. Every soul aboard drowned, and the navy logged it as a smuggling vessel lost in bad weather, and the coroner — who dined with the same officers who signed the report — agreed."

Xanthe had gone very still. "Eleven men."

"Ten men and one woman. A nurse who'd smuggled out her own brother and stayed aboard too long." Prue closed her eyes. "I identified what the sea gave back. Three bodies washed up over that fortnight. One of them was Nathaniel. I'm the one who told Charles his brother was dead, and I'm the one who watched him burn Nathaniel's papers in the study fire without reading a single page, and I'm the one who heard him say, 'We will not speak of this again,' and I obeyed him for two years because he paid my wages and grief makes cowards of us all."

* * *

The silence after that lasted a long while. Rain. The boy breathing. The pot cooling on the range.

"So the family Bible has no Nathaniel," Xanthe said finally, slowly, "because the family decided he was never born rather than admit what he died doing."

"The family decided he was a criminal," Prue corrected. "Drowned in disgrace, in their telling. A smuggler who got what smugglers get." Her mouth twisted. "But I was there, Mrs. Thackeray. I fed those boys. I bandaged them. I watched your husband's uncle carry a sixteen-year-old up from the cove on his back with shells falling twenty miles off and tears running down his face, and I will go to my grave knowing the difference between what the Thackerays were and what the Thackerays did. Your family weren't saints. But they weren't the sinners the navy's paperwork says either. They were something the paperwork has no column for."

"People who saved lives and were murdered for it."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

* * *

Xanthe sat back. Prue watched her work through it — watched the keeper's mind do what keepers' minds do, take a wild sea of fact and start marking channels through it.

"The pages cut from the ledgers," Xanthe said. "The years match. Charles burned Nathaniel's papers, but someone razored the shipping entries first."

"Or after. Or both."

"And the vicar's polished history — the one he wants for his memorial archive — would say what? That the Thackerays were respectable orchard folk, loyal to the Crown, touched by tragedy?"

"It would say exactly that. It would say nothing at all, which is worse." Prue leaned forward. "Now you understand why I looked at you the way I did, girl. Because you arrived with that letter naming you heir, and letters naming heirs are how this estate gets tidied away. Charles is dead, Elias is dead, and if the last Thackeray signs the right papers, the cellars get sealed, the story dies with me, and eleven people stay drowned twice over — once in the water and once in the record."

"I haven't signed anything."

"I know. I've watched you not sign things. It's the first thing about you I liked."

* * *

Ollie stirred in his chair and Prue rose to fetch the blanket off the hook, and when she turned back Xanthe was standing at the window, looking out at the dark slope where the north rows ran down toward the pressing room and the hillside beyond.

"The mouth of the cellar," Xanthe said. "Behind the pressing room."

"You won't find it without me. There's a trick to the door."

"Tomorrow, then." Xanthe turned, and her face in the lamplight was the face Prue had seen on Nathaniel Thackeray forty years ago, the one that wasn't fruit or ledgers but something else entirely. "Because whoever wrote that letter to the patrol in 1916 is still alive, Prue. People like that don't stop writing letters. They just change what they're informing on."

Prue felt the old cold move through her, the one she'd carried since the spring of 1916.

"There's something else," she said, before she could stop herself. "Something I've never said aloud. About who I think wrote that letter."

Xanthe came back to the table and sat, and pulled the typewriter closer, and rolled a fresh page into the platen with the small decisive motion Prue was already learning to read like weather.

"Then say it," Xanthe said. "Slowly. I'll type it as you go."
