## 12. The Typewriter as Witness

Prue said the name slowly, the way you'd carry water across ice.

"Garrett Voss."

Xanthe's fingers hovered over the keys. She did not know the name. She typed it anyway — G-A-R-R-E-T-T — and heard it become something else as it took shape in ink on the page: not a rumor anymore, but a fact with a body and a weight.

"Who is he?" she asked.

"Was," Prue said. "He was under-gardener here before I was. Left for Falmouth in 1913 and did well for himself, they say. Runs boats now." Her mouth tightened around the word *runs*. "I'm not saying he informed. I'm saying he knew every path to the cove, every cellar door, every night the lanterns were dark on purpose. And I'm saying that after the boat was lost, he was the only man from this parish who came into money."

Xanthe typed it all. Not because she believed it yet — belief could wait; belief was a luxury — but because Prue had carried it forty years and it deserved a place outside her chest.

"Read it back," Prue said.

Xanthe read it back. Prue listened with her eyes closed, like a woman hearing a hymn she'd only ever hummed. When Xanthe finished, Prue nodded once, hard, the way men do when a nail goes in true.

"That's me," she said. "On paper. That's me saying it." She stood, pressed both palms flat against the table as if to steady the whole house. "Now type the date at the top. And your own name underneath, at the bottom. So it isn't just talk floating about. Talk floats. Paper stays where you put it."

Xanthe dated the page: *Recorded the ninth of November, 1917, at Thackeray Orchard, by Xanthe Thackeray, keeper of the Gannet Light.* Then, beneath, she signed it — signed it with the typewriter's own line first, *Taken down verbatim*, and then her hand, which shook less than she expected.

Prue looked at the signature a long moment.

"Your husband taught you that?" she said. "The dating of things?"

"He taught me everything except how to stop doing it."

* * *

That night she could not sleep for the sound of keys.

Not the typewriter — she had covered it, out of some courtesy to the sleeping house — but the idea of it, the rhythm of it running behind her eyes. Elias had kept his log in strict cadence: wind, sea-state, vessels sighted, remarks. Forty years of keepers before him had done the same, because the Admiralty wanted facts and nothing but facts, and because facts, properly kept, outlasted the men who recorded them. A light log was a small act of defiance against forgetting. Nobody had ever said so aloud. It was simply understood, the way lighthouse families understood salt.

She got up past midnight, lit the lamp in the estate office, and uncovered the machine.

The office smelled of cold ash and old apples. Through the window the orchard lay silvered and wild, rows gone to thicket, trees leaning into each other like conspirators. Somewhere out there was a padlocked shed with something metallic catching moonlight through its boards, and somewhere beyond that a cove where boats had come at night, and somewhere beyond *that*, a world at war deciding what version of itself it would remember.

She rolled in fresh paper and typed a heading, and found her hands knew the form before her mind did:

> **TESTIMONY OF PRUDENCE HALLIDAY, taken at Thackeray Orchard, 9 Nov. 1917. Witness states as follows—**

It looked right. It looked, in fact, like the most honest thing she had made in two years.

Here is what no one tells you about grief, she thought, fingers resting on the space bar. They tell you time heals. They tell you to keep busy. What nobody says is that grief is a hold full of loose cargo, and unless you lash it down — unless you give it work, give it *shape* — it shifts with every swell and capsizes you at anchor, in harbor, in your sleep. For two years she had lashed hers to the light. Wind, sea-state, vessels sighted. The lamp turned; the log filled; the grief stayed stowed.

Then the requisition letter had come, claiming the island, decommissioning the station, threatening to carry away or scrap everything she had kept. And the grief had broken loose, and she had come ashore chasing a dead man's inheritance, and here she was in a ruined house full of strangers who were somehow becoming less strange — and the cargo needed new lashings.

Very well. This would be the work. Not the orchard, not the estate, not even the truth for its own sake. The record. Dates, names, statements, signatures. A log of the drowned and the saved, kept in Elias's cadence, kept in Elias's machine, so that when the government historians came with their polished accounts and their razored ledgers, there would be paper that floated and paper that sank, and anyone who cared to look could tell which was which.

She worked until three. When she finally banked the lamp, she had eleven pages, and she slept without dreaming for the first time since the island.

* * *

Fen came to the office the next morning unasked, which from Fen was a kind of eloquence.

He stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands — he always seemed surprised to be holding the cap, as if it had been pressed on him — and looked at the typewriter the way Tom had once looked at the lens: with the hunger of a boy seeing machinery that meant something.

"You're writing us down," he said. "All of it."

"I'm writing down what people are willing to say. Nothing more."

"And if what I'm willing to say gets me shot?"

Xanthe set down the cloth she'd been using on the keys. "Then you shouldn't say it. I won't trick you into testimony, Fen. I've seen what the sea does to men who were pushed overboard. I don't intend to be the weather."

He came in anyway. He sat across the desk from her, and for a long moment he studied the room — the ledgers with their razored years, the dust-sheeted furniture, the window onto the wild rows — and Xanthe watched him decide something, the way you watch a man decide whether to swim for it or go down with the ship.

"My name isn't Fen," he said. "It's Ferdinand Ackerley. Private, 2nd Battalion, Duke of Cornwall's Light Infantry. If you're going to write me down, write me down proper."

"Are you certain?"

"No." He almost smiled. It was the first one she'd seen from him and it changed his whole face, made him briefly young. "But you said nothing more than what people are willing to say, and I find — " he turned the cap in his hands — "I find I'm willing to say more today than yesterday. That's your doing, Mrs. Thackeray. Or your machine's. Hard to say which frightens me more."

She rolled the page. Dated it. Typed his statement of identity while he dictated it, spelling Ackerley twice, correcting her once.

Then he talked for an hour. Not the confession — not yet, she could feel the confession waiting below the waterline like ballast — but the edges of it. The convoy he'd been detailed to guard. The orders that didn't sit right. The night he walked away from a camp in Devon with forty-one shillings and a change of socks. He gave dates. He gave places. He gave the names of two sergeants who could confirm, if they were alive and willing, that he'd questioned an order before he ran rather than after.

Xanthe typed until her fingers ached. At the end she read it back, as she'd done for Prue, and Fen listened with his eyes shut, and when she finished he opened them and said, quietly:

"It sounds different. Said in your voice off the page. Smaller. Like it happened to somebody else."

"It happened to you. The page will prove that when everyone alive has forgotten your face."

He signed. His signature was careful, schoolroom-neat, and he stared at it afterward as though he'd written his own headstone.

"One more thing," he said at the door. "Ollie saw things, that summer. Things about the boats. He doesn't talk, but — " Fen hesitated. "He draws. Chalk, charcoal, anything. There's a drawing behind the loose stone in the washhouse wall. I found it when I was patching the mortar. You should see it before you ask him anything."

"Why tell me this instead of him?"

"Because asking him is like asking the tide to explain itself." Fen put his cap on. "You have to make a place where speaking costs nothing. You're good at making those. I don't know how, being what I am. But you are."

* * *

She went to the washhouse alone, in the grey middle of the day, and found the loose stone on the second try.

Behind it, flat against the brick, was a sheet of slate with chalk drawings worked into it, layered and reworked until the surface shone faintly like oiled wood. Boats. A dozen boats, drawn over and over in a child's insistent line — hulls too deep, masts too tall, the way children draw boats they have seen at night and never in daylight. Some boats had figures standing in them, arms raised. Some had figures lying flat. One boat, larger than the rest, drawn last and pressed hardest, had a great black scribble beside it, and beside the scribble, small and deliberate, a row of tally marks.

Xanthe counted the marks. Eleven.

She thought of the lost boat, the one Prue spoke of with the cold still in her voice after all these years. All hands. Eleven souls, if you counted the pilot-boy, and the village had counted him, Prue had said; they'd counted him first.

She stood in the cold washhouse with the slate in her hands and felt the purpose in her chest stop being an ember and start being a fire with a chimney — something with a direction, a draft, a way out into the world. This child had been keeping a log too. In chalk. On slate. Because slate wipes clean, and maybe he'd wanted it to wipe clean, and maybe he hadn't, and either way the marks were still here, eleven of them, patient as graves.

She did not take the slate. She put the stone back exactly as she'd found it and walked out into the orchard, and found Ollie where he usually was, sitting on the low bough of the biggest apple tree, watching her with those grey unblinking eyes.

"I saw your boats," she said.

He didn't move. A wind moved the branches around him instead, the whole tree swaying gently with the boy balanced at its center like a keel.

"They're very well drawn," she said. "The one with the tally marks. Eleven is the right number."

Something happened in his face then — nothing so large as expression, more a shift of light under ice — and he slid down from the branch and stood in front of her, close enough that she could see chalk dust still ground into the creases of his knuckles, months old, washed thin but never gone.

"After supper," he said.

Two words. His voice was rusty, cracked, a hinge unused since spring. But they were words, addressed to her, and Xanthe Thackeray — who had spent two years measuring life in portable things, who had learned to weigh a soul by what it could carry — stood very still in her dead family's orchard and received them like a signal flare.

"After supper," she agreed.

* * *

After supper, the kitchen filled itself.

This was the thing she was learning about the household: it convened the way weather convenes, without instruction. Prue brought her mending because Prue's hands could not be idle within earshot of anything important. Fen brought firewood he did not need to bring. Tom — back from a supply run to the village, cheeks wind-scoured — dropped onto the bench by the stove and read the room with a keeper's instinct and asked no questions, which was its own kindness.

Ollie sat at the scrubbed table. Xanthe sat opposite him with the typewriter, because the office felt too much like a chapel and this felt like a kitchen, and kitchens were where the truth of a house actually lived.

She dated the page. Typed the heading. Then she waited.

The fire ticked. Prue's needle whispered. Ollie looked at the typewriter for a long time, and then at Xanthe, and then — this was the moment she would remember, later, when the warrant came and the courtroom and the bells — he reached out and touched the platen, one finger, very lightly, the way you touch a horse you're deciding to trust.

"It talks," he said.

"When I make it," Xanthe said. "Like you. It only talks when someone makes it. And what it says stays said."

Ollie nodded slowly, as though this matched some private theory he had held for years. Then he began to talk.

Sparse at first. Single words, offered like stones across a stream: *Night. Tide. Lanterns.* She typed each word as it fell, not smoothing, not arranging — verbatim, the pauses marked with dashes, the repetitions left in, because she had learned from Elias that a log's authority lived in its refusal to tidy anything. Gradually the stream widened. The boats came to the cove on dark nights when the moon was new. Men came down the orchard paths carrying other men. Sometimes the carried men cried out and were hushed. Once — his voice dropped, and Prue's needle stopped, and even the fire seemed to lean in — once the soldiers came with dogs, and the boats went out early and one went out late, and the late boat was the big one, the one in the drawing, and Ollie had watched it from the gorse above the cove with his father's hand clamped over his mouth, and the boat had gone out into weather no boat should sail in, because the warning never came, because someone who should have hung the dark lantern in the vicar's tower had not hung it.

"Who told you the lantern story?" Xanthe asked gently.

"Heard them." Ollie's jaw set. "Under window. Before Papa went too."

*Before Papa went too.*

The kitchen was absolutely silent. Prue had gone white to the lips. Fen stood frozen with an armful of birch logs he no longer seemed able to put down.

"Your papa," Xanthe said, very carefully, "went on the boat?"

Ollie looked at her with those grey eyes and did the terrible arithmetic children can do, the arithmetic adults spend their lives avoiding. He nodded. Then he added, in that rusted hinge of a voice, the sentence that rearranged every piece of furniture in Xanthe's understanding of the house she now lived in:

"Papa hung the lantern. Every dark night. Papa hung it till the last night, and the last night it wasn't Papa's turn, and the lantern never lit, and Papa went anyway, because he wouldn't let the others go without him."

He pointed one small finger at the typewriter.

"Put that," he said.

Xanthe put that.

Her fingers shook on the keys and she let them shake and typed on regardless, because the record did not require steady hands, only faithful ones. When the page was done she read it back entire — the boats, the men, the lantern, the last night, the tally marks confirmed one by one against the slate in the washhouse — and Ollie listened with his chin up and his hands flat on the table like a small captain giving evidence of a sinking, and at the end he said, "Right," with such finality that Tom laughed and then choked it into a cough, and Prue wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretended it was the mending.

Xanthe signed and dated it. Then she gathered the pages — Prue's, Fen's, Ollie's — squared their edges, and weighted them under the glass inkstand on the desk.

Eleven pages of testimony. Three witnesses. One date on each, one name on each, one machine that did not forget.

She understood, standing there in the lamplight with her strange gathered household pretending not to watch her, that she had crossed some line of no return. Two years adrift, and now a course, and the course was set not by the Admiralty nor by solicitors nor by any magistrate living, but by a boy's chalk and an old woman's courage and a deserter's careful handwriting. Let them come with their polished histories. Let them raze pages and fund archives and bury the cove under patriotic marble. Paper stays where you put it.

She slept that night in the estate office, on the cot Fen had dragged in for her, with the testimony locked in the desk drawer and the key on a string around her neck next to Elias's ring.

She was woken near dawn by hammering at the front door — not Tom's knock, not Fen's, but the flat official rap of a man with documents — and downstairs, folded under the door, damp with sea fog that should not have traveled thirty miles inland, lay a buff envelope bearing the Admiralty crest.
