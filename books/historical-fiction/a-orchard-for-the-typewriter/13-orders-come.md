## 13. Orders Come

The envelope came with the Thursday boat, which was wrong in itself. The Thursday boat carried flour, paraffin, and the fortnight's post from Penzance, and it did not carry official correspondence — not to this island, not in eleven years of Thursdays. Xanthe saw the buff paper before the coxswain handed it over, saw the crown stamped in the corner, and felt her stomach perform a small, practiced drop, like a stone into a well.

She signed for it on the slipway, wind pulling at her collar, and did not open it until she was inside the tower with the door bolted and the kettle on. Some letters you read standing up. This one she read sitting down, at Elias's desk, with his typewriter waiting under its dust sheet like a patient animal.

*From the Board of Admiralty to the Principal Keeper, Thackeray Light.*

*Whereas by reason of altered strategic requirements arising from the present hostilities, and pursuant to the powers vested in the Board under the Merchant Shipping Act, it is hereby ordered that Thackeray Light be decommissioned with effect from the first day of March next...*

She read it twice. The words were the same both times. *Decommissioned.* The word had a bureaucratic neatness to it, the way *amputated* was a tidy word for what happened to a man's arm.

The light was to go dark. The lens — Elias's lens, the one he'd polished every morning of their marriage until the brass fittings knew his hands better than they knew hers — was to be removed by an Admiralty salvage crew and either transferred or scrapped, at the Board's discretion. The station buildings would be secured. Personal effects were to be cleared within sixty days.

*Personal effects.*

As if the logbooks were personal effects. As if the spare lamp wicks and the barometer that had been right about weather when the Met Office was wrong were personal effects. As if eleven years of her life could be packed in crates and carried off, or, failing that, burned.

She got up and put another shovelful of coal in the stove, not because it needed it but because her hands wanted work. Outside, the gale that had kept the Thursday boat late was still worrying at the rock, throwing spray over the lantern gallery in fistfuls. The light turned above her, steady as a heartbeat. It would turn tonight too, and tomorrow night, until the first of March. And then some man from Truro or London would climb these stairs with a wrench and take the heart out of it.

She sat back down and pulled the dust sheet off the typewriter.

---

The requisition sat on the desk between them all evening while she typed the day's entry. Wind SW force 9, backing W. Sea moderate to rough in approaches. Barometer falling since noon. Supply boat delayed four hours, mail intact.

Mail intact. She almost laughed.

Afterward she took the letter out again and went through it line by line, the way you'd go through a stranger's pockets. There was a schedule attached: salvage crew to arrive mid-February, weather permitting. A list of items to be surrendered — the lens assembly, the clockwork, the fog signal apparatus, the oil stores. A list of items to be *disposed of on site*, which was Admiralty for smashed and thrown down the rocks: the old backup generator, two decades of signal flags, the workshop bench Elias had built from salvaged oak.

And at the bottom, in smaller type, the sentence that made her sit very still:

*The Keeper is reminded that all station records remain the property of the Board and must be surrendered intact.*

All station records. The logbooks. Every volume since 1906, in Elias's hand and then in hers, every storm and every ship and every strange light seen where no light should be. She looked at the shelf where they stood — thirty-one volumes now, spines cracked, pages swollen with salt damp, the whole written history of this rock and everyone who had ever passed it in trouble or in trust. Property of the Board. To be surrendered *intact*, which meant surrendered away from her, catalogued by clerks who had never smelled a gale, filed in some basement in London where the salt would never touch them again.

She thought of the vicar's face in the mainland churchyard last month, that smooth certainty: *history belongs to institutions, Mrs. Thackeray. Memory is unreliable. Paper in proper hands is paper preserved.* He had said it kindly. They always said it kindly.

She got up, took down the current logbook, and held it against her chest for a moment, feeling the weight of it. Then she set it back exactly where it belonged, because the light was still commissioned until March, and a keeper does not hide things from the Board while she is still a keeper. But she stood there a long time, looking at the shelf, doing arithmetic.

Thirty-one volumes. Sixty days.

---

Tom Wrenn arrived two days later, rowing across from the village with his collar up and his face full of news he couldn't wait to spill. She watched him make fast, watched him clatter up the stairs two at a time the way he did everything, and let him get halfway through *Miss Thackeray, have you heard—* before she put the buff envelope in his hands.

He read it slower than she'd ever seen him do anything. When he finished he read it again, and then he looked up at her with an expression she recognized from funerals — the face people make when they want to ask if there's anything to be done and already know the answer.

"They can't just—" he started.

"They can." She poured him tea. "They can, and they are. March the first."

"But the light's needed. There's still convoys, still mines—"

"There'll be a buoy, apparently. Or a lightship further east. The Board has *altered strategic requirements*." She heard how flat her own voice came out and made herself soften it. "It's not a fight I can win, Tom. It's not even a fight I'm meant to notice is happening."

He turned the letter over as if something might be written on the back. "Sixty days," he said. "That's nothing."

"It's sixty days more than the drowned get."

That silenced him. He drank his tea and stared out the window at the sea doing its grey work, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — dropped the boyishness, picked up something steadier.

"Then we start now," he said. "What matters most? What goes first?"

She had been asking herself the same question for two nights, and hearing it aloud from him made the answer assemble itself, the way a log entry assembles itself out of scattered hours. She crossed to the shelf and ran her finger along the spines.

"The records stay mine," she said. "Not the Board's. Mine. Elias wrote half of them and I wrote the other half and neither of us was writing for a basement in London. We were writing so that somebody, someday, would know what actually happened out here." She pulled down the oldest volume, 1906, Elias's first year as principal keeper, and felt its weight settle into her palms. "Everything else can be scrapped. The benches, the flags, the generator. Let them. But the record comes with me, page by page, and if the Board wants it *intact* they can come and find it wherever I've put it."

"That's disobeying a direct order."

"Yes," she said. "It is."

Something moved through his face — fear, and then the excitement that lived underneath his fear, the thing that had made a seventeen-year-old beg to follow a widowed lighthouse keeper inland to a haunted orchard. "Good," he said.

She almost smiled. "Don't say good. Say practical. We're being practical." She was already moving, pulling the dust sheets off the crates stacked in the store room, counting under her breath. "The orchard house has dry cellars. Prue will grumble but she'll find room. We ship them over with the supply boat in lots — three volumes per crate, mixed in with lamp parts so nobody counts twice. And the typewriter goes with me personally. Nobody else touches it."

"And the lens?"

The question landed soft and heavy. She looked up the spiral stair toward the lantern room, where the great glass eye of the place turned and turned, catching the afternoon light and breaking it into pieces.

"Nobody can pack a lens in secret," she said quietly. "That goes the way the Board wants. Salvage crew takes it, or scraps it, whatever their discretion decides." She heard her voice hold steady and was grateful to it. "Elias kept that glass alive for twenty years. Whatever room they put it in, it'll still be the same glass. That has to be enough."

Tom didn't answer, but she saw him look up the stairwell too, and she saw that he understood it wasn't enough, and was kind enough not to say so.

---

They worked for three days.

By lamplight after her watches, and in the flat grey middles of the day between them, she went through the tower room by room, making lists in pencil on the backs of old weather forms. *Keep. Copy. Leave.* Three columns, and everything she owned had to walk past them and declare itself.

The keep column was short and brutal. Records. Typewriter. Elias's coat, which was not on any Admiralty inventory and never had been. Her mother's sewing tin. The photograph of the two of them on the gallery rail, squinting into sun. The barometer, which she told herself was a working instrument and knew perfectly well was a relic. Two lenses' worth of spare prism parts, small enough to wrap in flannel and lie about.

Everything else went into *leave*, and the strangeness of it was how little grief each individual surrender cost. A chair. A table. Eleven years of signal flags, faded to ghosts of themselves. She held each one, weighed it, and set it down, and discovered that she had spent her whole adult life measuring what could be carried — one trunk when she married Elias, one trunk when she buried him — and that the habit had been preparing her for exactly this. You survive by knowing the difference between what you own and what holds you up.

But the copy column — the copy column grew.

Because here was what she found herself doing on the fourth night, sitting at the typewriter with the 1914 logbook open beside her: she wasn't packing. She was transcribing. Storm of the ninth of October, force eleven, the trawler *Providence* seen burning off the point with no rescue possible. Names of the crew. Times of sightings. Elias's terse entries, and then, after April 1915, her own. And underneath the shipping entries, the other entries — the ones the Board had never asked for and never read. Lights seen in the cove at hours when honest boats don't run. Boats answering no signals. The night in November 1916 when nine men came ashore and only six walked on toward the mainland, and what she had typed about it, and what she had left out.

She stopped typing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the rock without pause or malice, the way it had worked for longer than there had been anyone to write it down.

If the records went to London, all of it went to London — the true ledger of the island, including the pages that could hang men or save them, depending on who read them. Halloway's kind of men would read them. Halloway's kind of men collected records the way spiders collect flies, patiently, and for the same reason.

And if the records stayed with her — well. Then they were evidence. Then they were ammunition. Then they were the only version of events that existed outside the heads of frightened people, and frightened people forget, and dead people correct nothing.

She fed a fresh page into the platen and typed the date at the top, the way she typed every date, and beneath it: *Certified true copy, compared against original this day.* Not because any authority had asked her to certify anything. Because someday someone would ask, and the asking would happen in a room where her word counted for nothing unless it was nailed down in advance, dated, witnessed by the only witness who had never lied to her — the record itself.

Her fingers moved. The keys struck. Outside, the light turned, spending its last commissioned months above an island that had already begun to forget it.

---

On the seventh evening Tom found her asleep at the desk, cheek on the 1914 volume, fingers still resting on the keys. He didn't wake her. He banked the stove, hung Elias's coat over her shoulders — she stirred at the smell of it, pipe smoke and paraffin and cold air, and settled again — and stood a while at the window looking at the sea.

In the morning she woke stiff-necked with the coat around her and the knowledge already waiting for her, fully formed, the way the worst knowledge waits.

She climbed to the gallery while Tom boiled water. The gale had blown itself out overnight and the sky was rinsed clean, and from the top of the world she could see the whole run of the coast, the island, the far grey smudge of the mainland where the orchard country began — where Prue and Fen and Ollie were keeping her other home warm without knowing yet what was coming to fill its cellars. Sixty days. Fifty-three now.

Below her, tied at the slipway, rode the supply boat that came every Thursday, and beyond it the open water, and somewhere out on that water, in offices with crowns on their paper, men were deciding what her life had been worth keeping.

Let them decide, she thought. Let them catalogue and scrap and dispose. They could take the glass and the brass and the flags. They could take the roof over her head and call it strategic necessity. But they had made one mistake, the same mistake every power makes eventually: they had put it in writing. Dated it, signed it, sealed it, sent it. Orders Come, said the file stamp, and orders could be answered.

She went downstairs, took up her pencil, and added a new heading to the third page of her private notebook, under the lists of keep and leave:

*Things the Board does not know it has given me: sixty days, a boat every Thursday, and a deadline. Everything I have ever finished, I finished against a deadline.*

Then she put on Elias's coat, and went to wake Tom, and told him to fetch the first crate up from the store room, because the Thursday boat would not wait, and neither would history.

Down at the slipway, as they loaded, the coxswain handed her a second envelope — thinner, local paper, no crown. The postmark read Penzance. Inside, a single line in a hand she didn't know: *They are coming to the orchard next. Ask your gardener what happened to the last keeper who kept records.*

* * *

She read it standing at the rail while Tom cast off, and the wind took the edge of the paper and fluttered it like something trying to escape her grip.
