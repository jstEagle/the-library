## 14. Tom Returns

I'd been three days on the road when the rain started, and four when it stopped being rain and became the sea thrown sideways at me. By the time I saw the orchard walls — black hedges against a grey afternoon, the gatepost with the Thackeray crest worn down to a suggestion — I was soaked to the skin and half-afraid I'd dreamed the whole place.

I hadn't. The gate was there. The mud was there. And there, past the first wild rows of trees, was Mrs. Thackeray herself, on a ladder, in a man's coat two sizes too big, sawing at a dead branch like it owed her money.

"Tom Wrenn." She didn't turn her head. "You walk like a man who's been running since Penzance."

"Been walking fast," I said. "Running felt like admitting something."

She came down the ladder and looked at me properly, and I saw what three weeks of the island hadn't shown me: she was thinner. The island had thinned her the way wind thins a tree, all of it going somewhere necessary. "You're dripping on my orchard," she said. "Come inside before you rust."

* * *

The kitchen at Thackeray Orchard was the warmest room I'd ever stood in, and I include the lamp room in July. Prue had a kettle on before I'd got my boots off. Fen was mending a crate by the window — he nodded at me the way he did everything, small and careful, a man rationing his movements. Ollie sat on the floor with a pencil and a piece of slate, drawing boats. He looked at me, looked back at his slate, and then, without any warning at all, held it up.

Three boats. One of them upside down.

"That's a good one," I said, because I didn't know what else to say, and because Mrs. Thackeray had gone very still by the stove.

"He speaks now," Fen said quietly. "A bit. When it matters."

The kettle screamed. Prue took it off and poured, and the ordinary business of tea went on around the slate and the drowned boat, and I understood I'd walked into a house where things were said by not saying them.

I drank the tea. I ate the bread and dripping Prue pushed at me. And then Mrs. Thackeray sat down across from me, folded her hands, and said, "Now. You didn't walk four days in November rain to admire my apples. What's happened?"

So I told them.

* * *

I'd gone back to the port town because she'd asked me to — asked me the day I left the island, actually, back in chapter one of all this, though nobody was writing chapters then. *Find out who's asking questions about Thackeray*, she'd said. *And find out who's answering them.* I'd taken the coastal road, slept in a hay barn, and spent two days being a young man of no consequence in the public rooms of the port, which is the easiest disguise there is. Nobody guards against a lad buying cider.

The vicar, the Reverend Mr. Calloway, was holding open evenings at the parish hall. *The Thackeray Memorial Archive*, the notice said, *for the preservation of our parish's honourable war record.* Free tea. I went.

"He's a smooth one," I said now, in the kitchen, with all of them watching me. "You'd like him, Mrs. Thackeray, is the thing. That's what makes him dangerous. He's got a voice like warm milk and he talks about your husband's family like they were saints carved on a church wall. Generations of service, loyalty to crown and coast, the brother who died a hero when his boat went down in the storm of — " I stopped. "Nineteen fifteen. The storm."

Fen's hands stopped on the crate.

"Go on," Mrs. Thackeray said.

"The archive's got a patron. He said it twice, like it was a medal. Funded by the Admiralty. There's a historian coming — may already be there, a Mr. — " I closed my eyes to get it right. "Aldous Vane. He's collecting accounts, the vicar said. Corrected accounts. He said that word three times. Corrected. Like the parish remembers wrong and needs a man from the Admiralty to help it remember right."

"Corrected," Prue said. The word came out of her like a stone out of a shoe.

"He's paying for memories, too. A shilling for a written account of any Thackeray matter. I watched a fisherman take the shilling. Old fella, half-blind. Wrote — well, the vicar wrote for him — a lovely page about how the Thackeray cellars were used for storing *apples* during the war. Apples." I looked around the kitchen, at the low door I now knew led down to cellars that had held men. "He said the word apples like he was laying a wreath."

Mrs. Thackeray hadn't moved. "Did you sign anything?"

"No, ma'am. I gave a false name and a true address and I've been worrying about the combination since Falmouth."

"Good," she said. Then: "The Admiralty. Not a parish committee, not the county. The Admiralty."

"That's the part that kept me walking fast," I said.

* * *

Fen set down the crate. He did it slowly, the way you put down something you might need to throw. "The Admiralty doesn't fund parish archives," he said. "The Admiralty funds cover stories."

"You'd know," Prue said, and there was no cruelty in it — or rather, there was, but it was the old kind, the kind that had worn smooth with use, like the crest on the gatepost.

"I would know," Fen agreed, and didn't flinch, and something passed between them that I was too new to the house to read.

Mrs. Thackeray stood up. She went to the dresser — the big one, the one I'd helped carry in from the estate office my first week, before I'd been sent off with a list of names and questions and the last of her money in my pocket — and she opened the bottom drawer and took out a folder of typed pages. I knew what they were. I'd carried the typewriter paper. I'd heard the keys at night through the wall of the keeper's cottage, that patient clatter like rain on glass, going on and on.

"Prue's account," she said. "Ollie's. Fen's, the parts he's willing to give. The ledgers' dates against the storm dates. It's a record. It's not evidence, not the kind that survives a committee." She laid her hand flat on the folder. "I knew someone would come for the story eventually. I thought it would be a lawyer, or a magistrate, or time. I didn't think it would be a historian with a budget and a head start."

"Head start's the word," I said. "The vicar's already got a dozen accounts, Mrs. Thackeray. All the same story. Saints and apples. And he told me — he looked right at me and told me — that the family papers had been *generously promised* to the archive. Promised. By whom, he wouldn't say. But he said it like a man who'd been told to say it."

"By someone who thinks the papers are his to promise," Mrs. Thackeray said.

"Or someone who wants you to think they're already spoken for," Fen said. "Make you feel the fight's lost before you start."

"Or both," said Prue. "Him and his kind do both. It's cheaper."

Ollie had stopped drawing. He was looking at Mrs. Thackeray with his whole face, the way children and dogs do, and when she caught him at it he held up the slate again and turned it over. On the back, in his careful, pressed-too-hard letters, he'd written one word.

*WHY?*

And that was the question, wasn't it. Why the Admiralty. Why a historian. Why corrected accounts about a family of orchard farmers and a lighthouse keeper's widow, when there was a war on and the whole coast was full of things worth correcting. I watched her not answer, and I watched her decide not to answer, and I understood that she had an answer and it was too heavy to say in front of the boy.

"Ollie," she said gently, "will you ask Prue for more bread for Tom? He's eaten everything and he's still thin."

The boy went. The door to the pantry swung. And Mrs. Thackeray said, low, "Because of the boat. Because of the men who didn't come back. Somebody in the Admiralty knows what was in that boat's hold, or who paid for it to be there, and they'd rather the parish remembered apples." She looked at me. "How soon is the historian collecting?"

"Vicar said by Christmas. Said the archive would be *sealed and dedicated* in the new year. Sealed." I let the word sit. "Like a tomb."

* * *

That night they gave me the loft over the dairy, which was cold but honest, and I lay there listening to the wind work the orchard and thinking about the difference between a record and a story. My father had been a fisherman and my mother had kept the accounts for three boats, and she'd taught me young that the ledger is the only witness that never drowns, never drinks, never takes a shilling. Mrs. Thackeray typed her logs the way my mother kept her books — every entry dated, every entry hers.

But a ledger in a drawer is just paper. The vicar understood that. Whoever was paying him understood that. The story that gets sealed and dedicated first is the story that stands, and the other one becomes the mad widow's folder in a dresser drawer, and then kindling, and then nothing.

I heard the typewriter start up below me, near midnight. That patient clatter. I lay in the dark and did sums, the way she'd taught me to do them without ever teaching me: how long to set type, how long to print, how many copies, how many days until Christmas. The numbers didn't come out kind.

In the morning she was in the kitchen before anyone, and she had the folder out, and she'd made a list.

"Tom," she said, when I came down. "Sit. Eat. And then I need you to do something harder than walking to Falmouth."

"Harder," I said. My bread paused halfway to my mouth.

"I need you to go back to the port town and be a young man of no consequence again. But this time I need you to find out who the historian is before the historian finds out who I am. Where he stays. What he's been told. What he's promised." She looked at me across the table, and the lamp behind her threw her shadow long up the wall, and for a second I saw her as the sea saw her: a fixed light, not moving for anything. "Because the race is real now, Tom. They're writing the ending ahead of us. And I mean to get to the last page first."

Prue came in with the kettle. Fen came in behind her, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at the list in her hand.

"There's a second thing," she said. "The vicar said the family papers were promised to the archive. I never promised them. Which means someone else has been speaking for this family — or someone believes there's still a Thackeray with the right to." She turned the page of her list, and I read the name at the top of it upside down, and my stomach went cold, because it was a name I'd heard in the port town twice in two days, once from the vicar's warm-milk mouth and once from a drunk magistrate's clerk who spat when he said it.

Halloway.

"Who's Halloway?" Ollie asked from the corner, quiet as a draft under a door.

Mrs. Thackeray folded the list. "That," she said, "is what Tom's going to find out."
