## 15. The Informer's Name

The estate office smelled of cold ash and mouse, and Fen had come to love it the way a man loves any room where nobody asks him questions.

He'd been sorting for three days now. Mrs. Thackeray worked at the far end of the long table, typing, her fingers moving with that machine-rhythm he'd learned to sleep beside on the island — no. Not the island. He'd never been to the island. He'd learned it here, in this house, these last weeks: *clack-clack-clack*, pause, the carriage bell, the soft ratchet of a new line. It was better than church bells. Church bells meant something was over. The typewriter meant something was being kept.

She'd given him the paybooks because she said his eyes were younger and his patience older. He hadn't told her what paybooks meant to a man who'd signed one under a false name and deserted the true one. She probably knew. She knew most things without saying them.

"Start with the winters," she'd said that morning, sliding the box toward him. "Prue says the boats ran in winter. Storm season. Nobody watches a cove in storm season."

So Fen started with the winters.

* * *

The paybooks were the Thackeray estate's own — wages paid to orchard men, cellar men, boatmen, all of it entered in a copperplate hand that deteriorated by the year until around 1912, when a new clerk took over with a fist like a boot. Fen made a list of names who drew pay only in the months November through February. Boatmen, then. Orchard men got paid through harvest; these men were paid when the trees were bare.

Eleven names. He wrote them in pencil on the back of a seed catalogue, because paper was paper and Mrs. Thackeray would want it dated and typed eventually, but first you had to know what you were looking at.

Then he went to the letters.

The undelivered post from the potting shed was still mostly unsorted — crates of it, decades of it, tied in bundles with string gone brown as sinew. Mrs. Thackeray had taken the ones addressed in Elias's hand; those were hers, and nobody touched them. The rest were fair game. Most were bills. Some were love letters somebody's father had never dared send. And some — maybe one in forty — were letters that had been opened, read, and resealed badly, the wax cracked and stuck down again with spit or paste, the flap wrinkled where a thumb had pried.

Fen knew intercepted mail. You learned the signs in the army, learning which of your own letters home would arrive trimmed by the censor and which wouldn't arrive at all. A man who steams an envelope gets lazy by the third one. This man had gotten lazier across four years of winters.

He laid the badly-resealed letters out along the windowsill in date order and sat cross-legged on the floor beneath them like a man at prayer, which, if he was honest, was about what it was.

* * *

It took him until the light went thin and gold across the floorboards to see it.

Not every letter was tampered with. Only certain ones. Letters from Plymouth. Letters from Falmouth. Letters with naval postmarks, or postmarks from ports where ships put in before crossing. Letters that mentioned — when he dared unfold them — sailing dates.

One bundle stood apart. These weren't letters to the Thackerays at all. They'd been found in the shed among the rest, misfiled or hidden deliberately: letters to a man named Curnow, addressed care of the estate, from a correspondent in Falmouth who signed himself only *T.* The letters discussed apples — prices, varieties, the blight — in language so thick with horticulture that it could only be code. *The late trees will be ready to move on the dark of the month. Twenty head, all sound stock. The usual ground.*

Twenty head. Sound stock. Fen had helped load enough men into enough hulls to know livestock language when it walked past him wearing a hat.

And at the bottom of each letter, always, a line that didn't fit: *Tell H. the road is good,* or *H. will want the toll early this time,* or once, chillingly plain, *H. has grown careless since his promotion.*

Fen sat very still on the cold floor.

There was a noise behind him and he flinched — a full-body flinch, hand going to a hip where no sidearm hung anymore, and Mrs. Thackeray stood in the doorway with a lamp already lit against the dusk, watching him with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave more than they should.

"You've found something," she said.

"I've found a man named Halloway," Fen said. "Or half of him."

* * *

They spread it all on the long table under the lamp: eleven boatmen's names, the Falmouth letters, three years of winters. Mrs. Thackeray read the way she typed — fast, complete, no line skipped — and Fen watched her face do what faces did when a person's family turned out to be built on a floor that wasn't there.

"T. is Tresidder," she said finally. "Silas Tresidder, Falmouth shipping agent. Prue mentioned him — he supplied the boats. He drowned." She tapped the ledger page. "Lost with the boat. With my uncle-in-law."

"So Tresidder can't have talked."

"No. But Tresidder wrote *to* someone. Care of this estate, which means someone here received for him. Someone with a name starting with H." She pulled the paybooks toward her and began turning pages with a speed that made the paper whisper. "H. H. H."

Fen had already done this part, alone, that afternoon, and had hated the answer so much he'd checked it twice. Now he watched her arrive at it herself, because some things a man shouldn't say to a woman about her own blood.

Her finger stopped.

"Henry Thackeray," she said. Her voice didn't change at all, which was how he knew it cost her. "My father-in-law. The man whose crest sealed the letter that brought me here."

"The same Henry Thackeray who—"

"Died in 1914." She closed the paybook gently, like shutting a door on a sleeping child. "Yes. That's the dead one."

"Then who's alive?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the wild apple trees with a sound like the sea deciding whether to come ashore. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something flat and careful, the voice she used for log entries, for facts that had to survive the night.

"Fen, what do you know about the county magistracy?"

"Not a thing, ma'am."

"Elias's mother had a brother," she said. "A nephew, rather — Henry's sister's son. He came to the estate sometimes when Elias was a boy. Elias never spoke of him except once, drunk on New Year's brandy, and what he said wasn't kind. He went into the law. He did well in the law. And last month, when I asked Tom to find out who funded the vicar's memorial archive—" she unfolded the folded list from her apron pocket, the one Ollie had asked about, the one with the single word on it — "the answer came back in two words."

She laid the list on the table between them.

**Magistrate E. Halloway.**

Edmund Halloway. Fen read the name and felt the dread come up through the floorboards like water through a hull — slow, cold, patient. Because he knew how this worked. He'd seen it work. A magistrate didn't need to break the law; he only needed to hold it, the way a man holds a gate shut, and decide who walked through and who rotted on the wrong side. An informer who became a magistrate could bury the informing. Could bury the boat. Could bury twenty years of drowned men under correct minutes and memorial plaques and a nice archive where the papers went to be polite.

"He's funding the archive," Fen said. "He's collecting the accounts. He's paying to be the one who writes what happened."

"Yes."

"And your papers are sitting thirty feet from a padlocked door he'd give a year of his life to open."

"They're not there anymore." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "Where do you think I've been sleeping? The shed's contents are under the kitchen floor now, under the slate, under the table where we eat."

Fen stared at her. Then he laughed — short, disbelieving, the first real laugh since he'd climbed over a wall in Devon with a rifle he'd refused to fire. "You moved a ship's bell under your supper table."

"I keep what matters close," she said. "That's the whole of my philosophy. It used to fit in one coat pocket."

* * *

But the laugh didn't hold, and neither of them tried to make it. She banked the lamp lower, and in the smaller light her face went older, and she said the thing Fen had been circling all evening without letting himself land on it.

"There's a problem, and you see it as clearly as I do. If Halloway informed — if he sold the convoy, sold the route, sold the men — then the proof runs through letters addressed to his uncle. Dead men make poor witnesses, but dead men also make convenient ones. Everything can be laid on Henry Thackeray's grave. The magistrate's version writes itself: a wicked old smuggler, betrayed by his own greed, boat lost, tragic, closed file. Halloway walks out clean and dedicates a plaque."

"And if the truth comes out," Fen said slowly, working it forward the way you worked a boat off a mudflat, "then it isn't just his shame. It's his power. A magistrate who informed during wartime — that's not scandal, ma'am. Men have been shot for less. He won't fight it with lawyers. There aren't lawyers for that. He'll fight it the other way."

"Which other way?"

Fen looked at the window, at the dark coming down over the orchard, at the kitchen where Prue was feeding Ollie and the boy was laughing at something — actually laughing now, weeks after he'd spoken his first word about boats that came at night.

"The way power always fights," Fen said. "Fire. Warrants. Names on lists. He'll find out what I am eventually — deserters don't stay hidden in villages, they get talked about in pubs. And when he does, he won't need to prove anything. He just needs the right constable to hear a rumour."

Mrs. Thackeray was quiet for a while. Then she reached across the table and squared the seed-catalogue page with its eleven pencil names, aligned it with the letters, aligned it with the paybook, the way she aligned everything — because alignment was how she prayed too, Fen was beginning to understand. The whole household prayed differently and all night long.

"Then we work faster than he does," she said. "Tom should be back within the week with whatever he's found about the archive. When he comes, we'll know how much time we have. Until then—" she gathered the letters into their bundles with hands that didn't hurry and didn't tremble, "—we keep reading. Every winter. Every boat. Every name. If Halloway sold those men, Fen, then somewhere in this paper there's a date and a payment with his fingerprints drying on it. Paper remembers. That's the whole reason I type at all."

"And if paper remembers," Fen said, "so do people. Tresidder's dead. But Tresidder had a wife in Falmouth, didn't he? Widows remember too."

Mrs. Thackeray looked up sharply — and he saw it land, the idea landing in her the way ideas landed in her, visibly, like a bird settling.

"Falmouth," she said.

"Two days' travel. Maybe three, with the trains the way they are."

"I'm not asking you to travel anywhere near a port town, Fen. Ports are the worst place in England for a man with your history."

"I know," Fen said. And found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "When do we leave?"

She held his eyes for a moment — measuring him, the way she measured everything that came within her walls, not for trust exactly, but for weight. For whether he'd hold.

"After Tom comes back," she said. "We'll need to know what we're walking into. And Fen—" she paused at the door, lamp in hand, the light throwing her shadow long up the stairwell, "—if Halloway learns a Thackeray widow and a stranger are asking questions in Falmouth, he'll learn it fast. Whatever we find there, we'll have found it in front of him. Are you ready for that?"

Fen thought about the convoy he'd refused to sail with. About the men who'd sailed anyway. About a mute boy who'd watched boats come in at night and men who never came back.

"No, ma'am," he said honestly. "But I don't suppose ready has anything to do with it."

Something crossed her face that on another woman might have been approval.

Outside, the wind rose through the bare orchard, and somewhere down the valley, faint as a lie told well, a dog began to bark — and did not stop.
