## 16. Harvest Without Hands

### Prue

Forty-one years I've walked these rows, and I never once saw them full of people.

That was the thought that came to me that morning, standing at the kitchen window with the kettle going and the fog burning off the orchard like a sheet being drawn back. The trees were heavy — heavier than they'd been since before the war, since before Mr. Charles drowned and the master stopped coming down to look at anything. Nobody had pruned them properly in three years. Nobody had picked them at all last autumn. The apples had gone over where they fell, sweet rot in the grass, wasps drunk on it into November. A waste. An orchard is a promise, and an unpicked orchard is a broken one.

But this year the grass was cut — Fen had scythed it, badly, in great drunken swathes that made me laugh the first time I saw them, because a man who can strip and clean a rifle blindfolded cannot seem to swing a scythe in a straight line — and this year there were ladders leaning against the barn wall, four of them, old ones, the rungs worn pale by hands long dead. And this year there were people.

Mrs. Thackeray came out first with baskets on her arm, six of them, nested, and she set them down by the gate like a woman laying out surgical instruments. She'd been up half the night typing — I heard the machine through my floor, that soft rattle like rain on a skylight, going past midnight, past one — but you'd never know it to look at her. She had her sleeves rolled. She had Elias's coat off and hung on the fence rail so it wouldn't catch on the branches. She looked, for the first time since she'd come up from the coast in that rattling trap with the boy Tom Wrenn and his one bag, like she belonged to the ground under her feet.

"Prue," she called. "Which rows first?"

I came out wiping my hands. "The Codlins," I said. "They'll drop if we don't. Then the Costards. The Russets can wait — they keep."

"And those?" She nodded toward the far corner, where the oldest trees stood, the ones gone half to mistletoe, their bark split and silvered. Nobody had told her what they were. I'd been waiting for someone to tell her, and dreading it, and here she was asking plain.

"Those are the Widow Makers," I said, and watched her take it. She didn't flinch. She'd stopped flinching at Thackeray things somewhere in the second week, or maybe she'd just learned to do it inside where nobody could see. "Planted by your husband's grandfather. They say he put a coin under each root."

"For luck?"

"For debt," I said. "He borrowed against every tree before it bore. But they've borne eighty years without him." I handed her a basket. "Start low. Mind the wasps."

Ollie came out of the woodshed when he heard the ladders scraping, the way he comes out for everything now, quiet as a cat but always near. He'd taken to carrying things. It was the first work anyone had given him here, carrying things, and he did it with both arms wrapped around whatever it was — basket, ladder, bundle of rags — like the thing might bolt. He stood at the gate now looking up into the Codlin row, and the light through the leaves made green coins on his face, and I thought: there's a boy who's never seen a harvest done right. There's a boy who's never seen a harvest at all.

"Well then," I said, loud enough for all of them. "We'll not get them all. We'll get enough."

And Mrs. Thackeray — Xanthe, though I couldn't yet say it, the name still sat in my mouth like a stone from another country — Xanthe Thackeray took hold of the lowest branch of the first tree and began to pick.

### Fen

The trouble with picking apples is that it leaves your mind unoccupied.

I'd learned to guard against that. In the army you learn it fast — the long marches, the night watches, the hours of nothing in Flanders mud — you learn to fill the silence with counting, with lists, with the names of streets back home. But apple-picking is worse than a march, because it's gentle. Your hands go up and come down, up and come down, and the sun moves, and somewhere behind you Prue is telling Ollie which ones twist off easy and which want a snap, and your mind starts walking places you've spent eight months teaching it not to walk.

The order. That's what comes. *Fire on my command* — no. Not even that. Just the road outside Ypres, the convoy idling, and the captain's voice, and my own voice saying *no, sir,* flat as a dropped plate, and the sound the world makes after a man says no. I said no and I ran, and every day since has been bought with that running, and some days — most days — I don't think about it. But today, with my hands full of fruit and the smell of apples everywhere, thick as ether, my mind went there and stood a while.

"Twist, don't pull," said a voice at my elbow.

It was her. Mrs. Thackeray. She'd come along the row quiet as Ollie does, and she was watching my hands, which were pulling, tearing, leaving stems behind like little wounds.

"Sorry," I said. "Not much of an orchard man."

"You were a soldier." She said it level, no question in it, and the bottom dropped out of my stomach. "Soldiers' hands know how to take things off quickly. You have to teach them the other way." She reached past me into the branch and took an apple — turned it once, cupped it, and it came away whole, stem and all, clean. "Like that. You're not defeating it. You're asking."

I laughed. I didn't mean to; it came out of me sideways, startled. "Asking," I said.

"That's what Prue says. Everything here, she says it like that. *Ask the tree.*" She filled my basket a little fuller without appearing to notice she'd done it. "Fen. I'm not going to ask you what you were. When you want to tell me, the typewriter will be there. Until then—" she looked up into the leaves, dappled, moving, "—until then you're the man who mowed the lawn in circles, and that's employment enough for anyone."

She moved on down the row. I stood there holding a basket of Codlins with my heart going like a drum in a parade, and after a while I started picking again, twisting each one, asking.

By midday my mind stayed in the orchard the whole time. First time in eight months. I don't know what to call that. I've been calling it nothing, because naming it feels like knocking it down.

* * *

### Ollie

There was a wasp in my collar and I did not scream.

That is a true thing. Before, a wasp would have been screaming. Before, everything was screaming, inside, where nobody could hear it, so loud that my voice got used up and there was none left over for out loud. But the wasp walked around in my collar and I stood very still the way Prue taught me with the bees — *bees smell fear, ducks too, most things do* — and I held my breath until my chest hurt and then it flew out and went to a bruised apple on the ground and I let my breath go.

Then I laughed.

It came out strange. Like a door opening in a house where all the doors had been painted shut. It came out rusty and too big and I put my hand over my mouth but more came out around my fingers, and Fen looked down from his ladder and nearly dropped his basket, and Prue dropped hers on purpose, and then Mrs. Thackeray was laughing too, up in the branches with the leaves shaking around her, and Tom Wrenn started laughing because everyone else was, and the wasp-birds — the wagtails, the ones with the black and white, the ones Dad used to say were the orchard's own birds — went up off the fence all together like a page turning.

I climbed the ladder after that. My own ladder. Fen held the bottom and pretended he wasn't holding it and I pretended I didn't need it held and we both knew and it was fine. From the top of the ladder the whole orchard was below me, all the rows going down and down to the wall, and the sea beyond it, gray-silver, and I could see everybody at once: Prue bent under the Costards, moving faster than any of us, faster than a person that age should move, her apron filling; Tom up the next tree over throwing apples down to Mrs. Thackeray, who caught them, every one, into the basket on her hip; Fen on the ground sorting the drops from the picked, laying them in two lines like soldiers and then, catching himself, like something else, like letters in a word.

I picked one with two hands. It was big as my head. It came away clean.

Down at the end of the row there was a tree nobody was working. The Widow Makers. I looked at them a long time. Their apples were small and dark red and high up, higher than our ladders, and under them the grass grew different, thicker, darker. Prue says Grandfather Thackeray planted them and buried money underneath and that men died in the war of the trees' own making, which doesn't make sense unless it means the cider, and I think it means the cider.

I ate my apple sitting on the lowest rung with my legs dangling, and the juice ran down my wrist, and I thought: I will remember this. This exact thing. The juice and the rung and everyone's voices going at once. Because before, remembering was only bad things, and I didn't know you got to choose.

Maybe I said some of that out loud. Maybe I didn't. Either way, Prue looked over at me like she'd heard it, and smiled the way she does with her whole face going crooked, and threw me another apple.

* * *

### Tom

I'd come back up from the harbor path with the post — two circulars, a bill for the trap horse, and a brown-paper parcel from the port town that Mrs. Thackeray went white holding and then put in her pocket without opening — and I found the whole lot of them up the trees like a picture off a calendar.

Well. Like a picture off a calendar where one of the pickers is a deserter, one's a mute lad, one's a servant older than the trees, and the lady of the house catches apples thrown from twenty feet like she was born to it. Which she wasn't. I've seen her hands. Keeper's hands, rope-and-brass hands, hands that have spent years polishing glass in the dark while the world tried to smash itself against the rocks below. Those hands aren't made for orchards. But there she was, sleeves rolled, hair coming loose, laughing — actually laughing, I'd not heard it before, and neither had anybody, you could tell, because Fen stopped dead the first time like a horse at a fence.

I leaned on the gate and watched a bit before announcing myself. That's a keeper habit too, or maybe just mine: watch the light before you touch the switch.

They looked — I'll write it plain, since nobody reads this but me — they looked like a family. Not a proper one. A family assembled from wreckage, the way after a storm the beach is strewn with bits of a dozen ships and you gather the planking that fits together and build something. Prue was the keel. That was clear enough. The rest of us were planking of various wrecks.

"Tom Wrenn!" Prue shouted the moment she saw me. "Don't lean! Fetch the second barrow!"

So I fetched the second barrow. And then I was up a tree, because in that household you do not get to be a visitor twice, and I threw apples down to Mrs. Thackeray and she caught them, every one, and between throws she talked — about the island, about the lamp, about how Elias used to count the fog signals in a storm the way she used to count waves, and how they'd once worked out that in nine years of marriage they'd measured eleven thousand storms between them and never once counted anything good.

"What would you count good?" I asked her, because it seemed safe up a tree.

She thought about it, really thought, head tipped back among the leaves. And then she looked down at the barrows, at Prue and Fen and Ollie and me, and she said, "I appear to have stopped counting altogether," and threw the next apple so hard I nearly missed it.

At the end of the day we pressed the first cider in the old barn press, the screws shrieking, the juice running brown-gold into a bucket that had holes patched with tar, and Prue poured out a measure for each of us in tin cups and one splash into a saucer for Ollie, who drank it solemn as communion. Fen raised his cup and didn't say anything, and nobody needed him to. Mrs. Thackeray raised hers to the trees. To the trees, she said. To the ones that bore anyway.

I drank mine looking west, because from the barn door you can see the water, and somewhere out past the horizon was my island and her lighthouse, dark now or soon to be, decommissioned, its lamp cold in a room that used to be the warmest place in England if you were a ship in trouble. I thought: we ought to save it. Somebody ought to save it. And I filed the thought away the way you file a spark in tinder, not knowing yet what it would catch.

* * *

### Xanthe

The ledger entry for that date, in the estate book I'd begun keeping beside the typewritten testimonies, reads simply:

*October 3rd. Wind SW light. Harvest begun, Codlin and Costard rows, 11 barrows pressed to 9 gallons. All hands well. Ollie spoke — laughing, not words yet, but the household agrees laughter counts.*

What it doesn't record is the arithmetic I did not do.

I have measured my life in portable things since the day I married Elias and learned that a keeper's wife owns nothing the sea won't eventually ask for. Two valises. One typewriter, thirty pounds, crated. Spare lenses wrapped in wool. His coat. I could stand in any room I'd ever lived in and name within a minute what would leave it with me if the orders came, and I did it constantly, compulsively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Even here, even at Thackeray Orchard with its forty acres and its cellars full of other people's secrets, I had caught myself in the first weeks doing the sums. *If we must go: the machine, the ledgers, Prue takes the boy, Fen takes—* wherever Fen takes whatever Fen takes. The sums ran themselves at night while the typewriter slept.

But that evening, walking back up from the barn with the press smell on my hands and the last light lying along the rows like spilled oil, I realized the sums had stopped. I couldn't say when. Somewhere between the wasp and the laughter, between Fen's crooked mowing and Ollie's two-handed apple, the inventory had quietly closed its books. I stood in the middle of the orchard and tried to start it again — *if we must go, what carries?* — and the answer that rose was absurd and enormous and true:

All of it. All of them. None of it packs.

An orchard cannot be carried. Neither, apparently, can a family, once you admit that's the word. And I understood, standing there with sticky hands in the cooling air, why Elias had chosen the light over the land. Not to escape the family shame — I'd thought that for years, pitied him for it. He chose the light because the light was the one thing that couldn't be inherited, sold, burned, or signed away. It could only be kept. And keeping was the whole of the job.

I had inherited forty acres and a locked shed and a name soaked in somebody else's sin. But the keeping — the keeping I'd brought with me, in the crates, in the cadence, in the habit of writing down every ship and storm and stranger. And now the strangers were writing back.

Behind me, up at the house, the kitchen window glowed yellow. Prue was singing something tuneless and old. Ollie's laugh carried across the grass, startling itself anew every few minutes like a bird remembering it could fly. Fen was stacking the empty barrows with a soldier's precision he'd never lose and no longer needed to apologize for. And Tom — Tom was late coming in from the barn, and when he caught up he was wearing the particular expression he wore when he'd had an idea too large for his mouth, and he opened his mouth—

—and down the lane, unhurried, crunching the gravel, came a motorcar.

None of us owned a motorcar. None of our friends did. In this county, in this season, a motorcar on the Thackeray lane meant exactly one kind of visitor, and Prue's singing stopped mid-note, and Fen's hand went to his hip where a holster hadn't hung in eight months, and Ollie went still as the shed.

The car stopped at the gate. A man stepped out, tall, gray-gloved, and lifted his hat with a courtesy so smooth it took me a moment to recognize the face from the parish photographs.

"Mr. Halloway," I said. "You're a long way from your courtroom."

"Just an admirer of orchards, Mrs. Thackeray," said the magistrate, smiling into the last of the light. "May I walk the rows with you?"
