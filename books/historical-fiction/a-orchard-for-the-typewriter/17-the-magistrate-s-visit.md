## 17. The Magistrate's Visit

He arrived at half past ten on a morning so still the smoke from the kitchen chimney rose in a straight grey line, as if drawn with a rule. Xanthe saw the motorcar first — a black Daimler crawling up the lane at a speed that suggested the driver feared the hedges would scratch it — and she understood, before the door of it opened, exactly who had come.

Magistrate Halloway stepped out into the yard and removed his hat. He was smaller than she expected. She had built him large in her mind, the way one builds storms large from the sound of them alone, but he was a compact man of perhaps sixty, silver-haired, neat as a ledger column, with the kind of face that had spent decades agreeing pleasantly with people while writing down the opposite.

"Mrs. Thackeray." He pronounced the name carefully, giving it back to her like something borrowed. "I hope you'll forgive an unannounced call. I was passing through on county business, and I thought — well. We are neighbors of a sort now, aren't we? Neighbors oughtn't to be strangers."

Behind him, the driver unloaded a wicker hamper. Game pie, she would learn later. A jar of quince jelly. Wine with a French label that had no business crossing a blockaded channel, which told her more about the magistrate than his card did.

"Mr. Halloway," she said. "We're not neighbors. The nearest neighbor is four miles off."

"Then we shall be the closest thing each has." He smiled. "May I see the orchard? I'm told the Thackeray apples were famous once. My father spoke of them."

It was not a request. It had the shape of one — the lifted brows, the open palms — but she had watched men ask permission for things they intended to take all her married life. Still, there was nothing to be gained by barring the gate of her own ground. She led him out past the ruined wall where the brambles had gone over like a tide, and Fen, who had been mending a spade handle by the barn, straightened slowly and did not come closer.

Halloway walked the rows with his hands clasped behind him, admiring the wildness in the way a man admires a fire he has not yet been asked to put out.

"Neglected," he said at last, gently. "Forty years of neglect. It's a shame. There were men who'd have given much for this land during the enclosure years." He touched a lichen-crusted branch as though greeting an old acquaintance. "Your father-in-law let it run, near the end. Grief does that. Or guilt. One never quite knows which, with the Thackerays."

"You knew my husband's father?"

"We corresponded." A pause measured to the width of a page. "Long ago."

They came around the bend of the wall and the house appeared, its east wing slumped like a tired animal. Ollie was on the step, shelling beans into a blue bowl, and Xanthe saw the boy's hands go still at the sight of the motorcar, saw him rise without a word and carry the bowl inside — a small evacuation, practiced, quiet, the instinct of someone who had learned early what visitors meant.

"Remarkable household you've gathered," Halloway observed. "A gardener of — Prue, is it? Forty years' service. A young man who does heavy work and gives no surname when the parish clerk asks. And a child with no school record anywhere in two counties." He said it all mildly, an inventory, a shopping list. "You do keep things, Mrs. Thackeray. I understand that about you. Keepers always do."

The word *keeper* sat in the air between them like a struck match.

"The boy is deaf-mute," Xanthe said, "and under my protection until someone claims him properly. The young man is hired labor. The old woman is family to this estate longer than either of us has been alive. If any of that requires a magistrate's attention, you may address it to me in writing."

"Oh, I shall write," he said pleasantly. "I write constantly. It's the curse of the profession. Everyone believes history is made by soldiers. It's made by clerks, Mrs. Thackeray. By men who write things down." He turned to look at her directly for the first time, and his eyes were pale, unhurried, patient as tide. "You'll forgive me — your reputation precedes you. The widow who keeps her husband's log. Who types every visitor, every boat, every storm. They say you'd record the wind's signature if you could get it to hold the pen."

"They say correctly."

"Then you'll appreciate my difficulty." He gestured toward the house, toward the office window above the lean-to roof. "There are papers in that building. Estate papers, shipping records, correspondence accumulated across sixty years and three generations of Thackerays. Papers which, in wartime, fall under certain — considerations. Some of what a family keeps is private. Some of it, in these years, is *materiel*." He said the word lightly, almost fondly. "I've come to offer you help with the distinction."

Inside, he said, if she didn't mind. He admired the hall, the worn flags, the sword over the mantel that hadn't been sharpened since Crimea. Prue brought tea without being asked and set it down hard enough to ring the china, and Halloway thanked her by name, which was its own kind of message: *I know everyone in this house, and how long they've been in it.*

* * *

The drawing room had not hosted company since before the war. Dust sheets lay over the furniture like snowdrifts; Halloway took the one chair already uncovered, which told her he knew the room better than she did. He settled the hamper on the table between them, opened the wine, poured two glasses, and pushed one across.

"I won't insult you by pretending this is a social call."

"I'd have been disappointed if you had."

He laughed — a genuine sound, brief and warm, and somehow worse than anything he'd said yet. "Elias chose well," he said. "Though I suppose he didn't choose. The light chooses its people, doesn't it? Very few can stand the solitude. Fewer still can stand it *happily*." He turned his glass by the stem. "My condolences on your loss, by the way. Belated. The *Speranza* went down in March of '17, didn't she? Off your rock. You logged her yourself."

Xanthe kept her hands still in her lap. "I log everything."

"Yes. That's rather the problem." He leaned forward. "Here is the situation as the county sees it. An estate changes hands. The heir arrives and within weeks begins collecting statements from — persons of interest. A gardener. A deserter, though we needn't use the word indoors. A child who was present, we believe, at certain nocturnal activities in the cove below this property. And she types it all up. Dates it. Certifies it." He smiled again. "You are assembling a case file, Mrs. Thackeray. Whether you intend one or not. And a case file implies a crime, and a crime implies a criminal, and the county is very interested in who you think the criminal might be."

"And if I think the criminal signed his name in a ledger?"

The pleasantness did not move. It was the stillness of a man standing on ground he owned. "Then you'd be wise to consider what happens to evidence that exists only in the hands of amateurs. Fires happen in old houses. Floods. Mice. Paper is fragile, and grief makes women careless." He sipped. "I am offering to take custody of the Thackeray papers. All of them. To have them catalogued, preserved, housed properly at the county archive in Truro, where trained hands will keep them safe from damp and from — enthusiasm. In exchange, the estate's legal position is regularized. Any irregularities in the tenancy of the land — squatters, unlicensed occupation, that sort of thing — are forgiven. Wiped clean. And you return to your light with your name unstained and your household intact."

"My household," she repeated.

"Is currently composed of a woman harboring deserters," he said, with the gentleness of a doctor delivering a prognosis, "which is an offense carrying up to two years, Mrs. Thackeray. For each man. I don't make the law; I merely apply it, and lately I apply it rather often — the war has made us all busy. But the law also permits discretion, and discretion is what I'm selling today. Cheaply, considering."

She looked at him across the dust-sheeted room and understood that this was the drawing-room version of the storm: not wind and water but courtesy, layer upon layer of it, and underneath, the same appetite. He wanted the papers. Everything else — the offers, the warnings, the game pie — was ballast to steady the approach.

"And if I decline?"

"Then I must do my duty, and my duty is thorough." He rose, replaced his glass on the tray, and buttoned his coat with small precise movements. "But do think about it first. Truly. You strike me as a woman who understands what a record costs. You've lived beside the sea long enough to know the sea keeps no records at all. It takes what it takes and leaves no accounting." At the door he paused, hat in hand. "Oh — one more thing. The potting shed. Padlocked these many years. I'd counsel you strongly against opening it. Some inheritances are better left sealed. Your father-in-law thought so. It cost him a great deal to seal it, and he was a stronger man than most."

"How do you know what's inside it?"

For the first time, something flickered behind the pale eyes — quick, quickly mastered. "I read correspondence," he said. "As I told you. Clerks make history." He set his hat on. "You have until the end of the month. I'll send a man for your answer."

The Daimler pulled away down the lane without hurrying, and the straight line of chimney smoke bent after it, and then the morning was still again.

* * *

They assembled in the kitchen that evening because no one suggested anywhere else. Prue, Fen, Tom — who had ridden back from the port town that afternoon and arrived with salt still on him and news he hadn't yet given — and Ollie, sitting close against Prue's side with his bowl of shelled beans forgotten in his lap. Xanthe gave them the visit entire: the tour, the tea, the offer, the warning about the shed, the two years per man.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a while. The range ticked. Outside, the first wind of the evening moved through the wild orchard, and somewhere in the dark an apple dropped.

"He knows about the shed," Prue said finally. Her voice was flat, but her hands, folded on the table, were white-knuckled. "Forty years I served that family and I never saw inside it. Never. And this man walks in and speaks of it like it's his own linen cupboard."

"He reads other people's post," Xanthe said. "That's how a man builds a career like his."

Fen had gone very quiet in the corner, and she watched him turn something over — not fear exactly; arithmetic. Two years per man. He was doing sums with his own neck and finding the figures generous to the crown.

"He gave you till month's end," Fen said. "Which means he thinks you'll fold. Men like that only give deadlines when they're sure of the outcome."

"Then we'll disappoint him."

Tom shifted forward on the bench. "There's something else. Before he came." He looked at Xanthe, apologetic, breathless still from the road. "In the port town. The vicar's memorial archive — it isn't the vicar's. I found the subscription list pinned up outside the chandlery. Half the names are Admiralty. And there's a historian already installed, a Mr. Ashcombe, going through the shipping registers at the customs house, asking after the storm years. Asking after the cove here. He's correcting accounts, the chandler told me. *Correcting* them."

Xanthe felt it settle over the room — the thing she had been circling since the razored pages, since the vicar's polished version, since Halloway's velvet offer. They were not being threatened by one man. They were being outrun by a version of events that was being written right now, in Truro, in London, in ink that would dry hard as concrete, while the truth sat padlocked in a shed waiting for a key.

Ollie tugged Prue's sleeve. When she bent to him, he pointed — at the dark window, toward the garden, toward the birdhouses along the south wall his father had built before the war took him. Then he looked at Xanthe, and his eyes were not a child's eyes just then.

Prue went still. "Boy," she whispered, "what is it you know?"
