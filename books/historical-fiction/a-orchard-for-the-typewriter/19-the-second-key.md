## 19. The Second Key

The birdhouse has been empty for a long time.

Ollie knows this because he checks. He checks every day, the way he checks the gate latch and the loose stone by the well and the place where the fence rail fits into its hole. Things that are supposed to be somewhere should stay there. That is what his father said once, when Ollie asked why they had to nail down the beehive lid in the wind. *Things that matter, you make sure of them.* His father said it with sawdust on his hands and a nail held in his teeth, so it came out sounding funny, like a song sung through wood.

Ollie does not talk. He stopped talking a long time ago, back when the boats came to the cove at night and the men went out and one night the boats came back wrong — too few, too quiet, and nobody on the beach would look at anybody else. Words got heavy after that. They sat in his chest like wet sand. So he keeps them, and he keeps other things instead: the latch, the stone, the rail, the birdhouse.

The birdhouse hangs in the old pear tree at the end of the kitchen garden, past where Prue's beans used to climb before the beans gave up and the brambles won. It is made of good oak, planed smooth, with a little pitched roof and a round hole cut neat as a coin. His father made it the winter before everything went bad. He said it was for the wrens. No wren ever moved in. Ollie used to think that meant his father was no good at guessing where birds wanted to live. Now he thinks maybe his father never meant it for birds at all.

Because the bottom board is not nailed like the others.

Ollie found this out a long time ago too, when he was smaller, when he still climbed trees without thinking about who might see him from the house. The bottom board lifts if you press the corner near the trunk side and pull. Underneath there is a hollow, lined with oilcloth folded over twice, and inside the hollow lies a key.

It is an ugly key. Long, iron, dark as a beetle's back, with a stem bent slightly to one side, like it was dropped once and straightened badly. It is nothing like the keys Prue keeps on her ring, which are small and bright and jingle. This one does not jingle. It sits in your palm and waits.

Ollie has taken it out and put it back more times than he can count. He takes it out when the nightmares come — the ones with the water and the bell sound, though he has never known why a bell, since there is no bell anywhere, only the church one and that sounds different. He holds it until it warms up and then he puts it back and presses the board down and climbs down and goes in to breakfast and says nothing, the way he always says nothing.

But today is different today. Today the lady is here.

* * *

Ollie watches people the way he watches weather. You learn more from watching than from asking, and anyway he cannot ask. The lady — Mrs. Thackeray, though Fen calls her Xanthe when he thinks no one hears — arrived in autumn with a coat too thin for the wind and eyes that kept measuring things. Ollie knows measuring eyes. His father had them. Sailors have them. People who need to know how much room is left, how far, how deep.

She measured everything when she came. The doorways. The pantry shelves. Fen, when she thought he wasn't looking. And then, bit by bit, she stopped measuring and started doing. Fixing the hinge on the scullery door. Writing at the table every evening with her fingers going fast on the typewriter thing she brought in a wooden case, making that lovely clatter-clack-ding noise like rain on a tin roof but tidier. Ollie could listen to it all day. Sometimes he pretends to be asleep in the corner chair just to hear it longer.

She wants to know things. He can tell. She asks Prue questions in a soft voice and Prue answers in a hard one, and between the two of them the truth gets passed around like something hot that might burn whoever holds it. She asks Fen questions too, and Fen goes stiff and changes the subject to turnips or the roof.

And she has been looking at the potting shed.

Ollie has seen her looking. Everyone looks at the potting shed eventually, because it is the one thing on the whole estate that refuses to explain itself. The padlock on the door is old and big and wrong — Prue says she has tried every key on her ring and half the keys in the estate office drawer and none of them fit, which is a strange thing for a lock to do on a farm full of tools. Once, when Ollie was carrying kindling past at dusk, he saw Mrs. Thackeray crouched at the gap between two boards, very still, the moonlight coming through and catching whatever metal lives inside. Her face was the face of someone listening to a door that hasn't opened in years.

He knows what is in there. Not all of it. Some of it. He was small enough, the year it happened, to be underfoot and unnoticed, which is the best way to see anything.

His father used the shed at night. Ollie remembers the padlock clicking open in the dark, remembers being lifted through the door by strong arms and set down among smells of oil and apples and canvas. Remembered men sitting on crates, some of them crying without any noise, the way men cry when they don't want children to know. His father moving among them with a lamp. His mother keeping watch at the door crack. And in the corner, under sacking, the shapes — long boxes, and one shape that was round and heavy and made a low hum when you touched it, a hum you felt in your hand-bones rather than heard.

The humming shape. The bell sound in his dreams. He did not know those were the same until right now, standing under the pear tree in the cold, holding the birdhouse in both hands.

* * *

He waits for the afternoon, when the light comes slantwise through the kitchen window and everyone gathers near the fire. Prue is paring apples, the peel coming off in one unbroken ribbon, which she claims means luck. Fen is mending a boot sole, pulling waxed thread through leather with his teeth gritted. Mrs. Thackeray is typing. Clatter-clack-ding.

Ollie goes out through the garden door. Nobody follows him; nobody ever follows him, because he is quiet and quick and people forget he exists, which used to hurt and now is useful. The grass is silvered with frost that squeaks under his boots. The pear tree stands bare, its branches like black writing against the grey sky.

He climbs. His boots find the old holds without looking — the knuckle of bark, the fork, the elbow branch. The birdhouse swings gently as he reaches it. He presses the bottom corner. Lifts the board. The oilcloth is cold and stiff.

The key is waiting.

He takes it. For the first time ever, he does not put it back. He slides it into his pocket, where it lies against his leg like a small hot coal even though it's freezing. Then he climbs down and stands in the frost and looks at the shed across the yard.

The shed looks back. It has one window, boarded, and a door that sags on its hinges, and the padlock hanging there fat and certain, sure that no one will ever come for it.

Ollie walks toward it. His heart is going fast, faster than climbing makes it go. Halfway there he stops, because a thought arrives all at once, the way thoughts do when they've been waiting years for permission:

This is not stealing. This is delivering.

His father hid the key in a birdhouse. A birdhouse is a house with a door. Keys live in houses. But keys are also meant to travel — that's the whole point of a key, it goes from a pocket to a lock, it moves. His father must have known that someday someone would come who needed the moving done. Someone with measuring eyes. Someone who writes everything down.

Mrs. Thackeray.

Ollie turns and crosses back to the kitchen, frost squeaking, key burning cold in his pocket, and pushes open the door on the smell of apples and fire smoke and ink.

* * *

They are all three still there. Good. He had worried, in the thirty steps between tree and door, that the moment would scatter — that Prue would be off feeding hens and Fen gone to the woodpile and the lady upstairs, and he would have to choose one alone, and choosing one alone might break it.

He stands in the doorway. They don't notice. Prue's ribbon of peel lengthens. Fen's needle bites. The typewriter clatters.

Ollie walks to the table. He stops beside Mrs. Thackeray, close enough that the typewriter carriage nearly brushes his sleeve, and he takes the key out of his pocket and sets it down on the table beside her paper.

Clack. Ding. Silence.

Prue looks first. Her knife stops mid-stroke and the peel breaks, and she stares at the key lying there on the page she'd been reading over, and her face goes through three weathers in one second — blankness, recognition, fear. "Where did you get that," she says, not really a question, more a prayer said backwards.

Fen has gone very still, the way soldiers go still, the stillness that is itself a kind of running.

Mrs. Thackeray picks up the key. Turns it in her fingers. The lamplight runs along the bend in its stem. "I've never seen this," she says quietly. Then, looking up at Ollie with those measuring eyes gone wide: "Ollie. Where has this been?"

He points. Out the window, across the yard, at the pear tree. He holds up two fingers, then makes them flap. Wing-shapes. Then he cups his hands into a little roof.

"The tree," Fen says slowly. He sets down the boot. "There's nothing in that tree."

"House," Ollie says.

His own voice comes out rusty, cracked down the middle like a plate left in frost. All three of them flinch. Prue puts her hand flat on the table as if the floor has tilted. Ollie almost stops — almost lets the words sink back down into the wet sand where they've lived — but the key is on the table now and there is no putting it back either, not really, not after showing it to people.

"Birdhouse," he says. "My father made it." Four words. Each one costs him, each one lands. "Bottom board comes away."

Prue has sat down. She sits down heavily, without deciding to, the way old people do when their legs vote without them. "Forty years I've hung washing under that tree," she whispers. "Forty years."

Mrs. Thackeray is already standing, key closed in her fist, her other hand flat on the table the way Prue's is. She looks at Ollie and doesn't ask if he's sure, which he loves her for, because he is sure, he has been sure since the moment the key touched his pocket. She looks at Fen. "You'll stand watch?" she says. "If anyone comes up the lane while we're inside, cough twice."

Fen swallows. Whatever war is happening behind his face, he loses it. "Aye," he says. "Go on, then."

* * *

The walk across the yard takes eleven steps. Ollie counts them, because counting is a thing he can do when talking is a thing he can't. Mrs. Thackeray walks beside him, not ahead, not behind. Beside. The frost has begun to sweat in the weak sun and the yard smells of wet stone and old apple.

At the shed door they stop. The padlock hangs in its hasp, smug. She holds the key up to the light, turning it, fitting its shape against the lock's shape in the air, and Ollie sees her understand what he understood under the tree: the keyhole shaped wrong for every key on the estate is exactly the right shape for this one. The lock was never broken. It was specific.

"Your father's," she says softly. Not to Ollie exactly. To the air, to the dead man, to the record she keeps. "This was your father's key."

Ollie nods.

She puts it in.

The lock resists — the mechanism grinding through years of rust and spider silk — and then, with a shudder Ollie feels through the door itself rather than hears, it gives. The padlock opens like a fist unclenching. She lifts it away and sets it on the windowsill, deliberate, leaving it visible, unlocked, a fact anyone can check. Ollie likes that about her. She leaves evidence where evidence belongs.

Then she pulls the door, and the past exhales.

Cold air rolls out, thick with the smell of apples gone soft, machine oil, canvas, mouse, and underneath it all something mineral and old, like the inside of a cave. The light through the opened door falls in a long gold wedge across the floorboards, and in that light Ollie sees his father's secret room the way an adult sees it, not the way a small boy hiding under tables saw it.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked along the walls, stenciled with names and ports — Falmouth, Penzance, Brest — some stamped with the franking marks of a post office, none of them ever delivered. Sacking over the tall shapes at the back. And in the middle of the floor, on a trestle his father built, the round heavy shape, uncovered now, green-black with age, its rope still coiled through the crown.

"Good God," Mrs. Thackeray breathes. She crosses to it and lays her hand flat on the curve, the way you'd lay a hand on a sleeping animal. "It's a ship's bell." She leans closer, reading the lettering worn shallow but legible, and Ollie watches her lips move around the name, and watches the name land on her like weather. He knows the name. He has known it since before he knew it meant anything. It is the name of the boat that didn't come back. The boat his father's brother was on. The boat everyone in the village pretends sank in the storm, plain and simple, act of God, nothing to be done.

"It didn't sink," Ollie says.

His voice again, rusty, but steadier this time. Mrs. Thackeray turns. She doesn't rush him. She waits, hands still on the bell, the whole shed waiting with her.

"Boats came to the cove," Ollie says. The words come easier now, like water finding an old channel. "Night boats. Men got out. Men got in. My father kept the list." He points at the crates. "Letters. Letters that couldn't be posted, because posting them would tell. My father kept them till it was safe." He stops. The last part is the hardest, the part he has never said to anyone because there was never anyone to say it to, and because saying it makes it true in a way that can't be unsaid. "Then somebody told. And the last boat went out into the storm anyway, because the navy was waiting and they couldn't turn back, and it didn't come home. And my father—" His chest seizes. Wet sand. He breathes through it. "My father locked all this up and put the key where only a child would find it, and then he died, and I never told."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a rook calls once and gives up.

Mrs. Thackeray comes to him and kneels, so that her eyes are level with his, and she says, "You're telling now."

He nods.

"That's the bravest thing done on this estate in four years," she says. "Including everything I've done. Including everything Fen's done. You kept it safe, Ollie. Things that matter, you make sure of them." She smiles, small and fierce. "You made sure."

He didn't tell her his father's saying. Which means she knew it too, or something like it, which means the world is less lonely than he thought, which is a discovery bigger than the shed.

* * *

They work through the afternoon. Fen joins them when the lane stays empty, and Prue too, though she stands in the doorway mostly, one hand pressed to her mouth, saying names under her breath as the crates are opened — names of boys she fed, names of men whose boots she mended, and once, terribly, her own name, on a letter addressed in her sister's hand that never reached her because reaching her would have endangered her.

The undelivered letters fill three boxes on their own. Some are sealed, some are open, read and re-read by men who had no one else to read to. There are love letters, money letters, a christening announcement, a death notice, a letter from a woman in France written in French that none of them can read but all of them handle carefully, as if the language itself were wounded.

And beneath the letters, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, the ledger.

Mrs. Thackeray unties it with fingers that are not quite steady. It is not the shipping ledger from the estate office, the one with pages razored out — Fen checked that one twice, and said so. This one is different: smaller, denser, written in a cramped hand Ollie recognizes instantly, because it is the same hand that labeled every jam jar in Prue's pantry and wrote *for the wrens* on the underside of a birdhouse. Dates. Boats. Names out, names in. Payments received, payments owed. And signatures — not many, but some, initials and flourishes, receipts for silence, and one name recurring at the bottom of the worst pages, month after month, patient as tide.

Mrs. Thackeray reads it standing, then sitting, then kneeling on the floor with the book flat before her like something in church. When she finally looks up, her face has gone the color of the frost outside.

"I need tonight," she says. "I need the lamp, and the typewriter, and no one speaking to me until dawn. Can the household give me that?"

"We can," Prue says, from the doorway, in the voice she uses for weather that's coming.

Ollie tugs Mrs. Thackeray's sleeve. She looks down. He points at the bell, then at her, then mimes writing.

"You want me to write it down too? The bell?"

He nods. Names fade, he thinks. Metal doesn't lie. Put it in the log. Everything in the log. Things that matter, you make sure of them.

"Yes," she says. "All of it. Every name, every date, every signature. By morning there will be a copy that no fire, no magistrate, and no missing page can touch." She closes the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person. "By morning, it will be a record."

Outside, the sun is going down red behind the orchard's wild rows, and the first star is out over the cove where the boats used to come. Ollie stands in the shed door with the empty birdhouse behind him and the unlocked padlock bright on the sill, and listens to Mrs. Thackeray carry the true ledger into the kitchen, where the typewriter waits with its keys up like a field of small black flowers ready to be planted.

That night, for the first time in four years, Ollie sleeps without dreaming of water.

He dreams instead of clatter-clack-ding, steady as surf, going on and on until morning.
