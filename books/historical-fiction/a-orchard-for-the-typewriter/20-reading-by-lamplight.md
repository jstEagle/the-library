## 20. Reading by Lamplight

The potting shed smelled of earth and iron and forty years of apples rotting into the floorboards. I carried the crates inside one at a time, past Prue's door, past Fen's window with its candle burned to a stub, and set them on the long table in the estate office where the ledgers lay open like patients awaiting examination. Ollie watched me from the stairs in his nightshirt until I told him it was keeper's business, and then he went — not to bed, I suspected, but to his birdhouse, guarding the key that had unlocked all of this as though it were an egg.

I shut the office door. I lit three lamps, because one felt like secrecy and three felt like work.

Then I sat down before my father-in-law's true ledger and began to read.

* * *

It was not a beautiful book. That was the first thing that struck me, and it struck me harder than any of what followed. I had imagined something grand — a confession bound in leather, a villain's flourish. What I held was a cheap ruled account book from a stationer in Falmouth, its cover stained with salt, its spine broken at the middle because someone had kept it open flat for years. It was a working book. A tradesman's book. The most terrible thing about it was how ordinary it looked.

The first entry was dated March 1915.

*Rec'd per M. Thackeray, sloop *Providence*, 6 doz brandy, 4 bales tobacco, 1 crate surgical — £14 10s.*

M. Thackeray. My husband's uncle — no. I stopped myself. I had learned by now not to guess. I turned back through the earlier pages, the ones before the war began, when the entries were only brandy and lace and the small honest dishonesties of a Cornish coast, and there he was: Marcus Thackeray, listed first as carrier, then as partner, then — after the winter of '15 — simply *M.T.*, as if the man had been worn down to his initials by the work itself.

Marcus Thackeray had been lost with all hands aboard the *Providence* in November 1916. Everyone in the parish said it was the storm. Everyone in the parish said it with the same careful flatness people use for things they have agreed not to look at directly.

I turned pages. The storm entries were there too — the great gales marked in the margins like feast days — and beside each one, payments.

*Nov. 3rd, 1916. To H., for word re: patrol schedule — £25.*

*To H.* I found him again and again across two years of columns. *To H., for silence re: cargo landed at Porthmear. To H., for warning withdrawn. To H., final settlement, convoy affair, £60.* Sixty pounds. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. Sixty pounds was roughly the price the Admiralty paid a widow for a husband's life at sea. Somewhere a woman had received her sixty pounds and somewhere else a man had received his, and both sums came out of the same night.

Halloway's signature appeared properly only twice, at the front of the book and near the end — not *to H.*, but *A. Halloway, mag.*, written in a confident looping hand, the signature of a man who signed things for a living and expected them to be honored. Between those two signatures ran eighteen months of drowned men.

I made myself read the names. That was the discipline of it; that was the keeper's part. You do not skim the log. You enter every vessel, every light shown or failed to show, every soul aboard. I took a pencil and wrote each name onto a separate sheet as I came to it, because names scattered through a ledger are gossip, but names gathered onto one page are evidence.

There were eleven. Eleven men lost on the *Providence*, and the ledger accounted for five of them explicitly — deserters and wounded soldiers being moved inland who had been aboard when the patrol boat found them, guided, the margin note suggested, by information purchased. The other six had been crew. Smugglers, yes. Men breaking the law, certainly. But the law that killed them had been bought and paid for in this book, sixty pounds at a time.

Ollie's voice came back to me across the kitchen table, that thin new voice, still astonishing as a bird's first song: *Boats come in the dark. Some men go up to the orchard. Some men don't come back.*

He had seen it. A child of seven or eight had stood at the cove and watched the boats go out and counted the ones that returned, and the whole adult world around him had arranged itself so that no one ever asked him to say the numbers aloud.

I typed that sentence into my head first, before I typed anything on paper: *No one asked the boy to count.*

* * *

Sometime past midnight, Prue knocked — two soft raps, the way she does everything, as though the world might be sleeping and deserve consideration anyway.

"I heard the lamps," she said. "Thought you might want tea."

She came in and stood over the ledger without touching it. Forty years in this house had taught her hands to wait.

"You've got to the *Providence*," she said.

"Yes."

"Marcus was a good man doing bad work," she said. "That's not an excuse. I've had thirty years to decide whether it is and it isn't. He wept over every name. He wrote them down himself, you know. The others wanted the book kept clean — numbers only — and Marcus said no. Said if men were going to drown for money, somebody bloody well ought to keep their names." Her jaw worked. "It was the bravest thing anyone in this family ever did, keeping that book, and they hid it in a potting shed."

"They hid everything in a potting shed," I said. "It seems to have been the family archive."

Something crossed her face — grief or laughter, indistinguishable at that hour — and she put the tea down and went, and left me alone again with the dead.

I worked on. The typewriter sat at the far end of the table under its dust sheet, and around two o'clock I uncovered it and rolled in a fresh sheet and began.

This was the part I had been training for all my life without knowing it. Ten years of logs. Every night of weather, every passing light, every vessel entered in Elias's cadence and then, after, in mine. A log is not literature. A log is testimony that has promised to behave itself: date, time, fact, fact, fact, initials. No adjectives unless the sea forces them on you.

So I built the certified copy as a log.

*I, Xanthe Thackeray, keeper, do certify that the following is a true and complete transcription of the account book recovered this date from premises at Thackeray Orchard, Cornwall, hereinafter called "the ledger." Each entry below reproduces the original in wording, figure, and order. Where the original is damaged or doubtful, I have marked it thus: [?].*

Then entry by entry, hour by hour, I fed the ledger through the keys. My fingers knew the work. There is a rhythm to transcription the way there is a rhythm to winding a clock, and I fell into it, and the room filled with the small hard sound of the typebars — the same sound Elias used to make at three in the morning while I slept in the next room and the lamp turned above us both, and I thought, not for the first time and with considerably more understanding than before, that he had grown up in this house knowing what was buried in its cellar and had chosen to spend his life recording weather instead. Recording truth of a kind that couldn't be bought. Perhaps the typewriter hadn't been his hobby at all. Perhaps it had been his escape route, and I had married into it without reading the manifest.

Toward four o'clock Fen appeared in the doorway. I heard him before I saw him — he still moves like a man expecting to be challenged — and I did not look up from the page.

"There's tea," he said. "Prue left it."

"Sit down, Fen."

He sat. He looked at the ledger upside down for a long moment, and I watched him decide whether to ask, and watched him lose the argument with himself.

"The convoy," I said. I kept typing. "November 1916. Your order. Is it in here?"

His breath went out slowly. "I never saw any book. I just — the order came through, and half the names on the escort list were wrong, and I knew they were wrong, because I'd drunk with those men in Plymouth and two of them were supposed to be on leave. Somebody had rebuilt that convoy on paper so it would sail short-handed through water nobody had surveyed." He rubbed his face. "I said I wouldn't take her out. That was the whole of it. Four words and a refusal, and I've been running from the paperwork ever since."

"Your name will be in my copy," I said. "As a witness. Not as a defendant. If you want it out, say so now, because once the pages exist, I won't falsify a record. I can't. It's the one thing I know how to do."

He was quiet for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Leave it in," he said finally. "A record with a hole in it isn't a record. It's just a smaller lie." He stood, paused at the door. "You're like him, you know. The old man. Marcus. Same voice when you talk about paper."

I didn't answer that. But after he'd gone I sat for a moment with my hands off the keys, letting it be true.

* * *

Dawn was a grey suggestion behind the apple trees when I typed the last entry and then the certification beneath it:

*Transcribed in full over the night of [date], being pages one through ninety-six of the said ledger, nothing added, nothing omitted. Signed, Xanthe Thackeray, keeper, Thackeray Orchard.*

And then, because a log requires witnesses, I woke the house.

They came down in the order of their courage, which is to say Prue first, then Ollie wrapped in a blanket and blinking, then Tom — no, not Tom, Tom was on the island still; the thought arrived and passed through me like cold water and I let it go — then Fen, last, standing at the edge of the room as though the doorway were a border he hadn't permission to cross.

I laid the transcript on the table beside the original, side by side, ninety-odd pages squared and numbered.

"This is a true copy of the ledger," I said. "It names eleven drowned men. It names the payments. Twice it carries the signature of Ambrose Halloway, magistrate of this county. I am going to keep the original and this copy in different places, and I am going to make a second copy, and possibly a third, because a thing that exists in one place doesn't exist; it only waits to stop existing." I looked around the table. "I need each of you to sign the last page as witnesses to the transcription. Not to its contents — to the fact that I made it honestly, tonight, and that these are your household and your names are freely given."

Ollie signed first. He printed O-L-L-I-E in enormous careful letters that took up a third of the page, and Prue steadied his elbow, and none of us smiled, because it wasn't funny; it was the smallest signature ever affixed to the largest truth this house contained, and we all understood that.

Prue signed. Then Fen — a fast slanted scrawl, a false-looking name, and I remembered suddenly that I did not actually know whether Fen was his real name, and decided it did not matter, because the ledger did not ask the drowned men for their christening certificates either.

I signed last, and blotted the page, and the record was complete.

Cold clarity — that was the phrase I reached for, sitting there while the others drifted back toward beds and kettles. People imagine clarity feels like sunlight. It doesn't. It feels like the hour before dawn on a clear night when you can see every star and know exactly how far away everything is and exactly how alone. I knew what we had. I knew what it was worth. And I knew, with a keeper's certainty about weather, that somewhere in the county a man with a magistrate's commission had either already heard rumors of a locked shed opened, or soon would, and that men like Ambrose Halloway did not survive by allowing documents to exist.

I separated the copies. The original went back into its oilcloth, back into the tin, back under the floorboard Ollie showed me — no. I reconsidered even as I knelt there. One hiding place had kept it safe for thirty years because no one knew of it. Now four people knew. I took the ledger out again and thought the way I used to think about spare lamps and flares: distributed, redundant, unguessable.

Half past six. The kitchen filled with the smell of Prue's bread. Ollie fell asleep at the table with his cheek on the witness page, and I lifted the book out from under him gently, and stood holding it, weighing it — and it weighed almost nothing. Ninety-six pages of cheap paper. Everything that family had been, everything that eleven men had died carrying, everything that a magistrate would burn a building to stop, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

That was when I saw the smoke.

Not from the chimney — Prue's chimney smoked cheerfully and always had. This was from the east range, low and wrong-colored, a greasy brown against the brightening sky, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the estate office window glass going.

I was moving before I finished understanding, the ledger thrust into Ollie's arms with instructions I prayed he'd remember, shouting the household awake, the cold clarity gone and something hotter in its place.
