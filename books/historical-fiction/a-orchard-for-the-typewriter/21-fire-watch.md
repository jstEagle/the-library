## 21. Fire Watch

Prue Tregose had smelled a hundred fires in her forty years at Thackeray Orchard — apple-wood smoke, leaf-smoke, the sweet char of prunings burned on still autumn evenings. She knew them all by their temper. So when she woke at half past two in the morning with her nose full of something that was none of those, she was out of bed and down the stairs before her mind had caught up with her feet.

The smell came from the north side of the house. The estate office.

She did not knock on doors or call out names. There was no time for ceremony and no breath to waste on it either. She went through the kitchen, hauled open the door to the yard, and stood a moment in the cold with her nightgown whipping around her legs while she took stock of what the dark was telling her.

A glow. Low and orange, behind the office windows. Not yet roaring — not yet past saving, maybe — but eating.

"Fire!" she shouted then, at last, and her voice came out cracked as an old gate. "FIRE!"

* * *

They came fast, that ragged household of hers. That was the thing Prue would remember afterward, when she let herself remember it at all: how quickly they moved, and how quietly. No screaming, no running in circles. Fen came out of the loft over the stable barefoot with a bucket already in each hand, moving like a man who had done this before, though where a deserter learned firefighting Prue never asked. Xanthe appeared in the yard with Elias's coat thrown over her shift, her hair unbound, shouting orders in the flat carrying voice of a lighthouse keeper — which, it turned out, was exactly the right voice for a fire. Tom Wrenn, who'd been sleeping in the tack room since his return from the port town, got the pump going before anyone told him to. Even Ollie was there, small and silent at the edge of the light, holding the harness room's coil of rope as if he'd been waiting all his life for someone to need it.

The office sat apart from the main house — old Mr. Thackeray's doing, so the smell of his pipe wouldn't reach the nursery, though there hadn't been a nursery in thirty years. A low stone building, slate-roofed, its single door facing the yard. By the time they reached it the window glass had gone and flame stood in the frames like men looking out.

"The ledgers," Xanthe said. Just that. Two words, and Prue heard everything underneath them: the shipping books, the pay records, every page they'd spent weeks sorting, every page that wasn't razored or rotted or locked away. Everything that could hang a magistrate or save a boat's crew of drowned men from being forgotten.

"They're gone, missus," Prue said. "You can't go in."

Xanthe didn't answer. For one terrible moment Prue thought she meant to try anyway — saw her take a step toward the door, saw the coat's collar rise with her breathing — and Prue put out her hand and gripped her arm hard enough to bruise through wool.

"You go in there," Prue said, "and I'll have buried three Thackerays off this land instead of two, and I'll not do it. Fen! The roof slates — look at the roof."

She said it because she could see what Fen saw: the fire hadn't reached the far corner yet. It would, soon. But between the door and the corner stood the iron-bound chest where they kept the papers that mattered most — the ones Xanthe had begun calling, in her dry keeper's way, the evidence. Fen looked at the chest. He looked at the flames. Then he wrapped his shirt over his mouth and nose, kicked open the door, and went in low along the wall like a man wading into surf.

What followed took perhaps ninety seconds and lasted, in Prue's memory, most of the rest of the night. Tom worked the pump and fed buckets up the line. Ollie passed them without being asked, small hands steady. Fen came out coughing with the chest dragging from both arms, his sleeves smoking, and dropped it in the wet grass, and Xanthe was on her knees beside it before he was upright, checking the lock with fingers that shook only a little.

Then they turned and fought the building itself, because you fight even what you're losing. They fought it until the pump ran dry and the well gave slow and the roof beams groaned their last argument. By four o'clock the office was a black shell with steam rising off it like breath off a beast, and the six of them stood in a line in the yard, soaked to the bone, saying nothing.

No one said arson. Prue noticed that especially. In forty years she'd learned what silence means in a household, and this silence was not grief and not shock. It was agreement. Everyone had done the same arithmetic — fire, north side, papers inside, magistrate's visit nine days past — and everyone had arrived at the same sum, and no one wanted to be the first to say it aloud, because saying it made it true, and true things demanded answers.

Xanthe broke it, of course. She always broke it.

"The chest," she said. Her voice was scraped raw with smoke. "Open it. Now."

* * *

They carried the chest into the kitchen, because the kitchen had a table big enough and light enough and because none of them wanted to stand near the ruins a moment longer than they must. Fen's hands were bandaged — blisters across both palms and forearms, the price of ninety seconds — but he insisted on working the lock himself, and after some struggle with a poker and Tom's knife it gave.

Inside: the true ledger, dry. The bundle of undelivered letters tied in oilcloth, dry. Xanthe's certified copy, made in her own hand on the typewriter, dry. And the ship's bell, cold and whole, sitting in the bottom like a heart that refused to stop beating.

Xanthe lifted each item out and set it on the table and touched it, once, as if counting. Then she sat down on the bench and put her face in her hands, and for a long moment the only sound was the kettle Tom had put on without asking, and the drip of six people drying in front of a stove.

"We lost the rest," she said finally. "The shipping ledgers. The correspondence files. Forty years of farm accounts." She laughed — one short, ugly note. "Someone spent a good deal of effort burning farm accounts."

"Funny thing about fires," Prue said. She was wringing out her nightgown hem over the sink, working by habit because her hands needed work. "I've seen lightning strike this land twice and burn nothing but bracken. I've seen a candle fall onto straw in '94 and a boy smother it with his boot. Fire takes what it's sent to take." She hung the cloth over the rail. "That one was sent."

Nobody answered. Nobody disagreed. That was the answer.

Ollie tugged at Prue's sleeve. When she bent down he pointed — at the window, at the dark beyond it, then made a shape with his hand: walking. Two fingers, pacing. Someone had walked here tonight. He held up fingers: more than one. Then he pointed back toward the cove road and drew the shape again, slower, going away.

"He's saying they came on foot from the lane," Fen said quietly. "And left the same way."

"How does he know?" Tom asked. "Boy sleeps like the dead."

But Ollie shook his head and pointed to his own eyes, then to the loft window that faced the orchard, and Prue understood. The boy didn't sleep like the dead. The boy slept like a sentry. Whatever years had taught him that, whatever boats and men who never came back had taught him, they had taught him to watch the dark and count what moved in it.

"Three," he said suddenly. His voice, still rare as frost in June, came out hoarse. "I counted three. Big boots."

He held up three fingers and then, after a moment, folded one down. Two big boots each. Six legs, three men, gone before the flames were high enough to see from the lane.

Xanthe wrote it down. Of course she did. She fetched the typewriter from the parlor — the machine had come inland with her and lived now on a table by the window like a member of the family — and rolled in a fresh sheet, and dated it, and typed: *Fire at estate office, commenced approx. 2 a.m., 4 November. Office and contents destroyed except items secured in iron chest. Witness statement, Oliver [surname unknown], age approximately ten: three unidentified men observed leaving via cove road.*

Her fingers paused over the keys.

"No names," Fen said. Not pleading. Stating terms, the way he did. "Not mine. Not yet. If that page leaves this house—"

"It won't leave this house unsigned and unsworn and alone," Xanthe said. "Nothing will. That's rather the point of everything we've been doing, isn't it?" She looked around the kitchen, at each of them in turn, and Prue watched her do it and understood that the woman was not looking for comfort. She was taking inventory. Measuring life in portable things, she'd said once, back on the island, and Prue had thought it a strange creed. Tonight she understood it. The ledger was portable. The copy was portable. Testimony, typed and dated and duplicated, was portable. A burned building was not. Halloway had miscalculated, or his men had, and the knowledge of it settled over the kitchen like warmth off the stove.

Except.

Except fear doesn't settle like warmth. Fear settles like fog.

* * *

They kept watch the rest of the night, though no one proposed it and no schedule was drawn up. Prue took the chair by the kitchen window first, because she was eldest and because she said so. Fen sat against the wall by the door with his bandaged hands in his lap and his eyes open. Tom paced the yard every hour with a lantern, walking the full circuit of the buildings like a man walking a deck, and each time he passed the kitchen window Prue raised her cup to him and each time he raised the lantern back.

Toward dawn, Xanthe came and stood beside Prue's chair. She had Elias's coat on again over dry clothes, and her hair was braided now, and she looked like a woman who had decided something in the hours between four and five.

"You knew him," she said. "My husband's uncle. The brother who went down with the boat."

"Jonah Thackeray." Prue said the name carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "Aye. I knew him."

"What was he like?"

Prue considered the question. Outside, the sky was turning the color of ash and milk, and the burnt office stood black against it, and somewhere down in the cove the sea was making its usual argument with the rocks.

"Reckless," she said at last. "Kind underneath the recklessness, the way a dog is kind under the bark. He laughed too easily for a man in his trade, if you understand me. Men in his trade ought to be quiet, and Jonah was loud with living." She looked at Xanthe. "Elias was the opposite. Quiet as a closed book. I used to think they weren't brothers at all till I saw them together one Christmas, and Jonah laughing and Elias watching him laugh, and the same exact look on both faces. Same man inside, different doors."

Xanthe nodded slowly. "The letters," she said. "In the undelivered post. Elias wrote to his father. Did you know?"

"I suspected. Old Mr. Thackeray burned what came, near as I could tell, and Elias stopped writing, and that was the end of it. Families are mostly things people don't say." She glanced at the table, where the oilcloth bundle lay where Xanthe had left it. "You'll find out what he said soon enough. Letters keep better than people do."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. Then: "Whoever set this fire wanted the ledger. Wanted the letters, maybe. They got farm accounts and a ruined roof." Her jaw tightened. "Which means next time they'll want the shed."

There it was. Said at last, plainly, the word they'd all been circling since two o'clock — not arson, never arson, but *next time*, which was worse, because arson was a crime committed and finished, and next time was a promise.

"The potting shed," Prue said. "Padlocked, keyhole shaped wrong, metal glinting through the boards. You've been sleeping twenty yards from it for two months, missus, and you've never once asked me straight out what's inside."

"I haven't," Xanthe agreed. "Because whoever locked it meant it to stay locked, and because I've learned in my life that doors open in their own order." She turned from the window. The dawn was coming up gray and thin, and the household was stirring — Fen flexing his bandaged hands, Tom coming in from the yard smelling of lantern oil, Ollie standing in the doorway with his father's birdhouse under his arm, the little wooden thing he carried everywhere and let no one touch.

"But I'll tell you what I know," Xanthe went on, and her voice had the flat certainty of a keeper reporting conditions. "Three men came here tonight wearing big boots, sent by someone with money and something to hide. They failed. They will not consider it finished. And the peace is coming — Tom says weeks, maybe days, the talk in the port town is of nothing else — and when it comes, every account that isn't written down gets written by the victors, and the victors have already shown us their pen." She looked at the birdhouse in Ollie's arms, and at the boy, and something in her face shifted — recognition, or decision, or both. "We're going to need whatever's in that shed. All of it. Before they come back for it."

Ollie looked up at her. Then at the birdhouse. Then, slowly, at Prue — and there was a question in the child's face, a permission-seeking, as if the birdhouse had belonged to somebody whose blessing still mattered.

Prue's chest went tight. She knew whose carpentry that was. She'd known the shape of those dovetail joints since before the war took the man who cut them.

"Aye, lad," she said softly. "It's time. Show her."

Ollie hugged the birdhouse to his chest, turned, and walked out of the kitchen toward the potting shed — and the whole household, scorched and soaked and sleepless, rose from the table and followed him into the gray morning.
