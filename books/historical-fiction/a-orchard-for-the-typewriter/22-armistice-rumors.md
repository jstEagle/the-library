## 22. Armistice Rumors

The news came up the lane on a bicycle, which was how most news came, and Tom Wrenn rode it the way he rode everything — too fast, standing on the pedals, his coat flapping behind him like something trying to escape.

He'd been in Falmouth three days. He'd gone down for lamp oil and spare wicks and the ordinary business of keeping a light fed, and he'd come back with his pockets full of gossip instead. That was Tom. Xanthe had learned that about him early: you could send him out for paraffin and he'd return with the price of coal, the state of the roads, and somebody's aunt's opinion on the war, all delivered at speed and none of it billed.

He came through the orchard gate without dismounting, which meant it was important. Tom dismounted for everything — gates, puddles, the sight of Prue's bees working the late rowan blossom. He treated the world as a series of small courtesies. So when he came through the gate still astride the machine, scattering hens, Fen looked up from the wheelbarrow he was mending and Ollie stopped mid-throw with an apple in his hand.

"Peace," Tom said, skidding to a stop by the well. "Maybe. Possibly. Within weeks."

The word sat in the air between them like a dropped plate that hadn't decided whether to break.

"They're talking terms," Tom went on, sliding off the bicycle now, breathless, cheeks burning. "In Paris. The Yanks have their points — fourteen of them, Wilson's got a list like a grocer's order — and the Germans have sent people, actual people, to talk. The paper in Falmouth had it on the front page. *Armistice may be signed before Advent.* That's what it said. Before Advent."

Prue came out of the kitchen doorway wiping her hands on her apron, and even she had stopped moving, which was its own kind of news. Prue did not stop for weather or visitors or the death of kings. She stopped now.

"Well," she said. "About time."

That was all. But her voice had a strange flatness to it, and Tom, who had spent enough months around this household to read its weather, saw that nobody cheered. Ollie let the apple fall from his hand and roll into the grass. Fen went back to the wheelbarrow, but his hands didn't move.

Tom had imagined this moment on the ride home — imagined ringing the bell, imagined Prue crying or laughing, imagined cider, imagined Ollie being swung up onto someone's shoulders. He'd rehearsed joy. What he found instead was a silence with weight to it, and he stood there holding his handlebars like a boy who'd brought the wrong parcel.

"It's good news," he said, uncertain. "Isn't it?"

* * *

It was Xanthe who explained it to him, later, in the estate office where the surviving papers lived now in a sea chest that could be carried out a door in under a minute. She'd moved everything twice since the fire. She would move it again tonight, probably, out of habit, just to keep the practice sharp.

"Sit down, Tom."

He sat on the crate that served as a chair. The office smelled of ash still, faintly, under the apples and ink. Through the window he could see the burned shell of the old records room, its rafters open to the sky like ribs.

"You're thinking of it wrong," Xanthe said. "You're thinking peace ends things. It doesn't end things. It *fixes* them."

"I don't follow."

"When there's a war on," she said, "nobody knows how anything will look afterward. Everything's fluid. A deserter might be pardoned. A smuggler might be called a patriot. A drowned crew might be called lost to weather, or might be called murdered — nobody's decided yet, so the truth still has room to breathe." She tapped the sea chest with one knuckle. "The moment they sign, all of that hardens. Like concrete. Whoever gets their version written first, fastest, with the most official stamps on it — that becomes the version. Forever. Schoolbooks. Memorials. Stone inscriptions. And everyone who remembers differently becomes a crank, or a liar, or a traitor."

Tom thought about that. He thought about the lighthouse logbooks, how a night's weather became fixed fact the moment he wrote it — *wind SW force 9, glass falling* — and no amount of remembering afterward could make it force 7. He'd never thought of history doing the same thing. History had always seemed to him like the sky: enormous, above everything, taking its own time.

"So we're racing," he said slowly.

"We've been racing since the fire." Xanthe pulled a typed page from the chest — one of hers, he recognized the typewriter's slightly crooked *e*, the letter Elias had never gotten around to having repaired. "But now the race has a finish line, and it's moving toward us. Before Advent, the paper says. That's — what — five weeks? Six?"

"Five," Tom said. "Advent Sunday's the second of December."

Of course he knew. Tom Wrenn knew every date on the church calendar and half the tides besides. Xanthe almost smiled.

"Five weeks," she said. "In five weeks, Halloway's friends in London will be able to close the file on the *Providence* and her crew. Lost with all hands, cause of loss: enemy action or stress of weather, take your pick. The informer becomes nothing. The payments become nothing. My uncle-by-marriage becomes a brave man drowned in service, and the magistrate who sold him becomes a pillar of the county who helped win the war." She said it evenly, but Tom saw her hand flatten against the chest lid, pressing down as if the papers might blow away. "And Fen spends the rest of his life a criminal, because after the armistice there'll be no appetite for mercy. Victorious countries don't pardon. They celebrate."

* * *

They told the others at supper, properly this time, though there was little left to tell. Prue already knew — Prue always knew things before the person carrying the news arrived, a gift Tom suspected involved either second sight or the postman. Fen took it standing by the window, looking out at the dark rows of the orchard.

"Five weeks," Fen said.

"Give or take," said Tom.

Fen nodded once. He'd been at the front; he understood deadlines of a different kind. "Then we don't wait for perfect," he said. "We go with what we've got. The ledger copy. The testimonies. My statement, if it comes to that."

"Not your statement yet," Xanthe said. "Not until we have somewhere safe to put it."

"And where's that?"

"The historian," Tom said, and then wished he hadn't, because both of them turned to look at him.

He'd held this back on the ride home, saving it like the last apple in a barrel, and now it came out all at once. "There's a man. In Falmouth. I heard the printer's widow mention him — she said if we were serious, there was one government man worth the risk. An Admiralty historian, but not the kind who writes what he's told. She said he'd been asking questions about coastal shipping losses for two years and getting stonewalled by his own department. She said—" Tom faltered, aware he was delivering hearsay wrapped in rumor wrapped in hope. "She said he was honest. Which she seemed to think was a professional failing."

Silence around the table. Prue snorted. Fen's mouth twitched — the closest he came to laughter these days.

"An honest man in the Admiralty," Prue said. "Next you'll tell me there's a dry stone in Cornwall."

"It's what she said."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment, her thumb moving along the edge of the typewriter page. Tom watched her do the arithmetic he couldn't see — risk against window, trust against time.

"A historian who suspects," she said finally, "is worth ten printers who believe. If the official account is going to harden anyway, better it cracks against a man inside the wall than outside it." She looked at Tom. "Can you find him?"

"I can find where he stays when he's down here. The widow knows. She said—" Tom hesitated again. "She said to tell you the window is shorter than you think. That the department's already drafting the final shipping-loss summary. To be published with the peace terms. As part of the official record."

"*With* the terms," Xanthe repeated softly. "Bound into the same book. So that anyone reading the peace reads the lie alongside it, stamped and sealed, and never knows the two were written by different hands with different purposes."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the wind had come around to the southwest, and the apple trees moved in the dark like a crowd shifting its weight.

* * *

Tom lay awake that night in the loft over the stable, listening to the house settle, and did his own arithmetic.

Five weeks. He counted what they had: one true ledger, copied in type and sworn by four signatures. One ship's bell. A crate of undelivered letters, some of them Elias Thackeray's, which Tom had not read and would not unless asked. One testimony from a mute boy who had spoken exactly once and might never speak in a courtroom. One gardener whose forty years of service made her either the most credible witness in Cornwall or, to a hostile bench, a resentful servant. One deserter whose evidence required confessing a crime that could hang him. And one widow with a printing press and more courage than capital.

Against them: a magistrate, a vicar, a memorial archive with Admiralty money behind it, and the whole gravitational pull of a country that wanted its war tidy.

It should have frightened him more than it did. What frightened him instead was smaller and closer: the thought of the lighthouse. His lighthouse now, in every way that mattered — the lens he'd been keeping trimmed, the log he kept in Xanthe's cadence because he'd learned it from her pages. When the armistice came, the decommissioning order would follow fast. Lights that guarded against enemies become redundant when enemies become customers. He'd seen the memorandum language already in the Falmouth office: *economies.* Somewhere in a Whitehall drawer there was a form with the island's name on it, waiting only for the signature that would end the reason any of them had ever met.

And yet — and this was the thing he turned over and over, the way he turned a new idea on the walk up from the harbor — maybe that was the point. Maybe everything they were doing was a kind of relighting. You couldn't stop the decommissioning. You couldn't stop the concrete setting. But you could get the true account poured into the record before it set, so that fifty years on, someone chipping at the official stone would find underneath it, intact, the other version. Typed. Dated. Signed by a keeper.

Tom fell asleep planning the ride to Falmouth, and the tide tables, and how many copies of the ledger copy they could afford to run.

* * *

In the morning there was frost on the orchard grass, the first of the season, and Ollie stood in it barefoot, breathing clouds, delighted. Prue hauled him inside by the collar and Tom laughed, and for a quarter of an hour the household was just a household, boiling water and arguing about whether frost would sweeten the last apples (it would, said Prue; it wouldn't, said Fen; Ollie settled it by eating one).

Then the postman's bicycle rattled up the lane, and among the circulars and the seed catalogue was a plain envelope with a Falmouth postmark, addressed to *Mrs. E. Thackeray, Keeper,* in a hand Tom didn't know — small, precise, unhurried.

Xanthe opened it at the table, in front of all of them, because those days were past when she opened things alone.

She read it twice. Then she set it down flat on the scrubbed wood and smoothed it with her palm, the way she smoothed a page into the typewriter.

"It's from the historian," she said. "He's coming Thursday. He says—" and here her voice did something Tom had never heard it do, something almost careful, the way a keeper's voice goes careful near a flare — "he says he has read the memorial archive's draft summary of Cornish shipping losses, and he has *objections.*"

She looked up at them, one by one.

"He asks that we have our documents ready. All of them. Because after Thursday, he says, whatever happens, there will be no second hearing."

Outside, the frost was already melting off the orchard rows, and somewhere beyond the headland the sea kept its own count of the lost.
