## 25. The Household Votes

Prue had set this table for forty years, and she knew its moods the way she knew her own hands. There were nights it held a supper and nothing more — bread, dripping, the small talk of people too tired to be anything else. And there were nights it became something older than furniture. A bench of judgment. An altar. A lifeboat.

Tonight it was all three.

She'd cooked properly for it, because you did not ask people to risk their necks over cold mutton. A ham boiled with the last of the orchard sage. Potatoes roasted in goose fat she'd been hoarding since Christmas. Cider from the barrel Fen had tapped that morning, sharp and brown and honest, made from apples nobody had planted on purpose. She carried it all out on the good dishes — the ones with the Thackeray crest chipped at the rim — because if this was to be a family decision, it would be made like a family: fed, and warm, and looking at one another across real crockery.

The kitchen was the only room in the great house that still behaved like a room. The rest of Thackeray Orchard stood with its windows boarded or blown, its corridors smelling of damp and forty years of nobody sweeping. But the kitchen had been Prue's kingdom through every master and every ruin, and it kept its heat the way a dog keeps a grudge. The range ticked. The lamp above the table threw a circle of yellow light no bigger than a cartwheel, and everyone sat inside it, because outside it the dark came right up to the walls and listened.

Xanthe sat at the head, though Prue noticed she hadn't taken Elias's father's chair — the tall-backed one at the far end, the master's seat. She'd pulled up a plain stool instead, and put the ledger in the middle of the table between the salt and the bread board, where everyone could see it.

It lay there like a sleeping animal. Leather gone green with cellar damp, corners gnawed by mice or worse, the brass clasp still bright because brass doesn't care what it records. Inside it were names. Names of drowned men. Names of paid men. And one signature, repeated in a fine confident hand down the margin of years, that belonged to Magistrate Halloway.

Prue had read it twice now, once alone in the potting shed with Ollie holding the lantern, and once aloud to Xanthe while the girl typed. Her eyes weren't what they were, but some things you could read blind. She'd known the shape of that betrayal for thirty years. She'd only ever lacked the paper.

"Before we start," Prue said, ladling cider into the tin cups, "I want it said plainly, so nobody leaves this table thinking they weren't warned."

She looked round them all. Tom Wrenn, the island boy, his face still carrying the yellow-green shadow of the beating Halloway's men had given him at the cove ten days past. He sat propped on two cushions and held his cup in both hands like it might bolt. Fen, who would not sit near the window, who flinched at boots on gravel, whose desertion papers were folded in his breast pocket like a second heart. Ollie, nine years old and silent until the orchard taught him otherwise, perched on his knees on his chair so he could see everything. And Xanthe Thackeray, widow, keeper, typist of truths, waiting the way she waited for weather — without hope of stopping it, only of recording it well.

"Publishing this," Prue said, tapping the ledger once, "is not like publishing a grievance. Grievances fade. This is a magistrate we're naming. A magistrate with friends in the Admiralty, the county, and very likely the churchyard, given how convenient some of these deaths have been. If we print it, he will come for us. Not with soldiers — with papers. Warrants, debts called in, tenancies ended, reputations salted. He'll do it slow and legal and smiling." She paused. "And some of us have more to lose than others. So we don't vote as a mob. We vote as households vote. One at a time, out loud, and nobody shamed either way. That's how I served four generations of Thackerays, and it's how I'll see this one out."

Xanthe nodded. "Prue's right. And before anyone votes — Tom, you've the worst wounds and the least stake. You could be on the morning boat back to your light and none of this need touch you."

Tom set down his cup. The boy had a way of going very still when he meant what he said next; Prue had seen it the day he'd arrived begging to follow her inland, and she saw it now.

"My light's being scrapped," he said. "Requisitioned, decommissioned, whatever the word is. Everything I kept there is in a crate in the scullery. So no, Mrs. Thackeray, I haven't got a stake. I've got nothing but what's in this kitchen." He looked at the ledger. "My dad kept a logbook forty years. Every ship, every storm, every lie the shipping office told about why boats went down. He used to say a record's the only thing a working man owns that can't be repossessed. I'm voting publish. Whatever it costs. It's the first thing I've owned in two years that's worth the price."

The lamp flickered in a draught from under the door. Nobody moved to fix it.

"Ollie," Prue said gently, "you needn't—"

But the boy was already nodding, quick and certain, the way children agree to things adults think are complicated. "They never came back," he said. His voice was still new to him, rusted from disuse, and he spent words like coins he'd found rather than earned. "The boat men. My da said they'd come back when the war stopped. But Mr. Halloway's men took the ledgers away and my da went quiet after, and quiet men die of it." He slid off his chair, crossed to the table, and laid his small hand flat on the leather cover. "Publish it. Say their names out loud so loud he hears them in his sleep."

Prue felt her throat go tight. Forty years in service taught you to swallow most things; she swallowed this one too, mostly.

"Fen," Xanthe said quietly.

Fen had been staring at the ledger the whole time without appearing to look at it — a soldier's trick, watching a thing sideways. Now he straightened, and Prue watched him gather himself the way you'd watch a man check his kit before going over.

"I'll speak," he said, "but I won't vote yet. Is that allowed?"

"It's allowed," Prue said. "This isn't Parliament."

He turned his cup slowly on the table. "You know what I am. What I did. Or didn't do — I refused an order, I ran, and refusing and running are the same word in a court. If that ledger goes to a printer, there'll be questions. Questions travel backwards. They find the man who helped sort the estate office, the man living under a false name in a house sheltering him, and they ask him to account for himself." He looked up, and his eyes were steady and miserable. "If I raise my hand, I'm not just risking arrest. I'm handing them the rope and showing them the knot. And I wouldn't only hang for myself. Harboring. Aiding. There's a chain of charges and every link of it runs through this kitchen."

"Then don't," Tom said simply. "Nobody here will think less."

"That's the trouble," Fen said. "I'd think less. Every day, for the rest of my life, which might be short anyway." He pushed back from the table and stood, and for a moment Prue thought he was going to walk out of the room and out of the story altogether. Instead he walked to the window, stood in the black glass where his reflection should have been, and said to the night: "Give me till the end. Let me hear everyone. Then I'll say."

Prue nodded. "Fair enough. Which brings us to me, since I called the meeting and I'll not hide behind the calling of it."

She wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though they were clean, and stood — she always spoke standing; sitting was for listening, and tonight she intended to be heard.

"I came to this house when Queen Victoria still had breath in her," she said. "I've buried two masters and outlived a third's reputation. I pruned these trees when the Thackerays were honest and I pruned them when they were something else, and I'll tell you the difference: the honest ones asked who the apples were for. The other kind only asked how many." She laid her palm beside Ollie's small one, on the ledger. "I knew about the cellars. I carried the blankets down and the broth down and I held the hands of boys younger than Fen while they shook, and I never once told a soul, because telling would have killed them. And then someone else told, and a boat went out one night in November and never came home, and one of the names on that list is a Thackeray who died doing what this household does." Her voice cracked and she let it, deliberately; there was no shame in it after thirty years. "So yes. Publish. Print it on the front page and nail it to the church door if the printer's short of paper. I'm an old woman with bad lungs and no pension worth stealing. Let him come for me. I've been expecting him since 1915."

She sat down heavily. The range ticked. Somewhere out in the ruined orchard, wind moved through the wild trees with a sound like the sea deciding.

Xanthe drew a sheet of paper toward her — not typing paper, just a page torn from her notebook — and wrote as each vote landed, dating it at the top the way she dated everything now. *Wrenn, Thomas. Publish. Ollie —* she glanced at the boy — "*surname pending*, publish. Prudence Garrett, publish." Her pen hovered. "That leaves Fen and me."

"You first," Prue said. "You're the heir. It's your name on the danger as much as his on the crime."

Xanthe set down the pen. When she spoke, it was in the voice Prue had learned to listen for — the level, unhurried voice of a woman who had kept a lamp burning through gales that killed stronger people, the voice that treated catastrophe as a line item.

"Elias chose the light over this house," she said. "I found his letters to his father last week. He knew. He knew what the family money was built on, and he walked away from it and spent his life counting ships so that somebody, somewhere, would know what actually happened on this coast. I inherited a name I never wanted and an estate I never asked for. But I also inherited his typewriter, and his habit, and his stubbornness, God help me." She touched the ledger's clasp. "Halloway offered me a pension and a clean name. Clean of what? The dirt isn't ours. Publishing doesn't make us safe. It makes us *true*. I've lived without true for a long time and I'm done with it. Publish. Whatever it costs — and I believe Prue when she says it will cost — I'll pay it, and I'll type the receipt."

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Ollie laughed — a single bark of a laugh, startled out of him — and even Tom smiled through his bruises.

All eyes went to the window, where Fen still stood with his back to the room.

Prue watched him the way she watched weather coming up the valley. She had fed this man for five months. She had mended his shirt collars and lied to the vicar about him and heard him cry out in his sleep in a voice that belonged to a nineteen-year-old on a beach in France. She knew what his silence cost him hourly. And she knew — the way you know a graft has taken, by the swell beneath the binding — that he had already decided, and was only finding the door out of the room he'd been locked in.

"The convoy," he said at last, still facing the glass. "The one the informer sold. I was ordered onto its escort. I'd seen the signals traffic — I wasn't supposed to understand it, but I did. I knew we were sailing into a trap and I said so, and I was ordered to say nothing, and the order came from—" he stopped. Started again, quieter. "The chain went high. Higher than Halloway, maybe. Or Halloway *is* the top of it and the rest is decoration. Either way, I refused. Not heroically. I was sick with it. I just — didn't go. And eleven hundred men sailed without their escort's warning, and I've counted them every night since like sheep I failed to shut in."

He turned around. In the lamplight his face was grey and resolved, a man presenting himself for inspection.

"If I abstain, I keep my skin and lose my soul, and I've done the arithmetic on that trade for two years and it comes up short. So." He crossed the room, pulled out his chair, and sat down among them. He did not raise his hand immediately. He looked at each of them in turn — Tom, Ollie, Prue, Xanthe — the way a man looks at witnesses.

Then he raised his hand, palm open, fingers spread, and held it there as though the air itself required signing.

"Publish," he said. "Under my own name. Not the false one. If we're going to tell the truth, I'll stand in it. Fenwick Marsh, private, 2nd Battalion, deserted October 1916, and everything I witnessed, sworn and typed and printed." His hand came down. He exhaled like a man surfacing. "God help us, but there it is."

Prue found she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"Then it's unanimous," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Six for publish, none against, one name restored to the roll." She looked round the lamplit circle — the keeper, the boy, the deserter, the beaten child of the light, and herself, last servant of a dishonest house trying to make it honest in the only currency left. "Well then. A household's decided. Now comes the harder part."

"What's harder than this?" Tom asked.

"Finding someone fool enough or brave enough to print it," Prue said. "Every press in Cornwall takes Halloway's advertising or Halloway's warnings, usually both. You can't set this type in Truro or Falmouth or Penzance without word reaching him before the ink's dry. And we've perhaps weeks — Tom says the peace terms may be signed inside a month, and once the armistice is signed, official history sets like concrete. Whoever controls the story in November keeps it forever."

Xanthe was already writing. Prue watched her date the page, enter the six votes in her neat keeper's hand, and close the entry with the time — *9:40 p.m., wind SW, moderate* — because that was who she was; she recorded the weather of everything.

"There was a printer in the port town," Xanthe said slowly, thinking aloud. "When I came through in September. A woman — widowed, they said, husband lost at sea or lost to drink, depending who tells it. Runs a small press behind her shop, prints pamphlets the respectable shops won't stock. The vicar called her 'that Quaker creature' in a tone that suggested she might be exactly what we need." She looked up. "It's a day's journey. Two, with the roads watched."

"Roads are watched," Fen agreed quietly. "Which means it can't be me. My face is on a notice in three towns."

"Then it's you and me," Xanthe said. "Keeper and typewriter. We've traveled lighter."

Prue stood and began gathering the plates, because decisions made, in her experience, lasted exactly as long as the washing-up took, and after that you needed work or you needed fear, and she preferred work. But at the doorway she stopped, and turned, and looked at them all still sitting in the lamplight around the ledger — this strange crew the orchard had collected the way an orchard collects birds.

"One thing more," she said. "Whoever goes, and whatever happens — this table stands. Anybody walks through that door needing it, there's a place and a plate. That's the vote underneath the vote. Mind you remember it."

Outside, the wind shifted and came round to the east, cold and carrying the smell of the sea, and somewhere down the dark lanes toward the port town, a bell rang the hour — ten strokes, unhurried, indifferent to what had just been decided in a kitchen above the cellars.

Xanthe closed the ledger, squared it on the table, and said, "We leave at first light."

* * *

Prue woke before dawn, as she had for forty years, and found the kitchen already lit.

Xanthe sat at the head of the table with the typewriter case open, feeding in a fresh sheet. Fen's false papers lay on the wood beside a new document — a letter of introduction, typed overnight, giving *Mrs. X. Thackeray, executrix of the Thackeray estate,* full authority to carry estate records to any lawful printer she chose. It was a thin shield. Prue had seen thinner ones stop bullets.

Beside the letter, squared and waiting, lay the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with string.

"You're taking the original?" Prue whispered.

"A copy stays hidden here," Xanthe said, not looking up from the keys. "In the birdhouse keyhole's hiding place, wrapped in wax. But the original travels with me. Halloway can burn an empty office. He can't burn what's on the road." She finished the line, rolled the platen, and tore the page free. On it, in her level keeper's hand, was written:

*25th October, 1918. Voted unanimous, six souls present. Departing for the port town at first light to find a press. Wind E, cold. Carrying the truth and hoping it weighs less than it feels.*

She signed it, dated it, and tucked it into the ledger's cover like a ship's log signed before a voyage.

Prue put a flask of cider in the satchel, bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth, and — after a moment's thought — a handful of dried apple from last year's harvest, because you did not send people into Halloway's country without something of the orchard in their pockets.

At the door, in the blue-grey dark, Xanthe paused and looked back at the kitchen, at the household still asleep upstairs and in the loft — Tom's bandaged ribs rising and falling, Ollie curled with the cat, Fen already awake somewhere, listening.

"We'll find the printer," she said.

Prue nodded. "And mind," she said, "whoever she turns out to be — feed her before you talk business. People sign braver on a full stomach."

Xanthe almost smiled. Then the door closed, and the footsteps went down the gravel path, and Prue stood at the window watching two figures shrink into the lane — one carrying a typewriter case, one carrying a nation's worth of dead men's names — until the mist took them both.
