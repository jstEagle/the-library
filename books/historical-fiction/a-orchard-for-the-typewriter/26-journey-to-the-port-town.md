## 26. Journey to the Port Town

Fen had learned in France that a road was a promise made to be broken. The map said six miles to the port town; the rain said otherwise; and somewhere between the third milestone and the fourth, Xanthe Thackeray stopped walking, set down the typewriter case on a stone wall, and looked at him as if he were about to confess something.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm walking," Fen said. "There's a difference."

"There is. One of them favours the left leg."

He shifted his weight and did not answer. The leg had been bad since Ypres — not the wound itself but the cold that lived in it now, a tenant that paid no rent and could not be evicted. He'd hidden it for two years from sergeants who would have shipped a lame man back to the front as fast as they'd ship a deserter to the firing post. Old habits. He started walking again, and after a moment she fell into step beside him, and neither of them mentioned it again, which was its own kind of mercy.

The typewriter case rode in his arms. She had insisted on carrying it herself for the first mile, then surrendered it without ceremony when the lane steepened, saying only, "You're stronger," which was true, and "It's heavier than it looks," which was truer than she knew. It was not paper weight he felt. It was every page of the true ledger copied out in her own hand, dated like log entries, each name of each drowned man typed twice over so there would be two records and Halloway's men would have to burn two fires. Fen carried them the way he had once carried ammunition boxes up communication trenches at night: carefully, and with the constant expectation of a flare going up.

"Tell me about the printer," she said.

"I told you what I know."

"Tell me again. Walking keeps my hands busy; talking keeps my head quiet."

So he told her again. The widow's name was Mrs. Tregose — Verity Tregose, though nobody used the Christian name except the tax man. Her husband had run a small press off Fore Street, printing bills of lading, hymn sheets, auction notices, the ordinary sediment of a port town's paperwork. He'd been lost on the *Providence Looe*, a coaster out of Fowey, in the spring of '16, one of the U-boat's quieter mornings. She'd kept the press running herself. She printed what kept bread on the table, and once or twice a year she printed something else — Fen knew because the men in his old battalion talked, the way soldiers talk, in half sentences and knowing looks. A pamphlet about the munitions strike at Newport. A handbill for the peace men in London. Nothing with her name large on it. Nothing you could prove.

"She'll want money," Fen said.

"We have money." Xanthe patted the pocket of Elias's coat, which she wore despite the rain, the collar turned up against her neck. "Not much. Enough."

"And she'll want a reason. Money's never the whole reason with people like that. People who've already lost what matters take risks for reasons, not for coin."

Xanthe considered this. Rain dripped off the brim of her hat. Ahead of them the lane bent downhill toward the sea, and beyond the hedge gaps Fen could see the harbour water lying grey and patient under the weather, masts rocking, gulls hanging motionless in the wind like things nailed to the sky.

"My husband chose the light," she said at last. "Over the family, over the money, over all of it. I used to think it was grief that sent him there. Now I think it was shame he couldn't carry, so he carried a lamp instead. I won't choose shame. Whatever it costs."

"That's your reason, then."

"That's my reason."

They walked on. Fen found himself thinking — not for the first time since the harvest, since the vote around the kitchen table where Prue had counted hands like a woman counting hens into a coop — how strange it was to be trusted. He had deserted in March of '17. He had walked away from a column outside Arras with his kit on his shoulder and an order in his ear he could not make his legs obey, and everything since had been concealment: a borrowed name here, a hired scythe there, the long careful business of being nobody in a country full of nobodies. Trust was a currency he hadn't held in years. Now a woman he'd known since autumn handed him a nation's worth of dead men's names to carry down a public road, and a boy who didn't speak had waved him off from the gate like a brother, and an old gardener had pressed a parcel of pasties into his hands and told him to feed the printer before talking business.

If Halloway's men took him on this road, it would go badly. Not just for him. For everyone whose names were in the case against his ribs. He knew this, and walked anyway, and could not have explained to a court why that was courage rather than folly. Perhaps it wasn't. Perhaps it was only that he had spent three years learning what happened when good men let bad paperwork stand.

* * *

The port town announced itself by smell before sight — tar and fish and coal smoke braided together on the wind. Then the roofs came up over the hillcrest, slate-grey and jumbled, and the church tower, and the crane arms of the quay sticking up like gallows in miniature. Market day. Carts queued at the town gate. Soldiers on leave lounged outside the King's Arms in their hospital blue, cigarettes going, voices too loud the way convalescent men's voices always were.

"Hold the case close," Xanthe murmured. "And if anyone stops us, we're taking the machine to be repaired. Broken type-bar. You're the carrier; I'm the owner. Keep your mouth Cornish if you can manage it."

"My mouth's been Cornish longer than it's been anything else."

"Good." And then, glancing at him sidelong: "Your hands are shaking."

"They do that."

"Noted," she said, and put her hand briefly on his sleeve — two seconds, no more — and then walked forward into the town like a woman entering her own kitchen.

They tried four printers.

The first was a stationer off Chapel Street with a treadle press in the back room, a soft man with ink-stained cuffs who listened to exactly none of Xanthe's prepared speech before holding up one hand like a policeman stopping traffic. "Whatever it is, Mrs. — forgive me, madam, whatever it is, the answer's no. I've a wife and three girls and a brother-in-law on the bench, and I print hymns and invoices and nothing else, and I'd thank you not to say another word, because words get remembered these days." He showed them out with the courtesy of a man showing out a contagious disease.

The second refused through a hatch, without opening the shop door at all.

The third was different, and worse. A lean man in a good coat, printing works near the quay, brass plate polished, an apprentice sweeping the step. He heard Xanthe out — all of it, attentively, nodding, asking one intelligent question about the number of copies — and then he said, gently, "Madam, do you know who holds the paper on this building?" and when she said she did not, he said, "Neither do I, entirely, but I know whose signature is on the mortgage, and it is not mine, and I have children." He did not report them. Fen watched his eyes and believed that. But he wrote something on a scrap of paper while they stood there — Fen saw the movement, couldn't see the words — and Fen got them out of that office fast, and did not relax until they'd crossed two streets and turned twice.

"He'll send word," Fen said quietly.

"To whom?"

"To whoever he's afraid of. There's usually only one somebody in a county."

Xanthe's jaw tightened. They bought bread and cheese at a stall and ate standing in the shelter of a fish-curing shed, watching the rain come in off the harbour in long slanting curtains. Fen divided Prue's pasties and gave her the bigger half without comment and she accepted it without comment, and both transactions went unremarked, the way courtesies go in households — because that was what they were now, he realized. Not employer and labourer. A household, split temporarily in two, the other half forty miles away keeping watch over Tom Wrenn's bandaged ribs.

"One more address," Fen said. "And then we go home and think again."

* * *

Verity Tregose's press was up a yard off Fore Street, behind a chandler's, above a boat-builder's loft, reached by stairs that smelled of sawdust and pitch. There was no brass plate. There was a card in the window, hand-lettered: PRINTING DONE WHILE YOU WAIT. BILLS. FORMS. NOTICES.

Fen knocked. After a long interval, footsteps, and the door opened on a chain — then closed, the chain rattled off, opened wide.

She was perhaps fifty, tall and raw-boned, hair going iron-grey at the temples, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms black to the wrist with old ink that soap had given up on years ago. Behind her, filling the room, stood the press — a flat-bed machine older than the war, iron framed, glass ink plates gleaming — along with racks of type in wooden cases, a paper cutter, reams wrapped in brown paper stacked to the ceiling, and a smell of oil and metal that hit Fen with such force of memory (his father had been a compositor, before the army took first him and then the trade) that for a moment he couldn't speak.

"You'll be wanting printing," the woman said. Her eyes went from Fen to the typewriter case to Xanthe's face and stayed there, reading it the way she probably read galley proofs — quickly, missing nothing. "Come in before the street reads you instead."

Inside, she bolted the door, then did a thing Fen had seen sentries do: lifted the sash an inch and glanced down into the yard, checking sightlines, before closing it again.

"Now," she said. "Who sent you?"

"A dead man's sister-in-law," Xanthe said, "sent me to myself, and I chose you off a list of one. Nobody living sent us, Mrs. Tregose. That's rather the point."

Something flickered at the corner of the widow's mouth. Not a smile. The place where smiles were kept.

Prue's instruction sat in Fen's mind — feed her before you talk business — and so he said, "We've brought food, ma'am. Pasties. Still warm-ish. Prue Kellow's pastry."

That landed. Verity Tregose's eyebrows went up a quarter-inch. "Kellow. Of Thackeray Orchard?"

"You know her?"

"I know her pasties. Forty years ago my Tom courted a girl up that valley and we stopped at the estate gate once and a gardener's girl fed us apples till we nearly burst. Small world, wartime." She fetched a kettle, three cups, a bottle of condensed milk, and they ate at a workbench cleared of type with one sweep of her forearm, and it was true what Prue said: people signed braver on a full stomach. Fen felt his own shoulders come down a half inch somewhere in the middle of the second pasty.

Then Xanthe set down her cup, unlatched the typewriter case, and began.

She told it plainly, without ornament — Fen noticed that, admired it. The letter sealed twice. The orchard gone wild. The potting shed, the crates of undelivered letters, the bell, the true ledger. The razored pages. Ollie's boats at night. The lost boat with all hands, and the name of the magistrate whose signature ran down the payment columns like a stain. The fire. Halloway's offer. The warrant that hadn't come yet but was coming. She spoke for perhaps twenty minutes and Verity Tregose did not interrupt once, and when it was done the widow sat very still with her ink-blackened hands folded, looking at the stack of typed pages Xanthe laid on the bench between them like a woman looking at something that might be a bomb or a Bible and knowing it was both.

"The Admiralty will call it forgery," Verity said at last.

"They may try."

"They will succeed, unless it can't be forged. Which means sworn statements, dated, witnessed, in more than one pair of hands, and copies in more than one place before anyone knocks on any doors." She was already turning pages as she spoke, quick and deft, a professional triage. "How many copies exist?"

"Two complete. This one, and one travelling south with a friend tomorrow on a fishing boat."

"One's not enough. Two's barely enough. I'll want a third set within the week." She looked up. "And you understand what happens to me if I set this. Not might happen. Happens. My press seized, my livelihood gone, and possibly worse — the Defence of the Realm Act has teeth and Halloway knows where they all are."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "I understand it exactly. I wouldn't ask it blind."

"No," Verity said. "You'd ask it having eaten my pasties and drunk my tea, which is worse, and cleverer." She stood, walked to the window, looked down into the yard for a while with her back to them. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, the boat-builder's adze knocked slow and even against a hull, patient as a heart.

Fen understood the silence. He'd stood in that exact posture himself, in a barn outside Arras, the night before he walked away — facing a wall, deciding which version of himself he could live inside for the rest of his life. He said nothing. Neither did Xanthe. They let the woman have her wall.

When she turned around, her face had settled into something decided.

"My Tom went down on the *Providence Looe*," she said. "Torpedoed in sight of land, they tell me. Eleven men. The Admiralty sent me a printed form of condolence — mass-produced, you understand, the same form they sent eleven widows — and then, when the questions came, why an unarmed coaster was routed through a known submarine line, why the escort was withdrawn, why the cargo manifest changed twice — the questions stopped being answered, and the men who asked them stopped being posted anywhere useful." She came back to the bench and laid one dark hand flat on the ledger copy. "For two years I have printed other women's auction notices and bitten my tongue to the root. So. Here is my price, and it isn't money."

"Name it," Xanthe said.

"You'll give me the full account — every page, every testimony, the lot — and I'll set it properly. Quarto pamphlet, five hundred copies to start, sold at tuppence so it travels. No name of mine on it, no address; a box number at the railway station will do. And when it's done—" her hand pressed flatter on the paper "—you'll put in the *Providence Looe*. Eleven names. I'll give them to you tonight. They go in the record, same as your drowned men. If the thing's worth doing, it's worth doing for everybody's dead."

Xanthe didn't hesitate. Fen watched her, and thought: there it is, the keeper's instinct, the whole coast lit at once. "Agreed," she said. "Every name gets its entry. That's the whole purpose."

"Then we begin tomorrow at first light. Type's short on certain letters; I'll cannibalize the jobbing case." Already she was moving, pulling down the brown-papered reams, testing the press wheel with one hand, alive in a way she had not been ten minutes ago. Then she paused, and looked at Fen directly for the first time since the door.

"You," she said. "You're the deserter."

The word went through him like cold water. Xanthe stiffened. But the widow's face held no menace — only the flat assessment of someone counting risk.

"How—" Fen started.

"Your boots. Army issue, resoled civilian. Your haircut's growing out regulation. And you flinched when she read out the warrant section — not at the warrant. At *harbouring*. You're the harbourage." She shrugged. "I've printed enough casualty lists to know a soldier's shape. Don't worry — this house doesn't keep files. But hear me: if they catch you before this prints, the whole record dies in a police cellar. So while you're in my town, you sleep upstairs, you go out after dark, and you don't stand at windows."

"Yes, ma'am," Fen said, and meant it, and was surprised to find that some knot in his chest — three years tight — had loosened slightly at being *seen* and not destroyed. Allies are scarce, he thought. Precious scarcer.

They worked out the schedule by lamplight: proofs in three days, the run in six, distribution through the market women and the chapel networks and one railway guard Verity trusted. Xanthe copied the *Providence Looe*'s eleven names onto fresh pages in her typewriter cadence, dated like log entries, and Verity read them aloud once, slowly, each name given its full weight, and set the last one down with both hands as if laying a wreath.

It was past ten o'clock when Fen climbed the narrow stair to the loft bed she offered him. Through the floorboards he could hear the two women still talking low — schedules, contingencies, the grammar of conspiracy — and beneath their voices the tick of cooling iron, the rain easing off the roof slates.

He lay down in the dark and did his soldier's accounting. Three printers refused; one had likely sold them already. One widow had said yes, and yes was everything, but yes was also a target now, painted on a yard off Fore Street. In six days there would be five hundred copies of the truth loose in Cornwall, and in six days Halloway would know precisely where they had been born.

Sleep came slowly. When it came, he dreamed of type cases, thousands of little lead letters in their compartments, and in the dream he was setting them one by one, and every letter was a name, and the press rolled on and on, printing the dead back into the world.

He woke before dawn to knocking at the street door below — three blows, hard, official — and to Verity Tregose's bare feet crossing the floorboards, steady as typefall, toward it.
