## 27. Chapter 27

The widow's press stood in a room behind her shop that smelled of ink, lamp oil, and apples — the last from baskets of windfalls she kept through winter for cider, she said, because a body needed something sour to trust.

Her name was Verity Codge, and she had set type for forty years, first for her father, then for her husband, and now for no one, since the respectable men of the port town had decided one after another that their business was better placed elsewhere. She looked at the pages Xanthe laid on her composing stone the way a horse-trader looks at a leg.

"Sworn statements," she said. "A true ledger copied out in a lighthouse keeper's hand. Names of drowned men. The signature of a county magistrate receiving payment for the loss of a ship." She turned a page with one ink-stained finger. "You understand what you're asking me to do."

"I'm asking you to print it," Xanthe said. "I've asked six other printers to do the same. You'd be the seventh refusal, and I'd go home and set it myself on Elias's typewriter, one carbon at a time, until my fingers wore out. It would be slower. It would be uglier. But it would exist."

Verity looked up at her then — really looked, the way people did when they were deciding whether you were mad or merely desperate — and something in her face shifted.

"Your husband was a keeper," she said. Not a question.

"On the island. He died before the war got its teeth in. I keep his light and his typewriter, though not in that order of devotion."

"My Tom went down off the Lizard in March of 'sixteen," Verity said. "Convoy work. The Admiralty sent me a letter saying the weather was to blame." She tapped the ledger copy. "And this says otherwise."

"This says who was paid to look away," Xanthe said carefully. "It doesn't say the sea was innocent. The sea is never innocent. But the sea didn't sign anything."

* * *

They worked three days.

Fen slept in the back room among the paper stock and came out only when the shop bell rang, which it did often enough that Xanthe began to understand how alone Verity had been. Customers came for bootlaces and candle-wicks and stayed to stare at the woman from the island and the tall nervous man who would not meet anyone's eyes. Once a man in a good coat asked Verity, pleasantly, whether trade was brisk.

"Brisk as ever," said Verity, whose face could have been set in lead type itself.

After he left, she locked the door, came into the back room, and said: "That was Halloway's clerk. He buys nothing. He comes to see who else buys nothing."

"Then we're nearly done," Xanthe said. "There isn't much left to see."

"There's everything left to see," Verity said. "He's counting your copies. When the count stops changing, they'll come for them."

So they changed the count. Verity printed two hundred and told the clerk's arithmetic there were fifty. Xanthe typed four additional fair copies of the whole document on a borrowed machine Verity kept for invoices, and Fen carried them out of the town one at a time over two days, sewn into the lining of an old coat, posted from four different villages to four different addresses: the historian at the Admiralty archive, a barrister in London whose name Prue dredged up from forty years of estate correspondence, the Cornish newspaper in Truro, and — this last on Xanthe's own insistence, sealed and addressed in her steadiest hand — to herself, poste restante, at the port town office, to be collected only when it was safe or never at all.

"A record wants redundancy," she told Fen. "That's the first law of any log. A keeper writes the weather twice: once in the book, once in the glass of the lamp she polishes while she thinks about what she wrote. If one fails, the other remembers."

Fen had looked at her for a long moment. "You talk like a woman who has already survived the worst thing," he said. "It makes the rest of us feel like we might too."

"You will," she said. "That's not comfort. That's arithmetic. There are five copies now, and only one of us needs to remain standing for the count to hold."

* * *

The proof sheet came off the press on the third evening, still warm, smelling of solvent, and Xanthe held it under the lamp and read her own testimony as if it belonged to a stranger.

STATEMENT OF XANTHE THACKERAY, KEEPER OF THE [island] LIGHT, taken at Thackeray Orchard, October 1918, and certified true by the hand of the undersigned.

Below it, the statements of Prudence Tregowan, forty years' service to the Thackeray family. Of Ollie Parnick, aged eleven years, given in his own words and written down exactly as he spoke them, because she had promised him she would change nothing. Of a man listed only as F., late Royal Navy, whose name she had offered to print and who had refused — not for his own sake, she suspected, but because a name made him real, and a real deserter could be shot, whereas an initial was only a ghost, and ghosts were harder to hang.

At the bottom of the page, where the imprint ought to have named a printer, Verity had set a single line:

PRINTED BY THE WIDOW CODGE, WHOSE TOM THE SEA DID NOT DROWN ALONE.

Xanthe read it twice. "They'll arrest you for this."

"They'll try," Verity said. "I buried a husband and both my parents in six years, Miss Thackeray. Arrest me for telling the truth about how Tom died and you'll find out what a woman with nothing left to lose sets in type next." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Besides, I'm seventy-one. What are they going to do, transport me?"

* * *

They took the morning coach back inland, Fen carrying the first bundle of printed copies in a seed sack, as if contraband apples were the most incriminating cargo a man could hold in orchard country.

The landscape had turned since they'd come down: hedges gone copper, fields stripped, woodsmoke standing straight up in the cold air like columns holding up nothing. Xanthe watched it through the window and thought about the difference between a log and a history. A log was written by whoever was awake at the time. A history was written afterward, by whoever was still standing, and it always sounded more certain than the night it described. She had spent four years writing logs. She was beginning to understand she'd spent her whole life inside someone else's history — the Thackeray history, the Admiralty history, the tidy version the vicar told with his hands folded — and that the only way out was to write the waking-down account, in the storm, with wet hands, while it was still happening.

"You're doing the face again," Fen said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're typing in your head. Your lips move."

She caught herself smiling and let it stay. "Two hundred copies," she said. "Cheap enough for a farmhand to buy with a week's tobacco money. That was Verity's condition. Not a shilling above cost. She said the truth shouldn't be a luxury good."

"And Halloway?"

"Halloway will buy every copy he can find and burn them, and it won't matter, because burning a printed thing proves it existed. That's the whole trick of print, Fen. My husband's typewriter can be denied — *a woman's fancy, a grudge, a forgery*. Ink on two hundred identical pages cannot. Every copy burned is a witness to the fire."

Fen turned his collar up against the draft rattling the window frame. For a while he said nothing, and then, in the voice of a man testing ice: "When they come — and they'll come — don't stand between me and them. Whatever we agreed at the kitchen table, that part's void. I'm the one with the crime attached. You're the one with the paper."

"The paper is the crime now," Xanthe said. "That's what nobody's understood yet. Halloway isn't afraid of you; deserters are a sad story, and sad stories get pardons after a war ends. He's afraid of the ledger, and the ledger is mine." She looked out at the passing hedges. "We stand together or the whole design fails. That's not sentiment. That's engineering."

* * *

Prue met them at the gate, and Xanthe knew before they reached her that something had happened, because Prue was walking fast, and Prue never walked fast unless the ground was on fire or the kettle was boiling over, and there was no smoke.

"The boy found something," Prue said, before hello. "In the birdhouse. No — not the key, that's done, that's old news. After. He went back with the ladder yesterday, poking about, the way he does, and there's a second compartment behind the false back, and in it—" She stopped, breathless, and pressed a small oilcloth packet into Xanthe's hands. "I haven't opened it. It's addressed."

The oilcloth was tied with tarred twine, sailor's knots, and the canvas beneath was stiff with age. On it, in faded iron-gall ink, in a hand Xanthe had last seen on shipping manifests and estate letters and once, unbearably, on a marriage register:

*For my grandson's wife, when there is one. If there is never one, burn it.*

Elias's father's hand. The old man's. The magistrate's friend. The brother of the drowned.

"He wrote it before he died?" Fen asked. "Years ago?"

"Before the war," Prue said. "Maybe before the boat was even lost. Maybe after. Ollie says the compartment's built to be found from underneath, through the birdhouse floor — a panel that drops when the house is lifted off its post. His father made the birdhouse. His father, the gamekeeper, the one who—"

"Who rowed the boats," Xanthe said quietly. She remembered Ollie's few words, hoarded like coins: *boats at night, men who never came back.* "The birdhouse was a dead-drop. Letters in, letters out. And the last letter in was never collected."

They stood in the yard in the cold, the packet between them, and none of them moved to open it.

"Burn it," Fen said finally. "That's what it says. Whoever wrote it gave permission."

"He gave permission to his own heir," Xanthe said. "Not to me. I inherited the orchard, but I wasn't the heir he meant, and I won't spend another man's permission like it was mine." She weighed the packet in her palm. It was heavier than letters should be. Something flat and hard lay within the folded papers. "But I'll open it. Tonight. By lamplight, in the estate office, with everyone in the room, and whatever's in it goes into the record tomorrow, sworn and dated, before anyone can say we sat on it. That's the rule we set ourselves. Everything into the light. Even when the light shows us things we'd rather it didn't."

Prue's jaw worked. "Even if it's a confession?"

"Especially then." Xanthe started toward the house, then stopped. "Prue. Who else knew about the birdhouse?"

"No one living, I'd have said. The gamekeeper built it. The old master used it. And—" Prue hesitated, and Xanthe watched forty years of loyalty collide with forty years of caution on her face. "And the vicar knew. He was young then, curate here. He ran messages for the cellars himself, God help us, before he got respectable. If anyone alive besides us knew there might be a last letter waiting — it would be him."

The wind moved through the bare orchard rows, and somewhere down the lane a cart creaked, ordinary as bread.

"Then we open it tonight," Xanthe said, "and tomorrow, first coach, I ride to the port town and put it in Verity's hands. Because if the vicar learns what we've found before it's printed, we won't have a record anymore. We'll have a rumor. And rumors are what men like Halloway keep for breakfast."

Inside, the household gathered without being summoned — Prue lighting lamps, Fen barring the door out of habit, Tom (arrived that same afternoon, mud to the knees, breathless, saying there was news, important news, a historian, an Admiralty man, already asking questions in the village) — and Xanthe cut the tarred twine with Elias's pocketknife and unfolded the oilcloth on the scrubbed kitchen table while five people held their breath around her.

The topmost paper was a letter. Beneath it, wrapped in flannel gone grey with age, lay a ship's bell the size of two fists, green with verdigris, and stamped around its crown in letters worn nearly smooth but not quite:

H.M.S. — and then the name of the boat that had gone down with all hands.

Tom forgot his news entirely. Fen sat down slowly, as if his legs had been borrowed. Prue crossed herself with a floury hand.

And Xanthe, keeper, widower's widow, heir by accident, picked up the bell in both hands and felt its silence the way she felt the dark between storms — full, and waiting, and about to speak.

"Read the letter," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Read it aloud. All of it. Whatever it says."

Outside, far off toward the coast, a church bell began to ring the hour — and stopped halfway, as if the ringer had seen something in the road below the tower that made him let the rope fall.
