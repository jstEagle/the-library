## 28. The Historian's Visit

He came on a Tuesday, in a government motorcar that could not have found the lane without directions and had not been given any.

I saw it first from the orchard wall — a black shape crawling up the coast road, throwing mud from the ruts like a dog shaking off water. Fen was on the roof of the barn with the field glasses before I'd finished my tea. He'd taken to watching the road the way Tom watched weather: not out of fear exactly, but because fear, given a job, stops being fear.

"Motorcar," he called down. "One man driving. One beside him in civilian clothes. No escort."

"No escort," Prue repeated from the doorway, in the tone she used for things that were worse than escorts.

Ollie was in the yard stacking kindling with the methodical patience of a boy who had learned that hands kept busy kept safe. He looked up at me. He didn't say anything. He hadn't said anything since the night he'd spoken about the boats, except to us, inside walls, when it mattered.

"It'll be the historian," I said. "Tom said he was coming."

Tom's letter had arrived three days earlier, carried by a fisherman who charged me double for the favor and delivered it damp. *The Admiralty has sent a man down. Not Halloway's sort. A Mr. Ambrose Vellacott, Historical Section. He's asking questions in the port town and not liking the answers he's getting. Thought you should know before he knocks.* Tom signed it with the little anchor he always drew, the one Elias used to draw too, though they'd never met. Some habits are contagious across death.

"He'll knock," Prue said. "They always knock first. It's the second visit you want to worry about."

"Then we'll make sure there doesn't need to be a second," I said, and went to change my collar.

* * *

Mr. Ambrose Vellacott turned out to be a thin man of perhaps fifty, with the stooped shoulders of someone who had spent thirty years bent over other people's paperwork, and eyes that did not miss much and pretended to miss less. He wore a good coat gone slightly shiny at the cuffs and carried a leather case that he held the way some men hold a Bible and others hold a weapon.

He stood at our gate and looked at the orchard for a long moment before speaking. The trees were bare now, November-stripped, their wild unpruned branches making black script against a white sky.

"You'd be Mrs. Thackeray," he said. "Ambrose Vellacott. Admiralty Historical Section." He produced a card, and then, after a pause, as if remembering it cost nothing to be honest: "Though I expect you've already decided what I am, so the card is mostly ceremony."

"You're the man writing the official account of the convoy losses," I said. "Nineteen fifteen through seventeen. The storms."

"The storms." He said the word the way a doctor repeats a patient's self-diagnosis. "Yes. That is nominally my brief." He glanced past me at the house, at Prue standing sentry in the doorway with her arms folded like a gate herself. "Might we speak somewhere warm? I've been in motorcars since Falmouth and I confess my hands have stopped belonging to me."

Prue looked at me. I looked at Prue. Somewhere between us an entire negotiation took place, and I won it by a single nod.

The kitchen. Always the kitchen. Drawing rooms are where people lie comfortably; kitchens are where lies get cold.

* * *

He sat where Halloway had sat, and the difference between them was instructive. Halloway had filled the room, had arranged his limbs and his voice and his silences like furniture placed to direct your movement. Vellacott sat small, drank his tea without comment on its quality — which Prue noted and approved of — and set his leather case on the floor beside his chair rather than on the table between us.

"I'll come to the point," he said, "because I suspect you're a woman who measures time the way a keeper does, in watches, and I'm taking up yours."

"You suspect correctly."

"In 1916, the steamer *Corvus* was lost with all hands off this coast. The official finding — my finding, I am ashamed to say, or the finding issued under my section's name — attributes her loss to storm damage compounded by navigational error. Eleven men. There was a board of inquiry. Statements were taken. The file was closed." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, precisely, like a man aligning something. "Three months ago I was asked to prepare a commemorative summary of wartime maritime losses for public release. A patriotic document. Names, dates, gallant service, the sea's indifference. Very moving stuff. I have written forty of them."

"And?"

"And I pulled the *Corvus* file to check the coordinates against the weather records." He looked up at me then, and whatever courtesy had been sitting over his face like a lamp-glass came off, and underneath was something rawer. "Mrs. Thackeray, the *Corvus* went down on a night when the barometer at this station — your husband's station, your station — recorded winds of no more than force four. Force four does not sink a steamer. Navigational error does not put a ship six miles off her plotted course in home waters, in a corridor every master on this coast knew blind. Something else happened to that ship, and the file knows it, because eleven pages are missing from the middle of it, and the remaining pages have been initialed by a reviewing officer whose initials appear nowhere else in the Admiralty's correspondence for that quarter."

The kitchen was very quiet. Ollie had gone still by the stove. Fen, I knew, was somewhere within earshot of the window, because Fen was always within earshot of windows now.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You don't know me. You don't know what's in this house. For all you know I'm the reviewing officer's cousin."

"I know more than that." He opened the case at last, and took out — not documents, but a single typed page, which he laid face-down on the table like a man laying down a card he wasn't ready to show. "I know that a magistrate named Halloway visited this estate in October. I know that a fire followed. I know that a printer in the port town — a widow, name of Cargove — has recently purchased type she cannot afford and paper she cannot store, and has told anyone who asks that she's printing almanacs. I know that a young relief keeper named Wrenn has been asking careful questions in taverns, and that two of Halloway's men were seen following him last week, and lost him, and were heard to swear about it in language I will not repeat in a kitchen." He paused. "And I know that the lighthouse log for the night of the *Corvus*'s loss — the keeper's log, the primary record, the one document no inquiry can proceed without — was requested by the Board and reported missing. Reported destroyed, in fact. Water damage."

"Elias kept that log," I said. "My husband. He died before any of this reached me."

"Yes. And you inherited a typewriter and, I would wager, a habit of keeping records that would embarrass a court stenographer." He turned the card over at last. It was blank. He'd shown me nothing; he'd told me everything. "Mrs. Thackeray, I am going to ask you a question, and I ask you to consider that the answer may matter more to dead men than to either of us. Do you have the log?"

* * *

I made him wait while I thought, and he let himself be made to wait, which was itself an answer of a kind. Halloway would have pressed. Halloway would have smiled harder. This man sat with his cold hands around his cup and waited, because waiting was how you learned whether a person lied slowly or quickly.

Here is what I weighed, and I weighed it honestly. Every instinct I had built since the letter arrived — since the potting shed, since the true ledger, since the fire that had eaten the estate office and left our copies untouched because we had moved them three days prior, a piece of luck none of us believed was luck — every instinct said: trust no one with a government card. The vicar had wanted the papers for an archive. Halloway had wanted them for a bonfire with better manners. Why should this one be different?

Because, said a quieter voice, the one that sounded like Elias on watch: because he came alone. Because he showed you the shape of the hole in the file instead of pretending there was no hole. Because a man building a cover story does not tell you the cover story is wrong.

Because, said a third voice, Prue's voice: because if he's lying, you'll know soon enough, and knowing sooner is worth more than trusting longer.

"The log survived," I said. "Water-damaged, as reported. Partially. But the pages that matter were typed, and typed pages read through water better than ink does. I have them."

Vellacott did not move. I watched him not move, and understood that not-moving was costing him something considerable.

"May I see them?"

"No."

His jaw tightened. "Mrs. Thackeray—"

"Not today, and not alone, and not into your case to carry back to a section office where pages go missing." I stood, and I'll admit my legs were not entirely steady, but standing was the point. "Mr. Vellacott, I have spent a year learning what happens to documents that travel toward London. They arrive lighter than they left. So here is what I'll offer instead, and you may take it or leave it, but you will not haggle. Everything I have — the log pages, the true ledger, the testimony of four witnesses including a deserter whose evidence could hang him and a child who watched boats come into the cove — is being prepared for publication. Sworn, dated, duplicated. One copy goes to print regardless of what happens to me or to this house. If you want corroboration for your suspicion, you may have it under conditions I set. Not the other way round."

For a long moment he simply looked at me, and I had the strange sensation of being measured by an instrument more honest than most.

"Publication," he said. "You understand what you're proposing. Halloway is a magistrate with patrons. The Admiralty does not enjoy being corrected. A woman publishing accusations against both—" He shook his head. "You'd be safer burying the ledger and forgetting the names."

"I buried a husband," I said. "I'm done burying things."

Something moved behind his eyes — not pity; I'd have resented pity — something more like recognition. He gathered his blank card back into his case and stood.

"Then let me tell you what I can defend," he said, "because that is the only currency I have. I cannot act on suspicion. I cannot act on a ledger of uncertain provenance produced by interested parties — Halloway's counsel will call it forgery by noon of the first day, and he will not be entirely wrong to try. What I can defend is testimony taken properly. Dated. Attested. Given freely, on the record, by named witnesses, with the original documents available for inspection by a neutral party." He paused. "A sworn deposition, in substance, even if no magistrate administers it. Multiple copies, independently held. And above all — corroborated. Your ledger says the *Corvus* was betrayed. Fine. Who paid? Who signed? Can a second source, independent of your ledger, place the same name at the same transaction?"

"Halloway's signature is in it. His hand, his initials, payments across four years."

"His counsel will produce four expert witnesses who swear it's a clever imitation, and two of them will believe what they're swearing." Vellacott buttoned his coat. "But if the ledger is genuine, then everything in it connects to everything else — paybooks, letters, shipping movements, the testimony of living witnesses. Truth is heavy, Mrs. Thackeray. It's the one thing that can't be forged convincingly, because forgery is always lighter than the thing it imitates. Give me weight, and I can defend it. Give me only the ledger, and I'm just another man with a document and a story."

At the door he stopped, and did not turn all the way around, and spoke to the doorframe rather than to me.

"For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is little — my brother was on the *Corvus*. Third engineer. I wrote the report that closed his file, and I wrote it from the papers I was given, and I was given papers that had been emptied." Now he turned, and his face was the face of a man who had carried something up a long stair and meant to set it down. "I am not here for the Admiralty, Mrs. Thackeray. The Admiralty thinks I'm here checking coordinates. I am here because eleven pages went missing and eleven men went missing and I have never once believed those numbers were a coincidence."

Behind him, through the open door, the bare orchard stood in rows like a congregation waiting to be called.

"Come back Thursday," I said. "Bring nothing. We'll begin with the log pages, and you'll read them here, at this table, where I can watch your face. If your face survives the reading, we'll talk about depositions."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you'll leave, and you'll write your commemorative summary, and you'll live with it the way you've lived with the first one." I held the door. "But I don't think it will come to that. You have the look of a man who checks coordinates."

He almost smiled. It was the first thing he'd done that hadn't been careful.

"Thursday," he said, and went out into the mud, and the motorcar took him away down the coast road slower than it had come, as if the driver too sensed that something had been decided.

* * *

They were all in the kitchen before the engine noise had fully faded — Fen from the barn, Prue from the scullery, Ollie from wherever Ollie went when he vanished, which was everywhere and nowhere. Four faces, and mine making five, and every one of them turned to me with the same question.

"Well?" Prue said.

"He wants what we're already building," I said. "Testimony. Dated, attested, duplicated. He can't defend a ledger alone — Halloway would shred it — but he can defend sworn words from living mouths, backed by documents a neutral man has inspected." I looked around the table at the people I had not chosen and could no longer imagine choosing otherwise. "He's giving us a door, is what he's saying. Officialdom doesn't often open doors. Usually you have to break them down."

Fen sat down slowly. His hands were flat on the table, and I noticed they weren't quite steady. "If I give testimony," he said, "dated, attested, on the record — that's not just evidence against Halloway. That's a confession. Desertion. In writing. In duplicate." He laughed, short and without humor. "You'd be handing the rope to the same men you're asking to hang him."

"That's why it's duplicated," I said. "That's why it goes to print the same day. A confession hidden is a hostage. A confession published is a shield. Nobody hangs a man whose hanging proves the book true."

He stared at me. Then, slowly, he nodded, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Ollie tugged my sleeve. When I looked down he pointed at the calendar on the wall — Prue's old farming calendar, days crossed off in pencil — and then at Thursday, and then he held up both hands, fingers spread, and folded four of them down.

Six days, he was telling me. Six days until the armistice rumors said the terms would be signed. Six days until history hardened.

"Thursday," I agreed. "We work tonight."

Prue was already reaching for the lamp. Outside, the wind had come around to the southwest, and far off, beyond the headland, the sea was doing what the sea does — keeping everything, giving back only what it chooses, and only ever to those who ask properly, with the record open and the light burning.
