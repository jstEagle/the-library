## 29. Testimony on the Record

The typewriter ate the night the way the sea eats a coastline — patiently, a little at a time, until you looked up and the shape of things was changed.

I worked at the long table in the estate office, the one Fen had cleared of rat-chewed ledgers and stacked with what remained worth keeping: the true ledger wrapped in oilcloth, Prue's testimony in my own fair hand from the first transcription, Ollie's words exactly as he'd spoken them, no smoothing, no adult grammar laid over a child's plain speech. Fen sat across from me cutting stencils for the widow's press, because she'd written that she could set type faster than any man in the port town but needed the pages numbered and true before she'd touch them. Tom was out on the road to meet the morning coach. Prue kept the lamp trimmed and the tea coming, and every hour or so she would stop behind my chair and read over my shoulder without apology, because it was her life I was typing, and she had earned the right to watch her forty years come off the platen one letter at a time.

"Say that again," she said once, when I reached the part about the winter boat.

"I've typed it as you told me."

"Then type it again. Some things want saying twice so they can't be unwound."

So I retyped it. The carbon took a second copy beneath the first, and I set them apart carefully, one to dry on the dresser, one under a book, and understood that this small act — the doubling, the separation — was the whole strategy now. A single record can be stolen. A single witness can be frightened. But two copies, held by different hands in different places, make a thing that must be denied twice, publicly, and denial is a poor swimmer.

By three in the morning I had fourteen pages of sworn testimony — we were calling it sworn, though no magistrate would swear it, and that absence was itself part of the record. *Taken in the absence of lawful authority willing to hear it*, I typed at the head of each set, and beneath that, *witnessed by the undersigned, who attest to the truth of what is here set down.* Then the lines for signatures.

Fen's line I left last. He looked at it a long time, the way a man looks at water he means to enter in November.

"If I sign it," he said, "it's not paper anymore. It's a rope with my name spliced into it."

"It's already a rope," I said. "This is the part where someone holds the other end."

He signed. His hand didn't shake, which surprised us both, I think. He wrote his full name — the name he'd deserted, the name that could hang him — and then, after a moment, added his rank and regiment in a soldier's neat block letters, because testimony given halfway is worse than none.

Prue signed. Tom, back from the road with cold in his coat and news in his face he was holding for daylight, signed. Ollie made his mark — a careful O with a little house drawn inside it, his father's birdhouse rendered small — and Prue steadied his elbow while he did it, and nobody said anything about the house, because some things don't need saying aloud to be understood by everyone in the room.

Fourteen pages, five sets. One for the Admiralty man. One for the widow's press, to go to type before anyone could reach it. One to be sewn into the lining of Elias's coat, which hung on the office door and had not left my keeping since the island. And two more, because Prue insisted, buried in separate tins in the orchard, marked on no map but the one inside her head, which forty years of Thackerays had never managed to lose.

"You're learning," she said, watching me count the sets a fourth time.

"I'm counting," I said. "Learning is what happens if I live long enough."

* * *

They arrived within twenty minutes of each other, and I do not believe it was chance.

Mr. Lewin came up the drive on foot at half past nine, carrying his case like a doctor's bag and his hat like a man who has stopped performing deference. He was younger than I'd expected — the Admiralty's historians always sounded ancient in their printed reports — with ink-stained cuffs and the particular tiredness of a clever man working inside an institution that prefers him stupid. He had come down from London on the night train, he said, and had walked from the station rather than wait for a cart, because waiting, in his experience, gave other people time to arrive first.

Mrs. Kellow's cart came rattling ten minutes behind him, and she did not wait to be announced. She was sixty if she was a day, built like the doorframe of a chapel, with a printer's thumb stained black to the second knuckle and eyes that had read every lie ever set in movable type in that port town and charged extra for most of them. She had lost her husband to the sea and her son to the war and her patience to the county, in roughly that order, and she looked at Lewin the way a cat looks at a dog that has wandered into its kitchen.

"So," she said, dumping her case on the estate office table next to his. "The Crown sends its clerk, and I send myself. Between us we'll get it done."

"Madam—"

"Don't madam me, young man. I set type for Halloway's election address in 'ten and watched him change three facts between the third and fourth printing. I know what a corrected account looks like. I've got ink in my blood older than your department." She turned to me. "Well, keeper? You sent for me. What have you got?"

What I had was the table, and I laid it out the way you lay out instruments before an operation: the true ledger, opened to the page with the payments and the names of the drowned; the deposition sets, still smelling of carbon; the shipping ledgers with their razored years; the undelivered letters, bundled and dated; Elias's letters to his father, which I had not shown to anyone before this morning and which I put down last, deliberately, because they were not evidence. They were mine.

Lewin went very still over the ledger. I watched him read the signature column the way you'd watch a man find a hole in the ice he'd been walking on all winter.

"This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This corroborates everything. The convoy dates, the payment schedule against the storm records, the crew lists against the Admiralty's own loss returns. Do you understand what you have? I've suspected the official account since the inquiry files crossed my desk in March. Suspected. There is a gulf between suspecting and being able to prove, and that gulf is staffed entirely by men whose careers depend on not crossing it." He looked up at me, and there was something raw in his face that courtesy had been holding down like a hatch cover in weather. "If this goes into my report and my report survives review, the lost boat's crew comes off the books as deserters and smugglers and goes back on as what they were."

"And if your report doesn't survive?" Mrs. Kellow asked pleasantly. She was already reading over the deposition pages, lips moving slightly, a compositor measuring type with her eyes. "Because it won't, will it. Not unless there's something they can't burn, can't buy, and can't bury in a ministry basement. Which is why I'm here." She tapped the pages. "I'll set this entire document. Five hundred copies minimum. Cheap paper, cheap ink, sold for a penny at the fish market and left in chapel pews and posted to every newspaper between here and Plymouth. You can't review five hundred copies, Mr. Government Man. You can only look foolish trying."

"That is precisely the kind of action that will bring charges against everyone in this room," Lewin said.

"Yes," said Mrs. Kellow, with the satisfaction of a woman agreeing about the weather. "That's rather the point. Fear works on secrets. It doesn't work worth a damn on five hundred pennies' worth of pamphlets scattered across Cornwall."

* * *

We did it properly, because if it was worth doing at all it was worth doing so that no hand could later unmake the doing.

Lewin produced paper — actual depositions forms, Admiralty stock, which made Prue snort into her apron when she saw the crown stamped at the top — and we transferred the substance of my typed testimony onto them in the prescribed order, question and answer, my questions put plainly, the answers given verbatim from the transcriptions with the witnesses standing by to confirm each page. Prue testified first, forty years compressed into nine pages, her voice steady and her hands flat on the table like a woman planting something. Fen confirmed his pages standing, in uniform pieces — he'd kept his jacket — and when Lewin asked him formally whether he understood the consequences of attesting, Fen said, "Sir, I have thought about nothing else since October," and Lewin wrote it down exactly.

Ollie would not sit in the chair. He stood beside Prue with his hand in hers and answered Lewin's gentle questions in his few words — the boats, the lights at the cove, the men who went inland and the men who never came back — and when Lewin asked him how old he was, Ollie held up fingers instead of speaking, and Lewin waited, patient as tide, until the boy was ready for the next one. At the end Lewin crouched to Ollie's height and said, "You are the bravest witness in this county," and Ollie considered this and said, "Prue says brave is just scared and staying," and Lewin laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and wrote that down too.

I signed as compiler and witness. So did Prue, and Tom, and Fen, and Ollie's mark went on every set.

Then the duplication, done in front of everyone so that no one could later claim otherwise: three originals, each countersigned by Lewin and Mrs. Kellow as independent recipients, each sealed separately. Lewin took his to carry personally to London — "not the post, not the departmental bag, my own coat pocket, and I'll sleep with it" — along with certified photographs he'd brought apparatus to take of the ledger pages, the plates developed on the spot in the darkened pantry while Mrs. Kellow stood guard outside making loud conversation about apple varieties in case anyone came up the drive.

Mrs. Kellow took her set to the press. "It goes to type tonight," she said, wrapping it in sacking like contraband, which is what it was. "Once it's in metal, Mr. Halloway, it can't be unmade short of melting my shop down around it, and he's welcome to try, because I sleep above the shop with my late husband's shotgun and very little patience."

And the third set went into the lining of Elias's coat, sewn in by Prue's own hand with the particular fierce stitching she used on things meant to outlast storms.

"There," she said, biting the thread. "Your husband carried the light. Now the light carries you."

* * *

They left together, oddly enough — the Crown's historian and the radical widow sharing the road to the station, arguing amiably about margins and whether the public could tolerate footnotes. At the gate Lewin turned back and said, quietly, so only I heard it:

"When the report goes in, there will be an attempt to discredit the sources. That attempt will begin with you. They'll say grief unhinged you, that a lonely woman on an island invented a conspiracy to give her loss a shape. I've seen it done. Be ready for it."

"I've been ready for it since I learned to type," I said. "Grief didn't invent the ledger, Mr. Lewin. Grief just taught me to read it."

He nodded slowly, and something passed between us that I would not know how to name until much later — the recognition of two people who keep records because the alternative is letting the sea decide what happened.

I watched them down the lane until the hedge took them. Three copies, four directions, five hundred pamphlets pending. Whatever Halloway did now, whatever fire or warrant or whispering campaign he loosed, he would be striking at a thing with too many heads to kill.

I was standing in the doorway of the office enjoying the first hour of strategic calm I'd felt since October when Tom came up from the cove path at a run, hatless, white-faced, and skidded to a halt on the gravel.

"He knows," Tom gasped. "There's a man in the village — bought drinks for the harbor boys, asking who'd been up the orchard drive this week. Xanthe — he described the widow's cart. Down to the sacking."
