## 30. Betrayal at the Cove

I'd been watching the cove for three days before I understood why.

It wasn't a decision so much as a habit gone wrong. Ever since the warrant came — ever since Xanthe stood in the kitchen with the paper flat on the table and her hands very still, the way she goes still when she's decided something — I'd taken to walking the coast path in the evenings, down past the old pilchard cellars, to where the rocks shelf down into water the color of gunmetal. It was the one place on the estate I could stand and feel useful without being asked anything.

Fen was gone by then. Not fled — Xanthe would have said *gone to ground*, and she'd have been right. He slept in the loft above the cider house and ate his meals at the kitchen table same as ever, but he'd stopped going past the boundary wall, stopped walking the lane to the village, stopped being a man who existed in daylight. He spent his hours in the estate office with the surviving papers, copying names in his careful, schoolroom hand, because Fen had decided that if they were going to take him, they'd take him with the record finished. He'd raised his hand at the kitchen table for that. I'd seen him do it.

The cove was the one place he couldn't stop going. Not because he wanted to — because the boat was coming.

The widow-printer had sent word through the fish-hawker, who sent word through Prue's nephew, who told Prue, who told the kitchen at large with the air of a woman announcing the price of flour: a copy of the ledger and the full set of sworn testimonies would come down from the port town on the Thursday boat, and the second set — the insurance set, the set that mattered — would go back up the same way, into the widow's hands, into a wall in her shop that she swore no magistrate's man had ever found and never would. Xanthe had listened to all of this with her chin up and her eyes on the middle distance, and then she'd said, "The cove, then. Thursday, after dark. Someone has to meet it."

And because I was the youngest, and the fastest, and the one with no warrant against his name, everyone had looked at me.

I'd said yes before I'd thought about it. That's the thing I keep coming back to. I didn't weigh it. I didn't lie awake doing sums about tides and constables. Xanthe asked, and I said yes, the way you'd say yes to passing the bread. It was only later — lying on the cold turf above the cove on the third night of watching, with my collar up and my teeth going — that I understood what I'd agreed to. Not the errand. The errand was nothing. A parcel, a boat, a walk home. What I'd agreed to was being the sort of person who could be asked.

* * *

Thursday came in grey and low, with a swell running out of the southwest that made even the gulls look seasick. I went down the cliff path at dusk with the parcel-sack empty against my back and Prue's oilskin over my jacket, and I took up my position in the hollow behind the old net-drying stones, where I could see the whole sweep of the shingle and the mouth of the cave where the Thackeray boats had once lain up between runs. The tide was on the make. By ten it would be high enough for a boat to come right in under the cave lip, the way Ollie said they used to.

Ollie had wanted to come. Of course he had. He'd stood in the doorway with his coat already on and that stubborn set to his jaw he'd learned, I'd have sworn, from watching Xanthe, and Xanthe had crouched down to his level and said, "Not this one. This one's mine to give away, and I'm giving it to Tom." And Ollie had looked at her for a long moment and then looked at me, and whatever he saw in my face had satisfied him, because he'd taken off his coat and hung it back on its nail with terrible dignity.

I thought about that a lot, in the hollow. How he'd hung up the coat. How he'd decided to trust me with something that mattered to him, without being asked, without being promised anything.

The rain started around nine. Fine stuff, more mist than water, beading on the oilskin and running into my collar. The dark came down thick. I couldn't see the cave mouth anymore, only the white stutter of the swell breaking on the outer rocks, and I counted the flashes the way I'd learned to count the lamp's rotation on the island — one, two, three, dark; one, two, three, dark — until the counting turned into a kind of prayer, and the prayer turned into nearly sleeping, and the nearly sleeping turned into the sound of boots on shingle.

That's what woke me. Not a boat. Boots.

I lay flat and listened. Shingle doesn't lie. It crunches under every foot, and these were many feet, and they were coming down the coast path from the village side in that deliberate, spread-out way that men walk when they've been told to cover a line. I pressed my face into the wet turf and looked sideways along the beach, and there they were: dark shapes against the marginally less dark water, six of them, maybe seven, working down the shingle with lanterns shuttered to slits. Not fishermen. Fishermen don't move like that. Fishermen don't carry truncheons where you can see them.

Halloway's men. It had to be. The magistrate had been careful since the courtroom — careful and courteous and everywhere — and somewhere in the village there was a man who'd sold what he knew for the price of a drink: the boat, the night, the cove. There's always a man like that. I'd known it in my bones since the island, since the storms, since every winter when the wreckers' tales came out. The sea doesn't sink ships. Men do.

And the boat wasn't coming. I understood that with a cold drop in my stomach. The widow's man would see the lanterns from offshore and stand away, and the parcel would go back up the coast, and the second set of papers — the insurance, the thing Xanthe had staked everything on duplicating — would sit in some boatman's hold until who knew when, because I'd be in no state to arrange a second meeting, or worse, because the cove would be watched now, every night, forever.

Unless the watchers stopped watching.

I didn't decide anything. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger this all goes into. I didn't stand up and make a brave choice. I just got up, the way you get up when the alternative is lying there being useless, and I ran.

* * *

Not toward the cave. Toward the far end of the beach, the open stretch below the coastguard ruins, where the shingle was deep and loose and loud. I crashed into it deliberately, arms out, boots slamming, making the noise of three men where there was one, and I heard the lantern-slits swing, and I heard a voice — a hard, official voice, a voice that had learned its hardness from Halloway — shout, "There! On the beach! Stop, in the King's name!"

I stopped. That was the plan, such as it was. Stop, let them see me, let them count one boy instead of a smuggling run, and then run again, and keep them running, up the beach and along the ruins and anywhere that wasn't the cave mouth, until the tide turned and the boat stood off and the whole night was wasted for them. It was a boy's plan. I was a boy. It was the only plan I had.

The first one reached me before I'd gone thirty yards. He was bigger than me and older and he'd done this before — I could tell by how he moved, cutting the angle, herding me toward the sea wall instead of chasing straight — and he had a lantern open now, and the light hit me full in the face, and he said, "It's a lad. It's just a lad," with something almost like relief, and then, "Where's the boat, boy? Who were you meeting?"

"Meeting?" I said. "I was looking for my dog."

Nobody has ever believed the dog. I knew that even as I said it. He knew I knew. And that's when it stopped being a chase and started being what it was.

The second one came up on my left, and the third behind, and the official voice — he'd hung back, he was the one who gave the orders and kept his hands clean — said, "Search him," and they did, and they found the empty parcel-sack, and the empty sack told them everything the full one would have. The official voice went quiet in a way I didn't like at all. Then he said, "You're the Wrenn boy. The relief keeper. You're one of Thackeray's."

"I'm nobody's," I said, which was a lie, and a stupid one, because I was everybody's, I was Xanthe's and Prue's and Ollie's and even Fen's, and I'd have said so under worse treatment than I got.

They walked me up the beach to the sea wall, and the official voice asked his questions — where was the boat coming from, where were the papers going, where was the deserter Fen — and I said *I don't know* and *I don't know* and *who?* until the questions stopped being questions. The first one, the big one, was tired and wet and had been standing on a cold beach for hours because of me, and he lost his temper the way tired men do, fast and without ceremony. He didn't use the truncheon. He used his fist, twice, and then a boot when I went down on the shingle, and the shingle did the rest. I got my arms up for most of it. Most of it.

The strange thing — the thing I'd want written down, if anyone writes me down — is that I wasn't frightened. Not properly. There was a moment, flat on my back with the rain coming down and a boot drawing back, when I thought, very clearly: *they can't make me not have come down here. Whatever they do to me, I came, and the boat will have seen the lights, and the papers are safe, and this is what it costs, and Xanthe pays costs like this every day and never says so.* It wasn't courage. It was arithmetic. It was the first time in my life I'd done sums about anything bigger than myself and liked the answer.

Then the boot landed and the arithmetic went white, and after that it was just noise and cold and the taste of blood and shingle, and the official voice saying, "Enough. Enough — he's a child, leave it," and the big one breathing hard above me, and then the boots going away up the beach, all of them, because somewhere out beyond the outer rocks a light had blinked — one, two, three, dark — and stood off, and stood away, and the night's work was done for everyone, one way and the other.

* * *

I don't remember getting up the cliff path. I remember deciding to, and I remember the kitchen.

Prue found me at the door, or I found her light — I think I'd have walked through fire toward that kitchen window, the way ships walk toward a lamp. She didn't ask anything. She got her arms under me and steered me to a chair and put the kettle on, and her hands shook, and she said, "You daft, brave, stupid boy," about nine times, in between tearing strips of linen, and it's the only eulogy I ever want.

Xanthe came down in her coat, half-dressed, having been at the office with Fen over the copying. She crouched in front of the chair the way she'd crouched for Ollie, and she took my chin in her hand and turned my face to the light, and she looked at what they'd made of me for a long, long moment. Her eyes were doing something I'd never seen them do. Not tears. Worse. The thing that comes before tears and refuses them.

"Did they get the sack?" she asked.

"Empty," I said. It hurt to talk. I said it anyway. "They got nothing. The boat stood off. I saw the light."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them she was the keeper again, the one who measures everything, and she said, "Then it cost exactly what it had to, and not a penny more," and she held my hand while Prue cleaned me up, and she never once told me I'd been foolish. Later I heard her in the passage saying to Fen, in a voice like a struck bell, "They beat a boy on my beach for an empty sack. Write that down. Date it. They've just made their own deposition."

Fen's voice, low: "Already done."

* * *

I'm writing this with my ribs strapped and my left eye a slit and my hand cramping around the pen, and I'm not writing it for sympathy. I'm writing it because I finally understand what this household is for. We're not hiding from the war anymore, any of us. We're the part of the record that has to be paid for in person.

And this morning, before I was properly awake, Ollie came into my room and put something on the blanket beside me without a word. It was one of his carved gulls, the little wooden ones he makes from orchard offcuts, the best one he's ever done, wings set like a thing that means to go somewhere. He looked at my face — at the eye, at the split lip — with an expression no child should have to practice, and then he said, clear as a bell, his first words in weeks:

"They'll read about you."

Then he went out, and I lay there holding the gull, and down in the lane I could hear hooves — a horse coming up the valley fast, too fast, a horse that meant a message — and Prue's voice calling Xanthe's name from the yard.
