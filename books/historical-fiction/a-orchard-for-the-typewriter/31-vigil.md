## 31. Vigil

The gull was warm.

That was the first thing I knew, sitting on the floor beside the bed where Tom lay: the wooden gull his father carved was warm from being held, and everything else in the room was cold. The window had a crack in it now where a man's head had gone through it, or near enough — glass starred out from one point like frost on a pane, and through the star I could see the yard lantern swinging and Xanthe running with her coat half on, and Prue standing very still by the gate with her arms crossed, which was worse than running. Prue still meant something had already happened that could not be undone.

I held the gull because Tom's hand had been holding it when they carried him in, and I thought if I kept it warm it would be like keeping him warm. That is how thinking works when you are nine. Later I learned most people keep doing it their whole lives.

Tom's face was the wrong color. Not red like when you fall. A yellow-gray, like the inside of an apple gone over. His eye was swollen shut on one side and the other one opened sometimes and looked at the ceiling as if the ceiling were far away and he was trying to read something written there.

Xanthe came up the stairs two at a time and then went quiet at the top, the way she does, like she was entering the lamp room and did not want to wake the light. She knelt. She took his wrist without asking and counted under her breath, and I know she was counting because her lips moved the way they move when she types numbers.

"He's breathing," she said, to all of us and to no one. "He's breathing. Fen — water. Prue —"

"Boiling," said Prue, and went down.

Fen came up with the basin and knelt on my other side, and the three of us made a kind of fence around the bed, and I thought: this is what a sheepfold looks like from inside.

* * *

They had come to the cove at dusk, four men on two horses and one on foot, asking after a deserter. Asking polite, Tom told it later, so polite you could hear the boots under the politeness. Tom met them on the lane above the beach with a lantern and a lie, saying he'd seen a fellow answering that description heading for Penzance on the afternoon coach, and one of them — a big man with a voice like gravel poured slow — asked why a farm boy would be walking the cove road at dusk in November, and Tom said he was looking for a lost ewe, and the big man looked past him down at the water for a long time, and then hit him.

Not at once. First the others got down off the horses. Then the big man hit him once, open-handed, almost gentle, the way you'd correct a dog, and Tom — who has his father's temper in a small body — hit back. After that it stopped being a conversation. They put him against the stone wall and worked on him a while, and then the big man leaned close and said words that Tom would not repeat even lying broken, and rode off toward the town, and Tom walked home, which Prue says is the bravest thing she ever saw a person do, walking a mile on legs that were failing him, holding a carved gull, leaving a trail of blood like a snail's so they could follow him if they wanted.

They didn't want. That was the worst part, Fen said. They wanted him found later, found hurt, found as a message.

* * *

Prue brought up the kettle herself, though her knees hate those stairs, and she sat on the end of the bed — sat right on it, which she never does, she treats beds like altars — and she began, without anyone asking, to talk.

She talked the way she talks when she's kneading bread, steady, both hands going, eyes on nothing. And what she talked was stories. One after another, all night long, every soul the orchard had ever sheltered, fed, smuggled inland in a cider cart under sacking, hidden in the cellars behind the apple racks. She talked them like a rosary, and I understood, even at nine, that she wasn't talking for us. She was talking for Tom, so that when he drifted up from wherever he'd gone he would surface into a room full of voices telling him about people who had been hurt before and lived.

"There was a gunner called Aldous," she said. "1915. Shell-shocked so bad he couldn't bear the sound of his own name — we called him Mr. Brown the whole six weeks he was here. Slept in the north loft. Afraid of the dark, so Ollie's father left a candle stub burning every night, and never once complained about the tallow. He went inland in March dressed as a drover. Lived, so far as we ever heard. There's a card somewhere — Christmas card, unsigned, postmarked Exeter. A man doesn't send a card unless he's living."

She said *lived* like hammering a nail home.

"There was a girl. Wren — no relation to our Tom, though isn't it a funny thing, names go round like rooks. She'd run from a house in Truro where the man of it beat her, and she was carrying, and we put her in the still-room and Prudence-that-was — my mother-in-law, God rest her — delivered the child herself with hands washed in boiled water and a prayer she pretended wasn't one. Girl stayed till lammas. Named the baby after the orchard. Rowena Apple-tree something-or-other." Prue's mouth did its crooked thing. "Child'll be seven now. Growing up somewhere with a tree for a godmother."

* * *

Through the small hours the fever came, and the vigil changed shape.

Xanthe would not leave the chair. She had sent Fen down for her typewriter case — not the machine itself, just the little tin of things inside it, the cleaning brush, the oil, the spare ribbons — and from these she took the thermometer and the scissors and a needle, and she worked over Tom's face with the lamp turned high, cleaning the cuts, and her hands never shook, and I watched her hands instead of Tom's face because hers were easier to look at.

Once, between the second hour and the third, Tom surfaced enough to speak, and what he said was, "Did they take the ewe?" and Prue laughed so suddenly and so loud that it startled us all, and then she pressed her apron to her mouth, and Xanthe said, "The ewe is safe, Tom Wrenn, and so are you, so stay."

And he stayed. You could see him deciding to. Like a man hauling on a rope.

Fen sat against the wall with his knees up, and in the lamplight I watched him look at Tom's face and then at his own hands, and I knew — the way I know things, sideways, without being told — that Fen was counting what it cost other people to keep him. He'd done the arithmetic already; I'd seen him do it before, at supper, silent. So I climbed down off my stool and went and sat against the wall next to him, shoulder to shoulder, and after a while he put his arm around me, careful, like I might break, when really it was him.

"They'll come again," he said quietly, to me, or to the wall.

"I know," I said.

"You should be asleep."

"So should you," I said, and he huffed — nearly a laugh — and didn't move.

* * *

It was deep in the night, past three by the kitchen clock striking faintly below, that Prue told the story she had never told.

I know it was never told because Xanthe went still in a new way — not keeper-still but struck-still — and set down the cloth she was wringing out.

"There was a boy," Prue said, "in the winter of '16. Sixteen years old. He came up from the cove in the snow with frostbitten feet and a naval coat two sizes too big, and he couldn't have been more than a child, and Mrs. Thackeray — Elias's mother, that is, a hard woman, a woman who kept accounts — she sat up three nights with his feet in tepid water, raising the temperature a degree at a time the way the doctor wrote to her from Falmouth, and she saved both feet and most of the boy. And when he was well enough to move on she gave him apples from the store and a shilling sewn into his coat hem, and he kissed her hand, this great lanky lad, and cried, and went over the hill to Bodmin road before light."

Prue paused. The fire ticked. Downstairs the clock gathered itself and struck the quarter, soft.

"And here's the thing," she said, "that I've carried forty years and told nobody, because it wasn't mine alone to tell. That boy wrote. Twice a year, all through the war — letters to the orchard, care of no name, just *the house under the hill*. And the last one came in September. He's in Devon now, convalescent, lost the hearing in one ear but kept the feet, kept the feet, Mrs. Thackeray, he wrote, she'll want to know about the feet. And he signs himself with a name he took from us, because we never learned his real one. He signs himself *Orchard*. Private Orchard. And there's a living boy walking around Devon wearing our name because one hard woman sat up three nights with a basin of water and refused to let a child be ruined entire."

She looked around the room, at each of us, last at Xanthe.

"That's what this house is," she said. "That's what it's been, under the rot and the shame and the locked shed and whatever your magistrate thinks he's going to find. It isn't land. Land is just where it stood. It's a basin of water in the night and somebody refusing."

Nobody spoke. Then Tom, from the pillow, in a voice like a hinge, said:

"The ewe."

"Go to sleep," said Prue, in the voice she uses for the hens, and he smiled — half his face could smile, the other half was too swollen — and slept, truly slept, breath going long and even, and Xanthe counted his pulse again and then sat back in the chair and put her face in her hands for exactly four seconds, I counted, and then took them away and was the keeper again.

* * *

Toward dawn Fen went down to make bread — he bakes when he can't sleep, which is often, and none of us complain — and Xanthe sent me to bed and I pretended to go, and lay in the loft listening to the house do its night sounds: the clock, the coals, the sea a long way off getting ready for morning. Once I heard the typewriter. Three lines, maybe four. Then it stopped. I found out later what she typed, because she keeps everything, even the pages she means to burn, and this one she hadn't burned. It says:

*Night of the 4th. Wind SW, moderate. One of ours hurt by men acting under color of law. He walked home. Note for the record: he walked home.*

Underneath, smaller, as if added later:

*Prue says a basin of water in the night is the whole of it. Entered into evidence: one basin, one night, five people awake.*

* * *

Gray light came. Prue went down and came back with bacon because she holds that grief and fear are best fought with breakfast, and Tom woke properly for the first time, both eyes, and ate half of it, and asked questions in a whisper — did they see the boat, did they search the cellars, did anyone follow him — and Xanthe answered each one flat and true, no, no, no, and then said, "You're not to go out alone again. None of us goes anywhere alone until this is finished. That's not a request."

"It wasn't a request anyway," said Fen from the doorway, flour to the elbows.

And that was when we heard hooves again.

Everyone in the room stopped — forks, breath, the works. But it was only the postboy, trotting casual up the lane, and he left one letter at the gate, and Prue fetched it in, and handed it to Xanthe, and I watched Xanthe turn it over in her hands.

It was official-looking. Thick paper. A stamp from the county court, and printed letters, and her name spelled out formal — *Mrs. X. Thackeray, Thackeray Orchard* — and she read it standing in the gray morning light with Tom's blood still on her cuffs, and her face did not change at all, which frightened me more than crying would have.

"What is it?" Prue said.

Xanthe laid the letter down on the table, square with the table edge, the way she squares everything.

"A warrant," she said. "For my arrest. Harboring a deserter." She looked round at us — at Tom bandaged on the settle, at Fen white as his flour, at me, at Prue — and something settled in her face like a lamp being trimmed to a steady flame. "They mean to try me at the assizes. Which means, gentlemen and ladies, that we are going to court — and everything we know goes on the record, or nowhere."

She picked the letter back up and folded it along its crease, precise as typing.

"Fen," she said quietly, "we need to talk about what happens when they ask you your name."
