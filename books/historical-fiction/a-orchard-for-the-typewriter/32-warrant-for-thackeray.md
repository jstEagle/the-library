## 32. Warrant for Thackeray

The constable came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for bread and lamp oil and the ordinary arithmetic of a household; warrants belonged to Fridays, to dark weather, to the kind of day you braced against. Instead the morning was mild and gold-edged, mist lifting off the orchard rows, and Constable Tregose stood at the gate holding his hat in both hands like a man arriving at a funeral he'd rather have missed.

"Mrs. Thackeray," he said.

"Tregose." I set down the pail of windfalls I'd been carrying. My hands did not shake. I had learned that about myself in the last year — that my hands held steady precisely when they had no business doing so. Grief had trained them. "You'd better come in. Prue will want to feed you whether you've earned it or not."

"I can't stop long." He glanced over his shoulder at the lane, empty except for a cart going the wrong way toward the coast road. "I'm meant to read it to you formal. On the step."

"Then read it on the step."

He unfolded the paper. He was an old man, or old enough, with a face weathered into permanent apology, and his voice when it came was pitched low, as though volume might make the thing more real than either of us wanted.

"Warrant issued out of the county sessions for the apprehension of one Thackeray, Xanthe, widow, of this parish, upon information laid that she did knowingly harbor and conceal one Fenwick Ossian, deserter from His Majesty's forces, contrary to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Contrary to the relevant acts."

"Fenwick Ossian," I repeated. Not Fen. They had his full name. That meant someone had given it to them, and there were only so many someones.

"That's the man living in the east cottage, is it?"

"You know very well it is, Tregose. You drank his cider at harvest."

He had the grace to look stricken. "I didn't know then what I know now."

"None of us did. That's generally how knowing works." I took the paper from him before he could protest — he let it go easily enough, which told me something too — and read it through myself. The clerk's hand was neat. The information had been *laid*, that lovely passive verb, by a person unnamed. I knew the shape of the signature behind it anyway. Halloway didn't need to sign his own arrows anymore; he had men who fletched them for him.

"You're not to leave the parish," Trecose said miserably. "The hearing's set for the fourteenth. At Bodmin."

"The fourteenth." Nine days. "And if I don't come?"

"They'll send for you. And Mrs. Thackeray — they won't be gentle about it. Not with Halloway backing the information." He put his hat on, took it off again. "I've known your husband's family since I was a boy. I carried apples for old Mr. Thackeray. Whatever you're planning—"

"Thank you, Tregose. Tell Prue on your way out that you'll take tea, because she'll ask, and I'd rather she heard it from you than stood at that window watching you go."

He went. Through the kitchen window I watched her waylay him on the path, watched his shoulders drop as she pressed a mug into his hands, watched him drink standing up like a schoolboy and leave the mug on the wall with exaggerated care. Then the lane was empty and the paper was still in mine.

I sat down at the estate office table — our table now, scarred and ink-stained — and read the warrant a third time. Then I did what I always did with documents that frightened me.

I typed a copy.

* * *

They found me there an hour later, all of them, drawn by the clatter of keys the way farmyard birds draw to a scattered bucket. Tom first, home from the port run, salt still on him. Then Prue, flour to her elbows. Then Ollie, who read better now than any child his age had a right to and who stood behind my shoulder sounding the words out until his face went pale in a way I hated.

Fen came last. He stood in the doorway of the office and looked at the warrant lying flat under my fingers, and I watched a man do arithmetic with his own neck.

"So," he said. "It's me they've come for after all."

"It's me they've come for. You they'll come for next." I turned in the chair. "Sit down. All of you. We're past the hour where anyone stands while someone else sits."

Prue dragged the bench in from the passage. Tom lowered himself onto it slowly, the way he'd learned to move since the cove — his ribs still bound, his left eye still shadowed faintly yellow at the edge. Eleven days since the raiders had worked him over on the shingle looking for Fen, and eleven days since he'd lied to their faces with a split lip and sent them up the wrong valley. He hadn't complained once. He complained constantly about porridge, about the price of nails, about the vicar's sermons, but not about the ribs. I understood that. It was a household habit. We wore our damage as competence, every last one of us.

"They'll hang him," Prue said. No preamble. She'd earned the right to skip them forty years ago. "War's nearly done. They'll want an example before the shooting stops and everyone starts remembering mercy."

"They might," I said. "Or they might use him to get to the ledger. Which is exactly why the ledger leaves this house today."

That got their attention. Tom straightened despite his ribs.

"It was always going to come to this," I said. "Halloway offered me a pension and a clean name and I refused. He burned the office rather than let the papers stand. Now he's stopped being clever and started being legal, because legal feels safe to men like him. A warrant. A hearing. A quiet conviction, and the record dies with the case." I tapped the typed copy. "So we do what we've been practicing since autumn. We duplicate. We disperse. We make it so that no one blow, no one fire, no one convenient death can finish us."

"The widow," Tom said.

"The widow. Mrs. Penhale at the print shop. She's already agreed to hold whatever we give her — she said, and I quote, 'I've buried two husbands and a printing press, I can bury a ledger.'" I looked around the table. "But it can't be any of you who carries it. If I'm taken on the road, they search me and find it, and it goes into an evidence box that Halloway's friends will unlock with a candle. If Fen carries it, same, worse. If Tom carries it—"

"I'm the fastest thing on two legs between here and the coast road," Tom said.

"You're also the boy who got his face rearranged drawing soldiers off this property nine days ago. They know your face now. It's the one face in Cornwall they're watching for." I hated saying it. I said it anyway. "It has to be someone they aren't looking for. Someone dull. Someone with a legitimate reason to be traveling to the port town with a heavy parcel."

Silence. Then Prue said, "You mean you."

"I mean me."

"You've got a warrant naming *you*," Prue said. "You can't travel. You can't so much as walk to the parish boundary without making it worse."

"I can't leave the *parish*," I agreed. "Nobody said anything about the parish line being guarded. Tregose isn't going to set men on the lanes to watch a widow carry apples to market. And I'm not going to Bodmin on the fourteenth to stand trial empty-handed while the only true copy of the truth sits in a farmhouse waiting to be burned." I stood. "I'm going to take it to her myself, tomorrow, market day, under the apple crates where I always take apples. And then I'm coming back, and on the fourteenth I will stand in front of whatever bench they seat, and I will say yes, I harbored him, I'd harbor him again, and here is why — and Mrs. Penhale will hold the rest of the argument in trust."

"And if they take the parcel off you at the market?" Fen asked quietly.

"They won't. Because they're not looking yet. Halloway thinks he's won. That's the one gift he keeps giving us — certainty. Men certain of victory don't search apple crates." I gathered the typed pages, squared them, wrapped them in oilcloth, and then, because I am who I am, wrapped them again in sacking that smelled honestly of fruit. "Now. There are things to settle before tomorrow, and I want them settled tonight, aloud, so no one can pretend later they weren't asked."

* * *

We settled them by lamplight, and it took most of the night, because love is inefficient and we were all full of it and pretending otherwise.

First: Fen. He wanted to run. Of course he did; running had kept him alive for three years and he was good at it, and every instinct in that lean, watchful body must have been screaming *go now, go tonight, go over the fields*. But there was nowhere left to run that wasn't someone else's war. Ireland was a coffin with a flag on it. The ports were watched. And — he said this himself, staring at the table, in the flattest voice I ever heard him use — if he ran, the warrant would be satisfied, the case closed tidy, and the ledger would become the rantings of a convicted woman's family.

"If you stay," I said, "you gamble your neck on that record mattering."

"If I go," he said, "it stops mattering, and then nothing I ran from was worth the running." He rubbed his jaw. "My father used to say a man should keep his debts where he can see them. I owe these people. I owe that boat's crew. I'll stay."

Second: the household, if I was taken. Prue had the answer before I finished asking, because Prue had been answering questions like this since before I was born. "This house has hidden men through two wars and one of each kind of panic. It doesn't need you to function, girl. It needs you to *come back*." And then, gruffer, because tenderness embarrassed her: "Ollie stays inside after dark. Tom stays off the cove. I stay armed with the hay fork, and I promise you I remember how to use it."

Third: the original ledger. Not the typed copy — the true book itself, with Halloway's signature in iron-gall ink, the bell's provenance written in Elias's father's hand. It went back into the potting shed, behind the loose board, under the seed trays. Ollie volunteered the hiding spot's second key and then, unprompted, recited the location of the first like a catechism: birdhouse, north wall, third nail. His father had built that birdhouse. His father had built half the hiding places on this land. The dead kept housekeeping here still; you only had to know where they'd left the keys.

Fourth — and this was mine, and I made it short because short things hold — I wrote out instructions and sealed them and gave them to Prue. "If I'm convicted, open it. If I'm transported — they won't transport a woman for this, but if they do — open it. If I die, open it, and give the middle page to the historian in Truro, the one who came in November. He's a careful man. Careful men are worth ten brave ones."

"What's the middle page?" Ollie asked.

"A list," I said. "Of names. Everyone that boat lost, and everyone the orchard saved, side by side. It's the whole argument in one page. Everything else is just proof of it."

He nodded solemnly, the way he did, as though I'd handed him a physical object to store in some interior shed of his own.

* * *

I packed that night the way I pack everything, which is to say I measured my life in portable things.

The typewriter stayed. It weighed more than conscience and it was staying anyway — it had logged storms on a rock in the Atlantic and it could log a trial from a farmhouse. Two spare lenses' worth of fittings stayed, wrapped in Elias's coat, because glass waits better than people do. What went into the market basket: apples, honest ones, a round of Prue's bread, the oilcloth packet, and beneath it all, folded small, one letter — Elias's own, the one where he told his father he knew about the boats and chose the light regardless. Not evidence. Ballast. Some things you carry not because the case needs them but because *you* do.

I slept maybe two hours. In the gray before dawn I stood at the office window and looked down the orchard rows, bare and pruned now, our winter work visible at last — the old trees opened up, the new grafts bedded in along the south row with Elias's letters tucked beneath them like roots of paper. Somewhere past the hedge, past the parish line, past all the lines men drew, the country was deciding how this story would end. I intended to have a word in that decision.

Tom walked me to the gate with the basket on his arm, refusing to be talked out of it, ribs and all.

"You come back," he said. Not a question. Tom had learned imperatives from Prue.

"I fully intend to annoy a courtroom and come home to bad porridge."

"Good." He handed me the basket. Then, quieter, younger, the twenty-year-old showing through the keeper's front: "When they read it out — the ledger, someday, somewhere — will my name be in it? For the cove?"

"Every name goes in," I said. "That's the entire point of a record. The ones they tried to erase get the largest type."

He grinned with the unbruised half of his mouth and went back up the path, and I turned down the lane toward the market and the parish line and whatever waited past it, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who intend to return as the same person who left, and I already suspected I wouldn't.

The mist was burning off. The apples rode heavy in the basket over the packet, over the truth, over everything.

At the crossroads, where the coast road bent toward the port town, a trap was waiting — horse, driver, and a gentleman in a good coat consulting a pocket watch, as though the appointment were mine and I were late.

Magistrate Halloway raised his hat.
