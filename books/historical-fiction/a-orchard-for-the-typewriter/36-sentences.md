## 36. Sentences

They gave Fen a chair, which surprised him more than the sentence did.

He had expected to stand through it — standing was what you did when you expected the worst. He'd stood at his court-martial in a room in Plymouth that smelled of wet wool and ink, stood while they read out the charge of desertion, stood while they took his cap badge off the table and didn't give it back. Four years of standing. This morning he'd walked into the county courtroom at Bodmin on legs that felt borrowed, surrendered himself at the door like a man handing over a parcel, and now a constable was setting a wooden chair behind him as if he were an old uncle at a christening.

"Sit down, Mr. Tresidder," said the magistrate pro tem — not Halloway, thank God; Halloway's name had been struck from the commission list three weeks ago, along with his reputation, and there were men in Truro still trying to work out how to say *disgraced* about someone who'd shaken their hands at hunt balls. "This is not a trial. It is a review."

A review. Fen sat.

Xanthe was in the front row with Prue on one side and Ollie pressed close against her skirts on the other. Tom Wrenn sat apart, near the aisle, because his ribs still wouldn't let him sit against anything solid for long. His face had gone from purple to green to a mottled yellow that made him look like something ripening, and every few minutes Ollie would turn around to check on him, the way you check a kettle.

Fen kept his eyes forward. If he looked at them too long he would start believing things, and believing things before the verdict came was how a man got broken twice.

The officer conducting the review wore a captain's rings but spoke like a clerk, which Fen had learned over four years of war was either the best sign or the worst. The Admiralty historian — Merrick, thin as a church candle, spectacles that caught the light so you couldn't see his eyes — sat at a folding table stacked with papers Fen knew intimately. The true ledger. The certified copy Xanthe had typed page by page through one whole night while the rest of them slept badly in the farmhouse. The depositions. His own statement, signed, which could have hanged him and hadn't yet.

"The Crown," the captain read, "having reviewed the circumstances of the deserter Fen Tresidder, late Able Seaman, Royal Naval Reserve, finds: first, that the order refused by the said Tresidder on the eleventh of March, 1917, concerned convoy routing subsequently shown by evidence to have been compromised; second, that refusal of the said order likely preserved the lives of his boat's crew; third, that his subsequent flight was undertaken under reasonable apprehension of unlawful proceedings."

He paused. Somewhere behind Fen a woman coughed.

"The sentence of death pronounced in absentia is quashed. In its place: imprisonment, suspended. Six months' detention in HM Prison Bodmin, wholly suspended upon condition of good conduct and residence at a fixed address." Another pause, longer. "Discharge from the Royal Naval Reserve will be issued. Character reference: to be entered as *good*."

*Good.* One word after four years. Fen heard it land somewhere far away, like a stone dropped down a well.

The captain looked up over his papers. "Mr. Tresidder. Do you understand what has been said?"

"Yes, sir."

"Do you wish to say anything?"

Every speech he'd rehearsed at two in the morning in the loft above the cider press went out of him at once. What came out instead was plain as bread. "I'd like it recorded that I never fired on a British ship. And that the men who drowned in the cove in November 1916 were doing what I did. They just didn't get to run far enough."

The captain's pen scratched. "So recorded," he said, and Fen understood that some clerk in London would copy those words into a book, and that the book would outlive everyone in this room. That was the whole strange point of the last six months. That was what the woman in the front row had taught him without ever once preaching it: write it down, date it, sign it, and the truth gets a body that outlives your nerve.

"That concludes the matter of Tresidder," the captain said. "The board will next consider recommendations arising from the Merrick report concerning HMS *Kestrel*, lost with all hands, November 1916—"

But Fen wasn't listening anymore, because Ollie had turned around and grinned at him, wide and gap-toothed, and Prue had put her hand flat against her breastbone the way she did instead of crying, and Xanthe Thackeray was looking at him with an expression he'd only seen her wear once before — the night she'd finished typing the last deposition and set the pages under a paperweight and said, quietly, *there, now it exists.*

It existed. He existed, officially, again. A man with a discharge paper and a suspended sentence and a fixed address, and the fixed address was an orchard going wild above a cove where men had drowned, and he found he couldn't think of anywhere in England he'd rather be bound to by law.

* * *

Outside, the November cold hit like a bucket of seawater, clean and honest after the stove-warmed courtroom. The town square was full of armistice bunting that nobody had taken down since the signing — red-white-and-blue drooping in the drizzle, gone grey at the edges, looking less like celebration now than laundry left out too long.

Tom fell into step beside him, walking carefully, hands in pockets. "Suspended," Tom said, testing the word. "Six months suspended. You know what that means?"

"It means if I steal so much as an apple off my own tree they'll come for me."

"You've got your own trees now, then." Tom grinned, winced, grinned anyway. "Listen to you. Fixed address. Character reference *good*. My mum would faint."

"Where is your mum?" Fen asked, because he'd never asked, because asking questions about home was a luxury deserters learned to do without.

"Penzance way. When this all settles — when your paper comes proper in the post — I'll write to her." Tom said it lightly, but his ears went red. "Tell her I've got employment. Keeper's assistant, private beacon, Cornish coast. It sounds grander than it is."

"It sounds exactly as grand as it is," said Prue, coming up on his other side with Ollie's mittened hand in hers. "You'll be polishing glass till your fingerprints wear off, boy, same as every keeper since lights were lit. It's honest work and it keeps you from thinking too much, and there's no higher praise I can give any occupation."

Ollie tugged Prue's hand and pointed across the square. A knot of townspeople had gathered outside the baker's, and they weren't looking at bunting. They were looking at the courthouse steps, at the five of them coming down into the rain, and their faces were doing something complicated — not hostility, not welcome, something being worked out in real time, like weather.

An old man in a fisherman's jersey stepped out ahead of the others. Fen knew the face from the port town, from the days of hunting a printer who'd take the job: one of the ones who'd refused, who'd looked at the floor and said *I've a family, mister* in a voice like an apology. He stopped three paces away and took off his hat.

"Name's Curnow," he said. "Had two brothers on the *Kestrel*."

Nobody breathed. Rain ticked on the brim of his hat.

"Read the printed thing," Curnow said. "Bought it off the widow's table, penny each. Read it twice." He looked at Fen, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once. "You're the one gave testimony about the convoy orders."

"I am."

"Then—" He stopped. Started again, angrier at himself for stopping. "Then somebody ought to shake your hand, and it might as well be me, because forty years I've been told my brothers were drunk and off their course, and you've written down that they were murdered by a magistrate's signature, and—" His voice cracked clean through. He put his hand out anyway.

Fen took it. The grip hurt. He held on.

Behind Curnow, the crowd shifted, and one or two hats came off, and a woman Fen didn't know said something to her son and pointed — not at Fen, he realized, but at Xanthe, at the tall woman in the grey coat standing very straight in the rain with her chin up and her jaw set like she was holding the whole square up by willpower alone.

"They're saying the review board's reopening the *Kestrel* findings," Curnow said gruffly, recovering, cramming his hat back on. "Merrick's report. Names in it. There's talk of compensation for the families. Talk of—" he lowered his voice, though half the square was leaning in — "talk of digging up what happened to the bodies. Whether any came ashore and were buried quiet, unmarked, by folk who were scared."

Prue made a small sound. Fen saw Xanthe's hand find the old gardener's arm.

"Some of them came ashore," Prue said, clear enough for the whole square. "Three of them. I buried them myself, January of '17, above the tide line where the ground's soft. No stones. I marked the spot with a thorn tree, and I can walk you to it blindfolded, and I'm seventy-one years old and I am *done* with being scared."

The silence afterward had weight, like snowfall. Then Curnow nodded, once, hard, and turned back to his neighbors, and Fen heard the words *thorn tree* pass through the crowd like a coin changing hands, and understood that something had shifted in the town's ledger too — that accounts were being reopened everywhere, in kitchens and chapels and fish cellars, and that he and his people had started it with a typewriter and a padlocked shed and sheer bloody stubbornness.

* * *

The drive back was long and wet and nobody talked much until the road climbed away from the coast and the hedges closed in, and then Prue said, from the back seat beside Ollie, "You'll want to see the post when we're home."

Fen turned. "Post? I've never had post in my life."

"There's a first time for everything." She was smiling in the way she did when she knew more than she'd say outright, which was most ways. "Big brown envelope. Government stamp. Came yesterday. I put it on the kitchen table and I put the paperweight on top of it, and then I moved the paperweight, because it seemed wrong to weigh a man's future down before he'd even been sentenced."

"What's in it?"

"If I knew that, boy, I'd be running the Admiralty instead of a cider press." She settled back. "Open it at the table. That's where good news and bad news both belong — at the table, with tea, where you can't make it worse than it is."

* * *

The envelope held three documents.

The first was the discharge itself, printed on thick cream stock, his name spelled correctly — TRESIDDER, FENWICK J., Able Seaman, RNR — with *DISCHARGED* stamped across it in violet ink and a character entry that said, in a clerk's careful copperplate, *Good*. He read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into a trick. He set it down and found his hands weren't steady, and didn't care who saw.

The second was the formal notice of the suspended sentence, conditions enumerated, penalties warned. He skimmed it. Paper could threaten him all it liked now; it had given him back his name on the same page.

The third was a single sheet, typed, unsigned except by its letterhead — the Admiralty, Historical Section — and it took him a moment to understand why it had come to *him* and not to Xanthe.

*Re: HMS Kestrel, Court of Inquiry reopened pursuant to the Merrick Report. You are requested to attend a further hearing at Devonport on the fourth of December to give evidence touching convoy orders of March 1917. Your expenses will be met. Be advised that the Board has resolved to treat the loss of the said vessel as resulting from enemy action facilitated by the unauthorized disclosure of routing instructions, and to revise the official record accordingly.*

*Be advised further:* — and here the clerk's neutrality strained, like rope under load — *that the Board has received correspondence from surviving family of the crew requesting that any remains interred in unmarked graves on the mainland be identified, exhumed with due dignity, and reinterred in recognized burial grounds with full honours. Your assistance in locating such graves may be sought.*

Fen read it aloud at the table, because that was the rule now, the household's rule: nothing important stayed silent. He read it while Prue's hands went still around her teacup and Ollie leaned his elbows into the flour and Tom forgot to wince and Xanthe listened with her head tilted, already composing, already dating the moment in her mind the way she dated everything — *fourth day of the review, afternoon, rain clearing* — filing it into the record that had become, somehow, the spine of all their lives.

When he finished, the kitchen was quiet except for the clock.

"They want me to show them where Prue buried them," he said. "The three that came ashore." He looked at her. "Under the thorn tree."

Prue set her cup down with a click. "I buried five," she said. "There were two more, washed up past the point in February, that I never told a soul about, because telling meant questions, and questions meant Halloway. Forty years I've kept that ground like it was planted." Her chin came up. "Well. It's planted now, isn't it. We'll dig proper graves. Cut proper stones. And every name goes in the record, and every name goes on the stone, and if the Admiralty wants to send an honour guard to an orchard in Cornwall, they can stand in the mud like everyone else."

Xanthe had risen and gone to the typewriter on its table by the window — the old machine, Elias's machine, the one that had logged ten thousand storms and was now logging justice, page by dated page. She rolled a fresh sheet in and began to type, and the sound filled the kitchen like rain on a roof: steady, patient, unstoppable.

"Fourth of December, Devonport," she said over the clatter. "We'll all go. Every one of us. You won't stand in front of that board alone, Fen — that's not how this house does things." She glanced back at him, and the lamplight caught the line of her jaw, and for a second she looked so much like a keeper scanning a horizon that he almost laughed. "And afterwards, when the ground's soft enough — we plant."

Fen looked at the discharge paper lying on the scrubbed pine, at the violet stamp and the copperplate *Good*, and thought about seeds. About what Prue had been saying all autumn with her hands in the dirt: that an orchard isn't an inheritance you receive, it's a debt you pay forward, row by row, to people you'll never meet.

The fourth of December was three weeks off. The frosts were coming, and after the frosts, the pruning, and after the pruning—

Prue was already at the dresser, pulling out the seed drawer, counting packets under her breath.
