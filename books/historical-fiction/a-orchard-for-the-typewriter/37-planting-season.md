## 37. Planting Season

Forty years I'd pruned these trees, and I'd never once done it in February. The old rule was March, when the sap woke and the wounds closed quick. But the old rules had gone into the ground with the Thackerays, one after another, and the trees didn't care what the calendar said. They cared about light and air and a clean cut. Everything else was superstition dressed as tradition, and I'd carried enough of that on my back to last two lifetimes.

So when the frost lifted enough for the spade to go in without ringing like a bell, we started.

I was up before anyone, same as always, with the kettle on and my hands wrapped round the first cup while the window fogged over. Out past the glass the orchard lay grey and dripping, the rows running crooked down the slope like a drunk walking home. Fifty years of neglect will do that to an orchard. The trees had grown wild and tall, reaching for each other across the gaps where their neighbours should've been, all whippy top-growth and dead wood in the shade. Nobody had pruned them since old Mr. Thackeray's time — *my* Mr. Thackeray, the first one, before the sons scattered and the letters went undelivered and the whole family story curdled into something nobody said aloud.

Now there was a new story typed out fair and square, forty-odd pages of it, sworn and printed and sold at sixpence in the port town. The trial was done. Halloway was finished — resigned his magistracy before they could take it off him, though the papers made it sound like he'd chosen retirement rather than been run out on a rail. Fen had his discharge paper folded in his breast pocket like a love letter. And Xanthe Thackeray, keeper, was sleeping upstairs in the room she'd claimed in October, with the typewriter case under her bed and her boots by the door, always by the door, like she might need to leave at any moment.

She wouldn't leave now. I could see it in how she'd started hanging things up.

* * *

The household assembled slow that morning, the way households do when there's no gale blowing them together. Tom came down first, his ribs still knitted but his grin fully healed, carrying the ladders off the wall of the barn where they'd hung since before the war. He'd oiled every hinge and joint the week before, unprompted, the way he did everything — quietly, thoroughly, and then pretending it was nothing.

"Morning, Prue." He set the tallest ladder against the doorframe and stood it there like a sentry. "Fen says the ground's workable past the third row if we go careful."

"Fen's been reading books about orchards," I said. "Fen's never pulled a sucker off a rootstock in his life."

"He reads fast, though."

They all read fast now. That was the strange thing about this winter. I'd spent forty years in this house mostly alone with my own company and the radio of my own thoughts, and now there were books everywhere — Ollie sounding out words by the fire, Fen going through Elias's agricultural journals page by page, Xanthe typing late into the night with the lamp turned low. The house had become a school where nobody was the master and everybody was learning.

Ollie came down next, hair flat on one side, and went straight to the window to look at the trees. That boy watched the orchard like other children watch the sea. His father had worked here before him — before the boat was lost, before everything — and some part of me believed the trees remembered him and were waiting for the boy to grow into the knowing of it.

"Which ones first?" Ollie asked. He spoke more freely now, but still carefully, like each sentence was a stone he was testing before stepping on it.

"The dead wood," I said. "You can always tell. Scratch the bark with your thumbnail. Green underneath, it's living. Brown and dry, it's finished its work."

"Like people."

"Some people." I gave his shoulder a squeeze. "Most hang on longer than you'd think."

* * *

Xanthe came out mid-morning wearing Elias's coat, which she'd finally taken from its hook in the estate office and had let out at the shoulders by the seamstress in the village. It fit her now the way grief fits eventually — not comfortably, but properly, with room to move inside it.

We worked the upper rows first, she and I, while Fen and Tom took the lower ground with the saws. The rule was simple: anything crossing the centre, out. Anything growing inward, out. Anything dead, diseased, or crowding, out. An orchard is not a wilderness; it's an argument between human intention and tree nature, renewed every year or lost forever.

"You're cutting harder than I would," Xanthe said, watching me take a whole limb off a Cox that had been strangling itself for a decade.

"I'm cutting like someone who means to still be here in five years."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Show me."

I put the saw in her hands and moved behind her, guiding the angle the way I'd once guided young Mr. Thackeray — the father-in-law, the one whose brother drowned, the one who burned the letter naming an heir because he couldn't bear to read what it might say. Different hands, these. Steadier. She cut where I told her and then, when I stepped back, she cut once more where I hadn't told her, and it was the right place, and I said nothing about it because saying something would have spoiled it.

By noon we had three barrowloads of brush and the rows were starting to look like rows again instead of suggestions. Ollie ran the barrows. Tom climbed and sawed and dropped limbs exactly where Fen asked him to drop them, which was a small miracle of trust between two men who'd met as strangers in autumn and now moved around each other like parts of one machine.

At dinner — bread and cheese and pickled onions, eaten standing at the kitchen counter because nobody wanted to stop — Fen raised the question that had been sitting in the room all morning.

"The new stock arrived at Penzance yesterday. I checked the telegram. Forty rootstocks, dormant, ready for planting."

"Where do we put them?" Tom asked.

"That's the thing." Fen looked at Xanthe. "There's the old rows, the gaps where trees died. Or—" He stopped, and I knew what was coming because I'd thought about it myself in the dark hours. "Or we start fresh. New lines. New layout. The old orchard was planted to a plan nobody alive remembers."

"There's a plan," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"Not written. In my head. Forty years of walking these rows, and I know where every tree stood and why. My grandmother's mother helped plant the original — before the Thackerays even bought the land, if you want the truth of it, though no Thackeray ever asked me that." I set down my knife. "But plans are for the planter. You're the heir. You decide."

Xanthe put down her bread. She looked tired in a good way, the way you look after crying or after finishing something long.

"I don't want to restore it," she said slowly. "I've been thinking about this for weeks. Restoring it would mean pretending nothing happened — putting the trees back where the Thackerays put them, growing the fruit they grew, keeping the shape of a life that ended badly for everyone in it." She glanced at Ollie. "And it would mean the orchard belongs to the past. To the dead. To the name."

"It does belong to the name," I said. "That's the trouble with land."

"Then we change what the name carries." She was standing now, moving to the window, looking out at the bare rows. "Elias kept the light because he believed a light wasn't owned, it was *kept*. For whoever needed it. That's what I want here. Not a Thackeray orchard. A kept orchard."

"A kept orchard," Fen repeated, tasting it.

"We plant where the ground wants the trees, not where the plan says. We plant varieties that'll feed people through winter, not just look pretty in a catalogue. And—" she hesitated, then went on, "—we mark every row with something true."

"Something true," I echoed.

"Elias's letters. The ones to his father. There are dozens of them, unsent, undelivered, in those crates from the potting shed. Pages and pages of a man talking to a father who never answered." Her voice caught slightly and steadied itself. "I can't send them now. But I can't burn them either. So we seal them in wax paper and tin and bury them at the head of each row. A letter per tree. When we dig to plant, we dig past them. Every year, pruning, feeding, harvesting — we're working above his words. Above what he knew and chose anyway."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Outside, a blackbird was working the hedge, patient as a clock.

Tom cleared his throat. "It's a bit like a grave, isn't it?"

"No," Ollie said, from the corner. He said it firmly, the way he said the few things that mattered most. "It's like planting seeds."

* * *

We started the next morning, and I'll tell you honestly that my hands shook doing it, though not from cold.

The letters came out of the typewriter room in bundles tied with string, each one in Elias's careful hand — and it was a careful hand, I'll grant him that; the man wrote like he trimmed wicks, precise and unhurried. Xanthe had sorted them already, dated them, read every one twice. She wouldn't say what was in most of them beyond the broad truth we all knew now: that Elias had learned the family secret young, that he'd written to his father asking questions and getting silence, that he'd chosen the light over the land because a light tells the truth about itself.

We wrapped each letter in waxed paper, then tucked it into a preserved-meat tin, then sealed the lid with candle wax dripped thick along the seams. Ollie pressed his thumb into each seal while it was soft — his idea, and a good one, a signature of sorts, proof that a child had blessed the work.

Forty trees. Forty tins. We buried the first one at the head of the lowest row, where the ground sloped toward the sea, and dug the hole deep enough that no spade would turn it up by accident but shallow enough that a determined hand with a map could find it. Then we set the rootstock in above it, teased out the roots, and filled the earth back round it, treading it firm.

Xanthe knelt and pressed both palms flat on the soil, just for a second, the way you'd steady a book you were reading in wind.

"What variety?" Tom asked, holding up the label.

"Dawn," I said. "An early eater. First apple of the year, ripens before anything else. Seemed right for the first row."

Fen wrote it in the ledger — the estate ledger, the honest one now, the one with nothing razored out of it. *Row one. Dawn. Letter 1–4, sealed.* He'd taken over the record-keeping naturally, the way he took over everything quiet, and Xanthe let him because she understood that a record kept by four hands is harder to lose than a record kept by one.

By the end of the week we had twelve trees in. The work was slower than any of us expected, because every hole turned up something — a horseshoe, a clay pipe, a child's marble, once a rusted key that made us all stop and look at each other before Ollie said, "Not that one," and we left it in the ground where it lay. The orchard had its own archive. We were only adding a layer.

On the twelfth day it rained, hard, the kind of Cornish rain that comes straight sideways off the sea, and we worked anyway because the rootstocks were heeled in and waiting and trees don't wait for weather. Xanthe stood in it with her hood back, mud to the elbows, laughing at something Tom shouted from the far row, and I looked at her and thought: *there*. There was the woman who'd come up my drive in October with a solicitor's letter and a face like a locked door, measuring her life in portable things, ready to carry away whatever could be carried.

She wasn't measuring anymore. She was planting.

* * *

The last tin went into the ground on a bright cold morning with the light coming gold through the bare branches. Row eight, the highest, overlooking the whole slope and the sea beyond it. Xanthe chose the letter herself — I didn't ask which one, and she didn't say, but I saw her hold it a moment longer than the others before wrapping it, and I saw her lips move once, reading something silently, before she folded it away.

We set the tree — a Kerns, a keeper's apple, stores till spring — and firmed the soil, and stood back.

Eight rows. Thirty-eight trees in the ground and two more to come when the nursery sent the replacements. Below us the older trees, pruned hard, already showing the fat buds that meant a blossom worth having. Above us nothing but sky and the long grey line of the water.

Fen read the entry aloud from the ledger. Ollie added his thumbprint to the page beside the writing, ink this time instead of wax, solemn as a magistrate. Tom leaned on his spade and grinned at all of us like a man who'd found something he hadn't known he was looking for.

And Xanthe — Xanthe stood at the head of the last row with the wind pulling at Elias's coat, and she said, quietly, not to any of us particularly:

"He kept the light. We keep the trees. Same work, really."

It was, I thought, the truest thing anyone had said on this land in fifty years. The Thackerays had *owned* this orchard, and owning had got them secrets and drownings and a burned letter and a magistrate's corruption running through it like blight through a trunk. Keeping was different. Keeping meant you answered to the thing itself — the light, the trees, the record — and not to your own reflection in it.

We walked back down through the rows together, leaving our bootprints in the fresh-turned earth, forty little letters lying dark and dry beneath the surface with a dead man's voice in every one of them.

I was last through the gate, as I always was, and I paused there and looked back at the orchard — the old trees and the new, the pruned and the planted, all of it mine to tend for whatever years I had left, none of it mine at all.

Inside, the kettle was going and voices were rising in the kitchen, arguing cheerfully about whether the new rows should have names as well as numbers. Xanthe was calling for suggestions. Ollie had proposed one already — something about boats, I'd have wagered.

I hung my coat on its peg and stood a moment in the hall, listening to a house full of people, and thought how strange and ordinary it sounded, and how long it had taken to get here.

Then I went in, and shut the door behind me, and the spring came on.
