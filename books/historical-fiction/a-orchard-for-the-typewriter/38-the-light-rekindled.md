## 38. The Light Rekindled

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing and everything, because Tuesdays were the days the post boat ran and every important thing in my life had arrived by water.

I read it standing in the estate office doorway, one shoulder against the frame where the fire had blackened the stone. Prue was at the table behind me, sorting what could be salvaged from the ashes — not much, a drawer of brass fittings, half a ledger stand, a paperweight Elias's father had owned that I suspected Prue had always hated. Tom was out with Ollie somewhere along the cove path. Fen had gone to the port town with the widow Mrs. Cade to argue about type sizes for the fourth time.

The letter was from the Board of Trade, or from someone writing on their behalf, and it took me three readings to believe it.

*Madam,* it began, in the bloodless hand of government, *we have considered your petition regarding the disposition of the decommissioned station at — * and there it was, the island's name, the coordinates I could recite in my sleep, the height of the tower above mean high water as though the sea might forget if no one reminded it.

They were granting it to me.

Not selling. Not leasing in any sense that involved rent I could pay. Granting — *for the maintenance of a private beacon, subject to inspection, the light to be shown at the discretion of the keeper thereof.* Someone in an office had decided that a decommissioned lighthouse in private hands was cheaper than dynamite, and that the widow who had kept it through the worst gales of the war might as well keep it forever. Perhaps my testimony before the historian had reached further than the courtroom. Perhaps someone with a stamp had simply wanted the paperwork finished before the year turned.

It didn't matter why. What mattered was the word *keeper*, printed twice, applied to me, official, unrevoked.

"Prue," I said. My voice came out strange, so I tried again. "Prue. They've given me the light."

She looked up from the brass fittings, and I watched her understand — really understand, because she had climbed those stairs herself in the years when she'd carried provisions out to Elias and his father, back when the Thackerays still spoke. Her hands went still on the table.

"Well," she said. Then, because Prue did not waste words on things that mattered: "About time somebody gave you something instead of taking it."

* * *

We went out on the first calm Saturday in November. Calm by our standards, which is to say the wind was under twenty knots and the swell under eight feet, and the fisherman who agreed to carry us charged double and apologized for it while doing it.

Tom stood at the bow the whole crossing, coat whipping, grinning like a man going to a fair. He had been unbearable all week — measuring things, counting things, making lists of lamp parts on scraps of paper he kept losing and rewriting. When the naval requisition had come through in the summer, when they'd carried away everything portable and told me the rest would be scrapped, I had thought that chapter of my life closed and sealed. Tom had never believed it. "Glass doesn't rot," he'd said, over and over, like a creed. "Steel doesn't rot. They can take the oil and the stores but the lens is bolted to its pedestal and nobody's carrying that down ninety steps."

He was right. Of course he was right; he was nineteen and right the way only nineteen-year-olds are, absolutely and without the mercy of doubt.

The island rose out of the grey water the way it always had — dark rock, white tower, the ruins of the keepers' cottages hunched below it like old men sheltering from weather. My chest did something complicated at the sight of it. Grief has layers, I've learned. There is the grief of loss, and beneath it the grief of return, and beneath that something else entirely, a kind of recognition that has no name in English: the feeling of coming back to a place that has been keeping your shape while you were gone.

The landing was wet and awkward. Ollie went up the ladder first, quick as a gull, then Tom, then me, then Prue last with her skirts gathered and her mouth set against the spray. She hadn't wanted to come. She'd come anyway. That was Prue entire.

The tower door was padlocked — the Navy had done that much — but the padlock was cheap and Tom had borrowed cutters from the fisherman without asking me first, which told me how long he'd been planning this. The lock parted with a snap that echoed off the rock.

Inside, the stairs smelled of salt and disuse and, faintly, of oil, as though the building itself remembered what it was for. Ninety-one steps. I counted them the way I had counted them ten thousand times, and on the seventy-fourth I stopped, because that was where Elias had scratched a mark into the plaster one winter — a small cross, nothing more, made the day we'd heard his brother's boat was lost. I put my fingers on it. The plaster was cold. It had waited four years for a hand that wasn't coming, and here was mine instead, close enough.

"Mrs. Thackeray?" Tom called down. "You coming? Or are we doing this at your pace?"

"At my pace," I said, and climbed.

* * *

The lantern room had been stripped of anything the Navy valued — the clockwork was gone, the fuel system was gone, the emergency apparatus was gone — but the lens remained. Of course it remained. It weighed more than a horse. It sat on its pedestal in the middle of the room like a captive queen, curtained in canvas some fitter had thrown over it, and when Tom dragged the canvas away the whole room filled with light.

Even dirty. Even filmed with salt and gull filth and two years of neglect, the glass threw light around that chamber as though it had been waiting, patiently, for someone to let it work again. Prisms within prisms, panels within panels, the whole ring of it — a machine for gathering a single flame and bending it into a beam that could reach eleven miles over black water. Elias had polished it every week of his working life. He used to say a lens was honest in a way people weren't: it showed you exactly what it was, and it did exactly what it was made for, and it never pretended otherwise.

"He'd have hated this," Tom said softly, running a finger along the frame without touching the glass. "The mess of it."

"He'd have started cleaning before he finished complaining." I pulled off my gloves. "Fetch the kit."

Tom had brought the kit. Of course he had — a crate packed with chamois leather and methylated spirit and soft brushes and the little brass tools for adjusting the panels, most of them salvaged from the estate office fire or bought secondhand in the port town with money Mrs. Cade had advanced against book sales. We worked through the morning, panel by panel, Tom lifting each prism element free while I cleaned both faces and he reseated it true, calling out the alignment marks the way Elias had taught him in the months before the war took the relief keepers away. Tom had been fourteen then, apprenticed, too young for service, too useful to dismiss. He had learned the light the way I had learned it — from a man who believed that tending a flame for strangers you'd never meet was the whole of religion.

By midday every panel was clean. By two o'clock the sun came through a break in the cloud and struck the lens full, and the room blazed — white spokes wheeling across the walls, across us, across the gulls hanging outside the gallery rail. Ollie stood in the middle of it with his arms out, turning slowly, letting the light pass through him, laughing without sound. Even now, even with his voice returned, Ollie laughed mostly without sound, as though sound were still rationed. But he laughed.

Prue had brought bread and hard cheese and a bottle of cider, and we ate sitting on the gallery under the tower, looking back toward the mainland, where the orchard country lay hidden behind twenty miles of moor. It was strange to see it from out here. From the island, Cornwall is a low dark line, unremarkable, easy to dismiss — and yet everything that mattered to me now was behind that line. I had spent years thinking of the mainland as elsewhere, as the place where life happened to other people. I had been wrong about that. Life had happened to me on both sides of the water; I'd simply been keeping different kinds of watch.

"They'll inspect it, you know," Prue said, around a crust. "Somebody from the Board, poking about, wanting everything shipshape."

"Let them come," I said. "I'll show them a logbook they won't forget."

Tom choked on his cider.

* * *

We stayed until dusk. That was the plan from the beginning — Tom and I, anyway; Prue and Ollie took the afternoon boat back with the fisherman, Prue declaring that she was too old to sleep on stone floors and Ollie going willingly enough once Tom promised to bring him something from the tower. Which left the two of us, and one task remaining.

The Navy had taken the lamp — the actual burner assembly — as scrap, and Tom had spent a month tracking it through a salvage yard in Falmouth and buying it back for eleven shillings and a promise. It sat in its crate now between us, and we lifted it out together and set it on the pedestal inside the lens, and Tom fitted the connections he'd rebuilt from memory and parts, and then there was nothing left to do but light it.

There was no oil system anymore. Tom had solved that with paraffin in a gravity feed, crude and temporary, good enough for a private beacon shown at the discretion of the keeper thereof. He primed it. He looked at me.

"You do it," he said. "You're the keeper. I'm just the man what carries things."

I struck the match. My hand was steady. Four years ago I had lit this lamp beside Elias for the last time, and I had thought — I had genuinely believed — that the part of me which knew how to do this had died with him, or been buried with the requisition papers, or at least gone dormant like the orchard rows, waiting for spring that might never be authorized.

The wick caught. The flame stood up straight in the draft, and the lens gathered it, bent it, threw it — a single white blade sweeping out across the November sea, once every ten seconds, steady as scripture.

Tom whooped like a boy, which he was. I didn't whoop. I stood at the gallery rail and watched the beam go out over the water and come round again, and I thought: *there*. *That is a thing I have made continue.* In a year when everything else had burned or been confiscated or buried in court records, this one flame was mine, authorized, lawful, shining.

"It should have a name," Tom said beside me. "Private beacons get names, don't they? On the charts?"

"Do they?"

"Dunno. They should." He squinted out at the dark. "Elias's Light," he offered, then shook his head. "No. Too much like a memorial. This isn't a memorial."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Memorials look backward. This light looked outward, and what it illuminated changed every time it turned — water, then rock, then water, then the far faint line of the coast. A light doesn't choose what it shows. It just refuses to stop showing.

"We'll call it nothing," I said. "Or we'll call it whatever the chart calls it. Names on charts are for strangers. We know where we are."

* * *

We came off the island two days later, after I had written the first entry in the new logbook — a proper logbook, bought in the port town, ruled in my own hand:

*Station: [island], private beacon, granted by Board of Trade. Keeper: X. Thackeray. Weather: clearing after gale, wind NW moderating. Lamp: lit 17:42, character fixed, visible approx. 9 miles. Remarks: Lens reinstalled and aligned by T. Wrenn, assistant. All panels true.*

*Remarks continued:* I sat with the pen above the page for a long while that night, in the cottage at the orchard with the wind worrying the eaves, trying to decide whether a logbook could hold remarks of a certain kind. Elias's logs had been strictly factual — ships, weather, visitors, stores. Mine had loosened over the years; grief will do that; I had begun recording things like *gull killed against gallery rail, buried below* because it seemed wrong that anything on that rock should go unwitnessed. So I wrote:

*Remarks continued: The household visited. The light was relit. It was found that everything necessary had survived — the glass, the stair, the mark at step seventy-four. Conclusion: some things cannot be requisitioned, decommissioned, or burned. Filed for future reference.*

Then, because the log demanded it: *All well.*

* * *

Fen met us at the gate when we walked up from the cove road, and I knew before he spoke that something had happened, because Fen never waited at gates; Fen moved through places like a man expecting to be asked to move along.

"The printer's done," he said. "Mrs. Cade sent word ahead. Five hundred copies. She says the binding is ugly but the type is clean and Halloway can't stop it now — it's past correction, it's distributed, she sent bundles to Truro and Plymouth and London the moment the ink dried." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — fear, yes, but underneath it a fierce and unfamiliar pride. "There's a copy inside for you. On your desk. She inscribed it."

I walked up the path through the bare apple rows, past the stakes and the grafting wax and the young trees Prue had ordered from the nursery, into the house, to the estate office that no longer smelled of smoke, and there it was. A slim volume, badly bound in grey boards, the title set in plain type with no ornament and no apology:

*The Thackeray Record: Being a True Account of the Orchard Station, Its Ledgers, Its Lost Boat, and Its People. As sworn and attested.*

And beneath it, smaller, the line Mrs. Cade had added without asking and had gotten exactly right:

*Kept by Xanthe Thackeray, keeper of the light.*

I stood holding it a long time. Outside, Tom was showing Ollie how the grafting wax worked, and Prue was shouting at both of them about bruising the bark, and the ordinary noise of a household rose up through the floorboards — arguing, laughter, Ollie's rare soundless laugh breaking through it all. Five hundred copies, moving through the world like seeds on wind. Tomorrow the reviews would begin, or the lawsuits, or both. Halloway would see it. London would see it. Whatever came next would come fast.

But tonight the light on the island was burning, and the record was loose in the world, and I opened the book to the first page and began to read my own name as though it belonged to someone I was finally ready to meet.
