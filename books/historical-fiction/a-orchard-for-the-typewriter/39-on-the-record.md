## 39. On the Record

The parcel came up from the port town on the morning boat, wrapped in sacking and tied with the kind of string that frays if you look at it wrong. Tom carried it up the cliff path on his shoulder, favoring his ribs the way he still did when he forgot himself, and set it down in the kitchen with a thump that made Ollie look up from his slate.

"That's it, then," Tom said. He was breathing hard and pretending he wasn't.

Prue came to stand beside me at the table. Fen stayed by the window, arms folded, doing his careful not-looking, which was how I had learned to read him looking very hard indeed.

I cut the string with Elias's penknife.

Inside were forty copies of *A True Account of the Loss of the* Merrow *and the Betrayal of the Thackeray Smuggling Ring, As Sworn and Typed by Xanthe Thackeray, Keeper*. Forty copies. The widow — Mrs. Tregose, who had a press in the back of her late husband's chandler's shop and no patience left for powerful men — had printed two hundred, but she'd sent me forty first because, she wrote on a slip of paper tucked into the sacking, "you'll want to see your own face before the town does."

She was right. I did want to see it.

I lifted the top copy out and held it the way I used to hold the spare lens parts: two-handed, close to my chest, aware of its weight and of how little weight there actually was. Forty-eight pages. Cheap paper, ink that would smear if you wept on it. The typewriter's face was all through it, every letter slightly imperfect in the way that machine had always been, the lowercase e's with their chipped serif like a tooth knocked crooked. Anyone who had ever seen my logs would know them anywhere.

"It smells like a proper book," Ollie said. He said little enough that when he did say a thing, we all stopped for it. He put his nose down into the stack and breathed in, and Prue laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had gotten out before she could stop it.

"Smells like ink and cheap paper," she said. "Same as the seed catalogues. Same as everything worth having."

I read the first page standing up. Not because I hadn't read it before — I had typed it four times over, sworn it once before the historian and once before the widow's witness, a fisherman named Coad who signed his name with an X and told me an X was as good as anything, "it's what they make us sign on the boats." I read it again anyway, standing, because I wanted to know how it felt in the body rather than the mind.

It felt like the night of the storm in January, the one where I'd sat in the lamp room with the glass rattling in its frames and typed the log in Elias's cadence because mine wouldn't come. It felt like that. Like holding a light steady while everything else moved.

* * *

We took sixty copies down to the port town on the Thursday market boat.

I had argued for selling them at a penny. Mrs. Tregose had argued for twopence, to cover paper. We settled on tuppence with the understanding that anyone who genuinely couldn't pay got one free, and that sailors got theirs free always, because it was their dead in those pages too — the crews of the convoys, the men of the *Merrow*, the deserters who never made the inland road.

Tom set up at the corner by the fish market with a crate turned on its side as a table. I stood behind him. That was the arrangement: he sold, I kept the accounts, because I had learned that a ledger is only as honest as the person who won't meet the buyer's eye, and I intended to be honest in every direction at once.

The town took us in that particular way towns have — sideways, in glances, in conversations that stopped half a beat too long as people passed. Halloway's shadow was still long. He was awaiting trial himself now, remanded to Truro, his patronage evaporated like spray off the breakwater, but a man's shadow doesn't lift just because the man has fallen; it lifts slowly, seasonally, like frost.

The first customer was a woman I didn't know, in a shawl the color of wet slate. She read the title page for a long time. Then she looked up at me and said, "My man was on the *Merrow*."

"I know," I said. "His name is on page nine. I copied it from the true ledger myself."

"He went down as a smuggler," she said. "Official. That's what they gave me. A smuggler, and no pension, and my boys grew up on other people's bread."

"The record says otherwise now," I said. "It says he was carrying wounded men inland and was betrayed for it. It names who was paid and when. It's been entered into evidence, and the historian's report is going to force a review." I hesitated. "It won't bring the pension back. But it may bring the name."

She bought three copies. One for herself, she said, one for her church, and one for her eldest boy, "who's old enough now to ask questions I never had answers for."

By noon the crate was half empty. Tom counted coins with his tongue between his teeth, and I wrote figures in my log book — I kept the account in the same book I kept the weather, because in my experience money behaves much like weather, arriving in systems you didn't order and leaving damage you have to walk out and assess.

A man in a good coat stopped in front of the crate around two o'clock. I knew the type immediately: the Admiralty historian's junior, or someone's clerk, or somebody's cousin twice removed from somebody who mattered. He picked up a copy, read the title page, and smiled the smile of a man about to be reasonable.

"Mrs. Thackeray. There are those who would say this publication is premature. The official inquiry—"

"The official inquiry closed eleven years ago," I said, "with a finding that has now been shown false. Premature would have been publishing it then, before I'd finished checking my figures. I'm a keeper. I don't publish until I've checked my figures."

He blinked. "There will be those who call this sensational."

"There will be those who call it anything they like," I said. "The pages are sworn. The witnesses are named and living and can be questioned. If anyone wishes to dispute a line of it, they may buy a copy for tuppence and write down which line, and I'll answer it in print, same as I answered the weather every night for six years. That's the whole of my method. It's not sensational. It's just that nobody's tried it on land before."

He bought one copy. I suspect it was to take apart. Good. Let him take it apart. Every bolt holds.

* * *

That evening, in the kitchen, Prue cooked something extravagant — a rabbit pie with a lard crust, apples stewed with sugar we couldn't strictly afford, the good candles brought out of hiding. Fen carved the rabbit without being asked, which was his version of a speech. Ollie arranged the apples in a ring around the pie dish with the gravity of a boy laying out a fleet.

One copy of the book lay at the center of the table, uncut pages down, like a centerpiece.

"For the signature," Prue said, when I looked at it. "You've not done it yet. Mrs. Tregose said you'd want to do it yourself, on the copy you keep."

They had all known. Of course they had. A household keeps fewer secrets than a family does, and this household had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around the fire, or the trial, or the night we voted at this same table — I've never fixed exactly where.

I took the pen. I had thought, all the weeks of typing, about what name would go on the title page. There had been a time — I'll admit it here, since the record is meant to hold even the small shames — when I considered signing nothing at all, letting the document stand anonymous like a log entry, keeper unknown. Anonymity is a kind of safety. Ask any deserter.

But anonymity is also a kind of erasure, and I had spent a year learning the difference between a record that protects and a record that hides.

I wrote: *Xanthe Thackeray.*

Then, beneath it, after a pause long enough that Fen stopped carving and Ollie stopped arranging and the clock on the mantel got loud:

*Keeper.*

Not *widow.* Not *heiress.* Not *niece-in-law of a magistrate's client* nor *daughter-in-law of a burned letter.* Those were names other people had given me, names that arrived sealed twice and named things I hadn't chosen. But *keeper* — that I had earned, one gale at a time, one log entry at a time, one polished pane of glass at a time, and then earned again through a winter of testimony and a spring of standing still while the world tried to blow past me.

Ollie leaned over and looked at it upside down, which is how children read, and pronounced judgment: "It's right."

Fen said, quietly, to his plate, "First thing I've signed my own name to in three years. Figured yours ought to be done properly." And I understood that the book had done for him what it had done for me — given him back his name, the one the army had taken and the desertion had buried. His discharge paper was framed above the dresser. Prue had done it. She frames things. It's how she loves.

Tom raised his mug of tea — we'd run dry of cider months ago and nobody mourned it much. "To the keeper," he said.

"To the record," I corrected, and we drank to both.

* * *

Later, alone in the estate office — rebuilt, bare-walled, smelling of new plaster and old apple wood from the shelves Prue salvaged — I sat at the typewriter and did what I do every night, because some rituals survive even their original purpose.

I dated the entry. November the fourth. Wind southwest, moderate, clearing. Then I wrote:

*Two hundred copies printed. Sixty sold or given in port town today. Remainder held against demand, which Mrs. Tregose predicts will be considerable once the London papers get hold of the historian's report. She says she can print another thousand inside a fortnight. I believe her. She has the eyes of a woman who has already survived worse than printers.*

*Note appended for whoever reads this log after me: the record is not finished because the book exists. Records are never finished. They are kept, or they are lost. Tomorrow the review board sits at Falmouth on the* Merrow *crews' status. Tom wants to sail across and watch. I have told him we will all go — the household travels together now; that is simply the shape of things. Prue has packed pies. Fen has polished his boots, which means he intends to be seen, and being seen is still, for him, an act of courage measured in inches.*

*I find I am not afraid. I note this with some surprise, as fear has been a working colleague of mine these past years and I shall miss it less than I expected.*

*All's well. Light trimmed. Glass clean.*

*Signed,*

*Xanthe Thackeray, keeper.*

I fed the page into the growing stack of the log — six years of nights, plus one year of days that had taught me more than the nights ever did — and sat for a moment with my hands flat on the desk, listening to the house settle around me. Somewhere upstairs Ollie was reading aloud to Tom, stumbling gloriously over the long words in his own copy of the book. Somewhere below, Prue was banking the kitchen fire the way she'd banked it for forty years and would bank it for forty more if we let her, and we would.

And tomorrow — Falmouth. The review board. Names read aloud again, this time by men with the power to unseal pension files and rewrite casualty lists and pull eleven drowned men out of the category of *smugglers, lost* and set them down where they belonged: *carriers of the wounded, betrayed, honored.*

I blew out the lamp. Outside, far off across the water, the island light still turned — ours now, granted, rekindled, Tom's pride and my inheritance both — sweeping its patient white arm across the dark and keeping its count.

Some records you write. Some you keep burning.

I intended to do both.
