## 40. First Blossom

**Keeper's Log — Thackeray Light, private beacon. 14 April 1919. Weather: clear, wind southwest, light. Visibility: excellent.**

I have kept logs in every weather a coast can offer. I have written entries by lamplight with the glass shivering in its frame and my hands too cold to feel the keys. I have written them in grief, in fear, in the grey hour after Tom came home with his face ruined and before we knew he would keep it. But I have never written one like this, sitting under an apple tree in April with my coat off and the sun warm on the back of my neck, while above me five white blossoms open on a branch no thicker than my wrist.

Five blossoms. Prue counted them twice this morning and made me count them after her, as though the number might change if either of us looked away.

The graft is Elias's father's Cornish Aromatic, cut from the last living tree of the old orchard — the crooked one by the wall that survived forty years of neglect because even neglect couldn't kill a Thackeray rootstock, which I suspect says something about all of us. Fen did the cutting. Prue did the grafting, her thumbs pressing the cambium together with the same unhurried certainty she brings to bread dough and to difficult truths. And Ollie tied the raffia, because his small fingers do what none of ours can anymore, and because Prue said a graft wants to be tied by someone who believes it will live.

He believed it. He never doubted. Children don't doubt things they've decided to love; they simply stand beside them and wait.

And now it has blossomed. Out of season, out of reason, eleven months after a scion was cut and bound to a stranger's rootstock, the little tree has decided to flower anyway — as if it had been waiting through the whole hard winter for permission, and the permission finally came.

Prue says first-year blossom on a graft is a extravagance, that the tree should be putting its strength into wood and root, that we ought to pick the flowers off and let it gather itself. She said this standing under the branch with her hand cupped under the lowest bloom like a woman catching water.

Then she said: "But I shan't pick them. Some years you let the tree spend what it's got. Some years the spending *is* the point."

I wrote that down. I write everything down now. It is the only inheritance I trust.

* * *

**Keeper's Log — continued, later the same day.**

The household gathered under the trees at four o'clock. I record the attendance as a keeper records a light observed: all present, all burning.

Prue sat in the chair Tom carried out for her — she pretends to resent being carried, but I have watched her accept it eleven times without once suggesting she might walk. Forty years on her knees among these rows have left her joints like old rope, and she has earned every ounce of being borne. She wore her good shawl, the blue one, the one she saves for occasions, and if an afternoon in April with five blossoms counts as an occasion then I am glad to be the kind of household that decides such things count.

Fen leaned against the trunk of the big Bramley, arms crossed, hat over his eyes, pretending to half-sleep the way he does when he is actually listening to everything. His discharge paper is folded in his breast pocket still, though he's had it since February. He takes it out sometimes at night and reads it by the kitchen fire, not because he needs reminding of what it says but, I think, because he cannot yet believe a piece of paper can be true. Suspended sentence. Discharge honourable in substance if grudging in wording. The historian's report went up to the Admiralty in March, and there are men whose names were read aloud in a courtroom who will be reviewed, and some of them, perhaps, restored. Fen does not speak of it. He plants instead. He has put in two new rows along the south wall with his own hands, and he talks to the trees when he thinks no one can hear him, low and steady, the way you'd talk to men you'd once stood watch with.

Tom sat on the grass with his back against the wheelbarrow, his bad arm resting in his lap where it rests now always, and his good hand turning the pages of the newspaper he wasn't reading. The scars have settled from angry red to a pale silver, and the swelling around his left eye has gone down enough that both eyes match again, mostly. He laughs with his whole body now, which he didn't for months. Yesterday he laughed so hard at something Ollie drew in the dirt that he fell off the bench, and Prue said the sound was worth more than the whole harvest, and nobody contradicted her.

Ollie stood in the middle of the orchard grass holding the book.

Our book. The printed one — cheap paper, sewn badly, the ink smudging if you rub it, three hundred copies run off Widow Tregose's press in the port town between jobbing work and funeral notices. *A True Account of the Loss of the* Mercy Ann *and Other Matters, Being Testimony Taken and Recorded at Thackeray Orchard, 1918.* That was the title Fen insisted on, formal as a court document, because he wanted no one to mistake it for a story. On the title page, beneath the title, in type as plain as type can be:

*Recorded and certified by Xanthe Thackeray, keeper.*

Not *heir*. Not *widow*. Not *Thackeray of Thackeray Orchard*, though the solicitor's letter named me so and the law agrees with the letter. *Keeper.* I chose the word the way you choose a graft stock — for what it will bear.

Ollie has claimed the reading of it. He learned his letters from Prue before the war and from me through the winter, patient lessons at the kitchen table with the typewriter as his example — he likes that letters are things you can make, that words come out of your fingers the way apples come out of trees, given time and tending. He reads slowly, with his finger under each line, and he reads aloud because for a year he could not speak at all and I think every sentence now is a small victory parade.

He opened to page nine. Page nine is Ollie's own testimony, taken by lamplight last October, dated like a log entry, typed exactly as he spoke it with nothing softened and nothing added. Boats in the cove at night. Men carried up the cliff path. Men who never came back. My husband's uncle's name among them, though Ollie didn't know whose name it was when he gave it, and I did not tell him until afterward, and he was quiet a long moment and then said, "So the orchard was keeping them too."

That is what he said. It is in the book, because I asked him to say it again so I could type it properly, and he did, word for word, because children keep faith with their own sentences better than adults do.

He began to read. The wind moved the branches overhead and the light came down through them in pieces, and his voice — thin, careful, certain — carried across the rows the way a bell carries across water.

* * *

**Keeper's Log — continued.**

I find I must set down what happened under the trees this afternoon, or part of it, because a log that records only weather and attendance is a poor sort of witness, and I promised myself long ago — standing in a gutted estate office smelling of smoke, watching the ashes of other people's lies drift through the rafters — that I would be a good witness or die trying.

Ollie read his testimony. Then he read Prue's, the account of the cellars and the wounded men carried inland on cider carts, and Prue listened with her chin up and her hands folded, hearing her own voice come back to her out of a stranger's press, and when he finished she said, "Well," in the tone she uses when she will not cry in front of anyone, and Tom passed her his handkerchief without looking, and she took it without thanking him, which is how those two conduct their entire friendship.

Then Ollie turned to the ledger pages — the certified copy, the names of the drowned read out in a courtroom in January while a magistrate's career came apart like wet rope — and here he slowed, because the names are heavy, and he read every one. All fourteen. He did not skip, though I saw Fen close his eyes at the fourth name and keep them closed until the ninth.

When he finished there was the particular silence that follows true things spoken aloud. Not an empty silence. A full one.

And then Fen pushed his hat back off his face and said, "Read the last line."

Ollie found it. The last line of the book, the line I typed at two in the morning in the port town while Widow Tregose slept in her chair beside the press and the gulls argued over the harbour, the line I added after the certification and before the colophon because it seemed to me the whole account needed a keel:

*"The dead were betrayed by the living. The living owe the dead a record. This is mine."*

Ollie read it out. Then he looked up at me across the grass and said, "That's a good ending, missus."

"It's not an ending," I told him. "It's a beginning. Endings are for novels."

Fen snorted. Tom laughed. Prue said, "Hush, the pair of you, let the boy finish," though there was nothing left to finish, and Ollie closed the book and held it against his chest the way you hold something alive, and the wind came through the orchard and the five blossoms trembled on their branch and held.

We stayed under the trees until the light went long and gold. Nobody proposed anything. There was no speech. Prue dozed. Tom showed Ollie how to fold a paper boat, using the newspaper he hadn't been reading, and they launched it in the rain barrel and argued about ballast. Fen walked the new rows with his hands in his pockets, checking ties, pulling one weed, leaving the rest because Prue says a row kept too clean is a row with nothing to say for itself.

And I sat with my back against the old Bramley — the great gnarled grandmother of the orchard, older than any of the betrayals, older than the informer's signature, older than the war — and I understood something I have been circling since a sealed letter arrived on my island two winters ago and cracked my life open like a nut.

I stopped counting what can be carried somewhere in the middle of last summer, in the middle of the harvest, with apple juice to the elbows and Ollie asleep against my shoulder and the whole household sticky and laughing and quarrelling in the rows. I had spent my widowhood measuring my life in portable things: the typewriter, the spare lens parts, Elias's coat. Everything weighed, everything assessed, everything ready to be picked up and moved when the sea or the Admiralty or the world decided to take the rest away. It was a way of surviving loss — own nothing you cannot flee with, love nothing you cannot carry.

But you cannot carry an orchard. You cannot carry a household. You cannot carry the smell of blossom or the sound of a boy reading your own words back to you under the trees. None of it fits in a coat pocket, and all of it, it turns out, fits in a life.

The orchard keeps us. That is what Ollie said last autumn and what I finally understand now, sitting under the grandmother tree with the logbook on my knees: not that we keep the orchard, prune it, graft it, defend it in courtrooms and in print — though we have done all that — but that it keeps *us*. Roots go down further than any record. Something planted in grief grows into shelter for people who arrive with nothing portable at all.

* * *

**Keeper's Log — final entry for this date. Dusk. Wind fallen calm. Lamp lit at the light at 8:12, as required, decommissioned or no.**

I walked up to the tower this evening to trim the wick, out of habit and out of principle. The Admiralty granted me the light as a private beacon in February — the historian's doing, though he'd deny arranging it, and I'd deny asking, and we would both be lying with perfect courtesy, which is the foundation of our friendship. The lamp burns now for no shipping schedule and no Trinity House. It burns because a light that has been kept for sixty years should not go dark while someone is willing to keep it, and because Elias tended it, and because I am his, chosen and choosing.

From the gallery I could see the whole valley: the orchard laid out in its long green rows, the new plantings along the south wall staked and tied, the house with its chimneys smoking, the kitchen window lit gold because Prue never trusts anyone else with the evening fire. Smoke from the cottage chimney too, where Fen lives now — he signed the tenancy papers in March with the air of a man signing a surrender and a rescue simultaneously. And down past the wall, the potting shed, unlocked these many months, its crates empty, its bell hung in the church tower where the vicar — the new vicar, the young one who reads our book and blushes when he shakes my hand — rings it on Sundays for the fourteen.

The bell carries. Names carry. Blossoms carry, on the wind, further than anyone intends.

Tomorrow Prue and I begin the spring pruning proper, and next week the rest of the scions go in — twenty-two of them, labelled in Ollie's careful pencil, each one cut from a tree that survived. In May the historian comes again; he writes now that he may bring his daughter, who wants to see "the orchard that beat the Admiralty," which is not what happened, but I have learned not to argue with the versions history tells about itself. In June the second printing. Widow Tregose writes that the first run sold out and that a bookseller in Plymouth wants copies, and that a man from London wrote asking whether the author would consider a larger edition, and whether the author might call on him, and what name the author prefers to be known by.

I have drafted my reply four times. The answer to the last question is the shortest and took the longest:

*Xanthe Thackeray, keeper.*

Below me the lamp turns. Below the lamp the orchard stands in the dark with its roots in the ground and its five white blossoms furled for the night, holding what they hold. Somewhere beyond the headland the sea works at the coast as it always has, patient, indifferent, taking what it takes. It took a great deal from me. It has not got the orchard. It has not got the record. It has not got the household, which gathers tomorrow at the kitchen table as it gathers every morning, six chairs now — seven, when the historian's daughter comes — and no one counting what can be carried, because there is nothing left to carry and nowhere left to flee, and neither of those facts feels like a cage.

They feel like the thing Elias must have meant, all those nights he polished the glass and typed the log in his steady cadence and never once wrote the word *lonely* though the island was lonely enough to drown in. He kept the light because keeping is what a man does with what he loves. I thought keeping meant holding on. The orchard has taught me it means handing on — scion to stock, testimony to page, page to stranger, stranger to friend.

First blossom. Five flowers on a whip-thin branch, spending everything it has, because some years the spending is the point.

Log closed. Lamp trimmed. All present, all burning.

— Xanthe Thackeray, keeper
