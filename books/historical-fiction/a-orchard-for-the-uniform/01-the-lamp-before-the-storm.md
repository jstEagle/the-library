## 1. The Lamp Before the Storm

The wick wanted trimming, and Cyrus Reyes knew it the way she knew the ache in her left knee before rain — not by looking, but by a lifetime of small attentions paid to things that could not speak for themselves.

She climbed the ninety-seven steps of the Saltmere light with the oil can balanced on her hip, her boots finding the worn centers of each tread without thought. Ninety-seven. Tomás had counted them once, early in their marriage, when she was still new enough to the tower to count everything — the steps, the panes in the lantern room, the days since she'd last seen her mother's face across the water in Vellamare. He had counted them aloud like a boy, delighted, as though owning a number made a thing more his. *Ninety-seven steps to the sky, Cira.* He'd called her Cira because Cyrus was a name for a king or a scholar, and he said she was neither; she was his wife who kept a light, and that was better than both.

Two winters since he'd gone down with the fever and not come back up. Two winters of climbing these steps alone, and still her hand went to the rail where his hand should be, the way a tongue goes to the gap where a tooth has been pulled.

The lantern room smelled of brass polish and burning oil and, beneath it, the cold stone breath of the sea. She set the can down on its iron ring and opened the housing, and the great lens turned above her with its patient grinding sound, its clockwork heart ticking through the weights she would wind again at midnight. The flame stood tall and steady in the draft, throwing her shadow enormous against the curved glass, so that a woman made of darkness walked the walls around her.

"None of that," she told the shadow, as she told it most evenings. Talking to oneself was one of the freedoms of widowhood. There were others — eating supper standing at the counter, leaving her hair unbound until dawn, singing the Vellamare boat songs off-key with no one to wince — but this one she used most: speech without audience, words spent freely because no one was listening to spend them back.

She trimmed the wick with the small silver scissors that had been Tomás's, then hers, then — if Rolo Ferro ever got his wish — the apprentice boy's someday. The charred curl fell away and the flame brightened by a degree only she would have noticed, and she felt the small satisfaction of it settle in her chest like a swallowed coal. That was the whole of her kingdom, she supposed: a flame kept at its proper height, a lens kept turning, a log book kept honest. Out there beyond the glass the water lay flat and pewter-colored under the last of the daylight, and the fishing boats were coming home in a straggle of brown sails, and the town below was lighting its windows one by one, yellow squares appearing in the dusk like a slow, patient sentence being written.

Saltmere. Three generations of orchards terraced down the hillside, three generations of hands in the workshops turning out barrels and hinges and the famous pressed-tin tiles that roofed half the churches on this coast. A town that had never quite known what to do with her. Twenty years married to Tomás Reyes and they still called her *the outlander* behind their hands at market — politely, the way you'd note a limp. *The keeper's wife* first, and now *the keeper herself*, which was stranger still, a woman holding a post men had held before her, tolerated because the Board's letter said she might and because the lamp had never once gone dark on her watch. Not once. Whatever else they said about her when her back was turned, no one could say the light had failed.

She wiped the glass panes with the chamois, working in the order Tomás had taught her — seaward panes first, while the arm was fresh, then landward — and watched the town assemble itself below her in lamplight and woodsmoke. The Vidal orchards made a dark tide of trees along the eastern slope, the late apples still hanging like small stubborn moons. Marta Vidal would be walking those rows now, she thought, as the old woman walked them every evening at this hour, touching the trunks the way other people touched children's heads. And the workshop chimneys would be cooling, and Ines Corvo would be banking the forge her husband had left her, and somewhere in the tavern the day's grievances were being poured out alongside the wine.

History happened in places like that, she'd always believed. Happened to people who owned things — orchards, forges, names that went back past anyone's remembering. It did not happen to lighthouse keepers, whose whole estate was a flame and a schedule. History was something that arrived from over the horizon, in books and newspapers and the mouths of travelers, concerning capitals and kings and coastlines she'd never see. Here there was only weather, and weather wasn't history; weather was just the same argument the sky had been having with the sea since before anyone kept records of anything.

She believed this the way you believe the floor will hold you. Without thinking about it. Without ever once testing it.

The wind had shifted since noon — she'd marked it in the log, *wind veering NNE, freshening, barometer 29.4 and falling* — and now it came against the glass with a fine salt grit that whispered like sand poured on paper. A storm out at sea somewhere, working up its temper. Tomorrow, maybe the day after, it would arrive here. She knew storms the way farmers knew their fields: which ones would sheer off south, which ones would come up the coast hungry. This one would come. She could feel it in the barometer's slow fall, in the way the gulls had gone quiet and inland, in the ache the storm left behind in her knee like a signature.

Below, the last boat cleared the harbor mouth and the harbor light — a stubby green thing she also tended, though it never appeared in the Board's letters — blinked awake under her earlier hand. The town looked peaceful from up here. From up here you couldn't hear the arguments in the tavern or see the ledgers gathering dust in the town hall. From up here Saltmere was only a pattern of lights, an arrangement of shelter, and she was the one who kept watch over all of it, and none of it was hers.

That was the arithmetic she'd stopped resenting and started simply carrying, the way you carry a stone in your pocket until you forget its weight: the town did not want her, and the work did, and the work was enough. Most nights it was enough. Some nights — the ones when the fog came in thick and the diaphone moaned out over the water and no ship answered — some nights the loneliness rose up like bilgewater and she had to bail it out hour by hour until morning.

Tonight was not one of those nights. Tonight was a good night, a working night, and she went down the ninety-seven steps with the empty oil can swinging, whistling one of the Vellamare songs badly, and let herself into the keepers' quarters where the stove needed feeding and the supper needed making and the log needed yesterday's weather entered in her careful, upright hand.

* * *

The log book lay open on the table where she'd left it that morning, and she sat with her tea going cold beside her and wrote the day into permanence: wind, swell, visibility, the passage of the coastal steamer *Almendra* at 14:20, showing two masts and a towline. Small facts. Honest facts. The only kind she dealt in.

It was while she wrote that she noticed the loose pages again — the storm records, the older volumes, the ones from Tomás's years — stacked in the corner cabinet where they'd lived since she'd boxed up his effects and then, unable to finish the job, unboxed most of them. She'd been meaning to sort them properly. Two winters meaning to. There were pages in his handwriting interleaved among the printed forms, she remembered suddenly, notes he'd taken during the great gale of '09, wave heights and wind speeds measured with a madman's precision while the tower shook around him. She'd found them the week after the funeral and closed the cabinet on them, because grief is a door as well as an opening, and some doors you shut to stay standing.

She looked at the cabinet now for a long moment. Then she drank her cold tea, banked the stove, and did not open it. There would be time. There was always time; that was the one thing the tower taught you, the long patience of it, seasons turning like the lens itself, everything coming round again.

Outside, the wind worried at the gallery rail. The barometer stood at 29.2 and falling.

* * *

She woke at midnight to wind the weights, as she had woken at midnight for six thousand-odd nights, her body keeping the schedule even in sleep. The stairs were colder now, the storm's breath already in the stone. Up in the lantern room the flame burned true and the lens turned and the darkness beyond the glass had organized itself into weather — cloud moving fast under the stars, hiding and revealing them, the sea gone the color of wet slate with white running on it further out.

She wound the weights. She checked the mercury. She stood a moment with her palm flat against the cold glass, the way she did every night, an act with no purpose she could name except that Tomás had done it, and before him the old keeper Duarte had done it, and it meant, perhaps, *I am here, and you are there, and between us we have an understanding.*

From the gallery she could see the whole coast. The town below, banked and sleeping. The orchard slopes black against the lesser black of the land. The road east toward Vellamare, empty. The sea, working itself up for whatever it intended.

And far out — far enough that she might have imagined it, far enough that she stood a long time deciding whether she had — a light. Not a star. Stars don't sit low on the water and hold their place while the swells lift and drop them. Not a fishing boat; the fleet was home, she'd counted them in. Something else, then. Something riding out there beyond the shoals with a light at its masthead, too far off to make out its shape, holding station in rising weather as though it had business here and all the patience in the world.

Ships bound for Vellamare passed well clear of Saltmere. Ships bound anywhere had no reason to lie off this coast at midnight with a storm coming down on them.

Cyrus stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair loose and the cold coming through her coat, and watched the distant light burn steady, and felt — without knowing why, the feeling arriving ahead of its reasons the way weather arrives ahead of the front — that the floor she had stood on all her life had begun, very quietly, to shift.

She went inside. She took up her pen. And beneath the day's neat columns of wind and swell and passing ships, her hand hesitated over the page before writing what the log required:

*Midnight. Wind NNE, hardening. Unidentified vessel standing off the shoals, showing one white light. Watching her.*

She blew out the lamp over the desk, climbed back up, and kept the light burning — hers, the only thing on this coast that had ever truly been hers to keep — while out in the dark the other light waited, patient as history, for morning.
