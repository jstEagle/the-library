## 2. Ledger of Small Names

The oil for the town hall's lamps came from Cyrus's stores, same as it had since before Tomás died, and so twice a month she walked down from the light station into town with a jug in each hand and the sea wind pushing at her back like an impatient hand between her shoulder blades.

She knew what they called her behind her back. *The lamp woman.* Not unkindly, exactly — there was a grudging respect folded into it, because everyone on this coast understood that a dark light meant drowned men — but it was not the same as being called by her name. She had lived above them for eleven years, buried a husband among their dead, and still she was *the lamp woman*, a function rather than a person, useful the way the pier was useful or the breakwater. Nobody invited the breakwater to supper.

It was market morning, and the square was loud with it — gulls screaming over the fish stalls, a man crying the price of apples from an orchard cart, children weaving through legs with the specific lawlessness of children whose mothers are haggling. The orchard fruit was early this year, small and hard-skinned, green-gold in the slant of sun that came over the eastern hills and turned the whole square briefly to honey. Cyrus bought nothing. There was no one to cook for, and buying single portions felt like an admission she wasn't ready to make in public.

The town hall sat at the square's far end, a squat stone building older than any of the houses around it, its foundation stones quarried so long ago that the chisel marks had weathered to softness. Its clock hadn't run in anyone's living memory; the hands were frozen at some quarter hour belonging to the previous century, and the town had long ago stopped correcting its watches by it. Above the door, carved into the lintel where the lichen had not quite eaten it away, ran a motto in letters worn nearly smooth: *THE FRUIT TO THE HANDS THAT TENDED IT.*

Cyrus had read those words a hundred times without ever once wondering who had put them there, or why such a thing needed saying.

She let herself in through the side door, as she always did — keepers of lamps did not use front doors; front doors were for people with business — and descended the half-flight to the clerk's room, where the air smelled of dust and cold iron and old paper going quietly brown.

Senhor Anselmo Ferraz was where he always was: behind his desk, in his high-backed chair, with his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead and his fingers stained to the second knuckle with ink. He was perhaps sixty, a dry stick of a man with the posture of a question mark and a voice like paper tearing slowly. His father had been clerk before him, and his grandfather before that, and it was said — though Anselmo never said it himself — that the family had held the post since the hall was built.

"Twice a month," he said, without looking up, "like the tide tables. You're the most punctual thing in this town, Senhora Reyes, and I include the church bell."

"The church bell stops when it rains."

"And you don't." He took the jugs, weighed each in his hand out of ritual rather than need, and entered the delivery in a ledger of his own — a slim modern thing, machine-ruled, its columns printed. "There. Lamp oil, four measures. Signed and witnessed. We are all properly documented."

He said it lightly, but something in the phrasing snagged on her. "Is documentation a joke here?" she asked. "You sound like a man apologizing for his own trade."

Anselmo looked up then, and the spectacles came down off his forehead onto his nose, which he did only when something interested him. He was, Cyrus thought, one of perhaps three people in the town who had ever looked at her with actual curiosity rather than polite arithmetic.

"Not a joke," he said. "A lament. Come here — no, come *here* — and I'll show you the difference."

He rose with the small cracking noises of a man whose joints had opinions, and shuffled past the printed ledgers, past the pigeonholes stuffed with receipts and tax rolls, to the far wall of his room, where a shelf of oak, black with age, held a row of books bound in leather gone the color of dried blood.

Cyrus had seen the shelf a hundred times. She had never once wondered about it. That was the way of the town's furniture — it simply *was*, like the headland, like the shoals, requiring neither attention nor explanation.

"This," said Anselmo, drawing out the thickest of them, a volume so heavy he carried it in both arms against his chest, "is why my trade is a lament. Mind the corner of the desk."

He laid it down on the table by the window, where the morning light fell strongest, and opened it with both hands, the way one opens a book one has been taught is holy.

The first page hit her before she understood anything else about it: the smell. Old ink has a smell — iron and smoke and something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a closed room — and this book breathed it out in a slow exhale of decades. Then the handwriting itself, close and patient, line after line after line, in a script so regular it might have been printed.

"What is it?"

"The ledger." He said it the way other men named their sons. "The orchard ledger. Begun — well. Turn the pages and see."

She turned the pages and saw.

It was a census of a kind, but not of people — of *claims*. Every family in the valley had its entry, sometimes stretching across three generations of ink, three different hands continuing the same account. *Vidal.* The name leapt first because Marta Vidal was the loudest voice in every argument the town ever had, and there she was on the fourth leaf, in a hand firm as fence posts: *Marta Vidal, widow, holds by inheritance from João Vidal her husband, twelve rows east of the stone wall, planted by his grandfather, forty trees bearing, six lost to blight in the year of the great wind.* And below it, in a later hand, additions — grafts noted, losses recorded, a daughter married and given two rows as dowry, each event dated and initialed.

Beside each orchard claim, entries of another kind: workshop shares. *One-eighth part in the cooperage, held by the Ferreira family since the founding. One-sixteenth in the press works.* Fractions of livelihoods, passed hand to hand like communion. Whole pages of names she knew from the market, from the churchyard, from the tavern's Sunday noise — except here the names were not people but *lines*, rooted and dated, growing rings around them year upon year.

And interleaved among the claims, pressed flat between leaves gone soft as cloth, were the things themselves: a pressed apple blossom, brown and papery now, its five petals intact. A curl of bark. Once, astonishingly, a small copper tag stamped with numbers, sewn to the page with thread that had rotted to rust.

"They tagged the trees," Cyrus said. It came out as wonder, which embarrassed her slightly.

"The founder's notion." Anselmo stood across the table from her, watching her read with an expression she couldn't place — something like hunger, something like grief. "Every tree in the first planting bore a numbered tag. Copper, so the salt wouldn't take them. And the number went in the book, and the name went with the number, and so long as the book survived—" He stopped. Shrugged. "So long as the book survived, nobody could say a Vidal hadn't tended what a Vidal planted. Or take it from him and pretend otherwise. That's what the lintel says, if you've ever read it. Fruit to the hands that tended it. This book is how you prove which hands."

"How old?"

"The founder began it the year the press works opened. My great-great-grandfather wrote the first line. I write the last ones." He touched the newest page almost tenderly — entries within the last decade, including, Cyrus noticed, her own husband's name. *Tomás Reyes, keeper of the light, holds by grant no land, but one-sixteenth share in the press works, granted in consideration of service to the harbor.* She stared at it. Tomás had never mentioned it. One-sixteenth of something, sitting in a dusty book while she kept his lamp alone.

"You didn't know," Anselmo said. Not a question.

"He never spoke of it. Of much of anything." She traced the line of Tomás's entry with one finger, careful not to touch the ink. Eleven years married to a quiet man and here was a whole sentence of him she'd never heard. "Who keeps it updated? You?"

"I do. And here is the lament, Senhora, the one I promised you." He closed the book gently, but not before she saw the thinning of entries toward the end — years passing in fewer lines, gaps widening. "When my father was clerk, every family came to him at least once a year. Births, deaths, marriages, a new graft, a tree taken by lightning. The book lived. Now?" He laughed, a dry tearing sound. "Now I could count on one hand the folk who've asked to see it in ten years. They know it's here the way they know the clock doesn't run. They don't *read* it. Why would they? Everyone knows whose trees are whose. It's in the blood, isn't it? In the ground." He shook his head. "People trust memory until the day memory fails, and by then it's too late to have written anything down. That is the whole of my trade in one sentence, and no one wants to hear it."

"But you keep writing anyway."

"I keep writing anyway." He set the spectacles straighter on his nose. "A clerk who stops keeping records is just an old man in a cold room."

Outside, the market noise swelled and fell like surf. Somewhere a child shrieked with laughter, and a dog barked itself hoarse, and none of it, Cyrus reflected, would leave any trace in any book. All that life, unwritten. She looked down again at where the ledger lay closed, its covers scarred, its spine cracked white with use, holding three generations of small names the way the headland held the town — silently, structurally, without anyone thinking to be grateful.

"Why show me?" she asked. It was not an accusation; she genuinely wanted to know. In eleven years Anselmo had exchanged with her perhaps a thousand words, nearly all of them about oil.

Anselmo considered her for a long moment through the smudged lenses.

"Because you're the only person besides myself who's come into this room in a month," he said finally, "and because you looked at the blossom." He tapped the page where the pressed flower lay. "Most folk flip past those. You went straight to it like a woman reading a letter."

"It's from the orchard."

"From the first planting. The founder's own tree, they say. There are others in there — bark, tags, a wedding ribbon someone pressed eighty years ago." He began, with visible reluctance, gathering the book back into his arms. "Everything in this town that matters ended up in here eventually. Everything that mattered to somebody."

He reshelved it, and the black oak shelf received it with the sound of a door closing, and the moment broke the way moments do — the market noise rushing back in, a cart rattling past outside, the ordinary world reasserting its jurisdiction.

But something had shifted in her, small and seismic, like the first tremor of ground that will later crack open. She had spent eleven years believing history happened elsewhere — in cities she'd read about, in wars she'd read about, in the capitals where ministers signed things and ships were launched and statues got erected. Here in this town there was only weather and harvest and the annual argument about the pier. But the ledger said otherwise. The ledger said history was twelve rows east of the stone wall, forty trees bearing, six lost to blight in the year of the great wind. History was a copper tag on a tree and a widow's firm handwriting and a wedding ribbon pressed flat by someone who had loved someone. History happened *here*. It had been happening here all along, patiently, in ink, and she had walked past the shelf a hundred times without wondering.

She thought of Tomás's name in it. *One-sixteenth share, granted in consideration of service to the harbor.* He had belonged here more than she'd known. Perhaps more than he'd let himself know. And she —

Well. She was the lamp woman. Four measures of oil, signed and witnessed.

"Same again in a fortnight," Anselmo said, settling back into his chair, the visit evidently concluded, the book already receding into the general furniture of the world.

"Same again." She paused at the door. "Anselmo. If someone wanted to read it — really read it, all of it — would you allow that?"

The spectacles came down. That look again, hungry and grieving. "Allow it? Senhora, I would weep with gratitude." Then he waved a hand, retreating into irony before the feeling could stand exposed. "Though fair warning: it's duller than sermons and heavier than sin. No one reads it anymore."

"No one," Cyrus agreed, and climbed the half-flight into the sunlight, and crossed the square under the worn words of the lintel, *the fruit to the hands that tended it*, and told herself the strange warmth in her chest was only the walk, only the season turning.

She was halfway up the cliff path, the lighthouse white against the pale sky ahead of her, when she stopped.

Out past the shoals — farther than the fishing boats ever worked, farther than anything had any honest reason to be — a vessel stood against the horizon. Gray hull, low in the water, funnels venting a smear of smoke that hung in the windless air like a signature. Not a merchantman. Not a fisherman. She had kept this light for eleven years and she knew every silhouette this coast produced, the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs.

This one she did not know.

She stood on the path with the jug-sellers' cries faint behind her and the pressed-apple smell of old ink still somehow in her nose, and watched the gray ship lie there, patient as history, waiting for morning.

* * *

That evening she climbed the tower at dusk and lit the lamp, and while the mantle came up glowing she found herself thinking not of the ship but of Anselmo's hands on the old binding, the tenderness of them, and of the blossom pressed flat between generations — petals that had been gold once, on a branch that had been alive once, in an orchard that was still, today, standing.

Nobody reads it anymore, the clerk had said, and laughed.

She trimmed the wick. Below her the town kindled its windows one by one, small and warm and unwritten, and beyond it the gray ship rode at anchor with its lights showing, and Cyrus kept hers burning above them all, and slept badly, and did not yet know why.
