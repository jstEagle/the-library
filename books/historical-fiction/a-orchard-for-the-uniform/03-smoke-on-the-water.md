## 3. Smoke on the Water

The morning the cutter came, Cyrus was cleaning the storm glass.

It was a small ritual, one of a hundred she had inherited along with the light itself — wiping the instrument's brass casing with a rag dipped in vinegar until it shone, then holding it to her eye and reading the water inside as though it were tea leaves. *Change,* the glass said that morning, the crystals settling into feathery filaments that drifted like smoke underwater. She had learned the instrument's language from Tomás's marginal notes, his cramped handwriting crowding the manufacturer's printed instructions: *Filaments = wind within two days. Trust it more than your knees.* Her knees had been telling her rain since Tuesday. The glass agreed with them for once.

She set it on the gallery rail to dry in the pale sun and looked out over the water because there was nothing else to look at, which was the truth of her life stated plainly: the sea ahead, the town behind, and herself on the narrow stair between them, belonging fully to neither.

The town lay below in its morning industry. Smoke stood up from the bakery chimney in a straight grey column; somewhere down the hill the blacksmith's hammer had already begun its patient argument with iron. The orchards on the eastern slope wore their September color, leaves going copper at the edges, fruit heavy enough now that the branches seemed to lean toward the ground like tired women. She could pick out Marta Vidal's roof by the patch of new slate, and beyond it the workshop with its long windows catching the light. Three generations of hands had built what she saw. None of them, she thought, had ever built anything for her, or expected her to build anything for them. Two winters of widowhood had taught her the exact dimensions of that distance. It was not unkindness, exactly. It was more like weather — something the town did around her without deciding to.

She was turning back toward the lamp room when she saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rose. This hung low over the horizon northeast of the shoal marker, a smudge that moved against the wind, and beneath it, when she fetched Tomás's glass from its bracket and braced her elbows on the rail, she found the shape that made the smoke: a vessel, dark-hulled, funnels fore and aft, sitting at anchor where no honest fishing boat would sit. Naval lines. She knew them only from picture papers, but some knowledge is older than its sources — the ship had the look of a thing designed to be obeyed.

Cyrus lowered the glass and stood very still.

*History happens elsewhere,* she had told herself for years, and had mostly believed it. Wars happened in newspapers, arriving a week late and softened by distance into casualty columns and maps with small flags. The last war the coast had seen was her grandfather's grandfather's affair, fought over things no one alive could name. The town below had gone on planting and forging and burying through all of it, indifferent as stone. She had assumed it always would. She had counted on it, in the way a person counts on the walls of a house they do not love but cannot leave.

The cutter sat on the water like a held breath.

She watched it for an hour. That was the keeper's discipline, the same patience the lamp demanded: watch, record, do not flinch. At half past ten the ship's boats went down from the davits — six of them, maybe seven, each pulling twenty oars or better — and began the long pull toward the harbor mouth. From this height they looked like insects, like beetles crossing a floor of hammered tin. She counted again to be sure of what she was seeing, though counting changed nothing. Soldiers did not come to fish. Soldiers did not come to buy apples.

Her hands, she noticed, were steady on the rail. It surprised her. Inside, some animal part of her wanted to bolt — down the stairs, through the town, anywhere away from what was coming — but the keeper's discipline held, and underneath the discipline something colder held too, the thought that arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *They will not want you either. You are nobody here. Whatever they take, you have nothing to lose.*

That was not true, and she knew it even as she thought it, but the thought came anyway, the way old pain comes before a storm, in joints long healed.

* * *

By noon the whole town knew. News traveled downhill faster than any boat pulled, carried by Rolo Ferreira, who had climbed the headland path at first light to check his rabbit snares and come back running with his cap in his fist and his voice pitched up an octave. Cyrus heard him shouting before she saw him, heard the word repeated door to door like a bell rung by hand: *soldiers, soldiers, soldiers.*

From the gallery she watched the harbor fill. The boats grounded on the shingle in disciplined order, men pouring out into water to their knees, forming ranks on the sand while officers consulted papers under a parasol-sized map board. The townspeople gathered along the quay in a loose crescent, keeping the distance people keep from a fire — close enough to see, far enough to pretend they hadn't chosen to look. She could not hear what anyone said. The wind carried the sound of boots on wet stone, the slap of halyards, an officer's voice rising and falling like a gull's, meaning nothing without words attached.

Then the column formed, and the soldiers marched up from the harbor into the heart of the town, and Cyrus felt the strangeness of it settle over everything like frost: their own streets, their own cobbles, worn smooth by three generations of cartwheels and bare feet, and strangers' boots ringing on them in step, in time, in a rhythm the street had never learned. The column passed the bakery and the smell of bread still reached her on the wind, absurdly ordinary, cinnamon and crust, while beneath it the boots went on beating their foreign time.

They stopped at the church.

Of course the church. Every instinct of power, ancient or modern, knew where the town's heart kept itself. St. Brendan's squatted at the center of the lower town, squat-towered and salt-stained, its door the same door where wedding parties spilled out and funeral processions gathered in, the same door against which the market notices were nailed every Friday — lost dog, auctioned boat, harvest fair. Cyrus had passed that door a hundred times without reading a single notice. They were none of them addressed to her.

Two soldiers stepped forward with a paste bucket and a rolled paper. A sergeant — she could tell even at this distance by the way he stood, weight settled, hands easy, a man whose work was other men — unrolled the notice against the door and smoothed it flat with the side of his hand, once, twice, the gesture almost tender, a man smoothing a sheet on a bed. Then he stepped back, and the officer read aloud.

She caught fragments on the wind. *By order... requisitioned... the exigencies of the service... all holdings, chattels, records...* Words built like seawalls, massive and impersonal, designed so that no wave of argument could break them. The crowd below had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. Quiet crowds are crowds doing arithmetic. She could nearly see the sums moving behind the upturned faces: how many rooms, how many boats, how many sacks of flour, how many names.

When the reading ended, the silence lasted three full seconds. Then Old Marta Vidal pushed out of the crescent — Cyrus knew her by the white plait and the unhurried spine, the walk of a woman who had buried a husband, two sons, and every illusion about the kindness of institutions — and planted herself squarely before the church door, directly beneath the notice, and said something short and hard that the wind stole entire.

The officer bent courteously to hear her. He answered. Marta spat — Cyrus saw it clearly, the arc of it catching the light — and walked away without waiting for permission, and the strange thing, the thing Cyrus turned over in her mind all afternoon, was that the officer let her go. He watched her go with an expression she couldn't read at this distance, and then he turned back to his men and the machine of occupation resumed: squads detailed to the harbor, to the warehouse, to the town hall.

To the town hall.

Cyrus's eye followed the small detachment as it climbed the steps of the low granite building where she had stood, only weeks ago, listening to the clerk joke about the ledger no one read anymore. She remembered the ledger itself — the great bound book on its lectern, the columns of names and shares, orchard rows measured in paces and poles, workshop fractions divided among families like inheritance cakes. She had touched the page edge, almost idly, and the clerk had laughed and said something like *careful, madam, that book has more lives in it than the graveyard.* She had smiled and meant nothing by it. Nobody had meant anything by it. The book had sat there being true for sixty years while the town lived on top of it the way soil lives on rock.

Now soldiers were carrying it out.

She saw the crates before she understood them — four men to a crate, the boxes too large for ledgers alone, stuffed with the hall's whole paper memory, deed copies, tax rolls, the registers of births that predated the church books. The crates went onto a handcart, the handcart rattled downhill over cobbles that must have shaken every page loose from its certainty, and Cyrus gripped the gallery rail and understood, with the slow cold certainty of the storm glass settling into filaments, that something had already ended. Not the town — the town still stood, the ovens still baked, the forge still rang. But the proof of the town, the paper spine that held three generations upright, was rolling downhill in a government cart toward a government boat, and there was nothing between it and the water except two bored soldiers and a wheel with a squeaking rim.

She stayed at the rail until dusk. This, too, was discipline, though it cost her more than the watching had. Below, the town lit its lamps one window at a time, obedient to the hour if to nothing else, and among the familiar lights she could make out the darker shapes of soldiers moving where no shapes should be — at the hall steps, at the warehouse doors, at intervals along the quay like stitches closing a wound. On the water, the cutter rode at anchor with riding lights doubled, and the smoke of its galley fires blew shoreward all evening, a thin bitter ribbon that she could almost taste on the wind: coal, tar, iron, someone else's dinner.

* * *

She went down at full dark, because the oil delivery was due and because standing still in the tower had become its own kind of unbearable. The keeper goes down; the keeper keeps accounts; the keeper lights the lamp whether empires move or not. She told herself this on every stair.

The lane smelled of woodsmoke and fear. Doors she had never known the color of were shut; curtains twitched in patterns that mapped her passage house to house. At the corner by Ines Corvo's forge, a knot of men stood talking in voices too low to carry, and they stopped when she passed and started again after, and she understood that she was outside the knot, outside all knots, the outsider still, even now — perhaps especially now, when the town's business was suddenly everyone's business and she had no share in anyone's.

Only Rolo broke the pattern, materializing beside her at the foot of the tower stairs with the shameless speed of fifteen-year-olds, breathless, eyes enormous in the lamplight.

"You saw them," he said. It wasn't a question. "From up top. What were they? How many? Did you see the guns?"

"Rolo."

"There were guns on the deck. I counted six." His voice cracked on the number, half terror, half the terrible shine of a boy who has waited his whole life for something to happen and cannot yet tell whether what has happened is wonderful. "Sergeant Ossa says they've taken the town hall. Everything in it. Even the—"

"I know," Cyrus said. "I saw the cart."

He fell silent. In the dark his face worked through several thoughts too fast to name, and landed on the one that frightened her most, because she recognized it: the look of a boy asking an adult to say the world is still shaped the way he thought it was.

"They can't just take it," he said. "Can they? There are laws. There's the ledger. It says whose land is whose — my grandfather's trees are in it, row forty-one, it's written down, it's *written*, you can't just—" He stopped himself, hearing the childishness in it, and flushed even in the dark.

"It's written down," Cyrus agreed quietly. "Yes."

But she was thinking of the cart, and the squeaking wheel, and the distance between the town hall door and the harbor water, which was four hundred paces, and the distance between the harbor water and the open sea, which was nothing at all.

"Go home, Rolo," she said. "Curfew soon, I'd guess. Boys get stopped first."

He went, reluctantly, looking back twice. She climbed her stairs. And at the top, before she lit the lamp — before the ritual that ordered every evening of her life and had become, without her noticing, the only appointment the town had ever made with her — she paused at the gallery rail one last time and looked at the church door.

She could not see the notice from here in the dark. But she could see the spot where it hung, a paler rectangle faintly visible against the black oak, and she understood that tomorrow the whole town would stand before that rectangle, and read it line by line, and learn exactly what the world now claimed it owed. She thought of Marta Vidal's spit arcing in the sunlight, of the officer bending courteously to listen, of the crates going down the hill.

The barometer had fallen a tenth of an inch since noon. The storm glass filaments had thickened. Wind coming, yes. Something else coming with it.

Cyrus lit the lamp. Its beam swept out across the water, once, twice, steady as a heartbeat, touching the anchored cutter on every turn — and she stood in the revolving dark and light and admitted, at last, what she had been refusing since morning:

History had not happened elsewhere after all. It had simply been at sea, coming toward them, and now it was in the harbor, and in the morning it would be nailed to the church door with paste and orders, and no one — not the town, not the ledger, not the light itself — had asked whether she was ready.

Down in the town, past curfew, a door slammed somewhere near the Vidal house, hard enough to hear even over the wind.

Tomorrow, she thought, they would all be knocking on each other's doors.
