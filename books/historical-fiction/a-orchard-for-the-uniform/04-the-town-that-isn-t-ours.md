## 4. The Town That Isn't Ours

The notices stayed on the church door through the night, and by morning they had gone soft at the edges, the paper drinking in the sea air until the printed words blurred like something seen through water. Cyrus read them anyway. She had walked down from the lighthouse before the fog had fully lifted, drawn by a gravity she could not name, and she stood among a dozen townspeople while the ink wept and the sentences dissolved.

REQUISITION OF PROPERTY FOR THE WAR EFFORT, they had said. ALL LANDS, STRUCTURES, VESSELS, AND RECORDS WITHIN THE BOUNDARY OF THE COASTAL DISTRICT ARE HEREBY PLACED UNDER MILITARY ADMINISTRATION.

*Records.* That word she remembered most clearly, because it was the one that made old Marta Vidal go still beside her — still in the way a struck bell is still, all the motion turned inward, ringing.

Now it was evening, and the tavern was full.

Cyrus sat near the wall with an untouched cup of cider going flat before her. She had been coming to this tavern for eleven years, since the week Tomás brought her down off the headland as his bride, and in eleven years she had never quite stopped feeling like weather — present, noticed, endured until it passed. The benches were worn smooth by three generations of the same families, and every family here could trace its line back further than her whole life put together. She knew their names. She did not know, not really, what the names carried.

But tonight the names were the only thing anyone talked about.

"They posted them on the church door," said Joaquim Ferro the cooper, his big hands wrapped around his mug as though it might bolt. "On God's own door. Like nailing up a dead man's debts."

"They'll post worse than that if we give them reason," said his brother Anselmo, younger, quicker, with the nervous energy of a man who had already decided and was waiting for the room to agree. "We comply. We inventory what they tell us to inventory. We sign what they put in front of us. The war will end, the army will leave, and we will still be standing."

"Will we?" This was Ines Cabral, the blacksmith's widow, who spoke rarely and therefore was heard always. Her voice was quiet but it cut the smoke like a blade. "Standing on whose land? Under whose roof? You sign a paper saying the orchards are theirs, Anselmo, and in five years there will be a barracks where the press house stands, and your grandson won't believe you when you tell him your grandfather planted those trees."

Anselmo flushed. "And if we don't sign? If we fight? With what? Rolo's slingshot?"

A few people laughed, but it was the thin kind of laughter that exists only to keep other sounds out. At the far end of the bench, the boy himself — Rolo, fourteen, all elbows and earnest eyes — looked down at the table as though someone had accused him personally of insufficient weaponry.

Cyrus watched the room the way she watched the horizon: for the thing that would come out of it.

* * *

Marta Vidal had been sitting by the fire since sundown, and she had not yet spoken, and everyone knew why, and no one dared ask her to.

She was seventy-one years old and had buried a husband, two sons, and every illusion she had ever owned, and she had done the burying without weeping, which had earned her a reputation the town respected the way sailors respect a rock they steer around. Her hands lay folded in her lap like sleeping birds. The firelight moved across her face and found nothing to hold.

Comply. Flee. Resist. She heard the words circling the room like gulls over a fish gutting table, all noise and no feeding. Comply meant handing over the town with a receipt. Flee meant becoming strangers somewhere else, landless, nameless — she had seen what happened to the fishing families after the great storm of '89, how quickly a person became nobody once they left the ground that knew them. And resist — resist was a word young men loved because they imagined it ended in glory rather than ash.

There was a fourth thing, and it was sitting in a locked cabinet in the town hall, and no one in this room had thought about it since they were children learning to write their fathers' names beneath their fathers' claims.

Marta unfolded her hands.

"The ledger," she said.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The room quieted around the word the way water quiets around a dropped stone.

Joaquim frowned. "What ledger?"

"The ledger." She let the silence do its work. "The book in the town hall. The one your grandfather signed and his father signed before him. Every orchard row, every workshop share, every well and wall and drying shed from the point to the mill stream. Every name. Three generations of names, written down so that no soldier, no magistrate, no king's clerk could ever stand on this ground and say it was never ours."

She had not been inside the hall in years — her knees saw to that, and besides, what was the use; the book kept itself. But she remembered it the way she remembered her mother's kitchen. The green baize cover gone grey at the corners. The smell of the iron-gall ink, sharp as blood. The way the clerk's hand had grown smaller and more cramped down through the decades, generation pressing into generation on the same pages, crowding toward the edges, unwilling to begin a new book, as though the book itself were the continuity, as though starting fresh would break something.

Her father had signed it in 1852. His hand had shaken, he'd told her, not from fear but from the weight of it — from understanding that a name set down in ink outlived the hand that wrote it, outlived the tongue that spoke it, became a kind of root.

"You're talking about paper," said Anselmo. "They have rifles."

"I am talking about the difference," Marta said, "between being robbed and being erased. They can take the trees. They took the trees before — you've heard the story, some of you, from your grandmothers, the year the soldiers came through and stripped the orchards bare and left nothing, not even the windfalls, and when the town asked for justice there was nothing to show because nothing was written. Nothing was *written*, Anselmo. That is why the ledger exists. Our grandparents swore it would never happen again. Not the taking — the taking happens, men are what they are — but the *forgetting*. Never again would we stand empty-handed with our mouths open and no proof of what we were."

The fire popped. Someone's spoon clinked against a bowl and went silent, embarrassed.

"And now?" Joaquim asked gently. He had always been gentler with her than the others; perhaps grief recognized grief. "Now they've taken the proof, Marta. It was on the barge with the rest of it. We watched it go."

"We watched it go," Marta agreed, and her voice did not waver, and it cost her more than any wavering would have. She had watched the soldiers carry the ledgers and deeds and parish rolls down to the water in their canvas crates, had watched the clerk Senhor Duarte stand in the doorway of his office wringing his soft hands, and she had felt something crack in her chest that she had thought was already broken past cracking. Paper. She was grieving paper. No — she was grieving what the paper held, which was herself, which was everyone, which was the only version of this town that existed outside living memory, and living memory dies with its keepers.

"So we write another," she said.

The words were out before she had fully decided to say them, and having said them, she found they were true, and having found them true, she stood up — knees protesting, spine refusing to protest with them.

"Paper can be copied," Marta said. "Names can be spoken. There is not one person in this room who cannot recite their grandmother's claim from memory — I've heard you do it at funerals, at weddings, over cards, complaining about boundary walls your great-grandfathers argued over. The knowledge is in us. The taking of a book does not take the knowing." She looked around the room, at their frightened faces lit orange and uncertain. "The question is whether we are a town or a collection of households waiting to be reassigned. Because if we are a town, then we do what towns do. We keep our records. However we must."

No one answered. But no one laughed either, and Marta had lived long enough to know the difference between silence that ends a conversation and silence that begins one.

* * *

Cyrus walked home under a sky scrubbed clean by wind, the stars hard and close, the lighthouse beam sweeping overhead in its long patient arc. She let herself in, banked the fire, and sat at the kitchen table with Tomás's storm logs open in front of her — she had taken to reading them lately, the way one visits a grave, touching what he had touched.

The tavern argument followed her home. It always did; the town had a way of seeping into the lighthouse despite the climb, despite her deliberate distance. Eleven years of buying her flour and lamp oil and mutton from these people, of nodding at market, of standing at the edge of their festivals, and she still could not say whether they considered her one of theirs. Tomás had been. Tomás had been woven into the place like a warp thread. When he died, they had been kind to her — casseroles, condolences, Ines Cabral repairing the lighthouse door hinge without being asked and refusing payment — but kindness to a widow and belonging to a neighbor were different weathers entirely.

*Records.* She thought about the word again, turning it over. All lands, structures, vessels, and records. She owned no land. Her vessel was a tower on a rock. Her records were storms.

And yet tonight in the tavern, when Marta Vidal had stood up — that small fierce woman, straightening joint by joint like a ship raising sail — Cyrus had felt something move in her that she had not felt since Tomás died. Not agreement exactly. Recognition. The recognition of watching someone refuse, publicly, at cost, to become invisible.

She had wanted to speak. She had opened her mouth and found nothing there, because what claim did she have? She had no orchard rows in her memory, no workshop shares, no great-grandfathers arguing over wells. Her people were inland farmers three provinces away and then, abruptly, nowhere — her parents gone, her girlhood sold off with the farm. She had married into a town instead of being born from one, and a marriage gave you a bed, not roots.

But Marta had said: *the knowledge is in us.*

Was it? Was anything in her?

She looked down at the storm log. Tomás's handwriting marched across the page — barometric readings, wind directions, the neat annotations of a careful man — and beside the entries, in the margins, in a smaller script she had always assumed was calculations, something else. She had noticed it weeks ago and told herself it was arithmetic. Tonight, with the tavern still ringing in her ears, with *ledger* hanging in the air like the smell of smoke, she looked at the marginal marks properly for the first time.

They were not numbers.

They were names.

Small, cramped, patient names, marching down the margin of a page recording the great gale of November 1909 — Ferro, Cabral, Vidal, Almeida, Duarte — dozens of them, interspersed with abbreviations she didn't recognize, columned and ruled as carefully as a surveyor's plot. Page after page. Year after year of storm records, and threaded through them, hidden in plain sight in a tower only she inhabited, a census.

Cyrus's hand went cold on the page.

Tomás had been dead two winters, and he was showing her something.

* * *

In her narrow room above the cooperage, Marta Vidal did not sleep.

She lay listening to the wind work at the shutters and thought about her grandmother, who had been nine years old the year the soldiers stripped the orchards. Nine years old, standing at a window watching strange men shake the trees her family had tended for sixty years, filling sacks with fruit that was the whole of the winter's keeping, and when her grandfather had run out shouting, a sergeant had laughed and said, *Prove it's yours, old man.*

That was the story. That sentence was the seed of everything — the ledger, the seals, the workshop press with its brass dies that stamped authenticity into paper, the whole quiet apparatus this town had built against the sentence *prove it's yours*. Marta had heard the story so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and she had passed it on worn smooth too, forgetting sometimes that it was not a fable but a wound.

Tomorrow she would go to the others. To Ines, who had steel under her quiet. To Joaquim, whose cooperage held barrels enough to hide things in. Even to the boy Rolo, who ran errands for the soldiers now with stars in his eyes and needed, badly, to learn what uniforms were for. And perhaps —

Perhaps even to the lighthouse woman.

Marta turned onto her side. She had never been unkind to Cyrus Reyes, precisely. She had simply never counted her, the way one does not count a guest when tallying the household. But the woman had stood at the edge of the tavern crowd tonight with her keeper's stillness and her dead husband's silence around her like a coat, and when Marta had said *we write another*, Cyrus's eyes had not slid away the way the others' did. They had sharpened. A woman who keeps a light alone for two winters knows how to watch, and how to be watched, and how to stand in a doorway between a storm and the people behind her.

Besides. Marta's grandmother had told her one other thing about that long-ago year, a detail she had always filed away as embroidery: that when the soldiers finally rowed away with their stolen fruit, the lighthouse keeper of that day had refused to light the lamp for them, and the cutter had spent half the night feeling along the coast in the dark, cursing, and had grounded a boat on the shoals for its insolence.

The keepers had always been part of it, in their fashion. Outsiders who chose the town, again and again, night after night. Perhaps that was a kind of belonging after all — not the kind you inherit, but the kind you perform until it becomes true.

Outside, the wind rose a register, and Marta closed her eyes.

* * *

At dawn, Cyrus climbed the hundred and eight steps to the gallery with the last of Tomás's marginal pages copied into her notebook, her heart hammering a rhythm she hadn't known since girlhood. The sea lay grey and glittering below, and beyond the point, where the naval cutter had anchored, she could see boats moving — soldiers, supplies, the machinery of administration assembling itself along her coastline.

She had not decided anything yet. She had only questions, and they burned: How many names? Why here? What had he been afraid of — or preparing for? Had he known, two winters ago, dying, that he was leaving her a message written in the only language the lighthouse spoke — patience, repetition, light falling nightly on what refuses to be seen?

She would need help to understand it. Which meant she would need people. Which meant the wall she had built between herself and this town would have to come down, stone by stone, in a season of soldiers.

Below her, on the cliff path, a figure was climbing — small, upright, unhurried, unmistakable. Old Marta Vidal, come up the headland at first light, carrying a covered basket and whatever intention she had formed in the dark.

Cyrus stood at the rail of the gallery and watched her come, and did not go down to meet her, and did not look away.
