## 5. Requisition

They came ashore on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the gulls had not yet decided whether the day was worth their voices.

Cyrus saw them first from the gallery — she saw everything first from the gallery; that was the nature of her work and the shape of her life. A launch pulled by six oars, cutting a thin white seam across the water between the shoals and the harbor mouth. Behind it, the naval cutter sat at anchor where the fishing boats usually moored in October, its gunmetal hull drinking the morning fog. She counted the men in the boat twice because the first count seemed impossible: twelve soldiers, rifles slung, kit bags between their knees like schoolboys' satchels.

She did not ring the bell. There was no bell to ring, no signal agreed upon, no plan at all, because until this moment the requisition notices nailed to the church door had been an abstraction — paper, ink, the government's handwriting. Now it was twelve men pulling toward her town with the unhurried rhythm of men who had done this before, in other places, to other towns.

The oars dipped and rose. The water off the point was glass-still, so still she could hear the creak of the thole pins carrying across the bay, a small wooden sound, almost domestic.

*History happens elsewhere,* she had told herself for eleven years, since the day Tomás brought her up the cliff path with her wedding trunk and told her she would learn to love the light. *To other people. In cities. In capitals.* But history was rowing toward the stone steps now, and it wore puttees, and one of the soldiers was young enough that his cap sat too large on his head, and another was whistling something tuneless under his breath as though this were any other morning's work.

Cyrus went down the stairs two at a time, which she never did, and stood at the base of the tower with her hands pressed flat against the cold granite, watching the launch come in.

* * *

Captain Ellery Hargrove arrived at the town hall at nine o'clock precisely, which the town would later agree was the most telling thing about him.

Cyrus was not there to see it. She heard about it in fragments through the morning, the way news moved through the town then — on foot, carried by children too quick to be stopped, embellished by each tongue it passed over. By noon she had three versions of the same hour. In one, the captain had demanded the keys to every building. In another he had apologized so many times that old Ferro the netmender had laughed at him. In the third — Marta Vidal's version, delivered flat and without ornament, which made it the most frightening — he had simply set a leather case on the clerk's counter, opened it, and begun to write.

"He didn't raise his voice," Marta said, finding Cyrus at the oil store where she drew her monthly ration. Marta stood in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat though the day was mild. "He didn't have to. He read out the inventory order like a lesson. Every building. Every boat. Every press, every lathe, every barrel of pitch." Her mouth worked around something bitter. "He knew our town better than we do, Cyrus. He had lists. Lists with our own street names on them, written in some office we'll never see, by some hand we'll never know."

"Did you speak to him?"

"I asked him if the Crown understood that these lands are held by deed and inheritance going back four generations." Marta's eyes were dry and terrible. "He said, 'Madam, I am instructed to inventory, not to adjudicate.' Those words. I've been turning them over all morning like stones. *Instructed to inventory, not to adjudicate.* As if we were a cargo."

After she left, Cyrus stood among the oil tins a long while, listening to the harbor sounds come faintly through the wall — the cutter's engine turning over, testing itself, a low animal note beneath the gulls.

* * *

It was the lamp oil that brought her face to face with him, and she resented that afterward — that it should be oil, her oil, the substance of her keeping, that made the introduction.

A corporal came to the light station at mid-afternoon with a requisition chit written in a beautiful copperplate hand: *One case lamp oil, drawn against stores, for officers' quarters, former customs house.* The former customs house had been empty for six years; now it would house the captain and two lieutenants. The corporal was polite in the way of a man following instructions about politeness, and Cyrus signed the chit because there was nothing else to do, and because refusing would only mean the oil was taken anyway, without signature, without her name anywhere in the transaction.

She carried the case down herself. Later she would tell herself she did it because she wanted to see the quarters, wanted to see with her own eyes what the army had made of the customs house — but the truth was simpler and worse. She wanted to look at him. You cannot hate a rumor properly. Rumors slip through the fingers. She needed a face to hang the dread on, the way a lamp needs a hook.

The customs house smelled of salt and disuse and, now, of boot polish. Soldiers had swept it, aired it, hung curtains that were clearly army issue, grey-green and wrong for the windows. Maps already covered one wall — maps of her coastline, charted in detail she had not known existed outside the lighthouse's own records. She stood in the doorway with the oil case on her hip and looked at those maps and felt something turn over slowly in her chest, like a boat coming about.

"You're the keeper."

He came out of the back room drying his hands on a towel, and she knew him at once, though she had never seen him — knew him the way you know a figure in a photograph you've studied. Captain Ellery Hargrove was perhaps fifty, tall but stooped at the shoulders as though he'd spent years ducking under things, with a long tired face and grey going white at the temples in patches, unevenly, as if the colour were leaving him in retreat. His uniform was immaculate and his eyes were bloodshot.

"Cyrus Reyes," she said. "Keeper of the light."

"Yes." He said it without looking at the tower behind her, which surprised her; most visitors looked, couldn't help themselves. "Reyes. Your husband was Tomás Reyes. He's entered in my predecessor's correspondence — there was some question years ago about the fog signal's maintenance schedule. Your husband answered it himself. Good hand, clear figures." He paused, and something flickered behind the fatigue. "I'm sorry. That was a long time ago, and I'm intruding on grief I don't understand."

"Two winters," Cyrus said. It came out flatter than she intended.

"Yes." He took the oil case from her — took it properly, with both hands, as though it might be heavy, though it was not — and set it inside the door. "Thank you for bringing it yourself. I'd have sent for it. I don't like sending for things when the person can choose to bring them instead. There's a difference, and I try to notice it." He said this without self-consciousness, the way a man recites a rule he has kept so long it has worn smooth. Then, gesturing at the maps: "You'll want to know what those are."

"I want to know why they're better charts than mine."

Something happened to his face then — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would have gone if the man had any left in stock. "Because the Admiralty pays draftsmen and you pay yourself in lamp oil. It isn't fair. Most of what's on those walls isn't." He straightened, and the weariness in him reassembled itself into posture, into rank. "I'll be inventorying the whole town over the next several days, Mrs. Reyes. Workshops first, then stores, then vessels. I'm told your light is already listed as a defended station, so you'll see soldiers at the tower within the week. They'll have orders regarding your supplies. Nothing will be taken that the light needs — I've written that into the schedule myself, initialled it, and my initials hold weight with the men. If anyone tells you otherwise, send for me."

"And if you're not here?"

"Then send for me anyway. I sleep badly. I'll hear you."

She had prepared, walking down the cliff path, a whole armory of cold sentences. She had rehearsed the particular tone Marta used, the one that made men look at their boots. And here was this exhausted officer offering her his sleeping hours as though they were a thing of value, telling her his initials would protect her lamp, being — there was no other word for it — *reasonable*, and she hated him for it with a clean, astonished hatred, because it is very hard to hate a man who is reasonable. Cruelty gives you somewhere to put your anger. Courtesy leaves it nowhere, and it turns inward, and it burns.

"Captain," she said, "do you know what this town is?"

He considered the question seriously, which she had not expected. He looked past her shoulder, out the door, toward the orchard rows visible on the far slope, the workshop chimneys, the roofs.

"No," he said. "I know what it contains. Tonnage, output, assessed value. I can tell you to the hundredweight what this town weighs." He rubbed his eyes with a thumb and forefinger, briefly, as though the gesture had escaped before he could stop it. "What it *is* — no. No one told me to find that out, and I find, at my age, that I've stopped looking for things I wasn't told to find. That's a fault, Mrs. Reyes. I recommend you never acquire it."

Then he thanked her again for the oil, and she walked back up the path with the afternoon wind pushing at her back like a hand, and she was angry, and she could not locate the anger's address.

* * *

That evening the town gathered in the tavern, and Cyrus went, though she was not often welcome there and knew it. The room was fuller than she had ever seen it — fishermen, orchard families, the workshop men with their burn-scarred forearms, women standing along the walls because every bench was taken. The lamps were lit and nobody spoke above a murmur, and the murmur had a sound she recognized from the storm logs: the sound of a barometer falling.

Marta Vidal stood near the fire and repeated what she'd learned at the town hall, item by item, her voice gaining iron with each line. The workshop presses. The cooperage. The orchard harvest, to be assessed at a price the army would set. The boats subject to inspection and possible impressment. And the records — the ledgers, the deeds, the town's papers — to be catalogued and transported for safekeeping.

"*Safekeeping,*" Marta said, and let the word sit in the smoke. "They will keep us safe from ourselves. They will carry away every page that says whose land this is, and when they are done keeping it safe, we will be tenants on our grandmothers' ground."

Old Ferro argued for compliance — sign what they ask, keep your head, wars end and paperwork returns. A younger man, Rolo's uncle, argued for flight, for the mountain tracks at night. Voices crossed and tangled. Cyrus stood by the door with her untouched cider and watched the captain's courtesy do its work even in his absence: because he had been courteous, the town could not yet make him a monster, and because it could not make him a monster, it could not yet make itself brave. Fear needs a face to push against. Hargrove had given them a reasonable man, and reasonableness, Cyrus thought, watching the argument fray, may be the most effective weapon ever issued to an occupying force.

She left before the arguing turned to shouting. Outside, the night was clear and cold, and the cutter rode at anchor with its riding lights burning, patient as a spider.

From the gallery, later, trimming the wick against the coming dark, she looked down at the customs house where a single window glowed yellow. He was awake, then. Working. Making his lists. Weighing her town to the hundredweight.

*He sleeps badly,* she thought. *I'll hear you.*

And she hated how much she believed him, and hated more the small treacherous part of her that had noted, filed away, the flicker in his face when he spoke of Tomás's handwriting — *good hand, clear figures* — as though the dead man were a colleague rather than a ghost. An enemy should arrive wearing cruelty like a uniform. This one had arrived tired, and polite, and human, and that was somehow the worst report the gallery had ever given her.

Below, far down the coast road, a second transport barge had appeared at the harbor mouth in the last light, riding low in the water, empty and waiting.

Waiting, she understood, to be filled.
