## 6. Seized

Marta Vidal had not wept at her husband's funeral.

She remembered this with a strange, cold clarity as she stood in the town hall square on the morning the soldiers came for the records — remembered standing beside the open grave in the churchyard above the orchard terraces, her hands folded, her spine straight as a fence post, while the priest spoke words that were meant to be about eternity and were really only about the hole in the ground. Forty years of widows' grief had passed through that square before her, and every one of them had let it show, and Marta had watched from the edges of those processions and promised herself, young and arrogant, that she would never give the weather of her face to a crowd. She had kept that promise through the fever that took two of her children. She had kept it through the winter the fishing fleet went down. She was seventy-one years old, and she had kept it so long that she sometimes wondered whether the promise itself had grown over the wellspring like scar tissue, whether there was anything down there anymore but stone.

She stood in the square now with her hands folded the same way they had been folded at the graveside, and she watched four soldiers carry the ledger out of the town hall on a stretcher, like a body.

* * *

They had come at dawn, but not rudely. That was the thing she could not stop turning over, the splinter in her mind: they had come politely. Captain Hargrove himself stood at the foot of the hall steps with his list in his hand, and he nodded to people by name where he knew them — he'd been here five days and already he knew names, which was either courtesy or strategy, and Marta suspected it was both — and his voice carried the flat gentleness of a man reading a burial service. The records would be transported for safekeeping, he said. For cataloguing. For the war effort. They would be inventoried and preserved.

*Preserved.* She wanted to ask him what he thought preservation meant, this man whose government had decided the town's existence was an inconvenience to be managed. But she said nothing. Her silence had been her armor for seven decades and it held.

Inside the hall, the soldiers moved with a terrible efficiency. They had crates — proper ones, pine, rope-handled, straw-packed — and they worked in pairs, lifting the document chests down from the iron shelving where three generations of clerks had stored them. Marta heard the sounds from the steps where she had planted herself at first light: the scrape of wood on wood, the muffled clink of seal-presses being laid in straw, the clerk's boy answering questions in a voice pitched too high. And underneath all of it, fainter than everything, the sound of paper. That dry, whispering rustle that was the sound of a town breathing out its whole life at once.

The Vidal name was in there. Of course it was; it was in there eleven times, in three different hands across ninety years — the orchard claim her grandmother's father had entered when the ledger was new and the ink had still smelled of the tannery, the workshop share bought back after the bad year, the boundary amendment argued and witnessed and signed. Every time a Vidal had needed to prove what was theirs, some clerk had opened one of those chests and found them waiting inside, patient as seeds. You did not think of it, most years. A name in a book is like a heart in a chest; you forget it is there until someone puts a hand on your ribs.

Now the hands were on the ribs, and they were lifting the whole thing out.

* * *

"Señora Vidal." It was the clerk's boy, Tomé, pale under his freckles, coming down the steps too fast. "They want the key to the map drawer. The tall one in the gray coat asked me and I told him I don't have it, and he looked at me like—" The boy swallowed. "You have it, don't you. The old key. Grandfather gave it you."

"I have it." She felt its weight in her pocket where it had lived since morning, since before dawn, since she had woken with the certainty of a woman who has dreamed of floodwater. A small iron key, older than the captain, older than the war, cut by a locksmith whose grave was up on the hill among the orchards. In the tall map drawer were the original surveys — the inked lines of every field line and water right, the drawings that made the ledger's numbers into land.

Tomé glanced behind him at the doorway. "If you don't give it, they'll break the lock. He said they would. He wasn't angry about it. That's what frightened me — he wasn't angry."

"No," Marta said. "They're never angry. Anger would mean it mattered to them."

She took the key out anyway. Not because she surrendered — she would need a long time yet to understand what she was doing, and even longer to forgive herself for the shape of it — but because she looked past the boy's shoulder, through the open doors of the town hall, and saw the barge at the quay, riding low and empty, and understood that keys did not stop barges. There was no lock in this town that could hold against the century that was arriving. What could be saved would have to be saved another way, and she did not know yet what that way was, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She walked up the steps past Tomé and into the hall herself.

* * *

Inside, the light came down through the high windows in long dusty columns, and the soldiers moved through the columns like men working a harvest that belonged to someone else. They were careful. That was the unbearable part. They wrapped the oldest volumes in sacking before they crated them. One of them — a boy no older than Rolo Ferro, freckled, solemn — actually blew dust from a cover before laying it down, an unconscious little tenderness, and Marta watched him do it and felt something crack somewhere below her sternum, quietly, the way ice cracks on a pond before anyone has fallen through.

Captain Hargrove stood at the clerk's desk with the inventory list, checking items off in a clear, unhurried hand. He looked up when she approached, and there was nothing cruel in his face, nothing gloating. Only tiredness, and the particular blankness of a man doing arithmetic he does not enjoy.

"Señora Vidal," he said. "I understand the map drawer is yours to open."

"It is mine to keep," she said. "But keeping is over, isn't it? That's the order."

He had the grace not to answer. He waited while she crossed to the tall cabinet against the north wall — the one piece of furniture in the hall older than the building around it, walnut gone black with age, carved along its crown with vines some dead craftsman had imagined growing forever — and she put the small iron key in the lock and turned it, and the drawer rolled open on its old runners with a sound like a long sigh.

Maps. Ninety years of maps, rolled in linen, each one labeled in a clerk's careful copperplate. *Vidal. Ferro. Almeida holdings, east terraces.* She touched none of them. She had promised herself she wouldn't, and she kept that promise too, though it cost more than the other had ever cost.

"These are the originals," Hargrove said, coming to stand beside her. He did not reach past her. He looked, and she saw him look properly — saw him read the labels, saw him register that the names on the maps were the names of the people he had spent five days requisitioning. Something moved in his face and was put away again, filed, locked. "They'll be returned," he said. "When the war is over. Everything is documented. Nothing will be lost."

"Do you know how many times this town has heard that sentence?" Marta said. She kept her voice level; she was proud of that, even then, even afterward. "Soldiers took the orchard fruit once, a lifetime ago. Left nothing. My grandfather stood where you're standing and was told it was temporary. So the town built this hall and filled these drawers, so it could never be temporary again. Paper doesn't weep, Captain. It just waits. Longer than armies."

Hargrove was quiet a moment. "I have orders," he said finally, and it was almost gentle, the way a tide is gentle going out.

"I know," Marta said. "Everyone here knows. That's what frightens us. Not the cruelty. The obedience."

She turned and walked out of the hall, down the steps, into the square, because she had calculated — without knowing she was calculating — exactly how long she could remain in that room before her face betrayed her, and the calculation had come up short.

* * *

They brought the ledger out at midmorning.

Not in a crate with the others. Two soldiers carried it on the stretcher — someone's idea of care, or perhaps just necessity, because the volume was enormous, oak boards and brass corners and a spine that had been repaired twice, once with leather and once, in the lean year, with sailcloth — and they carried it slow down the steps and across the cobbles toward the quay, and the whole town came out to watch it go.

That was the part no order had commanded. No officer had instructed the townspeople to line the street. They simply came, the way people come for a coffin: Ines Ferro from the forge with her sleeves still rolled from the morning's fire, ash on her forearms; Rolo Ferro hanging back at the corner with his hands jammed in his pockets, wearing the stricken face of a boy watching something he cannot name get taken; the fishwives with their baskets set down; old Afonso leaning on his stick; the priest without his vestments, hatless, which was as close to ceremony as he could manage uninvited. Even Cyrus Reyes came down from the lighthouse road and stood at the edge of the square, apart, the way she always stood apart, and Marta saw her and thought: *even the stranger knows what this is.*

A funeral. That was what it was. The town had buried men and women and children in ninety years, and it had never once lined the street for paper, and now it lined the street for paper, and every soul in that square understood why without having to say it. Because the ledger was not paper. The ledger was them. It was the proof, in ink and wax, that they had existed — that their grandparents had cleared the terraces and dug the wells and built the workshops and quarreled over boundaries and made peace over cider, that all of it had happened *here*, to *these* people, and could not be wished away by any government that found the fact inconvenient. Take the houses, take the boats, take the orchards themselves — the town would still be the town, because the ledger said it was. Take the ledger, and they became squatters in their own history. Ghosts with good posture.

The stretcher-bearers reached the quay. The barge lay against the pilings, and a gangplank ran down to the cobbles, and the soldiers began to carry the great book up the plank toward the hold where the other crates were already stacked and lashed.

And Marta Vidal, who had not wept at her husband's grave, who had not wept when the fever took the girls, who had stood dry-eyed through the worst winters the coast had ever thrown at her — Marta Vidal began to cry.

It did not feel like crying. It felt like the ice giving way. There was no warning, no gathering, no dignified brimming. One moment she was standing with her hands folded and the next her face was wet and her breath had gone ragged and a sound was coming out of her that she did not recognize as her own voice — low, cracked, animal, sixty years old if it was a day, because that was how long the scar tissue had held and it broke all at once, everywhere, without mercy. Ines Ferro was beside her then — Marta never learned how the woman crossed the square so fast — and Ines took her arm, and Marta gripped it hard enough to bruise and could not make herself let go.

"Señora," Ines said softly. "Señora, look away if you must—"

"No." The word tore out of her. "No. I'll watch. We should all watch. Someone should remember what it looks like, going away."

So she watched. She stood in the square of the town her family had helped build and she watched the soldiers carry her history up a gangplank into the belly of an empty barge, and she wept openly, horribly, in front of everyone, and discovered — this was the strangest discovery of her long armored life — that no one looked away from her. No one pitied her. Old Afonso took off his hat. The fishwives bowed their heads. Rolo Ferro, at the corner, wiped his face with his sleeve and pretended he hadn't. The town received her tears the way ground receives rain, and Marta understood, dimly, through the wreck of her composure, that her grief was not hers alone anymore and never had been; she had only been carrying the town's share along with her own, all this time, and the weight of it had finally come due.

The ledger disappeared into the hold. The hatch closed over it with a boom that rolled across the water and came back off the headland like an echo looking for a home.

Then the barge cast off, and the empty-and-no-longer-empty ship slid out past the harbor mouth into the bright indifferent morning, and the wake spread long and silver across the bay, and Marta stood in the ruins of her seventy-one years and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any soldier had:

*Paper doesn't drown. Paper can be copied.*

Her grandfather had built the ledger because soldiers had taken the orchard once. He had not stopped them taking it; he had made sure it could be proven afterwards. That was the inheritance. Not the land — the *record*. Not the walls — the names.

She dried her face with her sleeve, slowly, deliberately, in front of the whole square, and when her eyes were clear she found Ines still holding her arm, and beyond her, at the edge of things, the lighthouse keeper watching her with an expression Marta had never seen on that guarded face before — something awake, something lit.

"Marta," Ines murmured, "what are we going to do?"

Marta looked at the water where the barge had been, and the stone in her chest had changed weight overnight; it was no longer grief. Grief sits still. This had begun, very quietly, to move.

"We are going to need," she said, "to talk to Cyrus Reyes."
