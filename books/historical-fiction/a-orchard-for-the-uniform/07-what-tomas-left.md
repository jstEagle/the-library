## 7. What Tomás Left

The rain had come in off the sea at dusk and settled in for the night, a fine grey drizzle that beaded on the glass of the lamp room and ran down in long, patient lines. Cyrus Reyes had counted the hours by that sound for two winters now — the tick of water against glass, the slow rotation of the lamp above her head, the creak of the stairs she knew the way her tongue knew the roof of her own mouth.

She had been putting off the task for eleven days. She knew the number precisely because she was a keeper, and keepers counted things: hours of oil, turns of the clockwork, days since the soldiers came, days since the ledger went onto the barge with the rest of the town's memory. Eleven days of Marta Vidal's face when the crates went down the ramp, and eleven nights of telling herself she would sort Tomás's things *tomorrow*, and eleven mornings of finding a reason not to.

Tonight there was no reason left. The supply of excuses, like the supply of lamp oil, was finite, and she was a woman who did her arithmetic honestly.

The effects were in the second bedroom, the small room facing north that Tomás had used as a study and that she had used, since his death, as a place to store what she could not bear to look at and could not bring herself to throw away. She lit the little brass lamp on the desk — his lamp, his desk, his chair that still held the shape of him if she let herself imagine it — and stood a moment in the doorway with her hand on the frame, the way one stands at the edge of cold water.

"All right," she said aloud, to no one, which was how she said most things now. "All right."

* * *

Tomás Reyes had been a methodical man, and his method was everywhere in the room. His papers were squared. His pens were wrapped in oiled cloth. His boots stood side by side beneath the coat hook as if he had only stepped out to check the wind and would come back stamping and shaking off the rain, laughing at her for tidying his things, saying *Cyrus, mi vida, a man's disorder is his map of himself.*

He had died in the winter of his fifty-first year, quickly, in the boat shed, with the mending in his hands, and she had not been there. That was the fact she circled every night the way the lamp circled the dark: not that he was gone, but that she had been up in the light room with the fog signal going and had not heard him fall. She had come down at dawn and found him peaceful and cold, and the world had divided itself, cleanly, into the time before and the time after, and she had lived in the time after ever since.

She began with the desk because it was the smallest grief. Drawers of spare nibs. A tin of sealing wax, most of it used. His account books for the light station, kept in a hand so neat it looked printed. She stacked them by the door for the army inventory — Hargrove's clerks would want everything listed, everything weighed, everything owned — and tried to feel nothing about it, and failed, and went on anyway.

Under the account books was a bundle of letters tied with the green string he'd used for everything. Letters from his mother, dead now herself, from a town inland that Cyrus had never seen. Letters from a cousin in the shipping trade. She untied the string and read the first lines of two or three, and put them back, because grief was a tide and you learned to read its times or it took you under.

It was when she lifted the bundle out that she saw the false bottom of the drawer.

Not false, exactly. Tomás had been too careful a man for anything so theatrical. It was simply that the drawer's floor sat an inch higher than the wood beneath it should have allowed, and there was a seam, and when she pressed at the seam with her thumbnail — the way he had once shown her, years ago, laughing, how you opened the hidden compartments in the old sea chests, *you press where the wood wants to give* — the panel tipped up like a loose paving stone.

Beneath it lay a notebook.

Not his storm-log notebook. Not any notebook she recognized. It was a cheap thing, quarter-bound in grey card gone soft with handling, its cover warped by damp, and it had no title. She turned it over twice before she understood that the absence of a title was itself a kind of message. Tomás had labeled nothing in his life that he wished kept secret. The light station's oil was labeled. The boat shed's tools were labeled. The jars of her preserves in the cellar, which he could not have cooked to save his soul, were labeled in his tidy hand. This book had no label because this book was not meant to be found by anyone who did not already know what it was.

She sat down in his chair. It held the shape of him. She opened the notebook.

* * *

The first page was dated four years before his death.

She knew his handwriting the way she knew the sound of the fog bell — instantly, in the body, before the mind had finished its work. The small precise capitals. The way his *e*'s leaned like grass in a wind off the water. But the hand on this page was working faster than his account-book hand, working with a kind of hunger, and what it was writing was not accounts.

It was names.

*Vidal, Marta — orchard, east slope, 4.2 hectares, share of press, right of first milling — witnessed, J. Ferro, spring 1889.*

*Vidal, Marta — amended, autumn 1891: east slope reduced, see p. 41, dispute settled by council.*

*Ferro, Joaquim — boatwright's yard and slip, with rights of way to the strand — witnessed, M. Vidal.*

*Cordero, Ines (née Cordero) — smithy, half-share, held in trust for minor son — witnessed —*

The page went on. Page after page after page. Orchard rows and workshop shares, water rights and milling rights, the small precise architecture of a town's ownership laid out in her dead husband's hand, copied — she understood it slowly, the understanding arriving like cold water rising in a hold — copied from the ledger.

From the ledger in the town hall. The ledger the clerk had joked about, eleven days and a lifetime ago, tapping its cover and saying *nobody reads this thing anymore, Mrs. Reyes, it's just furniture now.* The ledger the soldiers had carried down to the barge in crates stenciled with inventory numbers, while Marta Vidal stood on the quay and wept like a woman at a graveside.

Cyrus turned the pages with fingers that did not feel entirely like her own.

He had not copied all of it. She could see that already. The entries thinned and thickened, whole sections marked in the margin with his small capitals — *NOT COPIED — RISK* — and others marked *COPIED TWICE — VERIFY.* There were dates in the margins, dates stretching across four years, and she found herself reconstructing the pattern of them the way she reconstructed weather: a burst of entries in the spring of one year, nothing for months, then a run of nights, three dates together, the kind of dates that meant he had been up past midnight doing it, doing it while she slept ten feet away, doing it while she —

While she what? While she kept the lamp. While she trimmed the wick and wound the clockwork and believed, with the whole of her competent lonely heart, that history happened elsewhere, to other people, to towns with harbors worth taking and families with names worth stealing. While her husband lay beside her in the dark with a stolen town folded under his pillow.

*Stolen.* The word arrived and she flinched from it, because Tomás had not stolen anything. He had *copied.* He had copied a public record in a public building that any citizen might read, except that no citizen did, except that the clerk called it furniture, except that now an army had carried it away and the town's proof of itself had gone with it, and Tomás — methodical, quiet Tomás, who had lived in this town twenty-two years and been tolerated by it the way she had been tolerated, the outsider's outsider, the mainland man who married the lighthouse woman — Tomás had known.

He had known before any of them. That was the thought that sat down in her chest and would not get up. He had looked at that ledger full of small names and seen what she had never let herself see: that a record no one reads is a record that can be taken, and that a town which cannot prove it owns itself can be owned by anyone who arrives with a stamp and a requisition form. He had seen it years before the cutters came. And he had said nothing. To her. To anyone.

She set the notebook down on the desk, carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

* * *

The rain thickened. She got up and checked the lamp because the body does its duties even when the mind has gone to pieces — a habit, a mercy, a rope to hold. The wick was true. The rotation was true. Below her the town lay under its wet roofs, the tavern dark, the church dark, the workshop dark, and somewhere in the dark of it Marta Vidal was sleeping in a house that was no longer, on any paper that mattered, hers.

Cyrus came back down the stairs and did not go to bed. She sat in Tomás's chair and read the notebook from the beginning, properly this time, the way she read anything that mattered: twice, slowly, with a pencil in her hand.

The earliest entries were careful, almost tentative, as if he were learning the ledger's grammar. Then the hand steadied and the entries came in runs, and she began to see the system of it. He had not copied in the ledger's order. He had copied by family — all the Vidal entries together, all the Ferro, all the Cordero — and in the margins, in his smallest hand, he had written the things the ledger itself did not say. *Marta's father planted the east slope himself, 1861, from cuttings off the old trees.* *The Ferro slip rebuilt after the '78 gale, whole town turned out, see council minutes.* *Old Cordero signed over half the smithy to keep the forge lit through the bad winter — the ledger calls it a sale; it was not a sale.*

He had copied the town and then, around the town, he had copied its heart.

And interleaved between the pages — she found them as she went, thin sheets folded into the notebook like pressed flowers — were the storm logs. Not copies of them. The actual logs, the station's own records, the ones she signed every month and filed on the shelf in the light room, the ones she had *read*, or believed she had read, a hundred times. But these were older ones, ones from before her time as keeper, from Tomás's years, and when she held them to the lamp she saw why he had kept them here.

The storm logs were full of his handwriting. That was nothing; he had written them. But between the barometer readings and the wind speeds, in the columns where a keeper notes *visibility* or *remarks*, Tomás had written remarks. Small ones. Innocent ones, if you did not know. *Visibility poor. Ledger p. 41 verified against council minutes, agrees.* *Wind backing SW. Copied Vidal amendments, 3 pages. Oil low.* *Visibility good. Finished Ferro section. She suspects nothing.*

*She suspects nothing.*

Cyrus read that line four times. The pencil in her hand made a small mark on the page before she stopped herself. *She suspects nothing.* He had written it the way he wrote the barometer readings, flat and factual, a condition of the weather. And she understood that for four years she had been a line in her husband's secret arithmetic — a variable, a constant, the wife who does not ask, the wife who believes the study door is closed for the accounts — and that he had loved her, she never doubted he had loved her, and he had still not trusted her with this. Or had not dared to. Or had decided, in his methodical way, that the safest place for a secret was inside a woman the whole town had already decided saw nothing but her lamp.

The betrayal did not come all at once. It came the way the tide comes, in small pushes, each one seeming to recede before the next arrives higher. She was angry at him for the secrecy. Then she was angry at the town for making secrecy necessary. Then she was angry at herself for the years of *she suspects nothing*, for the competent loneliness she had worn like a uniform, for believing that history happened elsewhere — because Tomás had known it did not, Tomás had known it was *here*, in the orchard rows and the smithy shares and the press rights, and he had fought for it alone in a cold study while she slept, and now he was dead and the ledger was gone and the fight had fallen to the one person he had never told there was a fight.

She got up again. She made tea she did not drink. She sat back down.

* * *

Toward the small hours, the rain eased, and she found the last written page, and it was not like the others.

It was dated eleven days before his death. The hand was his, but slower, the letters spaced like a man choosing each one. There was no barometric remark, no wind, no visibility. There was only this:

*The ledger is furniture until the day it is the only thing. I have watched the clerk joke about it for years and I have said nothing, because a man who says the dam is cracked becomes the man responsible for the flood. I am a coward in the small ways and I know it. But the copy is nearly done, and when it is done I will tell her. She keeps the light; she will understand that a record is a kind of light. You keep it burning so that ships can find the shore. You keep the names so that people can find themselves.*

*If I do not tell her — if it falls to her to find it — she will forgive me. She is better than I deserve. And she will finish it, because she is a keeper, and keepers do not leave a light half-lit.*

Cyrus read it once. Then she read it aloud, to the empty room, in a voice that broke on the second sentence and did not entirely recover, and the saying of it was somehow the thing that turned the night.

Because he had *known*. He had sat in this chair with his heart already failing in him — she would learn later that the doctors said it had been failing for months, that the breathlessness he blamed on the stairs was something else — and he had written her a letter he never sent, an instruction he never gave, and he had trusted her to find it. Not by accident. He had trusted her the way you trust a keeper: to keep the watch, to notice the thing out of place, to press where the wood wants to give.

*She suspects nothing* had been the old entry. *She is a keeper* was the last one. Between those two lines lay the whole distance he had traveled in his love for her, and she had only ever seen the first one, and she wept for that, briefly and without shame, alone in the room that smelled of his ink and his oil and his forty years of the sea.

Then she stopped weeping, because there was work.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and rinsed. She had not slept. She sat at the desk with the notebook open before her and made, in her own hand, on a fresh sheet, a list — because she was a keeper, and keepers counted things, and the counting was the only prayer she had ever learned.

*Pages copied: by her rough tally, perhaps three-fifths of the ledger.* *Sections missing: the workshop families' claims — she had found the gap already, a stretch of margins reading NOT COPIED — RISK in a cluster, as if those pages had been watched too closely by someone; the later amendments; the final register of signatures.* *The original: on a barge, or already inland, in a crate with a stencil number.* *Time: unknown, but the army did not requisition towns to give them back.*

And one more line, which she wrote last and underlined twice:

*Who else knew?*

Because Tomás had not worked entirely alone — she saw it now in the margins, in the little attributions, *verified against Ferro's papers*, *M. V. confirmed the 1891 dispute from memory* — *M. V.* Marta Vidal. Marta, who had stood on the quay and wept for paper. Marta, who had shouted in the tavern that the ledger proved the land was theirs by right, with a certainty that had sounded, even in her grief, like a woman reciting something she knew by heart.

Cyrus closed the notebook and held it a moment in both hands, feeling its weight, the weight of a town in grey card and cheap paper, and understood that she was holding something that men in uniform had already carried away once and would carry away again if they knew it existed. Something worth lying for. Perhaps worth worse than lying.

Outside, the light went pale against the brightening east, and she rose to put it out, and the routine of it steadied her — wick down, lamp out, glass cleaned, the day's first entries in the log. But when she came to the remarks column her pencil hovered, and then she wrote, in her own hand, in her own plain style, the first entry of a new record that no one would understand but the people who needed to:

*Visibility clearing. Found what he left. It is not finished. We will finish it.*

She blotted the page. Then she took down her coat, and the notebook, wrapped in oilcloth from the boat shed, and set out down the cliff path toward the town, toward the east slope, toward the house of Marta Vidal — to ask an old woman what her husband had never told her, and to tell her what he had.
