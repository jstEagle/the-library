## 8. The Hidden Copy

Marta Vidal received her standing in the doorway like a fortress receives a herald: without surprise, without welcome, and without any intention of yielding ground.

"You're the lightkeeper's widow," the old woman said. It was not a question. Nothing about Marta Vida was ever a question. Her hair was white as gull-wing and bound so tightly it seemed carved onto her skull, and her hands — enormous hands, orchard hands, hands that had stripped a thousand trees of their fruit — were folded over a stick of blackthorn as though the stick had asked for protection rather than the reverse.

"Tomás Reyes was my husband," Cyrus said. "He died two winters ago."

"I know when he died. I know when everyone dies in this town. That is what you've come to learn, isn't it?" Marta stepped back from the threshold, which was, Cyrus would understand later, the closest thing to an embrace the woman offered anyone. "Come in before the wind decides to come in with you."

The house smelled of woodsmoke and dried figs and something older beneath both — paper, maybe, or the memory of paper. Marta put water on to heat without asking whether Cyrus wanted anything at all, and then sat opposite her at a table scarred by four generations of knives, and waited. She was very good at waiting. Cyrus understood that she herself was being weighed on some scale whose markings only Marta could read, and that the reading would take exactly as long as it took.

"He copied the ledger," Cyrus said finally. She unwrapped the oilcloth and set the notebook between them. "In the storm logs. Years of them. Pages and pages — family names, parcel numbers, shares of the workshop. He never told me."

Marta did not touch the notebook. She looked at it the way one looks at a grave that has been wrongly marked.

"He wouldn't have told you," she said. "Telling a wife makes her a witness, and witnesses get asked questions, and questions asked under a lantern in a guardhouse come out wrong. He was protecting you." A pause, dry as August. "Or he didn't trust you. With Tomás it was usually both."

Cyrus felt the barb go in and decided, deliberately, not to bleed where this woman could see it. "The soldiers took the original. Yesterday. On the barge, with everything else from the town hall."

"I watched them carry it out like a corpse." Marta's voice did not change at all, which was somehow worse than weeping. "Forty years that book sat in that hall. Every family on this slope and the next one, every row of every orchard, every share of the press and the vats — written down so no officer, no magistrate, no man in a uniform could stand on our land and say *prove it*. And they carried it out in a crate stenciled with a number." Her knuckles whitened on the blackthorn. "So. What did he leave you?"

"Not enough." Cyrus opened the notebook to the last page in Tomás's hand — the page she had found at dawn, the one that had sent her down the cliff path with her heart going like a ship's engine. "There are notations. Margins, dates, references to pages of the ledger I've never seen. And this—" She turned the notebook toward the window light. At the bottom of the page, in handwriting smaller than any other on the sheet, almost furtive: ***The rest sleeps where the rain comes in. Lead keeps secrets better than stone.***

Marta read it twice. When she looked up, something had shifted behind her face — not softening, Cyrus thought, but repositioning, the way ballast shifts in a hull.

"The lamp room," Marta said. "The flashing on the seaward side. It always worked loose in the equinox gales; your husband used to climb out and reseal it himself, wouldn't let the mason near it. We thought him particular." For the first time, the old woman's mouth moved in a way that might once, decades ago, have been the beginning of a smile. "Particular men keep this town alive."

* * *

Cyrus climbed back up the cliff path with the wind at her back pushing her along like a hand between the shoulder blades, and all the way up she rehearsed nothing, thought nothing, felt only the notebook against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The afternoon was failing into that particular pewter light that meant weather gathering somewhere beyond the horizon's sight-line. The barometer in the gallery had been sliding for two days. She noted it automatically, keeper's habit, and kept climbing.

The lamp room at dusk was her oldest ritual: glass cleaned, brass polished, wick trimmed, the great lens turning its slow silent vigil above a coast that had never once thanked her for it. Tonight she performed each task with hands that wanted to hurry and forced themselves not to. Hurried hands dropped things. Hurried hands were seen.

She waited until full dark, until the light was established and turning, until the last fishing boat had made the harbor mouth and the army camp below was a scatter of cook-fires like fallen stars. Then she took the storm lantern, unshuttered, and went to work on the seaward side of the gallery deck.

The lead flashing ran in sheets along the joint where the gallery rail met the tower wall, tucked and soldered by some Victorian craftsman long dead. Most of it sat flush and honest. But at the north quadrant — the quadrant that caught the equinox storms full in the face, the quadrant Tomás had always resealed alone — the third sheet stood proud of its bed by the width of a coin.

She got her fingernail under the edge. The solder had been cut and rejoined so cleanly, so many times, that the seam was invisible unless you knew to look — and even now it resisted her, the metal remembering forty years of Tomás's careful hands and two winters of neglect. She fetched the smallest pry from the gallery toolbox, worked it in gently, gently, the way he must have done it, and the sheet lifted with a sound like a held breath released.

Behind the lead, set into the cavity between flashing and masonry, lay six oilskin packets.

They were flat, rectangular, each about the size of a psalter, each tied with waxed cord, each labeled in Tomás's upright hand: *Ledger — folios 1 through 40. Ledger — 41 through 88. Ledger — 89 through 140.* And so on, to the sixth, which read simply: *Remainder.* They were cold from the stone and dry as autumn leaves. The oilskin had done its work through two winters and who knew how many before that; when she pressed a packet between her palms there was no give of damp, no smell of mold, only the faint animal smell of dressed hide and the ghost of ink.

Cyrus sat down on the gallery deck with her back against the tower wall and the six packets in her lap, and for a long moment she could do nothing at all.

She had expected — she realized now, in the way one realizes things only after they are already true — she had expected papers, plural, scattered, partial, hopeful. What she held was systematic. This was not a man scribbling at a hobby. This was an archive, built in secret, sealed against salt and storm, maintained across years of Sunday climbs and resealings, growing packet by packet while his wife trimmed his wicks beside him and never once saw his hands move.

She untied the first cord.

The pages inside were writing paper of good stock, ruled faintly in blue, filled edge to edge with Tomás's small precise script — the same hand from the storm logs, but here steadier, more patient, a hand writing not observations to be read in haste but records to be trusted for generations. Family name at the head of each entry. Parcel boundaries described by landmarks she knew intimately: *from the split granite by the well path to the third olive south of the Vidal boundary wall.* Orchard rows numbered. Workshop shares listed in fractions — eighths, sixteenths, one stubborn thirty-second belonging to a family whose name Cyrus had seen on a boat, rotting in the harbor mud since before her time. Dates of registration. Dates of amendment. In the margins, occasionally, Tomás had added notes of his own in pencil, lighter, more human: *Old Ferro's claim disputed by his brother, settled 1891, see folio 12.* Or, once, simply: *Marta wept at this signing. First and only time.*

She turned page after page while the lens turned above her, throwing its beam seaward and around, seaward and around, and the town below slept in the lee of its hill not knowing that its whole history lay in a widow's lap forty feet above its rooftops.

Folios one through forty. Forty-one through eighty-eight. She counted, checked the labels against the contents, checked the contents against the notations in the storm-log notebook. Somewhere past the middle of the fourth packet the arithmetic began to trouble her, and by the fifth packet she was counting twice, three times, running her finger down the folio numbers like a woman searching a crowd for a face that isn't there.

The sixth packet — *Remainder* — confirmed it.

It was thinner than the others. Inside were fewer than twenty folios, and they ended abruptly, mid-entry, the final line trailing off with a penciled mark Tomás had used elsewhere to mean *continued*: a short horizontal stroke, like a horizon. But there was nothing continued after it. No seventh packet behind the flashing — she pried loose every sheet along the whole seaward run, her fingers numb with cold and urgency, finding only stone and mortar and a single dried starfish wedged in a joint like a joke left by the sea.

The workshop families' folios were gone. Not missing by accident — Tomás's labels accounted for every packet he had made — but absent, unfinished, the transcription broken off at the exact place where the record became most valuable: the claims of the cooperage families, the press shares, the vat rights, the whole industrial spine of the town. Everything Cyrus had glimpsed that morning in the town hall, everything the soldiers had crated and barged away, everything that gave the orchard families' names their meaning — that portion existed here only as a horizon line and silence.

She sat with it until her teeth began to chatter, and could not entirely blame the cold.

* * *

Later — much later, the lamp burning steady above her, the night gone properly black and smelling of coming rain — Cyrus spread the packets across the gallery floor inside the circle of the storm lantern's light and tried to think like her husband.

He had been careful. Careful men did not lose things; careful men *allocated* them. Six packets, six hiding places? No — she had searched the flashing thoroughly. But the lighthouse was old and Tomás had been its keeper for eleven years and its student for longer than that, courting her during those first years with facts about it the way other men courted with flowers. *Do you know the cistern holds nine hundred gallons? Do you know there's a room under the oil store the Board forgot to put on the plans?*

She went down the stairs slowly, lantern high, looking at her own home as if for the first time. The walls were granite five feet thick. There were service cavities, old flues, a disused ventilation shaft from the days of argand lamps that now went nowhere and held nothing except, at its mouth, a wooden grille Tomás had refastened himself the spring before he died. She remembered because she had teased him about it. *Fixing a vent that vents nothing,* she'd said, and he'd smiled in that closed way of his and said, *Everything in this tower does something, Cy. Even the things that look like they don't.*

The grille came away after ten minutes of work she would feel in her shoulders tomorrow. Behind it the shaft dropped away into darkness, and her lantern light found, three feet down on a stone ledge, a canvas satchel, dry, buckled shut.

Her heart slammed so hard she had to put her hand flat against the cold wall.

Inside the satchel: no papers. Instead, a leather wallet of tools — fine blades, a bone folder, a jar of powdered pounce, a penknife whose handle was worn to the shape of Tomás's grip — and beneath the tools, folded once, a single sheet of heavy paper bearing the watermark of the county stationer in the port town, the kind of stock official documents were printed on. Blank. Pristine. A dozen more sheets like it, rolled and tied.

And a note, in the small furtive hand:

*Cy — if you're reading this, then either I'm dead or careless, and I was never careless. The blank stock is for finishing what the packets start. A copy is only paper until it's sealed and struck on proper stock; then it's a record, and records can be entered, and entered records cannot be quietly drowned. The workshop press does the striking. You know where it is. You know who remembers how it works. Don't trust the clerk. Trust Marta. Trust the ones whose names are in the book, because they are the only people alive with a reason to protect it. I'm sorry for the half of it you'll have to finish. I ran out of winters. — T.*

Cyrus read it four times. The lens turned above her. Below, in the dark, the army camp fires burned down one by one, and somewhere out on the water the supply barge rode at anchor, heavy with a town's stolen proof.

*A copy is only paper until it's sealed.*

She looked down into the shaft, at the tools of a trade she hadn't known her husband practiced, at the blank official stock that could turn ink into law, and she understood at last the shape of what Tomás had built and what he had left unfinished, and why the workshop — with its press and its seals and its strike-plates for every registration year back to the founding — was not merely a building the army happened to want.

It was the other half of the archive. And the army was going to knock it into rubble in six weeks.

Cyrus folded the note along its existing creases — Tomás had creased it, meaning he had read it over himself, meaning he had rehearsed this moment, meaning some part of him had stood where she stood and imagined her hands shaking exactly as hers were shaking now — and she put it away inside her bodice, against her skin, where it seemed to burn.

Then she stood a long time at the gallery rail, looking down at the sleeping town, at the dark shapes of the orchards on the east slope, at the workshop's long roofline just visible against the harbor water. Worth killing for. The thought arrived without drama, the way true thoughts do, plain as a tide table. Men in uniforms had already carried off the original without a struggle; the duplicate in her keeping was worth precisely as much as nobody knew about it, and would be worth her life the moment anyone did.

She thought of Rolo, the apprentice boy who idolized the soldiers. She thought of Ines, the blacksmith's widow, who had not spoken a full sentence to her since the funeral. She thought of Marta Vidal's folded hands and her scale with its unreadable markings.

Trust the ones whose names are in the book.

The barometer had fallen another tenth of an inch. Rain reached the coast before midnight, fine and needling, and Cyrus went down into the tower and wrote in the storm log, in her own hand, in the column reserved for remarks: *Visibility poor. Wind backing. Found the remainder of what he left. It is not finished either. Nothing he built was finished. We will finish all of it.*

Then she blew out the working lamp, and in the dark she began, silently, to make a list of names — and discovered that the first name on it was not Marta's at all, but a boy's, and that she had already decided, without quite admitting it, that she would need to find young Rolo before the soldiers did.
