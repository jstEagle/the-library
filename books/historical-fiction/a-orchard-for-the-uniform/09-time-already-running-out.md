## 9. Time Already Running Out

The orders arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside the usual dispatch of supply manifests and personnel rotations, as though the demolition of a working town were a matter no more consequential than the movement of boots and biscuit tins. Captain Ellery Hargrove read it standing at the window of what had been, until six weeks ago, the harbor master's office — a room with a good view of the water and a bad smell of old tobacco that no amount of scrubbing would lift — and he read it twice, because the first time his eyes had refused to accept the plain sense of the words.

*By direction of the War Department: all structures within the designated coastal zone not required for military use are to be cleared by the first week of November. Priority to the workshop complex at the head of the mill road.*

He set the paper down on the desk, which was someone else's desk, in a chair that was someone else's chair, beneath a map of a coastline that had belonged, until very recently, to people whose names he was still learning.

Six weeks. The workshop.

He went to the window again. From here he could see the whole sweep of the harbor, the gray water going silver where the morning light caught it, the fishing boats hauled up and silent since the requisition, their owners forbidden to put out past the buoy line. And above the rooftops, half-hidden behind the smoke of the bakery ovens the army had kept running because soldiers needed bread even if townspeople were being fed from requisition stores, he could see the long slate roof of the workshop complex, its chimney standing against the sky like a finger raised in perpetual objection.

Hargrove had spent nineteen years in uniform learning to distinguish between orders he agreed with and orders he obeyed. The two categories overlapped far less often than the recruiting posters suggested. But there had always been a third category, thin and dangerous as ice over deep water: orders that made him doubt whether the man signing them had ever stood where Ellery Hargrove was standing, smelled what he was smelling, seen what he was seeing.

This order belonged in the third category.

* * *

The workshop complex at the head of the mill road was not one building but five, joined and jumbled together across a century of additions — the foundry with its tall windows, the press house, the drying lofts, the seal room where the town's official stamps and matrices were kept under lock, and the long shed where apprentices had learned their trades for three generations. It had been the heart of the place. Every barrel hoop, every boat fitting, every printed notice and sealed document in the district had come out of those buildings. When Hargrove had walked through it during the initial inventory, his surveyor's pencil had paused over the entry for the press equipment and written *irreplaceable* before he could stop himself, and then, being an honest man even in small things, he had not crossed it out.

Now the War Department proposed to replace it with rubble.

He summoned Sergeant Dawes and Lieutenant Pell, who arrived in his office within ten minutes, Pell flushed from running up the hill, Dawes moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had learned that haste solved nothing.

"Read this," Hargrove said, and passed the telegram across the desk.

Pell read it fast, the way young officers read everything, and looked up bright-eyed. "Demolition detail, sir? We've the sappers coming through on the transport anyway — efficient timing."

"Efficient," Hargrove repeated, without inflection.

Dawes read it slower. The sergeant was forty-one, older than his rank suggested he'd ever be willing to be, and he read documents the way he did everything else — thoroughly, and with visible reservations held in reserve. He lowered the paper. "Workshop, sir? That's the building with the presses. The seals."

"The seals," Hargrove confirmed. "Every official matrix for the district. Property registers, transfer deeds, authentication stamps. If a document from this coast is to mean anything in a court or a registry anywhere in the country, it has to carry one of those seals."

"And they want it pulled down." Dawes handed the paper back. "Before winter."

"Before winter. Which means before the storms close the harbor, which means the timing is deliberate. Someone wants this done while the shipping lanes are still open." Hargrove stood and took his coat from the peg. "Someone also wants the seals out of existence before anyone has occasion to need them. I'd like to know who, and I'd like to know why, but neither question appears on the telegram. So we will do what the telegram says, and we will do it correctly, and we will do it in a manner that leaves nothing for anyone to reproach us with later." He settled his cap. "Come and look at the building with me. I want a full structural assessment before I sign anything."

"Sir?" Pell, falling into step beside him. "Is there — forgive me, sir, but is there some question about the order?"

"There is always a question about an order, Lieutenant. That's what makes them orders." Hargrove pushed open the door into the cold bright morning. "The question is never whether we'll obey. The question is what obeying will have cost by the time it's finished."

* * *

They walked down through the town, and Hargrove made himself see it the way he had tried not to see it during the inventory: not as a requisitioned asset, a column of figures, but as a thing that people had built and mended and argued over for longer than any of them had been alive. The orchards on the slopes above the mill road stood heavy with late fruit nobody was allowed to pick; the army had bought the harvest at a fixed price and shipped it south, and now the apples fell and rotted where they landed, sweetening the ditches, feeding wasps in numbers that made the air hum. A woman was sweeping her doorstep with the particular violence of the dispossessed — clean, clean, keep something clean — and she did not look up as the three uniforms passed.

The workshop doors stood open, as they had since the requisition, guarded by a single bored private who came to attention so abruptly he nearly dropped his rifle.

Inside, the smell hit him the way it always did: oil and iron and cold ash and, underneath it, something like paper and glue, the smell of a hundred years of making. Shafts hung motionless where the belt-drive had been shut down. Tools lay where their owners had left them on the last morning — Hargrove had ordered that nothing be touched, partly because inventory required it and partly, though he had not admitted this to himself at the time, because a workshop abandoned mid-task seemed less like confiscated property than like a crew that had stepped out and might come back. There was a half-finished hinge on the bench nearest the door. There was a mug, glazed blue, with tea gone to black varnish in the bottom.

"The structure itself," Dawes was saying, walking the central aisle with his lantern raised, tapping walls with a knuckle like a physician percussing a chest. "Stone below, brick above, roof trusses sound as anything. This'll stand another hundred years if you ask it to. Demolition won't be hard work — but it'll be a shame, sir, if you don't mind my saying."

"I mind your saying it precisely because you're right," Hargrove said. "Show me the seal room."

It was at the back, past the press house, a low stone-vaulted chamber with iron-bound door and a lock that had defeated the army locksmith twice before he'd conceded it could only be opened by the key, which was held — had been held — by the town clerk. The clerk was a dying man now, bedridden in the house on Chapel Lane, and the key sat in Hargrove's own strongbox, catalogued as *item 114, brass key, provenance uncertain*. Provenance uncertain. As if a thing could lose its history by changing hands.

Pell unlocked the door — Hargrove watched him do it, and noted, in the corner of his mind where he filed such observations, how casually the lieutenant handled the key, how little ceremony attended the opening of a room that had been opened perhaps four times a year for a century.

Inside, on shelves cut directly into the stone, lay the matrices. Hargrove lifted one down and turned it to the light: a steel die the size of his palm, its face engraved with the town's arms — an orchard tree, a lamp, a wave — and around the rim, in letters worn nearly smooth by decades of pressing, the words that gave every document from this coast its legal weight. Beneath it, rows upon rows: smaller dies, counter-seals, the great press plate for land transfers, the registration stamp for births and deaths and marriages. Everything a community used to make its promises binding.

"You could forge half the county with these," Pell said, with the admiration of a young man for machinery of any kind.

"No," Hargrove said quietly. "You couldn't. You could *authenticate* half the county. There's a difference, Lieutenant, and it happens to be the entire difference between law and theft." He set the die back in its slot, aligning it exactly as it had lain. "Which is rather my point. These are the only instruments on this coast capable of making a document real. And in six weeks we're to melt them down with the rest of the scrap."

Dawes said nothing. Dawes was good at saying nothing. It was, Hargrove sometimes thought, the sergeant's principal contribution to the service.

* * *

That evening Hargrove wrote the reply to the War Department that he had been composing in his head since morning. He wrote it four times.

The first draft asked for clarification: *Request confirmation that demolition of workshop complex includes destruction of district seal matrices, given ongoing administrative requirements of occupation.* Too weak. They would confirm, and he would have his answer in writing, and then he would have to obey it with the paper staring at him forever after.

The second draft argued: the seals would be needed to process compensation claims, transfer titles, register the displaced population. Sound reasoning. Also transparent. Any staff officer worth his salt would read between the lines and conclude that Captain Hargrove had gone soft on the locals, and softness, once suspected, followed a man like a scent.

The third draft requested a delay on logistical grounds — sappers unavailable, weather unreliable, transport overloaded. Dishonest, and worse than dishonest, ineffective: the telegram had anticipated every excuse by ordering demolition *within six weeks*, a phrase that meant someone, somewhere, was counting.

He burned the first three drafts in the stove, which had been the harbor master's habit too, judging by the scorch marks, and sat looking at the fourth draft for a long time before he wrote it.

In the end he wrote what a soldier writes: *Orders received and understood. Demolition to commence on schedule. Requesting clarification regarding disposal of seal matrices and press equipment — destruction or removal?* Because that much, at least, was true. That much was procedure. If there was a reason behind the order — if someone above him wanted those seals dead for reasons of profit, or convenience, or the tidier erasure of claims that might someday be contested — then let it show itself. Let the reply come back in writing. Let there be paper. Paper was the only weapon a middle-aged captain with no connections and a reputation for exactness could hope to hold.

He sealed it, marked it for the morning transport, and set it on the corner of the desk with the outgoing mail. Then he took off his cap, which he had worn indoors for two hours without noticing, and pressed the crease flat, and felt suddenly and completely tired in a way that had nothing to do with his body.

There was a knock. Dawes, with the evening report and a cup of coffee he hadn't asked for.

"Anything?" Hargrove asked, meaning the town, meaning the mood, meaning all the things a commanding officer asks when he can't ask the real question.

"Quiet." Dawes set the cup down. "Fisherman's wife brought fish to the cookhouse again. Wouldn't take payment. Left before anyone could argue." He paused. "And the keeper's woman came by the hall this afternoon. Asking after records. Storage arrangements, that sort of thing."

"Cyrus Reyes." Hargrove had met her twice — once when arranging the oil contract for the light, once by accident on the cliff path, where she had looked through him with the flat courtesy of a woman deciding he was weather, to be endured until it passed. Widow of the previous keeper. Outsider, by marriage if not by birth; the townspeople were polite to her the way one is polite to a guest who has stayed too long. "What kind of records?"

"Storm logs, she said. Old ones. Wanted to know if the hall cellar was accessible." Dawes shrugged. "Told her the hall's sealed pending inventory. She thanked me very properly and went home."

"Hm." Hargrove turned his cup slowly on its saucer. It was nothing. People asked about records; a lighthouse keeper's widow had lived half her life among logs and registers and would think of them naturally. It was nothing, and he noted it anyway, because nineteen years had taught him that the things that turned out to matter almost never announced themselves as anything at all.

"Sir," Dawes said, at the door, not turning around. "The demolition order. The men have heard. Some of them are from farming stock, up-country. They're asking — the ones who talk to me are asking — whether the town gets told before the buildings come down. Properly told, I mean. With time."

"They get told when I tell them," Hargrove said. "Publicly, at the town hall, with the notice posted and read aloud, and with whatever time the schedule allows. We are not vandals, Sergeant. Whatever else this requisition is, the men under my command will conduct themselves as if the people watching them might one day write the account of it. Because they might."

"Yes sir." Dawes hesitated. "Only — six weeks isn't much of an account to write."

"Good night, Sergeant."

When the door closed, Hargrove sat alone with the lamp and the map and the smell of other men's tobacco, and he thought about the seal room, and the steel dies lying in their stone slots like sleeping animals, and the words worn smooth by a century of honest pressing. *An orchard tree, a lamp, a wave.* Somebody had designed that seal. Somebody had cut it, believing it would outlast him, believing that the promises it stamped would hold after everyone in the room was dust.

In six weeks he was going to help destroy it, unless the War Department answered his letter in a way that let him do otherwise, and he knew already, with the certainty that settles in the stomach before the mind consents, what the answer would be.

Outside, the light on the headland swept its slow white arm across the water — once, twice, patient as breathing — and Hargrove found himself watching it the way he had watched it every night since arriving, without quite knowing why, as though it were counting something, or keeping a promise, or waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

He snuffed the lamp and went to bed, and did not sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn he rose and wrote one more line to add to his letter before the transport left:

*Respectfully request that no destruction of records or matrices occur pending reply to this correspondence.*

Then he signed it, sealed it, and sent it into a machine that ate letters like that every day and returned, when it returned anything at all, the same word: *Proceed.*

---

*Six weeks,* he thought, standing at the window as the town woke below him, woodsmoke rising straight in the windless cold, a figure climbing the cliff path toward the light with a basket on her arm. *Time enough to tear it all down. Time enough, God willing, to find out why.*
