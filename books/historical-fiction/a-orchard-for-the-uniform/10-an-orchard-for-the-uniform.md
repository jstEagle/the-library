## 10. An Orchard for the Uniform

Marta Vidal had not walked the orchard rows since her husband's funeral, and even then she had gone only as far as the gate. The trees did not need her grief; they had their own seasons to keep. But this morning, with the requisition notices still pasted to the church door and curling at their edges like dead leaves, she went in among them anyway, because a woman who meant to speak of inheritance ought to stand on the ground it grew from.

The grass was wet through her boots. The air smelled of bruised windfall apples going soft beneath the trees — that sweet, fermenting rot that had scented every September of her seventy-three years. Above, the branches hung heavy, unharvested, because half the pickers had already taken army wages down the coast and the other half were too frightened to climb ladders while soldiers' cutters sat in the bay like patient gulls.

She stopped at the third row, where the oldest tree stood. It was older than the ledger, older than the town hall, older than any paper that had ever claimed to own anything. Her grandfather had been a boy when its first fruit came in. He had told her the story when she was small enough to sit inside his coat, and she had thought it only a story, the way children think everything is only a story until one morning they wake up old enough to be inside it themselves.

Marta put her hand against the bark. It was rough as an old woman's knuckle.

"You'll tell it today," she said to the tree, or perhaps to herself. "You've held it long enough."

* * *

They gathered in her kitchen at noon because her kitchen was the last place left that felt like hers. Ines Ferro came with her sleeves rolled and flour still on her forearms, having abandoned bread at some stage no baker would forgive. Rolo came skidding in from the harbor path with salt spray dried white on his collar and his eyes full of the cutter he'd been watching all morning, counting gun ports the way other boys counted stars. And Cyrus Reyes came last, hesitating at the threshold the way she always did, as if the town's doorways had never quite agreed to admit her.

"Sit," Marta said. "All of you. I'm going to tell you why there's a ledger."

Rolo opened his mouth — she could see the question forming, something about whether this was a lesson or a story or a waste of an afternoon when there was real trouble outside — and Marta looked at him until he closed it again. Some things you taught the young by letting silence do the work.

"My grandmother's grandmother planted these orchards," Marta began. "Before there was a town worth naming. Before the harbor wall, before the church, before anyone wrote anything down about us anywhere." She poured tea without asking, because asking would have made it a courtesy and this was not a courtesy; it was a testimony. "And for three generations we worked the land and kept what we grew, and nobody bothered us, because we were too far from anywhere important to matter."

"That sounds nice," Rolo said.

"It was," Marta said. "That's how I know it's true."

She let the tea steep. Outside, the wind had come around from the southeast, and it moved through the orchard with a sound like someone turning pages.

"The year my grandfather was fourteen — that's more than a hundred years ago now — the war came up the coast. Not our war. It never is. A fleet put in for water and provisions, and with the water they took the harvest. Every apple, every pear, every cask of cider pressed and waiting in the cellars. They took the winter grain from the granary. They took the salt fish hanging under the eaves of every smokehouse along the shore." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced levelness for so long it had become the shape of her mouth. "And when the officer was done, he gave my great-great-grandfather a paper. A receipt, he called it. For the uniform."

"For the—" Rolo frowned. "What uniform?"

"The army's uniform," Marta said. "The cloth, the buttons, the boots. That's what the receipt said the provisions were valued at. An orchard's worth of food, a winter's worth of grain, a whole town's labor — priced out against uniforms. As if we were owed coats." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "My grandfather kept that paper his whole life. Showed it to me when I was younger than you, Rolo. *Valued in kind against regimental clothing, per quartermaster's assessment.* His hands shook holding it, and he wasn't a man who shook. He said: *They didn't take our food, girl. They took the proof we ever grew it.*"

Ines had gone still, her flour-dusted hands flat on the table.

"So he made the ledger," Marta said. "Not him alone — the families together, everyone who'd lost something. Every orchard row measured and written down. Every workshop share, every press, every vat, every loom, every boat and net and drying rack. Who owned what. Who had earned what. Signed and witnessed and sealed at the town's press, because a seal is a promise made of wax and wax doesn't tremble." She touched the table's edge. "It took them two years. My grandfather copied pages till his fingers bled at the corners. He said writing your name down was the only weapon poor people ever got, and it was a good one, because it cut both ways — it proved what you owned, but it also proved you'd promised to be honest about it."

"They never came back?" Cyrus asked. She spoke rarely in gatherings like this; when she did, her voice had a careful quality, like someone setting glass down on stone.

"Oh, armies came back," Marta said. "Twice in my lifetime, before this one. Smaller parties, surveyors, assessors. And each time, the men who met them at the church door brought out the ledger and read aloud from it — *this row belongs to So-and-so, this press to Such-and-such* — until the officers understood that taking the town meant taking a hundred named families, and named things are harder to swallow than anonymous ones." She lifted her chin. "Paper is the difference between being robbed and being erased. Robbed, you can rebuild. Erased, there's nothing left to rebuild *from*."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the hill, faintly, a drill called and was answered.

"And now they have it," Ines said quietly. It was not a question. Nothing in this room was a question anymore; they had all stopped asking questions sometime in the last week, when the notices went up and the barges began loading.

"Now they have it," Marta agreed. "Carried off on a barge like so much scrap iron. And without it we are exactly what my grandfather feared: anonymous. A town with no names. Land that can be listed as *unclaimed improvement* and signed away by a clerk in a city none of us will ever see." She looked at each of them in turn. "I am telling you this so you understand what we're fighting for. Not the land. The land will grow apples for whoever stands on it. We're fighting for the *names*. Once those are gone, we were never here."

Cyrus's cup had stopped halfway to her lips. Marta watched her — the widow, the outsider, the woman who had kept the light through two winters alone and still flinched when townsfolk greeted her as though surprised she existed. There was something working behind those grey eyes, something Marta couldn't read yet, and it made her uneasy in a way she didn't show.

"Why are you telling us?" Rolo asked. "Really. Why now?"

"Because the young forget slowest," Marta said, "and because you asked me once, boy, why I wouldn't leave when the others started packing. Now you know. I am the last one alive who heard the story from the man whose hands bled copying it. When I go, if no one else holds it, it goes with me." She rose, stiff-kneed, and went to the dresser, and took from its lowest drawer a thing wrapped in linen.

She unwrapped it on the table. A sheet of paper, brown as autumn, crackled and soft with age. Faint ink, faded to the color of weak tea. *Valued in kind against regimental clothing.*

Nobody touched it. Nobody breathed near it.

"This is the receipt," Marta said. "This is the debt. And somewhere between this table and whatever office Captain Hargrove answers to, that debt is going to come due." She folded the linen back over it with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid over someone you loved. "I don't know how yet. But debts don't die just because the paper leaves town. I've outlived three priests and two wars, and I promise you that much."

* * *

They left one by one through the afternoon, and Marta stood in her doorway watching them go down the hill toward a town that was already learning to walk carefully past its own soldiers.

Ines went first, back to her bread, though Marta noticed the widow paused at the gate and looked back at the house a long moment, as if memorizing it. Rolo went next, running, always running, though slower than usual, his head down — and Marta saw that he did not run toward the harbor and the cutter this time. He ran toward home.

Cyrus went last.

The keeper stood at the gate a moment, and then she turned and looked up the hill, past the kitchen window, toward the orchard rows climbing the slope in their crooked, stubborn lines.

"How much of the ledger did your grandfather copy himself?" Cyrus asked.

"Half the orchard sections, and all the workshop shares," Marta said. "Why?"

Something moved across Cyrus Reyes's face — quick, guarded, like a bird crossing a window — and was gone.

"No reason," she said. "Only that I've been sorting my husband's papers. Storm logs mostly. He had a strange habit of writing in the margins."

Marta felt the words settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well, falling and falling, not yet touching bottom.

"Tomás Reyes spent four winters keeping my light," Cyrus went on, "and I never once asked him what he wrote in the dark beside me."

She turned and walked down the path toward the headland, and Marta stood in her doorway a long time after she'd vanished into the gorse, with the linen-wrapped receipt heavy under her arm and the wind rising out of the southeast, carrying the smell of deep water and, faintly, of change.
