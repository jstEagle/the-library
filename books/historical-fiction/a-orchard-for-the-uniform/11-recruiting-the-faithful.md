## 11. Recruiting the Faithful

Cyrus spent three days deciding whom to trust, and in that time she learned what a strange currency trust was — how it could not be counted or weighed, only spent, and once spent never recovered.

She had not trusted anyone since Tomás died. That was the plain truth of it, though she had dressed it up for two years in gentler language: she had kept to herself, she had managed alone, she had preferred her own company. The lighthouse made solitude easy. The stairs did the work of a moat, and the lamp gave her an excuse — always something to trim, to polish, to watch. She had told herself she was a woman who tended a light for strangers at sea, and that this was enough.

But Tomás had not believed in enough. He had copied a ledger by lamplight over four winters, page by page, hiding his work inside storm records where no clerk would ever look twice, and he had done it without telling her, and he had died with the secret folded into oilskin behind lead flashing, and now the whole weight of it sat on her chest like wet wool. She could not carry it alone. She had tried, these three days, carrying it up and down the stairs, carrying it into dreams, carrying it through the motions of feeding the gulls and trimming the wick and answering the sergeant's flat-eyed questions about fuel stores. It would break her back before it broke anyone else's.

So: whom to trust.

Marta Vidal first, because there was no choice in it. The ledger was Marta's inheritance more than anyone's; she had been the one weeping on the quay when the barge carried the town hall records away, an old woman crying over paper while soldiers looked anywhere but at her. If Cyrus showed her nothing else, she must show her that the names were not all gone.

Ines Correia second, because Ines had hands that knew how to keep quiet. The blacksmith's widow worked iron the way other women kneaded bread — without ceremony, without complaint, with a kind of muscular patience that Cyrus had watched from across the market square for two years and envied without admitting it. And because Ines had lost everything already. A woman with nothing left to lose was either the safest keeper of secrets or the most dangerous, and Cyrus had decided, after long nights turning it over, that grief like Ines's went downward into silence rather than outward into talk.

Rolo last, and Rolo reluctantly. The boy was fifteen and hungry-eyed and half in love with the uniforms marching up from the landing stage; he saluted when they weren't looking and polished boots for coppers when they were. But Rolo had been Tomás's shadow too, running messages for the old keeper before Cyrus ever arrived in this town, and there was a sharpness under the hero-worship that reminded her of Tomás himself — the same quick fingers, the same way of looking at a thing until it gave up its workings. And besides, she reasoned, if she left him out he would sniff around anyway, and a boy sniffing around uninvited was worth ten informants to any sergeant with sweets in his pocket.

* * *

She asked them separately, which was itself a lesson in how afraid she'd become. Not together — together they might refuse as a crowd, and crowds have courage neither way. One at a time, with a pretext apiece.

To Marta she said, "I've found some of Tomás's papers. I don't understand them."

Which was true, in its way. She did not understand them — not their full shape, not yet, not what they would ask of her.

To Ines she said, "I need help with something heavy, up at the light."

Also true. Nothing in her life had ever been heavier.

To Rolo she said nothing at all. She simply caught him loitering at the foot of the tower with his hands in his pockets, watching the naval cutter ride at anchor like a fat grey gull, and said, "Thursday evening. Bring your eyes and leave your mouth." He grinned, thinking himself favored. She hoped he would still be grinning Thursday night.

* * *

They came after dark, singly and by different paths, as she had instructed — Marta first, leaning on her stick, refusing the arm Cyrus offered; then Ines, who said nothing at all but looked at the stairs with the expression of a woman assessing a load-bearing wall; then Rolo, breathless, having run the cliff path though no one had chased him.

Cyrus lit the lamp room first. It was habit, and also strategy: no one questions a keeper doing keeper's things. She trimmed the wick, checked the clockwork, let the great lens begin its slow rotation, and only then brought the four of them down to the kitchen, where she had banked the stove low and set out nothing — no bread, no wine, none of the hospitality that would make this gathering feel ordinary. She wanted it to feel like what it was.

"Close the shutters," she said, and Ines did it without question, and the kitchen became a small sealed world with the wind outside testing the seams.

Marta lowered herself onto the bench nearest the stove and fixed Cyrus with the look she used on fishmongers short-weighting her herring. "Papers," she said. "You said papers."

"I said I didn't understand them." Cyrus knelt at the cupboard beneath the window — the one Tomás had rebuilt the winter before his cough began, though she hadn't known then why a man would reinforce the bottom of a cupboard that held nothing but candles. The false back came away in her hands the way it had come away a hundred times since, smooth as a drawer, and she lifted out the first oilskin packet and felt again the terrible lightness of it. Paper should not weigh so much. Paper was the heaviest thing she had ever held.

She laid it on the table between them and unwrapped it.

For a moment nobody moved. The lamp above turned its patient circle; the beam swept the shuttered window and was gone, swept and gone, like breathing.

Marta put out both hands — both, as if to receive communion — and drew the topmost page toward her. Her lips moved. Cyrus watched the old woman read her own history in a dead man's handwriting: *Vidal, Marta, widow of Joaquim — orchard rows 14 through 22, east slope, with water rights to the stone channel — share in the press-house, one part in nine.* Row after row. Name after name. The Almeidas, the Ferreras, the Okonkwos who had come up from the fishing sheds three generations back and been given ground instead of scorn because someone had written them down. Every family. Every claim. Every small name the big ledger had recorded so that no soldier could ever again take an orchard and leave nothing.

"This is—" Marta's voice failed and restarted, lower. "This is the ledger. This is the ledger entire."

"Not entire." Cyrus heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when feeling pressed too hard against restraint. "Tomás copied most of it. Not all. There are gaps — whole sections missing. The workshop families' claims are gone. I've been through every packet twice."

"Gone?" Ines spoke for the first time, and her voice was iron quenched in water — a hiss and then stillness. "Gone where?"

"I don't know. Either he never finished, or—" She stopped. She had not said the next part aloud to anyone, and saying it made it real: "Or someone took them. Before he died, or after. There are pages here dated two months before his death, and pages dated a year before, and nothing in between. Someone was keeping pace with him. Or he was keeping pace with someone."

The stove ticked. The beam swept, swept.

It was Rolo who broke the silence, and Cyrus was glad afterward that it was the boy, because what he said needed saying by someone young enough to be blunt. "So we finish it." He was leaning over the table, forgetting entirely to be cool, forgetting the uniforms and the cutter and the copper-polishing. His finger hovered above the page without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping animal. "We copy out the rest. From memory. Marta knows the workshop shares, doesn't she? Everyone knows their own. We write it all down and then—"

"And then what?" Ines asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely.

And there it was, the question Cyrus had been circling for three days like a moth round a lamp. And then what? A copy of a ledger, in a lighthouse, in a requisitioned town, against the army of a nation at war. Ink against bayonets. What was the and-then?

"It goes in the record," Marta said quietly. "That is the and-then. You are too young to remember why the ledger exists, boy, and you, Ines, were a child. I am not too young. I was seven years old the year the soldiers came through and stripped the valley — before the ledger, before any of it. They took the fruit off every tree and the tools out of every shop and when my father protested they laughed and said, prove it was yours. Prove anything was yours. There was nothing to prove it with. My father stood in our own orchard like a stranger." Her hand flattened on the page, on her own name. "This book exists so that no one can ever say *prove it* to us again. So yes — and then what? Then it exists. Then when the day comes, and it will come, when someone asks us to prove who we are, we will not be standing in our own orchard like strangers."

Nobody answered her. Nobody needed to. Cyrus watched Ines's jaw set, watched Rolo stop hovering and actually touch the page, feather-light, and felt the thing happen in the room that she had been afraid of and starving for in equal measure: the secret stopped being hers. It passed out of her hands and became theirs, and the terror of that was immediately, confusingly braided with relief, the way a rope goes slack the instant someone else takes hold.

"There's more you should know," Cyrus said. "Before anyone commits to anything. What we're doing is theft, by their reckoning. Copying seized documents. If they find these packets here, they won't fine me. They'll take the light station, and they'll take us. Rolo, they'll take *you*, and boys your age go somewhere very different from old women." She looked at him steadily. "I need you to understand that before you grin at me again."

The grin had already gone. What replaced it frightened her a little — a steadiness, an attention, far too old for his face. "My father fished these waters twenty years," he said. "The army took the boat in September. He signed whatever they put in front of him because they said refusing meant no compensation at all. He reads slow. They knew it." He shrugged, and the shrug was the saddest thing in the room. "I'm not scared of them finding out. I'm scared of them being right that nobody'll do anything."

* * *

They talked until the small hours. It was agreed — not voted, exactly; Marta declared and the rest of them found themselves nodding — that the copying would happen here, in the lighthouse, at night, disguised within the storm logs. That Ines would learn Tomás's shorthand, the dense little system of hooks and tails he'd invented for speed, which Cyrus had spent three days deciphering and could now teach. That Rolo would be their eyes and ears in the town, counting soldiers, noting schedules, learning which sergeant drank and which prayed. That no one — no one — would speak of it outside this room, not to husbands, not to sweethearts, not to God in confession if it could be helped.

Cyrus taught Ines the shorthand that night, right there, because waiting felt dangerous. She wrote the alphabet of hooks on scrap paper by stove-light: *this stroke is T, this tail is S, see how the word folds up small, smaller than the eye expects.* Ines bent over the page with her burn-scarred forearms on the table and learned it the way she did everything — silently, completely, on the second pass. Watching her, Cyrus felt the strangest grief and gratitude tangled together: Tomás's hand alive again in another person's fingers. Two winters dead, and still teaching.

"You loved him," Ines said at one point, not looking up. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite sympathy. It was an observation, the way she might observe that a weld would hold.

"He never told me what he was doing." Cyrus heard the hurt in her own voice and let it stand. "Four years of sneaking down here while I slept, copying other people's names, and he never once said *Cyrus, sit with me, this matters.* I found it in a cupboard. Like laundry."

"He was protecting you," Marta said.

"He was *deciding* for me." The words came out harder than she intended, and the room went careful. She stared at the stove door. "Everyone keeps deciding for me. The army decides I'm useful enough to keep lighting the lamp. Tomás decided I was better off ignorant. This town decided, twenty years ago, that the keeper's wife from up the coast wasn't quite one of yours, and everyone's been very polite about it ever since." She looked up. Three faces watched her — old, scarred, young — and she realized she had never said any of this aloud, not even to Tomás, especially not to Tomás. "So if we do this, we do it as equals. I'm not asking anyone's permission and no one's giving me orders. I found his work and I can't finish it alone and I am asking — I am *asking* — for your hands."

The silence after was long, and the beam swept the shutters, swept, swept, and Cyrus understood that she had just done the thing she had not done since Tomás died: she had extended trust, terrified and total, across a table, to three people who could destroy her with a sentence spoken to the wrong sergeant.

Marta reached across and closed Cyrus's hand over the edge of the oilskin packet, folding her fingers down around it like closing a book.

"Then we're agreed," the old woman said. "Tomorrow night we begin."

* * *

They left as they had come, singly, by different paths, Marta first with her stick tapping the stair-treads like a metronome, then Rolo, who paused at the door and said, "Mrs. Reyes — the missing sections. The workshop families." He hesitated. "My uncle's name would be in those. If they exist anywhere—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

"If they exist," Cyrus said, "we'll find them."

After Ines left — last, always last, checking the shutter latches on her way out as though the tower were a forge she was banking for the night — Cyrus stood alone in the kitchen with the dead fire and the open packet and the enormous new fact of what she had just done.

She had expected to feel lighter. Everyone said that was how it worked, burdens shared and all of that. Instead she felt exposed, the way the tower must feel in a lightning storm — taller than everything, full of metal, waiting. Any one of them could end her. Any one of them could be caught and broken. She had handed three people the power to erase her, and in exchange she had received — what? An old woman's blessing. A widow's silence. A boy's fierce, foolish hope.

Outside, the wind had backed around to the northeast, and the barometer in the passage — Tomás's barometer, which she consulted each morning the way other people consulted mirrors — had begun, she noticed as she climbed to check the lamp, a slow and stubborn fall.

Below in the dark, the requisitioned town lay quiet. Somewhere in it, a workshop full of presses and seals waited under guard for the wrecking crews. Somewhere offshore, the cutter rode its anchor chain.

Cyrus trimmed the wick, and set the lens turning, and began to plan how, exactly, one stole a town's memory back from an empire — starting with the question she had promised Rolo an answer to:

Where were the missing pages?
