## 12. Rolo's Eyes

Rolo Ferreira had learned to tell time by the soldiers' boots.

Not the clock in the workshop — that had been sold or crated or burned, nobody knew which — and not the church bell, which the army had silenced on the second day for reasons no one ever explained. Boots. The garrison changed its patrol at six, at noon, and again when the light went amber over the harbor. Their hobnails struck the cobbles in a rhythm so regular it might have been a metronome set by God, and Rolo had come to measure his whole life against it: how long he could linger at the well, how long he could stand outside the requisitioned town hall breathing the smell of fresh paint and government paper, how long he could watch Captain Hargrove cross the square with his coat collar turned up against the northeast wind before someone asked him what he was looking at.

What he was looking at was a man.

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten trouble of his life now, and he was fifteen years old and had never been so confused in it.

* * *

He had been eleven when the first real soldiers came — not these tired men in salt-stiffened wool, but the ones from the recruiting parade two towns over, brass buttons and bayonets that caught the sun like a field of small bright fish. His father had lifted him onto his shoulders to see. *Look sharp, boy,* his father had said. *There goes something bigger than us.* And Rolo had believed it with his whole chest, the way you believe things at eleven: that the uniform made a man more than a man, that discipline was a kind of shining, that if he stood very straight and kept his boots polished maybe someday the shining would have room in it for him too. His father was dead now — lungs, the workshop dust, three winters back — but the belief had outlived him, stubborn as a nail in wet wood.

So when the cutter came, when the notices went up on the church door in that beautiful printed type, some traitorous corner of Rolo had thought: *there they are. The real thing.*

And then he had watched them nail paper over the doors of the town where his mother was born.

* * *

The lighthouse smelled of lamp oil and cold stone and, lately, secrets. Rolo climbed the spiral stair two steps at a time the way Mrs. Reyes hated, his knuckles rapping the rail, and came out into the gallery wind with his cap jammed down hard.

She was already there, of course. She was always there. Cyrus Reyes stood at the rail with her hands folded, watching the water the way she watched everything — as though the sea owed her an explanation.

"You said you'd tell me about the missing pages," he said. No hello. He'd stopped saying hello to her somewhere around the third night of copying, because she'd told him once, in Tomás's shorthand, that hellos wasted oil-burning time, and he had decided to adopt all her habits at once, the way boys do when they've decided who they want to become.

"I said I'd tell you what we know." She didn't look at him. "We know the workshop families' claims are gone. Every page. We know your father's name would have been on one of them."

Rolo's stomach did the thing it did when the stairs went out from under him in dreams.

"We know," she went on, "that Tomás copied in order. Orchard lands first, then the workshop shares, then the common claims. The order breaks off after page forty-one." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were gray as the water below. "Which means either he stopped — which he didn't do anything — or the pages he hadn't finished were taken from wherever he hid them. Before he died. Or after."

"By who?"

"That," said Cyrus Reyes, "is the question I promised you an answer to. And here is my answer: I don't know yet. But I know someone who might."

* * *

The someone was Captain Ellery Hargrove, and Rolo found this unbearable in a way he couldn't have explained to anyone without sounding like a fool.

Because here was the thing. He had spent four evenings now in the lamplight of the watch room, learning a dead man's shorthand from Ines Costa's patient blacksmith's-widow hands, copying names that the empire had carried off on a barge — and every one of those evenings, part of him had been doing arithmetic about patrols. Not because he wanted to betray anyone. Because the sergeant who walked the cliff path at half past nine walked it the same way every night, left boot first, pausing exactly where the path narrowed by the gorse, and knowing this felt like being trusted by the world itself. Knowing the enemy's habits was power. Knowing them and loving them — that was the wound.

Because he did love them. He loved the way the soldiers' tents went up in perfect squares. He loved the bugle calls, even muffled by fog, even calling men who had come to take his town apart board by board. Most of all — and this was the shameful center of it, the thing he turned over at night like a stone with something alive under it — he loved Captain Hargrove.

He'd seen the captain give his own blanket ration to old Pilar Duarte when the requisitioned houses ran cold. He'd seen him stand in the rain at the church door reading the notice he himself had posted, reading it twice, his mouth moving slightly, like a man rereading a letter that had gone wrong in the sending. He'd heard him say — Rolo had been carrying crates ten feet away, invisible, the way boys carrying crates are always invisible — *"We inventory what we're ordered to inventory, Sergeant. We don't inventory people."* Said quietly. Said as though it mattered. Said as though the difference between those two things weren't the entire difference between a man and a machine, and Hargrove knew it, and still wore the buttons, and still signed the orders.

How did a person do that? Rolo lay in his cot above the empty smithy and asked the ceiling. How did you keep the shining and lose the rot? Was there a way to be that kind of soldier, the kind who read his own orders twice? Or was the uniform a door that only opened one way?

His father would have known. His father had died believing in bigger things, and look where the bigger things had put their boots.

* * *

"You're staring," Marta Vidal said.

They were in the tavern's back room, which the army hadn't bothered to requisition because there was nothing in it to requisition except a table, four chairs, and Marta Vidal, who was worth more than everything else in the town put together and knew it. She was sorting dried beans by lamplight, which is what she did when she was thinking, moving each bean from bowl to bowl with a precision that made Rolo think of the lens turning in the lighthouse.

"I wasn't staring."

"You were staring at that officer through the window like a lovesick cat at a birdcage."

Heat climbed his neck. "I'm trying to understand him."

Marta stopped sorting. She looked at him across the beans, and her face, which was mostly angles and weather, softened into something almost gentle. "Ah," she said. "You're fourteen."

"Fifteen."

"Fifteen." She picked up another bean. "When I was fifteen the tax men came from the capital, and I hated them, and I also stood outside the customs house for an hour watching the clerk's hands move, because they moved like music. Beautiful things can be attached to terrible purposes, Rolo. It doesn't make the purpose less terrible. It makes it harder to see, that's all."

"He gave his blanket away."

"He posted the notice on the church door."

"He reads."

"So did Judas, probably." But she said it without teeth, and then she set the bowls aside entirely and leaned forward on her elbows, and Rolo understood he was about to be talked to the way adults talk when they've decided you're in it. "Listen to me. You admire him because he's good inside a bad thing, and you want to know if the goodness counts. I'll tell you what counts. What counts is what a man does when his orders and his eyes disagree. Your father's eyes disagreed with his orders every day of his life in that workshop, and he stayed anyway, because the work was ours and the hands were his. Hargrove hasn't chosen yet. Maybe he never will. Don't build your soul on a man who hasn't chosen."

"And if he chooses right?"

Marta smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Then we'll be glad, won't we. And it will change nothing about what we do tonight."

* * *

Tonight was pages forty-two through fifty, and the shorthand for *orchard boundary,* which Rolo kept getting wrong because Tomás Reyes had written it with a flourish that looked like a wave breaking and Rolo's hand only produced a sad flat squiggle.

Ines sat beside him in the watch room, her big scarred fingers turning the original toward the light. "Again," she said. "You're writing it like you're afraid of it."

"I am afraid of it."

"Good. Write it anyway. Fear's just respect with your heart going fast."

Below them the lens turned; above them the wind worried at the glass; around them the dead man's handwriting crept across the pages line by line, orchard by orchard, family by family — *Vidal, Duarte, Costa, Amaral* — names resurrected in pencil by a blacksmith's widow and a smithy's orphan while the legitimate copies slept on a barge or in a warehouse or in whatever drawer empires keep for other people's grandmothers.

And it was going fine, it was going beautifully, until Rolo's eye snagged on the marginal note beside the Amaral entry and his whole body went cold.

Tomás's hand. Small, cramped, angled into the margin the way he wrote everything that mattered:

*Cf. quartermaster's manifest, autumn '11. Pages offered. Declined.*

Rolo read it three times. Then he read it aloud.

Ines went very still. Outside, far below on the cliff path, the sergeant's boots came left-first past the gorse, right on schedule, and the sound of them had never seemed so loud.

"Cyrus," Ines called down the stairwell, quietly, the way you call a name when the house is full of sleepers. "Come up here. Bring the lantern."

* * *

Cyrus read the note twice and then stood with her back to them, looking out the seaward windows, and Rolo watched her shoulders and understood that grief has a shape and it was standing in the room with them.

"'Declined,'" she said at last. Her voice was level, which meant nothing; he'd learned that much. "Someone offered to sell him the missing pages in the autumn of 1911. Two years ago. Before any of this. Someone inside the records office." She turned. "The clerk?"

"The clerk's dying," Ines said. "Been dying since spring. But dying men talk, if you bring the right question and sit long enough."

"And the buyer?" Rolo said, and hated how eager his voice sounded, and said it anyway. "Who buys ledger pages two years before there's a war to hide them in? Who'd even—"

He stopped. Because the answer had arrived whole, the way answers do when you've been carrying the question around for days without knowing it: a man who wears the uniform like it fits, who inventories everything, who keeps accounts within accounts, whose coat pockets Rolo had once helped fill with requisitioned lamp oil and had noticed — had *noticed*, filed away, the way he filed away boot-rhythms and patrol times — were heavier than they should have been.

"No," he said aloud, to his own thought.

"What?" said Cyrus.

"Nothing." His ears were burning. "I was thinking about—" He gestured vaguely at the sea, at the war, at everything. "About who'd buy pages. That's all."

But Cyrus Reyes was looking at him the way she looked at the barometer in the passage, and Rolo knew — the way you know a wave has decided about you before it reaches you — that she had seen straight through to the shape of what he was thinking. And that tomorrow, or the day after, she would ask him to use it.

* * *

He walked home along the cliff path in the dark, past the gorse, counting his own footsteps instead of anyone's boots, and tried to make the thought behave.

It couldn't be Hargrove. Hargrove had come with the cutter eight months ago; the note was from two years back. Unless Hargrove had been here before, in plain clothes, on survey — unless the exhausted courtesy was older than the occupation—

Or unless it was Brandt. Quartermaster Brandt, with his soft voice and his ledgers within ledgers, the man who weighed everything twice and smiled like a shopkeeper. Rolo had liked him too. That was the horror of it; there wasn't a single soldier in the garrison Rolo hadn't found something to love in, and now every one of those small loves was a possible knife.

At the bend by the gorse he stopped. Below him the harbor lay black and oily calm, the cutter riding its chain, and beyond the breakwater — farther out than the cutter, anchored deep where the supply ships swung — the dark bulk that the fishermen had begun, in whispers, to call *the vulture.*

The supply ship. Due any day now, riding ahead of the falling glass, bringing flour and rope and replacement parts for the demolition crews.

Bringing, perhaps, a quartermaster home from the mainland.

Rolo stood in the wind with his cap in his hands and his loyalties laid out around him like the tools of his father's trade — hammer, tongs, the little jeweler's screwdriver for delicate work — and understood, with the terrible clarity of fifteen, that adolescence was not a time of life at all. It was a battlefield. It was this exact spot on this exact path, with the sea on one side and the town behind you and the uniform you loved walking its rounds below, and everyone you cared for lined up on opposite sides of a question you hadn't finished asking.

He put his cap back on. He turned toward the town.

First, he told himself, he would go to the dying clerk. Alone, tomorrow, with the right question. He wouldn't tell Mrs. Reyes yet — not until he knew whether the name at the bottom of it was one he could bear to give her.

The barometer was falling. Somewhere offshore, the vulture rode its anchor. And in the margin of a dead man's copy, in a wave-shaped flourish Rolo could finally write correctly, the answer to the missing pages waited for someone brave enough or foolish enough to go and fetch it.
