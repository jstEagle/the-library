## 13. Copying by Lamplight

Ines Ferreira had not held a pen for anything but accounts in eleven years, and her hand showed it. The first letters she set down in the lighthouse's storm log came out stiff as fence posts, the ink pooling where she pressed too hard, thinning to ghost-grey where she lifted too soon.

"Again," Cyrus said, not unkindly.

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and burnt oil and, beneath it, the cold mineral breath of stone that had stood against a hundred winters of sea. Outside, the wind worried at the gallery rail with a sound like a man trying doors. Below them the town lay dark — darker than it had ever been, Ines thought, though she knew that was nonsense; towns did not darken because their people were scattered into barracks and borrowed rooms. But it looked dark. It looked like a mouth with the teeth drawn.

She dipped the pen again and wrote: *Wind SW, freshening. Barometer 29.4.*

"Better," said Cyrus Reyes.

It was the third night. The first night, Ines had climbed the hundred and eight steps with her heart going like a hammer on sheet iron, certain that every soldier in the garrison could hear it, certain that the whole enterprise was madness dressed up as sentiment. Rolo had gone ahead of her with the lantern, whistling — *whistling*, the boy, as if they were off to net fish instead of committing whatever crime this was — and Marta Vidal had come last, slow on the stairs, one hand on the wall, muttering names under her breath like a rosary.

*Almeida. Bastos. Carvalho.* The orchard families. The workshop shares. Three generations written in a clerk's careful hand and carried off on a barge like so much ballast.

Now it was only the two of them, most nights, because Marta's knees would not forgive her the stairs more than twice a week, and Rolo was needed at home before his mother woke. Two women and a dead man's handwriting, and a lamp burning above a stolen town.

"Your T is too angry," Cyrus said, leaning over her shoulder. Ines caught the smell of her — lamp oil and wool and something faintly floral, dried blossoms maybe, kept in a tin somewhere below. "Tomás crossed his T like this." She took the pen from Ines's fingers — took it gently, the way you'd take a bird — and wrote a single letter in the margin. It looked nothing like a T. It looked like a mast with the sail half-furled, a crossbar laid low and casual, as if the man who wrote it had all the time in the world.

"He wrote like he talked," Ines said. "Like nothing could hurry him."

"He was never late for anything in his life," Cyrus said. There was no bitterness in it. That was the strange thing, Ines thought. Widowhood had not made Cyrus Reyes bitter; it had made her precise. "He simply believed the world would wait. And mostly—" she stopped herself, and Ines saw her swallow whatever the end of that sentence was, "—mostly it did."

Ines took back the pen. She wrote the T again, mast and furled sail, and it came out crooked, but it came out.

* * *

The shorthand was worse.

Tomás Reyes had learned it, Cyrus explained, from a shipping agent in the capital, years before he ever came north — a system of hooks and loops and raised dots meant for recording cargo manifests at speed. He had adapted it, added his own marks, until it became a private country with its own grammar. The storm logs were full of it. To any inspector flipping through the station's records, the pages read as weather: gusts recorded in compressed notation, sea states abbreviated, the tedium of a keeper's duty rendered in a clerk's efficient hand. Only the key — a folded page tucked inside the oilskin packets behind the lead flashing — unlocked what the weather actually said.

*Orchard of João Almeida, east slope, twelve rows, share confirmed 1871 by witness P. Bastos.*

That was a squall line, in Tomás's shorthand. That was a barometer falling.

"It's beautiful," Ines said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it. Beautiful things were dangerous now. Beauty was how they got you.

"It's maddening," Cyrus said. "He changed three symbols after the fourth year and didn't note it anywhere. I've spent two weeks working out that this mark—" she tapped a loop like a sleeping eel, "—used to mean *witnessed*, and then starts meaning *confirmed by payment*. As if those were the same thing."

"Weren't they?" Ines asked, before she could stop herself. "Back then?"

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, cheekbone and jaw, a face built for looking out windows. "Sometimes," she said finally. "And sometimes the payment was a favor, and sometimes the favor was a debt, and sometimes the debt was the kind you don't write down. Which is why we copy it *exactly*, Ines. All of it. Even the parts that shame us." She turned back to the log. "Especially those."

Ines bent over her page. Her husband had been dead six years, drowned off the point with two other men when the *Santa Luzia* went down, and she had learned since then that grief was not a wave but a tide — it went out and came back on its own schedule and paid no attention to what you were doing. Tonight it came back while she was writing the name *Ferreira*.

Her own name. Her husband's claim to a quarter-share in the cooperage, entered in the ledger in 1868, witnessed, confirmed, real as the stone under her feet. Somewhere on that barge, in some army crate, the page that proved the Ferreiras had ever existed was gathering salt damp. And here she sat, copying it out by lamplight in a dead man's secret alphabet, hoping her hands remembered enough of her schoolroom to be worthy of it.

Her eyes blurred. She let them. She wrote through it, and the letters wavered, and she thought: *let them waver. Let whoever reads this someday know a woman cried over these names, and that the crying was not weakness. It was the ink holding.*

* * *

They developed a rhythm across the week, the way water develops a channel.

Cyrus worked the original — Tomás's transcription, the oilskin packets brought up two or three at a time from behind the flashing and returned before dawn — reading each passage aloud in a voice pitched barely above breath. Ines transcribed into fair hand, the storm-log entries framing the true text like a mat frames a picture. Every third page, Cyrus checked Ines's work against the shorthand itself, catching errors, correcting habits.

"You hook your *r*s like my grandmother," Cyrus said on the fifth night. "She was Portuguese too. From the fishing villages south. Did yours keep accounts?"

"She kept everything," Ines said. "Six daughters and a boat. You learn to write small."

"You write like someone rationing paper."

"I *am* rationing paper. Where do you think I'm getting it? The army issues paper to widows now?"

That earned her something rare: a short laugh from Cyrus Reyes, quick and surprised out of her, there and gone like a tern. Ines found she wanted to earn it again, which was a dangerous wanting. She had spent six years wanting very little, on purpose. Wanting was how the sea got its hooks in you.

But the work pulled at her anyway. Night after night she climbed the stairs, and night after night the names came up out of the oilskin like fish rising to a light: *Nunes, Oliveira, Peixoto, Quintela.* Families she'd known her whole life. Families whose orchards she'd picked in as a girl, whose workshop benches she'd swept shavings from, whose quarrels and weddings and funerals had been the whole architecture of the world. Reading them aloud in that hushed room, Ines understood for the first time that a ledger was not a record. It was a body. Each entry a bone. Take away the bones and the town was just meat on a tide flat, and the army could do what it liked with meat.

"We're reassembling a skeleton," she said one night, apropos of nothing, and Cyrus looked up from the shorthand key and nodded slowly, as if Ines had said something wise instead of something grim.

"That's exactly what we're doing," Cyrus said. "And skeletons stand up. Eventually."

* * *

On the eighth night, the soldiers came early.

Not to the lighthouse — Ines heard the boots pass along the road below, four men maybe, lanterns swinging, headed for the harbor sheds where the requisitioned nets and tackle were being crated. But the sound of them, the easy careless rhythm of men who owned the ground they walked on, went through Ines like a splinter finding its depth. Her pen stopped mid-word. Ink gathered at the nib and dropped, a black star blooming on the page, right through the middle of *Ribeiro*.

"No," she whispered.

"It's fine." Cyrus was already moving, calm as arithmetic, drawing the blotting sand. "We cut around it. Or we leave it — a stain is honest. Storm logs have stains."

"A stain is a signature," Ines said. "You taught me that. Everything on these pages has to look like it's always been here. Like weather." She stared at the ruined name. Ribeiro. Old Amaro Ribeiro, who used to trade her husband barrels for mackerel and always shorted him a finger's width and always smiled while doing it. Dead two years now, and his name bleeding out under her pen. "I can't waste him. I can't waste even him."

Cyrus said nothing for a moment. Then she took a fresh sheet, cut a strip the width of the damaged line, and showed Ines how to lift the blot with the corner of a blade, how to lay the strip beneath and rewrite the name so the repair vanished into the paper's grain. "Tomás taught me this," she said. "He said a keeper's first job isn't lighting the lamp. It's maintaining the glass. You don't get to be proud about repairs. You get to make them invisible."

"And is that right?" Ines asked. "Making things invisible? Isn't that what they're doing to us?"

Cyrus's hand paused over the page. Outside, the soldiers' laughter drifted up, thin and far, and the sea kept its old indifferent time against the rocks.

"There's a difference," she said at last, "between hiding a thing and keeping it safe. I have to believe that. Some nights it's the only thing I believe." She smoothed the repaired page. "Besides. We're not hiding these names. We're the only ones not hiding them."

* * *

By the second week, Ines's hands had begun to change.

She noticed it first at her loom — no, she had no loom anymore, the loom had gone with the house; she noticed it first at Marta Vidal's kitchen table, shelling beans, when her thumb moved across the pods in a new pattern, quick and economical, and Marta looked up sharply.

"Your hands," Marta said.

"What about them?"

"They're doing his work." Marta nodded toward the window, toward the lighthouse on its bluff, gray in the afternoon haze. "Tomás Reyes's work. I watched those hands for thirty years across a market stall. He counted money like that. Quick, from the wrist, no wasted motion." She resumed shelling, but her eyes stayed on Ines a moment longer. "Be careful what you borrow from the dead. They lend without telling you the terms."

Ines laughed it off, but walking home she flexed her fingers and felt the truth of it. Her hands were learning a stranger. Every night they shaped his hooks and loops, crossed his low-slung crosses, rested the pen at his angles. Muscle memory was a kind of haunting. By month's end, she suspected, she could forge Tomás Reyes's weather well enough to fool his widow — and the thought should have frightened her, and did, and underneath the fear ran something else, something warm and shameful:

Pride. She was good at this. Six years of numbness, of surviving on mending and other women's charity, and here at last was work that mattered, work with weight, and her hands — plain mender's hands, account-keeper's hands — were equal to it.

*Hope,* she thought, climbing the lighthouse path in the blue dusk, *is just fear that's decided to be useful.*

* * *

The eleventh night, Cyrus showed her the packets themselves.

All this time Ines had worked from loose sheets Cyrus carried up in a satchel, the source itself kept below, hidden. But tonight Cyrus went down the stairwell's turn and came back with an armful of oilskin bundles, tied with waxed cord, smelling of lamp grease and cold iron, and laid them open on the chart table under the great lamp.

"Do you understand what you're looking at?" Cyrus asked.

Ines looked. Page after page of Tomás's hand — the ledger entire, or nearly, copied in that patient unhurried script, dates and witnesses and acreages and shares, the whole skeleton of the town laid out in a dead man's alphabet. Years of nights, she realized. Years of Tomás sitting somewhere in this same room, copying by this same light, while the town slept below him trusting to paper in a clerk's drawer.

"Why?" she asked. "Why did he do it? Nobody knew. Nobody thanked him. If the army had never come, he'd have died with this hidden and useless."

Cyrus touched the edge of a packet the way you touch a sleeping child. "I've asked him that every night for two weeks," she said. "I think — I think he knew what men are. What committees are, and clerks, and governments. He'd seen the story Marta tells, the one about the orchard and the uniforms. He believed that someday, somebody would come and take the book. Maybe soldiers. Maybe creditors. Maybe just rot and damp." Her voice caught, steadied. "He built a lifeboat for a town that wasn't sinking yet. Because he knew that one day it might, and there wouldn't be time to build one then."

Ines looked down at her own hands, ink-stained, cracked at the knuckles, learning the dead man's habits hour by hour. "Then we finish the boat," she said.

"We finish the boat," Cyrus agreed.

They worked until the small hours. Wind rose out of the southwest, freshening, and Ines recorded it twice — once as weather, once as truth — and somewhere past two in the morning her pen faltered on a name she didn't recognize, a family called Duarte, orchard share, 1859, and she realized with a small cold shock that she had never heard of any Duartes. A family erased so thoroughly that even the town's memory had let them go. Only the paper remembered.

"Who were the Duartes?" she asked.

Cyrus frowned, checked the key, checked the date. "I don't know," she said slowly. "There's no Duarte plot on any map I've seen. No Duarte grave in the churchyard." She turned the page, and Ines watched her face change — watched the blood go out of it the way tide goes out of a cove.

"What?"

"The next entry," Cyrus said. "Duarte again. And again. Fourteen entries. And then—" her finger stopped on a line of shorthand, and she translated it aloud, her voice gone flat and strange, "—*claim denied, 1861, by vote of signatories.*"

Ines leaned close. The lamplight flickered in the draft from the stairwell, and the shadows moved across the page like something breathing.

"Cyrus," she said quietly. "Denied by whom? Who signed the ledger that refused them?"

Cyrus didn't answer. She was already reaching for the earliest packet, already turning back toward the beginning, toward the list of names that had founded the book — and Ines saw, in the set of her shoulders, that she was afraid of what she would find there. Afraid of whose signature waited at the head of the ledger like a signature at the foot of a warrant.

Outside, the wind backed hard to the southeast, and the barometer in the corner began, very softly, to fall.
