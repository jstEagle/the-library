## 14. The First Inspection

The barometer had still been falling when Ines left, and by the time the sky went fully dark it had fallen past the painted mark that meant *storm within three days*, and Cyrus had stood a long while at the gallery rail watching the sea work itself into a long gray temper. The packets were back behind the lead flashing now — she had checked twice, then a third time, pressing the cold metal flat with her palms as if she could seal it shut by will alone — but the fear had come home with her from the lamp room and would not settle. It moved through her like weather. It found every loose shutter in her and rattled it.

She told herself it was only because she was new at this. Two winters of keeping a light alone had taught her to sleep through gales, to eat with one hand while trimming the wick with the other, to stand at a rail in wind that leaned on her like a drunk man leaning on a fence. But keeping a secret was different from keeping a light. A light wanted to be seen. That was the whole of its purpose — to burn where anyone might look, so that looking would keep them safe. What she had hidden behind the flashing was the opposite of a light. It was a dark thing kept for the sake of others, and it made her feel, for the first time since Tomás died, that the lighthouse itself was lying.

She was on the second landing with the storm log open when she heard the boat.

Not the oar-clocks first — those came later, muffled and wrong — but the lantern-glow on the water below, swinging in the swell, green-white and steady in a way no fishing boat's lantern ever managed. Fishing boats swung their lights like hands. This one held its light like a fist.

Cyrus closed the log without hurrying. That was the trick, she thought, though her heart had already begun its stupid gallop: never hurry. Hurrying was a confession written on the body. She set the log on its shelf, squared it, took up her oil key, and started down the stairs with the unhurried tread of a woman who had nothing in this tower but oil and wicks and forty years of other people's storms.

The knock came as she reached the door — three blows, official, spaced like punctuation.

"Light station," she called through the wood. "State your business."

"Corporal's party, requisition survey." A voice she half-knew — Sergeant Doyle, the one who'd helped carry the clerk's ledgers down to the barge, the one who had said *sorry about your records, ma'am* in a tone that suggested he meant it and knew saying so changed nothing. "Routine inspection. Captain Hargrove's orders. Every government structure inventoried before the demolition schedule is finalized."

*Demolition.* The word went through her like a fishhook. The workshop — they were already speaking of it as a thing to be torn down rather than a thing that stood. She put her hand on the latch and made herself count to two.

"Do you have paper?" she asked.

A pause outside. "Ma'am?"

"A requisition order. An inspection order. Something with a signature on it. I don't open this door to men I can't name."

Another pause, longer. Then, muffled, the sound of paper being unfolded against the wind. "Order 114-C, countersigned Hargrove, Captain Ellery. Inspection of coastal navigational structures, all outbuildings, stores, and—" a breath, the sergeant reading quickly, wanting to be done with reading— "records."

*Records.* Her mouth had gone dry. She looked back up the stairwell, up toward the lamp room where Tomás lay hidden in oilskin and lead, and thought: he wrote *records* because someone above him wrote it first, and someone above *that* wrote it first, and somewhere at the top of that chain a man she would never meet had decided that even a lighthouse's papers belonged to the war.

She opened the door.

Three of them came in out of the wind — Sergeant Doyle, tall and sandy and tired around the eyes; a corporal with a notebook; and a boy who couldn't have been seventeen, carrying a lantern and looking at everything the way Rolo looked at things, which was to say hungrily, which was to say dangerously. Water dripped off their cap brims onto her clean floor. The wind came in behind them and guttered the hall lamp, and Cyrus moved past them to shield the flame with her body, an instinct older than any of them, and when she turned around she found all three watching her do it.

"Mind the draft," she said. "That lamp's fed from the main supply. If it goes out, I've got to bleed the line and relight, and you'll be standing here in the dark while I do it."

"Yes, ma'am," said the corporal, and wrote something down.

*Yes ma'am.* She could work with yes-ma'am. She had spent eleven years in this town learning how little yes-ma'am bought a keeper's widow, but tonight it was currency, and she intended to spend all of it.

"Where do you want to start?" she said. "Oil store's below. Lamp room's up one hundred and twelve steps, and I'll tell you now my husband counted them every week for nine years and I'm not going to have his count disturbed by boots."

Doyle almost smiled. Almost. "We'll try to step soft, ma'am. Corporal Voss will take the stores. Private—" he glanced at the boy— "Ansel, you're with me. We'll do the living quarters and the log room. Ma'am, if you'd walk with me? Captain's orders are that the keeper accompany any inspection of records."

*Of course they are.* Of course the army, which trusted no one, trusted least the person whose house it was searching.

* * *

They went up together, Doyle and the boy and Cyrus, and the stairs did what stairs do in a lighthouse, which is turn and turn again, so that climbing them was like being wound. Doyle talked as he climbed, the way men talk when silence frightens them more than questions.

"Been keeping this light long, ma'am?"

"Eleven years. Alone these last two."

"That's hard work for a woman alone."

"It's the same work," Cyrus said, "as for anyone."

He had the grace to look abashed. The boy, Ansel, looked at her sidelong with something like awe, and she understood suddenly that to him she was not a widow or a suspect but a kind of creature out of a story — a woman who lived inside a light. Boys were like that. Rolo had been like that, once, before the soldiers came and gave his hunger a uniform to fix on.

The living quarters took ten minutes. Doyle looked under the bed with genuine embarrassment, apologized twice, and found nothing but Tomás's spare coat, which Cyrus had not been able to give away and could not wear. The boy touched the coat's collar before she could stop him, and she watched his fingers learn the wool and saw him understand, without being told, whose coat it had been, and something in his face went young and sorry, and she hated herself for the softness that rose in her in answer.

The log room was worse.

It was a small stone chamber off the second landing, built by the Trinity men before the town had a voice in anything, and it held the station's records: the storm logs going back sixty years, the oil consumption books, the visitor registers, the correspondence files. And interleaved among the storm logs — where Tomás had worked for years, in the evenings, in the lamplight, telling her he was *just tidying the entries, love, you know how the old clerks ran on* — the partial transcription lay in its careful disguise, pages of his close small hand tucked between official forms.

Cyrus stopped on the threshold and let Doyle go in first.

"Storm logs," he said, pleased, in the way of a man finding exactly what he expected. He pulled the ledger for '08 down off the shelf and opened it across his forearm. "Captain wants dates and barometer readings cross-checked against the naval logs. There was a gale in October of '08 that nearly cost the *Meridian* her rudder. They think the light may have failed."

"The light has never failed," Cyrus said.

"Not what I said, ma'am. They think it may have."

"Then they think wrong, and you can write that down."

But he wasn't listening anymore. He was turning pages, running his thumb down columns of wind and water, and Cyrus stood in the doorway with her hands folded and her pulse ticking in her throat and understood that she was watching a man search for one number in a book that hid another book entirely. Tomás's transcription began on the verso of the March '09 storm entries. If Doyle turned past March—

"Here," Doyle said. "October. Barometer twenty-eight-six at midnight, rising by four. That matches the *Meridian*'s log. Good. Ansel, note it."

The boy noted it. Doyle flipped forward, looking for November's continuation, and Cyrus saw his thumb arrive at the page edge where March began, and she spoke before she had decided to speak, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing she had done since Tomás died.

"Sergeant. Before you go further — I'll want that logged."

He looked up. "Logged?"

"You're handling station records with bare hands, in a wet coat, over an open notebook. If you tear a page, or transfer ink, or drop saltwater on the binding, that goes in my incident report and mine alone. Captain Hargrove's inventory covers structures and stores. These books are the property of the Board of Trade, not the Army, and if your captain wants to answer to the Board for damaged navigation records, he's welcome to correspond with their office himself. I can give you the address."

She had invented perhaps a third of that. She had no idea whether the storm logs belonged to the Board of Trade. But the words came out level and slow and possessed of an authority she did not entirely feel, and she watched them land on Doyle the way cold water lands on a stove iron — hiss, shrink, retreat.

"I wasn't going to damage anything, ma'am."

"No," she agreed. "You weren't. But accidents need witnesses, and you have one. Read your dates and put the book back on the shelf, and we'll both be able to sleep."

The boy was staring at her. Doyle stared a moment too, and then something shifted in his face — not anger, but the particular relief of a man who has been given permission to stop doing a job he disliked. He read his October dates. He closed the book. He slid it back onto the shelf with both hands, carefully, the way one returns a sleeping child to a bed, and stepped back.

"Thank you, ma'am," he said, and meant it, and she could have wept.

* * *

The oil store below took another twenty minutes. Corporal Voss counted drums and tested seals and wrote figures in his notebook, and Cyrus answered his questions about burn rates and winter reserves with numbers she knew better than her own birthday, and all the while the fear sat in her chest like swallowed ice, melting slowly, refusing to be hurried. Because the inspection was nearly done — nearly — and it had gone well, it had gone *too* well, and some superstitious keeper's part of her mind insisted that luck spent early is luck borrowed against disaster.

It was the boy who nearly undid it.

They were coming back up through the lower passage, single file, lanterns swaying, when Ansel stopped. Just stopped, mid-stride, and turned, and shone his lantern up at the ceiling of the passage where the old lead flashing ran along the roofline joint — the same flashing, the same joint, that continued upward through the whole height of the tower to the lamp room above.

"What's up there?" he asked.

"Roofing," said Cyrus.

"It runs all the way up, doesn't it. You can see it from the gallery. There's a gap near the top — where the metal's lifted. I noticed it when we landed. Looks like something could get behind it."

The passage went very quiet except for the wind, which chose that moment to lean on the tower and make every pipe and cable hum. Cyrus felt the fear return all at once, full strength, and she made herself look at the boy — really look — and saw not suspicion in his face but only curiosity, bright and blameless as a gull's eye.

"Water gets behind it every winter," she said. "That gap's why I've a repair order in with the district office that's been ignored for two years. Birds nest in there. Gulls, mostly. Sometimes a stormcock." She paused, and let her voice dry slightly, the way a busy woman's voice dries. "If you're volunteering to climb one hundred and twelve steps and lie on the gallery in this wind to look at bird nests for the Army, son, I'll fetch you the ladder myself."

The boy flushed to his ears. "No, ma'am. Sorry, ma'am."

"Then let's be about it."

Doyle laughed — short, surprised, real — and clapped the boy's shoulder, and they finished the passage, and Cyrus walked last with the lantern held high and her hand shaking so badly inside her sleeve that she pressed it flat against the cold stone wall just to make it stop, and the stone was indifferent, and the indifference steadied her.

At the door, Doyle produced his form and read the findings aloud: structure sound, stores correct, records intact and properly kept. He signed it. He hesitated.

"Ma'am. About the ledger business. The town hall records." He looked out at the black water rather than at her. "I carried some of those boxes. It didn't sit right. I grew up in a place like this. Smaller. Nobody wrote our names down anywhere either, till the census men came, and my mother acted like it was Christmas." He cleared his throat. "Anyway. Light's in good order. Best-kept station on this coast, I'd say."

"I know," said Cyrus. "I keep it."

He almost smiled again, and this time he let it finish, and then the three of them were down the steps and into the boat and the lantern-glow was sliding away across the dark water, swinging now like a hand instead of holding like a fist, and the sea took the sound of the oars and folded it away.

* * *

She did not go up immediately.

She stood in the doorway with the wind pulling her hair loose and listened to the tower above her — the great lens ticking as the clockwork turned it, the flame breathing in its glass, the whole machine of attention she had tended for eleven years going about its patient work — and she waited until her legs agreed to hold her without the doorframe's help.

Then she climbed. All one hundred and twelve steps, counting them as Tomás had counted them, and at the top she knelt at the lead flashing with a cold chisel and a mallet wrapped in cloth, and she did not pry the metal open — not tonight, not with the barometer falling and her hands unsteady — but laid her palm flat against it instead, over the place where the packets slept, and held it there a long time, the way you'd check a fever, or a heartbeat.

"They came," she said aloud, to Tomás, to the tower, to whoever keeps such accounts. "They went through everything. And they left."

The wind backed another point to the southeast. Far out, past the shoals, thunder muttered like furniture being moved in a room above.

And it was then, kneeling there with her hand on the hidden names, that she saw it: the supply ship's riding light, far down the channel, making for harbor ahead of the weather — and strung along its deck, catching the lightning's first pale flicker, the shapes of crates being lashed down for the run in. Cargo. Army cargo, coming with the storm.

She stayed at the rail a long time, watching it grow.
