## 15. Hargrove's Ledger

The requisition office had been the town's reading room once. Hargrove knew this because the shelves were still bolted to the walls, empty now, their brass rails green with salt damp, and because someone — a librarian, a schoolmistress, he did not know who — had left a small card pasted inside the door frame that read *QUIET, PLEASE* in faded copperplate. He had not removed it. It seemed the only instruction in this entire enterprise he was willing to follow.

He worked by lamplight after the clerks went to their billets. The accounts of a requisitioned town arrived as ledgers, and ledgers were the one country where Captain Ellery Hargrove had never felt like a foreigner. Columns did not lie, or rather, they lied so predictably that a patient man could read the lies like handwriting. He spread them across the long reading table: the tonnage manifests for the transport barges, the valuation schedules, the receipts for livestock and timber and oil, the paymaster's disbursements, the quartermaster's requisitions against his own requisitions.

Outside, the sea worked at the rocks below the point with its old patience. The lamp above him turned. He had grown used to its sweep crossing the window at intervals — a slow white breath over the ceiling, over the papers, gone. Some nights it was the only thing that told him time still moved.

He began with the timber.

The town's workshops had produced, according to the valuation schedule, some four hundred cords of seasoned oak and fruitwood, inventoried, priced, and allocated to coastal defense construction. The allocation figures were correct. The prices were correct. The tonnage shipped, however — Hargrove laid his ruler along the column twice, then a third time with a pencil mark at each entry — the tonnage shipped exceeded the valuation by nearly a hundred and forty cords. Somewhere between the orchard groves and the army's books, wood existed that no one had ever priced, and someone had been paid for it anyway.

He sat back. The lamplight flickered in a draft from the ill-fitted window, and the great light swept the room again, and he watched the shadows of the empty shelves lean and right themselves like men standing up from a table.

It could be error. It was almost always error. In eight years of logistics he had learned that fraud was rare and arithmetic was everywhere; that the world conspired toward mistake far more often than toward crime. He wrote *query — cordage variance* in the margin in his small square hand and turned to the livestock schedules.

* * *

By midnight he had stopped believing in error.

The pattern was too clean. Errors scattered; they fell where carelessness fell. This lay along a single line. The discrepancies clustered wherever Quartermaster Brandt's countersignature appeared — on the fruit shipments especially, crates of apples and pears and quince listed as spoiled or undersized and struck from the valuation at a fraction of their worth, then logged onward to the supply depot at full weight and full price. Spoiled fruit does not weigh two thousand pounds to the crate. Spoiled fruit does not arrive. And yet the depot's inward manifests, which Brandt could not have known Hargrove would request, showed the crates arriving whole, sound, and promptly sold.

Someone was buying an orchard for nothing and selling it back to the army at a profit. Someone was doing it under a captain's signature chain, which meant the chain reached above Hargrove, because Brandt answered to Major Cleve, and Cleve answered to the district command, and the district command had signed the requisition order that hung framed on this very wall.

Hargrove took off his glasses and pressed the bridge of his nose until he saw faint constellations.

He thought about the widow who delivered his lamp oil. Reyes. She came every fourth day down from the light station with the can balanced on her hip, set it inside the door without knocking, took the empties, and left. Her eyes were the color of wet slate and she looked at him — he had catalogued this without meaning to — the way one looks at weather coming in off the water. Not hostile. Assessing. As though he were a front she was deciding whether to batten down against.

He had caught himself, twice now, wanting her to knock. Wanting her to set down the can and say something true about the town, about what the requisition had done to it, about the old woman Vidal who stood in the street each morning looking at the boarded workshop windows as if they might blink first. He wanted, absurdly, to be told what he had done. No one would tell him. His orders were models of administrative clarity and moral silence, and his men were tired and mostly kind, and the townspeople had arranged themselves around the soldiers like water around a hull — present, necessary, untouched.

And now there was this. A ledger full of quiet theft wearing the army's own ink.

The lamp swept through again. *QUIET, PLEASE*, said the card on the door frame.

Hargrove pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began, methodically, to copy out the suspect entries in his own hand — not the official abstract, his own private one, dated and initialed, folded into quarters and placed in the breast pocket of his tunic where the orders rode. If it was nothing, he would burn it. If it was something —

If it was something, then everything he had done here with such care — the inventories, the fair valuations, the courteous notices, the insistence that the blacksmith's widow be paid before winter — all of it was ballast on a ship whose cargo hold had been cut open from within. He had been keeping honest accounts for dishonest ones. He had been the respectable face of a robbery, meticulous enough to make the robbery look like government.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, and the great light above him turned and turned, laying its white bar across the room like a hand smoothing paper flat.

* * *

In the morning he sent for Brandt.

The quartermaster arrived smelling of bay rum and the depot's coal smoke, rubicund, deferential in the way of men who have decided you are harmless. He stood at ease before the reading table while Hargrove laid three documents side by side and said nothing for a long moment, letting the silence do what silence does.

"Quartermaster, explain the fruit."

"Sir?"

"The orchard lots. Forty-one through fifty-eight." Hargrove tapped the valuation schedule. "Struck as spoiled. Yet the depot received them intact. I have the inward manifests."

Something crossed Brandt's face — not guilt, quite. Recalculation. A man checking which version of the truth the officer had already purchased. "Spoilage in transit is common, sir. Sea air. Brine damage—"

"These crates never went to sea. They went by cart to the depot. Your own escort receipts show it."

Brandt's eyes went to the window, to the shelves, to the card in the door frame — anywhere but the columns. When they came back they had settled into a blandness so complete it was almost impressive. "There may be clerical irregularities, sir. The volume of material moving through this district — errors are inevitable. I'll open an inquiry."

"You will do nothing of the sort." Hargrove heard his own voice go flat and quiet, the voice he used for gunnery corrections. "You will produce your original purchase records, your correspondence with Major Cleve's office regarding disposal of perishables, and the deposit slips for salvage proceeds. Today."

For half a second — less — the blandness cracked, and beneath it Hargrove saw something quick and cold and appraising, the look of a man measuring a doorway he may need to carry a body through. Then it closed over, smooth as harbor water, and Brandt saluted.

"Of course, sir. I'll have my clerk assemble the files."

"Do it yourself," Hargrove said. "Your clerk has been reassigned to pier duty as of this morning. Effective an hour ago."

That, at least, landed. Brandt's ears reddened. He saluted again, turned with parade correctness, and left, and his boots on the stairs sounded unhurried, which frightened Hargrove more than running would have. Guilty men ran. Dangerous men walked.

* * *

He spent the afternoon walking the town, because a man can only sit with columns for so long before the columns begin to breathe.

The workshop stood behind its hoardings, the army's red-and-white notices bleached already by salt wind. Through a gap in the boards he could see the great press, shrouded in sacking, and the seal presses racked above the bench like sleeping birds. Six weeks, his orders said, and then the building would come down and its press and its seals with it. He had signed that schedule himself. He had thought nothing of it. Authentication equipment was obsolete when the central registry held the authority — that was procedure, that was modernity, that was the whole point of the war's great machine, that memory should live in one place where it could be governed.

Standing there in the cold, with Brandt's counterfeit columns folded against his chest, Hargrove understood for the first time what else lived in one place where it could be governed. Everything. Every proof of who these people were.

A boy was watching him from the lane — the apprentice, Rolo, the one who trailed the sergeants adoringly and knew the name of every rifle in the company. The boy didn't wave today. He looked at Hargrove with an expression the captain could not read, something between hope and apology, and then he was gone down the alley like a bird startled off a wire.

At the harbor, the transport barge sat low with its cargo of the town hall's records — crated, tarped, bound for the registry depot. Two soldiers stood guard, bored, smoking. Hargrove looked at the crates a long time. Paper. Tons of it. The names of orchards and shares and marriages and debts, sealed and stamped and now property of the Crown, riding at anchor in a town that could prove nothing anymore.

*We took their ledger,* he thought, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, *and we kept the accounting ourselves.*

His pocket, with its folded pages, seemed suddenly very heavy.

* * *

That evening the widow Reyes brought the lamp oil.

She came in the blue hour, when the room was all shadow except the one lamp he'd lit for the clerks' return tomorrow. She set the can inside the door, gathered the empties, and would have gone — she always went — but he said, quietly, "Mrs. Reyes."

She stopped. Waited. Did not turn fully; gave him her profile, sharp as a keel.

"The oil quality has been excellent," Hargrove said, which was not what he meant, and they both knew it, and the silence stretched between them like a rope going taut.

"We render it ourselves," she said at last. "Tomás — my husband — preferred it that way. Said bought oil burned with someone else's profit in it."

She left before he could answer. The door clicked shut. The card on the frame said *QUIET, PLEASE*, and Hargrove stood alone in the empty reading room with the lamp turning overhead and the stolen orchards folded in his pocket, and understood that the woman who kept the light above this town knew exactly what he was, and lit the way home for him anyway.

He sat down at the reading table, opened Brandt's expected files — delivered late, thin, and wrong — and began, line by line, to build the case that would either save this town's honor or end his career attempting it.

Far above him, the great lamp turned, sweeping its patient white bar across the water, across the shoals, across the dark — keeping faith, as he could no longer entirely do, with everyone it touched.
