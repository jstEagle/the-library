## 16. The Missing Pages

The storm season had not yet begun, but the sea had opinions anyway. All through the third week of October it threw itself against the rocks below the light in long gray fists, and inside the lamp room the oil smelled of iron and patience, and the four of them worked by a single shaded lantern while the wind worried at the gallery rails like a dog testing a fence.

They had been three nights at the sorting. It was Rolo who had proposed it — the boy who could find order in anything, who alphabetized his master's chisels and once reorganized the entire coal store by weight of burn. *We can't copy what we haven't counted,* he said, and so they counted.

The oilskin packets came out of the wall cavity one at a time now, no longer handled like relics but like what Tomás had surely intended them to be: working documents. Cyrus had learned to read her dead husband's system the way she had once learned to read weather. The packets were numbered in his small slanted hand along the fold — Roman numerals pressed hard enough to emboss the waxed cloth. I through XXIV. Each packet held between eight and fourteen leaves, each leaf a faithful transcription of a ledger page, copied in Tomás's hand or in the clerk's older copperplate where Tomás had traced over fading ink to keep it legible. Marta had wept quietly the first night she saw her own family's name rendered there, the Vidal orchard claims going back to a grandfather she had never met, and after that first night she did not weep again but grew instead very still and very exact, as though grief had been distilled into diligence.

Cyrus kept the tally on a slate hung by the lantern. Packet I: orchard families A through D. Packet II: continued. She had drawn the whole ledger as Tomás must have understood it — a map made of names, the town rendered as inheritance rather than geography.

It was Ines who found the gap.

She found it the way she found everything, silently and without drama, the blacksmith's widow who had buried her hands in cold metal for eleven years and come up with nothing to show for it but competence. Near midnight on the third night she laid down her pen, flexed her fingers, and said, in the flat voice she used for facts:

"There's a seam here. Between fourteen and fifteen."

Cyrus came around the table. The lantern threw their shadows enormous against the curved stone of the lamp room, four giants bent over a circle of yellow light. Ines had the last leaf of packet XIV in one hand and the first leaf of XV in the other. XIV ended mid-register — the Ferreira family's workshop share, the entry complete, the clerk's flourish closing it. XV began without preamble, deep in a different section entirely, orchards again, the Almeida holdings.

"Perhaps he skipped forward," Rolo offered. "To save paper. Maybe he went back for the middle later."

"He numbered everything," Cyrus said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, the voice of someone standing on a ledge looking down. "He wouldn't skip."

She took the slate down and studied the map of packets she had built. I through VII, orchard families, unbroken. VIII through XI, common lands and water rights. XII through XIV, the fishing shares and the salt-house. Then XV, jumping back to orchards — no. Not back. Forward past something. She counted what should have fallen between the salt-house and the Almeida orchards, running her finger down the ledger's known structure from the fragments of Tomás's marginal notes, from the references scattered through the copied pages like signposts pointing at country that wasn't there.

The workshop families. Every claim to every bench and lathe and press and seal. Three generations of men and women who had built boats and barrels and bells in the long stone building at the harbor's edge. The section that mattered most now — because Hargrove had announced the workshop would be demolished, because its press was the only instrument left that could authenticate what they were making, because the workshop families were the ones whose proof would die with the walls if it wasn't already safe.

Gone. Packets that should have held perhaps thirty leaves. Nothing.

* * *

They searched until dawn. That was the terrible arithmetic of it — four people turning a lighthouse into an anthill, shaking out every packet twice, checking the numbering against itself, checking the wall cavity with a lantern and a mirror on a stick, checking the storm logs again for some notation they might have missed. Tomás had been a thorough man. He had hidden copies of his hiding places, cross-references tucked into the log entries for storms twelve years dead. If there were more packets, there would have been a thread leading to them.

There was no thread. There was a hole in the record shaped exactly like the future.

"It doesn't make sense," Rolo said, somewhere in the smallest hours. He had gone pale, the way boys go pale when the adult world shows them its underside. "Why would he stop there? Why copy everything else and leave out—" He stopped himself, hearing it.

"Because he didn't leave it out," Cyrus said. "Because it was taken out. Or taken away." She heard the words come out of her mouth and knew they were true before she knew why. "Look at the cut. Ines is right — it's clean. XIV ends complete. XV begins complete. Whoever removed those pages took finished work. Whole packets. Numbered ones." She laid her palm flat on the table, on the spread leaves glowing amber in the lamplight. "Tomás didn't lose these. Someone found them."

Marta had not spoken for an hour. Now she spoke, and her voice was the old voice, the tavern voice, the one that had argued compliance and defiance with equal contempt for anyone slow enough to need convincing. "Who?"

"Someone who knew about the copying," Cyrus said. "Someone close enough to touch the wall."

The lantern hissed. Below them the sea kept up its patient demolition of the rocks, wave after wave after wave, the sound of a thing being erased very slowly.

* * *

Ines left first, as she always did, slipping down the spiral stair before full light so that no early riser in the village would mark her coming from the keeper's house. Rolo followed an hour later with a legitimate errand — a crate of lamp glass to carry to the chandlery, a boy's chore, invisible. Marta stayed. She stayed the way old women stay when staying is a decision, planted at the table with her hands folded over the incomplete map of her town.

"Think," she said. "Think back. You lived here six years before Tomás died. Who came to this house?"

"Almost no one. That was the point, wasn't it? A widow keeps a light and minds her business. The town decided early I was cold, and I let them believe it because it was easier than arguing." Cyrus stood at the seaward window. The morning was the color of pewter; the cutter lay at anchor in the roadstead like a knife left in bread. "He went to town alone, most weeks. The hall, the chandlery, the tavern sometimes. He'd be gone half a day."

"And when he came home?"

Cyrus closed her eyes and made herself walk those days again, the ones she had never examined because examining them would have meant admitting she wanted to be invited along. Tomás coming up the cliff path with his coat collar up. Tomás setting down his satchel. Tomás — and here something moved in her memory, a door she had assumed was a wall —

— Tomás burning papers once. In the kitchen stove, late, thinking she was asleep. She had watched him from the stair for a minute without understanding, watched him feed the fire leaf by leaf with great care, not throwing them but placing them, and she had thought: *accounts.* And then she had thought: *he'll tell me if it matters.* And then six years of marriage had taught her that Tomás told her almost nothing, gently, completely, the way a lighthouse tells you nothing about the rocks except where they are not.

"I don't know," she said aloud, and hated how true it was. "I don't know who he trusted. I didn't ask. I thought — I thought whatever he carried was his to carry."

Marta looked at her for a long moment, and there was no judgment in it, only the terrible equality of two women who had both spent years not asking questions. "Then we think about who else knew the ledger well enough to know what was missing."

"The clerk."

"Alcides." Marta said the name the way you'd spit a pit. "Forty years behind that desk. He inked half those pages himself. If Tomás was copying in secret, Alcides either helped him or didn't notice — and a man doesn't fail to notice his own ledger leaving his own hall page by page."

"Unless he was paid not to notice."

"Or paid to help." Marta unfolded her hands at last. Outside, the light was strengthening, mean and thin. "Either way, girl, there's a man in this town who knows where those pages went. And he's the only one."

* * *

Cyrus went down into the town that afternoon, which she almost never did, and felt the strangeness of walking streets that had become a garrison. Requisition notices still hung on the church door, curling at the edges. Soldiers moved crates on the quay with the unhurried efficiency of men who had done this in nine other towns. A boy too young for any of it whistled while he coiled rope. Life insisting on itself, the way grass comes up through cobblestones.

Alcides the clerk lived above the chandlery now, in rooms requisitioned down to a single chamber, the rest given over to naval stores. Cyrus climbed the outside stair and knocked, and when there was no answer she knocked again, harder, and put her ear to the wood.

A cough answered her. Then, faintly, a voice like dry paper tearing: "If you've come about the inventory, the inventory's done. Everything's inventoried. Ask me nothing else, I'm dying and I'm tired."

Cyrus stood on the stair with the wind pulling at her skirt and her heart going strangely loud. Through the door she could smell camphor and sickness and, beneath it, the ghost-smell of ink — the smell of the town hall, of the ledger, of forty years of small names written large enough to survive.

"Señor Alcides," she said. "It's Cyrus Reyes. Tomás Reyes's wife."

Silence. Long enough that she thought he had drifted off. Then the coughing again, worse, and the shuffle of a body deciding to stand, and footsteps like a man walking on his own grave, and the bolt drawing back.

The door opened on a face like a collapsed tent. Alcides had been old when she'd arrived in town; now he was ancient, translucent, propped against his own furniture. But his eyes — his eyes went to her face and then past her shoulder, down the stairs, along the empty street, quick as a young thief's, before he stepped aside and let her in.

"You'd better sit," he said. "And you'd better tell me who else knows you're here. Because I sold those pages, señora, seven years ago, to a man in a uniform — and if the army has sent you, I'll tell you the same thing I'll tell God, which is that a man who can't feed his daughter will sell anything, even the truth, even his own hand's work."

The room tilted slightly. Cyrus gripped the back of the chair.

"Sold them," she repeated. "Which pages."

"The workshop pages." Alcides lowered himself onto his cot with the care of a man handling broken glass. "Every leaf your husband brought me to check against my memory. I kept them a week. I told him I needed to verify the old entries." His mouth twisted. "I verified them straight into a quartermaster's saddlebag, for money I spent on medicine that didn't work. Your husband died believing his copy was whole." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and utterly clear. "So. Now you know where the missing pages are. The question, Mrs. Reyes — the question I've lain awake with every night since the ships came — is whether the man who bought them has any idea what he's carrying."

Outside, far out past the shoals, a smudge of smoke sat on the horizon where the horizon should have been empty. A supply ship, making for the harbor, beating against the falling glass.

The barometer in the corner of Alcides's room gave a small, sick shudder downward, like a heart missing a beat.
