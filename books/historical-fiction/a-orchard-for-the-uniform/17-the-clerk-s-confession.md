## 17. The Clerk's Confession

The room above the cooperage smelled of pitch and camphor and the sweetish rot that came off a man when his body had begun the long business of leaving him. Aldous Ferro had been the town clerk for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once slept past dawn; now he could not stay awake past noon, and the indignity of it sat on his chest heavier than the fever did.

He heard her on the stairs — not Cyrus Reyes, though she came too, later. He heard Ines first, the blacksmith's widow, whose tread was like her late husband's hammers: even, unhurried, impossible to ignore. Then the door opened on a draft of cold harbor air and the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet but meant to.

"You sent for me," Ines said. It was not a question. She set down the covered dish someone had pressed on her at the tavern — Marta Vidal's fish stew, no doubt, because Marta fed even men she despised, which was its own kind of judgment — and stood with her arms folded, looking at him the way one looks at a tool that has been left out in the weather.

"I sent for you," Aldous said, "because I am dying, and dying men are owed nothing, and so whatever they give is freely given." He tried to smile. His mouth had forgotten how. "I learned to talk like that from the requisition manuals."

"Then say it plainly."

"I will try." He pulled himself higher against the pillows, and the effort cost him something he would not get back, and he knew it, and did it anyway. "Bring the lamp closer. What I have to show you needs light."

* * *

He had kept the duplicates in the false bottom of his document chest for eleven years, and no one had ever found them, because no one had ever looked, because no one read the old ledger anymore — that was the joke he'd made at the town hall, wasn't it, the day Cyrus Reyes stood there with her keeper's posture and her outsider's eyes, *no one reads it anymore*, and he had laughed at his own joke the way a man laughs at a headstone he has carved for himself and not yet noticed.

The chest came open under Ines's hands. Beneath the neat strata of tax rolls and mooring fees lay a board that looked like every other board, and beneath the board, wrapped in waxed linen gone brittle as autumn leaf, were pages.

Not copies of pages. The pages themselves.

"The clerk is supposed to make fair copies," Aldous said. "That is the whole of my trade, Ines — I copy true things into truer hands. But a man who copies everything learns what can be copied twice." He watched her lift the first folio, watched her thumb find the watermark of the workshop press, the little olive branch that three generations of families had trusted more than any crown or ministry. "When the army's agents came through two years ago — surveyors, they called themselves, quiet men with good boots — one of them took an interest in the ledger. Not official interest. Personal interest. He offered me money for duplicate leaves. Originals, he said, carried more weight than copies. He was right. That was the obscenity of it. He was right."

Ines turned a page. Her face did not change, which was worse than if it had.

"How many?" she asked.

"Fourteen leaves. All of them from the workshop sections. The Fenn claims. The Corveras. The Ostrander shares." He recited the names like a man reciting his own crimes, which was precisely what he was doing, and found that the recitation neither freed nor condemned him — it simply emptied him further, the way a tapped cask empties, slowly, by gravity, whether or not anyone is there to catch the run. "I told myself the ledger still held its truth. I told myself paper is only paper. I told myself a great many things, Ines, and every one of them was a coin I accepted instead of refusing the money."

"And the man's name?"

"Brandt." He said it and felt the name leave him like a splinter coming out sideways. "Quartermaster Brandt. He wore civilian clothes then, but he had a soldier's way of standing, feet planted like fence posts. He'll come back. Men like that come back where the pickings proved easy. And when the requisition came—" He coughed, deep and wet, and rode it out gripping the bedframe until his knuckles went the color of tallow. When he could speak again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "When the requisition came and they took the whole ledger, I understood what I had done. I didn't just sell fourteen leaves. I taught them the ledger could be sold. I made it merchandise before they ever laid hands on it."

* * *

Cyrus Reyes arrived as the light was going copper over the harbor. Aldous heard her boots on the stair — a keeper's tread, deliberate, each step chosen — and braced himself the way one braces for weather one has earned.

She filled the doorway with lamplight behind her, and for a moment he saw her as the town had always seen her: the stranger, the widow, the woman who married Tomás Reyes and never quite became one of them no matter how many winters she trimmed the wick above them all. He had helped make that estrangement too, in small ways, thirty-one years of small ways — the registry entries filed a day late for outsiders, the welcome recorded but never warm. Shame, he was learning, was not a single stone but a wall built of pebbles.

"Aldous Ferro," she said, "you look terrible."

"I look honest," he said. "For the first time in years. Dying does it faster than confession, but they work along the same lines." He gestured at the open chest, at Ines sitting sentinel beside it. "Ines will have told you."

"She started to."

"Let me finish it, then. A man should sign his own confession." He gathered himself. Outside, the gulls were making their ugly argument with the wind, and somewhere below a barrel hoop rang against stone, the ordinary music of a town that did not yet know how thoroughly it had been robbed. "Mrs. Reyes. Cyrus. You found Tomás's hand in the storm logs. I know you did — you've been burning lamp oil at strange hours, and a lighthouse burns honest oil or dishonest oil, but never secret oil, not without someone noticing." He almost smiled. "Tomás came to me six years ago. Asked to see the ledger. Sat with it three nights running in this room, copying in that shorthand of his, the one he invented for ship's logs because he said plain writing tells plain lies. I asked him why. Do you know what he answered?"

Cyrus had gone very still. Even across the room Aldous could see her hands close, one over the other, the way hands close around something that might fly away.

"He said, 'Because paper burns, Aldous, and ink fades, and the only thing that survives either is a second copy in a second place.' He'd already guessed. Your Tomás guessed what I only confirmed — that this town's memory was one chest in one hall, and chests can be carried." Aldous's breath caught; he waited it out. "I should have told him about Brandt. I should have told him I'd already begun selling pieces of the thing he was copying to save. But he looked at that ledger like it was scripture, and I couldn't bear to be seen by eyes like that. So I let him copy, and I let Brandt buy, and I told myself the two sins cancelled, and here I lie, dying, with both of them still on the scale."

"What do you want from us?" Ines asked, flat as her anvils.

"Nothing you can give." He shook his head, slow, careful of his own fragility. "Absolution isn't sold in shops, and even if it were, I couldn't afford it — that lesson I learned at last. No. I want to be useful. Once. Before the ground closes over the account." He reached beneath the pillow with a hand that trembled like a compass needle near iron, and drew out a single sheet, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling. "This is the bill of sale. My signature. Brandt's mark. The dates, the sums, the leaves described by section and line. If the missing pages ever surface — and they will, men like Brandt keep trophies, they keep proof of their cleverness the way magpies keep buttons — this paper proves they were stolen, not lost. Proves the record was mutilated by purchase, not by accident or age. It makes your restored copy unimpeachable. Or it damns me properly. Either way, it belongs to the town, not to me."

Cyrus crossed the room and took the paper. She read it standing, by the lamp, and Aldous watched her read and thought: *she reads like Tomás read*, and the thought hurt more than the fever, and he welcomed the hurt because it was the last honest thing available to him.

"Why now?" she asked quietly. "You've had years to bring this forward."

"Because until the soldiers came, my crime was theoretical. Money for paper — who's harmed? Then they put the notices on the church door and carried the chest to the barge, and I stood on the quay and watched Marta Vidal weep, and I understood that I had unscrewed the door for them myself." His voice cracked on the old woman's name and he let it. "Marta. God help me. She brought me stew tonight. She doesn't know. She thinks I'm only dying."

"You are only dying," Ines said. "The rest is what you did while living."

"Yes." He closed his eyes. "Yes. That's well said. Write it down somewhere. You people are good at that."

* * *

They stayed while the light failed entirely, Cyrus asking questions in her level keeper's voice — dates, sums, descriptions, the shape of Brandt's accent, whether he'd mentioned a ship, a schedule, a superior — and Aldous answered everything, pouring himself out leaf by leaf like a ledger being wound down to its final entry. It was, he discovered, the best he had felt in eleven years. Not lighter, exactly. Truer. There was a difference, and the difference was that truth weighs more, and he had spent his life mistaking weightlessness for peace.

Near the end, when his voice had worn down to a whisper and Cyrus had to lean close to hear, he caught her sleeve with two fingers.

"The supply ship," he whispered. "Brandt's masters will want their profits inventoried before the storms close the roads. Ask Hargrove — no. Don't ask Hargrove. Watch the harbor. Watch what comes in on the next ship, before the barometer finishes falling." His fingers tightened, surprising strength in a dying grip. "And Mrs. Reyes — tell Marta the stew was good. Tell her it was the best thing I ate in thirty-one years. Let her decide what that's worth."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment, and he braced for contempt, or pity, or the cold arithmetic of a woman deciding whether a traitor's last gift was worth the candle. Instead she folded the bill of sale into her coat with the same care he imagined she gave the wicks, and said:

"I'll tell her."

Then she was gone down the stairs, and Ines banked the fire, and Aldous Ferro lay listening to the wind come up off the water — a wind with weight behind it, a wind with weather riding on its back — and thought about fourteen leaves of paper in some quartermaster's coat, folded against a heart that had none, sailing toward this coast with the storm.
