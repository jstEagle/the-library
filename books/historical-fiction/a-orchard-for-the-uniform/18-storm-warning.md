## 18. Storm Warning

The barometer began its long fall on a Tuesday, in the hour before dawn, when the glass still held the cold of the night and Cyrus Reyes carried it down from the lamp room the way she carried it every morning — cupped in both hands like an egg she had promised someone she would not break.

Tomás had taught her to read it. *Tap it gently*, he'd said, in his careful, accented English, *so the mercury can make up its mind.* She tapped it now against her palm and watched the column shudder and slide. Twenty-nine inches and falling. By evening it would be lower.

She set the instrument on the kitchen table, beside the oilskin packets wrapped in flannel and hidden beneath a false bottom in the flour bin, and stood looking at both — the weather and the secret — as if they might explain each other.

They did, in a way. Everything in this town had always come by sea: the fish, the soldiers, the requisitions, the grief. Why not the rest of it too. Why not the missing pages, folded in some quartermaster's coat, riding toward her on a supply ship whose name she didn't yet know but whose coming she could feel in her wrists the way old sailors felt rain.

*Tap it gently.*

Outside, the gulls had gone quiet. That was the first true sign, more than any glass. For three days they had wheeled and screamed over the harbor as if arguing among themselves, and now they were gone — inland, upriver, anywhere but here — and their absence lay over the water like a held breath. The sea itself had changed color, from slate to something darker and greener, a bruise spreading under the skin of the world.

Cyrus filled the kettle and lit the stove and tried to pray, which was not something she did often or well. What came out instead was arithmetic. Six weeks until the workshop was scheduled for demolition; Hargrove had said so plainly enough, standing in her doorway with his cap in his hands like a man calling on a sick relative. Six weeks minus the eleven days already gone. Five weeks minus whatever time it would take to transcribe what Tomás had copied and authenticate it with the press and seals that were even now gathering dust under guard. And somewhere out on that darkening water, a man named Brandt who was said to have bought pages of the ledger the way other men bought tobacco — folded flat, carried close, kept warm against the body like something loved.

She did not know how Marta Vidal had learned his name. Marta knew things the way the tide knew the moon; there was no asking how. But three nights ago, in the lighthouse kitchen, with Ines's hands steady on the shorthand pages and Rolo asleep sitting up with his cheek on the table, Marta had said it quietly, the way you name a disease: *Brandt. Quartermaster's corps. He'll come with the next supply run, and the next supply run will come before the storm does, because army men are never smart enough to be afraid early.*

Now the glass said otherwise. The storm was not waiting for anyone.

* * *

She climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the gallery at midmorning, though there was nothing yet to see, and stood in the wind — which had backed around to the southeast, another sign, warm and wet and wrong — and looked down at the harbor town that had never quite been hers.

From here the requisition notices on the church door were white rectangles, too small to read, like teeth knocked loose and scattered. The soldiers' tents on the green made pale circles. The barge that had taken the ledger was long gone; in its place rode two navy cutters and, tied at the coal wharf, a squat grey steam tender that coughed smoke all day and went nowhere.

The orchards climbed the hills behind the town, bare now, their rows showing like ribs through thin winter skin. Cyrus had lived below those trees for nine years and above them for six, and she could not walk one row of them without feeling like a trespasser. The trees belonged to names. The names belonged to a book. The book belonged to the army now, locked in some hold or office, and the people the names described walked those rows as tenants of their own history.

But Tomás had known. That was the thought she returned to, the splinter she could not work free. He had known years ago — long before the war, long before any cutter appeared offshore — that paper could be stolen from a town like marrow sucked from bone. He had sat in this very lamp room between storms, copying names in a shorthand he had invented himself or inherited from some clerk grandfather, sealing pages in oilskin behind lead flashing where no damp could reach them, and he had told her none of it. Not once. Not in nine years of marriage, not in the last winter when his cough got deep and they both pretended it wasn't what it was.

*Betrayal curdling into purpose* — that was how she had come to think of it, those two feelings stirred together in one pot until she could no longer tell which was which.

Below her, on the road from the town, a figure was climbing the path with the particular hunched urgency of someone carrying news. Rolo, fourteen years old and all elbows, taking the steps two at a time, and Cyrus felt her stomach drop before he reached the door, because boys only ran uphill like that when the news was bad or enormous, and lately the categories had merged.

"The supply ship," he said, breathless in the doorway, cheeks whipped red by the wind. "Ines heard it from the harbormaster's boy. Three days out. It put in at the naval station last night to top off coal because—" he stopped, gulped air, "—because the captain wants to beat the weather. They're rushing it. Everyone's rushing."

Three days. Cyrus looked past him at the southeastern horizon, where the sky had begun to organize itself into long grey bands like fingers closing.

"And Brandt?"

"Confirmed. Ines's cousin saw the manifest — quartermaster's department, one officer, four clerks." Rolo's voice dropped, and something moved across his face that she recognized and hated seeing there: the conflict of a boy who admired uniforms. "He travels well," the boy added, almost apologetically. "Better than Hargrove. Big cabin. Trunks."

Of course he did. The men who profited always traveled well. Cyrus had understood that much of the world without ever wanting the lesson.

"Thank you, Rolo." She hesitated, then asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since dawn. "Your mother — does she know you're coming here? Still?"

He understood what she was really asking. Whether she was safe to him. Whether the circle was. His family were soldiers' kin from three generations back; his uncle served in the very regiment camped on the green; and every evening he walked home from the lighthouse carrying secrets heavier than his school satchel, wearing his admiration for Captain Ellery Hargrove like a coat he couldn't take off because it fit so well.

"She thinks I'm doing keeper's work," he said. "I am, mostly. I mean—" He flushed. "I'd never tell. You know that."

"I know," Cyrus said, and meant it, and watched him go down the steps lighter than he'd come up, and wondered at herself for trusting a fourteen-year-old with the only unburned history of a town. But trust, she was learning, was not a thing you decided. It was a thing that happened to you, like weather.

* * *

That afternoon she went down into the town, against her own instinct, because the circle had agreed to meet and the meeting could not wait for good judgment.

The wind had strengthened through the day. Walking the harbor road she felt it shove at her back like a large impatient hand, and spray came over the sea wall in white rags, and the fishing boats — what remained of them, half requisitioned, half rotting — strained at their moorings and knocked their hulls together with a sound like slow applause. Above the rooftops the smoke from the bakery chimney bent hard inland and shredded.

Marta Vidal's house sat at the end of Rope Walk, stone-walled, salt-stained, older than anyone could say. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and the particular dust of rooms where old women have lived alone a long time and organized everything to their own logic. Ines was already there, the blacksmith's widow, a big quiet woman with burn-scarred forearms, and she had brought the transcription — the finished pages, forty-one of them now, copied in Tomás's shorthand and waiting only for translation and seal.

Marta poured tea no one drank.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe less. The harbormaster's boy says the barometer at the naval station read twenty-eight-six this morning." Her eyes went to Cyrus, sharp as gull beaks. "Yours?"

"Lower than yesterday. Falling fast."

"Then the ship will arrive with the storm on its heels, or inside it, or not at all." Marta said this without satisfaction, which surprised Cyrus until she understood: the old woman wanted the confrontation clean. She wanted her reckoning with Brandt in daylight, witnessed, entered into record. A wreck would rob her of it. Grief, Cyrus had learned watching Marta weep over the seized ledger, is a form of order. Storms disorder everything.

"If he comes," Ines said slowly, "he comes here. To this harbor. And we are—" She stopped, choosing the word with a smith's care. "We are what? Thieves to him. Criminals. If he learns his stolen pages are missing from his own theft—"

"He won't learn it from us," Marta said. "No one approaches him. No one speaks to him. No one so much as looks at him longer than politeness allows. We know nothing. We are a town being requisitioned, and requisitioned towns keep their heads down." She looked around the table, and her gaze lingered on Rolo, who had followed Cyrus in and stood dripping by the door, not quite invited, not quite sent away. "Especially the young ones. Especially the ones who admire uniforms."

Rolo's chin came up. "I don't admire *him*."

"You admire the buttons," Marta said, not unkindly, "and the boots, and the way they march. That's worse, at your age. Buttons are how they catch us."

The fire ticked. Outside, the wind found some loose shutter down the street and worked at it, patiently, rhythmically, like a debt collector knocking.

Cyrus turned her teacup in its saucer and said what she had been turning over since morning. "There's another thing. If the storm is what the glass says it will be, the light will matter. More than it has in years. Whatever else happens — whatever we do about Brandt, about the pages — I will be in that lamp room running the light through all of it. And if a ship founders on the shoals, I—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandmother ran a light on the Atlantic coast for thirty years. She used to say the lamp doesn't ask what's in the hold. It doesn't ask who built the ship or what they stole to build it. You trim the wick. You turn the clockwork. You save whoever the sea gives back to you."

"Even him?" Rolo asked.

"*Especially* him," Marta said, before Cyrus could answer, and there was something terrible and magnificent in the old woman's voice. "Because then he owes us. A drowned man owes nothing and tells no tales. A saved man owes everything and tells them whether he means to or not." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Let the sea do our persuading. The sea has always been better at it than we are."

* * *

She climbed home alone in the last light, and the world had gone the color of pewter and oyster shell, and the pressure in her ears made her swallow again and again like a diver.

Inside the lighthouse she did the rituals. Filled the oil reservoirs. Cleaned the lens with linen and a whisper of vinegar, though it didn't need it. Checked the clockwork that turned the great lamp, counting the weights down their shaft. Tapped the barometer — gently, so the mercury could make up its mind — and read twenty-eight-point-nine, and wrote the number in the log in her small square hand, and beneath it, after a moment, wrote: *Glass falling rapidly. Sea making up from SE. Gulls gone inland. Expect heavy weather within 36 hours.*

Then, because the logbook was the one place in the world where she could write anything and it would simply be weather, she added: *Supply ship expected. God help small boats.*

It was while winding the clockwork that she noticed the smell.

Faint, sweetish, wrong — coming from the base of the tower, from the storeroom where the oilskin packets had lived before she moved them to the flour bin. Damp. Not the ordinary salt damp that lived in every stone of the building like an old tenant, but fresh water, river-water damp, the smell of things soaking.

She took the lamp down and knelt at the storeroom door and saw it: a dark seam of water along the floor, seeping from the old cistern channel in the foundation, rising with the pressure change the way water rises in a well before a storm. Two winters ago, in the last great blow, the same channel had flooded to a hand's depth. She remembered mopping it for a day afterward and thinking nothing of it.

Nothing of it. Because there had been nothing in the room then worth drowning.

Now she imagined the storm surge — thirty, forty feet of driven sea against the tower's base — and the cistern channel brimming, and the storeroom ankle-deep or worse, and she stood in the cold draft with her heart going hard and understood that the building itself was conspiring against her. That the sea, which Marta wanted to do their persuading, had its own agenda entirely, and its agenda was to take everything back — ships, papers, names, all of it — into the one great ledger where nothing was ever written down.

She spent two hours moving the packets higher. There was nowhere truly high; the lamp room was a furnace of heat and vibration when the light burned, fatal to paper, and she would not risk fire. In the end she settled on a shelf above the stair landing, third flight, sealed in a bread tin, the tin wrapped in flannel and strapped with cord. It was not safe. Nothing was safe. It was merely less drownable, and she stood back and looked at it and thought: *this is what my life has become, deciding which disaster to lose to more slowly.*

Then she went up, because the light must be lit whether the world ends or not, and lit it.

* * *

Night came down black and early, the clouds stacked low over the water like wet wool, and the beam swung out across a sea that was already talking to itself in long deep voices.

Cyrus stood in the gallery with the wind tearing at her coat and listened. Under the growing roar of the surf there was another sound, farther out, rhythmic and industrial — the chuff of a steam engine working hard against weather. She could not see the ship. She could feel it, the way you feel a knock at the door before it lands.

Three days, Rolo had said. But the army was rushing it. Everyone was rushing.

She counted the intervals of the sound and judged the distance and did not like her answer. Tomorrow, perhaps. Tomorrow night at the latest. The quartermaster and his trunks and his well-traveled cabin, driving straight into the jaws of the first great storm, carrying her husband's stolen handwriting against his chest like a saint's medal worn by a man who deserved no saints.

And her — standing above it all with a lit lamp she was bound by duty and conscience and something older than both to keep burning. The lamp doesn't ask what's in the hold.

She went inside, warmed her hands at the stove, and opened the logbook to record the watch. Her pen hovered over the page. In the end she wrote only the numbers: wind, glass, sea state. The plain facts of a plain night that was not going to stay plain.

Far offshore, unseen in the murk, the sound of the engine grew a little louder, and the sea lifted its voice to meet it, and Cyrus Reyes trimmed the wick.
