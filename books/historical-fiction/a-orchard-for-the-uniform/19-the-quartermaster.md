## 19. The Quartermaster

The supply ship came in on a Tuesday morning, riding low and ugly in the water, her paint the gray of old dishwater. Cyrus watched from the gallery while she warped toward the quay, and even at that distance she could see the ship was wrong — too heavy in the stern, riding like a man carrying money he hadn't earned.

She had learned to read ships the way other people read faces. Tomás had taught her that much before he taught her anything else, standing beside her on this same gallery with his hand warm at the small of her back, naming the vessels that came up the coast: *that one's a fruit boat, you can tell by the dunnage; that one's navy, look how she keeps her boats shipped.* He had loved ships the way he'd loved paper — for what they carried hidden inside them.

This one carried something of hers. She was nearly certain of it.

The barometer in the lamp room had begun its slow fall two days ago, the needle creeping down the dial like a thing trying not to be noticed. Marta Vidal had come up the path yesterday with her shawl knotted tight and her face set like winter, and she had said only: *The supply ship. Brandt will be aboard it. And after Brandt, the weather.* Old women spoke like that here, folding prophecy into small talk, and Cyrus had stopped doubting them somewhere around the time she'd found her dead husband's handwriting folded into oilskin behind lead flashing.

She went down to make herself ready.

* * *

Quartermaster Silas Brandt came ashore an hour later, and Cyrus saw him first from the fish shed, where she had no honest business being.

He was not what she had imagined. She had pictured a large man, soft and sweating, some grossness of appetite made visible. Instead he was slight, narrow-shouldered, with a clerk's stoop and a face so unremarkable that her eyes slid off him twice before they settled. He wore his uniform well but without vanity, and he moved along the quay with quick, light steps, pausing to speak to soldiers who straightened when he passed. He smiled easily. That was the worst of it. He smiled like a man who had never once been struck in the face by anything harder than weather.

And beneath his left arm, where the coat hung a little stiffly away from the body, there was the shape. A fold of thickness in the cloth, square-cornered, held close as a prayer book. She watched him climb from the quay to the requisitioned customs house, and she watched his hand go once — briefly, unconsciously — to press the shape flat against his ribs, the way a man touches a wound or a talisman.

Her hands were shaking. Not with fear, or not only fear. With a hatred so sudden and so complete that it frightened her, because she had not known it lived in her. This small smiling man had bought her town's memory off a dying clerk for less than the cost of his boots, and he carried it against his heart like a keepsake, and he would sleep tonight with his breath rising and falling over the names of orchards he would never walk in.

*You are a vulture,* she thought at his retreating back. *A tidy little vulture in a good coat.*

Then she went home, and took down her ledger of lamp oil, and began to plan how to feed him.

* * *

The trick of it, she understood, was that she could not go to him. A lighthouse keeper petitioning a quartermaster was a woman asking a favor, and asking favors put you below him, and below him she could learn nothing. But a quartermaster — any quartermaster, any army anywhere since armies began — needed three things above all things: oil, water, and knowledge of the ground under his feet.

She had all three.

So she did nothing for two days except let the need ripen. She kept the lamp lit. She trimmed the wick. She filled out the requisition forms the army had imposed on her — *Lighthouse Station, Keeper: C. Reyes. Consumption of mineral oil per week* — in her neat schoolroom hand, and she waited until the harbor pilot's boy told her, grinning through missing teeth, that the quartermaster had been asking after fuel stores along the coast, because the supply line was stretched thin and winter was coming on, and the garrison's coal allotment had been cut in favor of ammunition.

On the third evening, she rowed in herself. It was a thing she did sometimes anyway — Tomás had always handled the town errands, and in the first year of her widowhood she had forced herself to take them up one by one until none of them frightened her anymore — so the sight of the keeper's dinghy nosing against the quay drew no particular attention. She tied off, climbed the steps, and walked to the customs house door where a sentry shifted his rifle and looked at her with the flat patience of a young man bored past courtesy.

"Keeper Reyes," she said. "I've business for the quartermaster. Fuel stores."

The word *fuel* worked on him like a key. He went inside, and she stood in the cold smelling tar and wet wool and the ghost of somebody's dinner, and she rehearsed what she had come to say until the words felt like someone else's.

Brandt appeared in the doorway himself, which surprised her. She had expected a sergeant, a screen of subordinates. Instead there he was, small and pleasant, holding the door with one hand.

"Keeper Reyes." Her name in his mouth sounded like an entry already filed. "Come in out of the wind. I'm told you're the authority on oil on this coast."

"I'm the authority on my own lamp," she said. "That's smaller, but it's true."

His laugh was quick and genuine-sounding. He led her into a room that had been the harbormaster's parlor in better years — she remembered it with lace curtains and a model schooner in a glass case — now stripped to a desk, a cot, crates stacked in ranks like headstones. A single lamp burned on the desk, and she noted automatically that it was turned low, burning rich and smoky, wasting itself. A man who didn't understand oil.

"You'll forgive the state of things," he said, sweeping a hand at the crates. "We're living out of boxes until the army finishes deciding where everything belongs."

*Everything,* she thought. *He means the town.*

"I won't waste your time," she said. "You're short on fuel. Everyone on this quay is short on fuel. I have a store of mineral oil at the station — better than what your barges bring, if what I see burning on your desk is a sample — and I know every cistern, every cache, every cellar between here and the north point that held oil before the requisition. My husband surveyed half of them."

Something moved behind his eyes — not greed exactly, but its cousin, the bright attention of a man hearing a number get bigger. "That's generous of you, Mrs. Reyes."

"It's practical," she said. "My lamp burns whether the war cares about it or not. Oil keeps better in company than alone." She let that sit, then added, lightly, the hook she had spent two days sharpening: "And I confess I'd welcome the call. Two winters alone in that tower, you start talking to the gulls."

There. The loneliness, offered up like bait — because men like this trusted lonely people, believed loneliness made a person pliable, never guessing it could also make them patient.

It worked. His face opened. "Two winters," he said, and for a moment something almost human crossed his features, some shadow of sympathy. "I lost a brother at— well. Never mind. Yes. Yes, I think we can arrange an exchange. Come tomorrow, we'll draw up the transfer properly, and you'll show my sergeant these caches of yours."

"Tomorrow," she agreed, and stood, and made herself smile at him.

As she rose, his hand went again to his left side — that small unconscious press, palm flat against the stiff fold in his coat — and their eyes met for half a second over the gesture, and she knew, with a certainty that went through her like cold water, that he knew she had noticed, and that neither of them would ever say so.

* * *

She rowed back in the dark with the wind freshening out of the southeast, and halfway across the harbor she shipped her oars and simply sat, letting the boat swing, letting the cold come up through the boards into her bones, because she could not row another stroke while her hands shook like that.

Bargaining with him had been worse than she'd feared, and she had feared it well. It wasn't the lying — lying had gone strangely easy, smooth as oiled rope, and that ease itself was a horror she'd examine later, at night, when the lamp was lit and there was nothing to do but think. It was that he was *kind*. He had spoken of his dead brother with real grief in his voice. He had laughed at her joke about the gulls. He was, by every evidence, a decent man wearing the same uniform as the requisition, and the pages of her town's history were folded under his arm next to that grieving human heart, kept like a charm against bad luck.

Vultures, she thought, rowing again, were at least honest about what they were. They circled in daylight. They did not smile at the dying.

She thought of Marta's face the day the records went onto the barge — the old woman standing on the quay with her fists at her sides, tears cutting clean lines down a face that hadn't wept in decades. *Grief for paper,* Ines had called it afterward, and been right, and been wrong. It had been grief for the proof that they existed. And tomorrow she would pour oil into that proof's thief's lamps and show his sergeant the secret cellars of her neighbors' houses, and thank God the caches she knew best had been emptied years ago, so that what she surrendered would be worthless.

Unless he asked about paper. Unless he asked about records, about the workshop, about seals and presses and authentication. Then she would have to lie to that kind face directly, and she did not yet know if she could.

At the station, Rolo was waiting on the steps with a lantern, fourteen years old and trying to look like a man who waited on nobody. His face when he saw her was the question itself.

"Well?" he said. Too fast, too loud, the way he'd been doing everything lately — torn down the middle between the boy who idolized the buttons and brass, and the boy who sat nights copying names by lamplight because the buttons and brass had stolen them.

"He'll come tomorrow for the oil," she said. "And I found where he keeps them."

Rolo's breath went out of him. "Where?"

"Under his arm." She climbed the steps past him, then paused, looking back at the harbor — at the low gray ship, at the falling glass, at the first thin clouds sliding up from the southeast like a curtain being drawn across the whole horizon. The wind smelled different now. Iron and salt and something older underneath.

"Rolo," she said quietly. "Get word to Marta and Ines. Tell them to come tomorrow at dusk. And tell them—" she looked at the sea again, at the long swell already beginning to organize itself out beyond the point, "—tell them to hurry. Because whatever happens with that man and those pages, I don't think the weather is going to give us much more time."

Out beyond the shoals, where the deep water began, the sea had started to speak in the low continuous voice it saved for very large things still far away.
