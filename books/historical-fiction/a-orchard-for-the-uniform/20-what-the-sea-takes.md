## 20. What the Sea Takes

The barometer had been falling for three days, the needle sliding down its dial like something drowning in slow motion. Cyrus had learned to read it the way other people read faces — the small tremors of hesitation before a decision, then the long, committed plunge. By Thursday evening it stood lower than she'd seen it since Tomás was alive, and the gulls had come inland in their hundreds, sitting sullen on the orchard walls with their backs to the sea, as if they wanted no part of what was coming.

She spent the afternoon preparing the way her husband had taught her: filling the reserve oil cisterns, testing the spare mantle, winding the clockwork that turned the lens, checking every bolt on the gallery rail against the wind's coming arguments. The army men at the station below had stopped pretending they were there for anything but shelter; Sergeant Doyle had helped her haul the last drum up the stair without being asked, and had not made a joke about it, which from Doyle was a kind of tenderness.

"You'll want to keep off the gallery after midnight," she told him. "When it comes, it comes all at once."

"Yes, ma'am." He looked out at the water, gray-green and wrong, the swell already long and low and fast. "It's like the whole ocean's holding its breath."

"It is. Then it lets it out." She said it without drama, because it was simply true, and Doyle nodded and went down the stairs, and Cyrus climbed to the lamp room to wait.

The light came on at dusk as it always did, the great lens gathering the flame into its five faces and throwing them seaward, and Cyrus stood inside the revolving glow and felt the old familiar peace of it — the one thing in this coast that had never been requisitioned, never seized, never argued over. Whatever the ledger said about who owned the orchards, no one owned this. She kept it. That was enough.

It was not going to be enough tonight.

* * *

The wind arrived an hour after dark, not building but arriving, the way a wave arrives — a single vast shove against the tower that made the glass hum in its frames and set the lamp flame shivering until the governor caught it. Then the rain came sideways, and the sound began: the long roar of it off the sea, punctuated by the deeper percussion of surf hitting the shoal two miles out, a sound like artillery heard through the earth.

Cyrus worked. That was the whole of it, the whole of what she was now: she trimmed the wick when the flame guttered, wiped salt spray from the landward panes, wound the clockwork every four hours, logged the barometer, logged the wind, logged the state of the sea in Tomás's careful shorthand — *swell high, breaking shoal, vis. nil* — and between these tasks she stood at the windows and watched the darkness where the supply ship ought to be.

They'd signaled it at noon from the signal station: *making harbor before the blow*. Quartermaster Brandt aboard, Rolo had reported gleefully, back from his errands among the soldiers with news the way other boys brought home fish. And Brandt meant the pages. The missing sections folded in a soldier's coat, riding toward her town through a rising sea.

She should have wished the ship sunk. She knew that. Marta would have wished it, and Ines would have said nothing and thought it loudly. Somewhere out there in the black was a man who had bought a town's memory by the pound and worn it under his coat like jewelry, and if the sea took him, the sea would be doing justice more efficiently than any hearing Hargrove might arrange.

But Cyrus had kept a light for eleven years, seven of them beside Tomás and four alone, and there was a place in her that did not know how to wish for a wreck. It wasn't mercy exactly. It was older than mercy, simpler, almost mechanical: *there are people on the water, and I have a light, and therefore I light it.* Everything else — the ledgers, the soldiers, the thefts — happened on land, among the living and their paper. Out here there was only the water and the rock beneath it, indifferent to both, and her small stubborn flame standing between them.

So she ran the light, and hated herself a little for hoping, and kept watch.

* * *

At half past eleven the ship showed her lights.

Cyrus saw them first as a smudge, a doubling of the dark — then the green and white of a steamer's running lamps, far out, making for the harbor mouth with the wind dead astern. Even at this distance she could read the trouble in them: the lights were too low, too steady, the ship driving straight in instead of standing off to wait for morning. No master who knew this coast came in on a falling glass at midnight. Whoever was conning her either didn't know the shoal or believed the lighthouse would carry him past it.

And the light would. It always had. That was the covenant of the thing — you kept your end, the flame and the turn and the character of the flash, and any master worth his ticket could lay his course by it and live. Cyrus checked the mechanism with hands that were suddenly not steady. The clockwork turned. The lens revolved. Five flashes, eclipse, five flashes, the rhythm she could recite in her sleep, had recited in her sleep, had once dreamed entire.

The ship grew. Rain snatched at the image of her, gave her back, snatched again. Cyrus pressed her forehead nearly to the cold pane and watched, and did the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: *two miles to the shoal, making maybe nine knots with the wind behind her — twenty minutes, less.* The harbor mouth lay east of the shoal by half a mile. Half a mile, in this sea, with a following wind pushing her faster than judgment.

She thought of the signal station, dark now, its flag halyards surely frozen or fouled. There was no one to warn. There had never been anyone to warn; that was the whole meaning of a lighthouse, that the warning was the light itself, constant, impersonal, sufficient. *Trust me and stand off.*

The ship did not stand off.

At twelve minutes past midnight — she would check the log later and find she had written the time in handwriting unlike her own, jagged, pressed too hard — the steamer's bow lifted on the long swell, hung there absurdly against the sky, and did not come down the same way. From the tower it didn't look like much. A stutter in the lights. A slight change in the angle between green and white. But Cyrus knew what a change of angle meant, and a moment later the sound confirmed it: a grinding, groaning boom that came across two miles of water and up through sixty feet of stone tower and into the soles of her feet, the sound of a ship meeting rock, of iron surrendering its argument with the world.

Then the steam whistle began, and did not stop.

* * *

What happened next, she did afterward in pieces, and each piece felt less like decision than like reflex — the body's knowledge outrunning the mind's objections.

She rang the station bell, six strokes, the wreck signal, and saw lanterns bloom below among the soldiers' billets like startled flowers. She got the Coston flare box open with fingers that tore a fingernail doing it and fired two red flares out over the water, marking the shoal's position, telling any boat that put out from the town *here, here, not here, here*. She wrote the log entry because Tomás had drilled it into her: *the record first, always, even at the end, especially at the end* — *12:12, steamer ashore on the outer shoal, whistle blowing, flares shown* — and then she went down the hundred and eight steps two at a time with a lantern in her hand, out into a wind that hit her like a door thrown open by God.

The beach was chaos of the most organized kind. Hargrove was there — she registered that with some distant surprise, the captain in his greatcoat soaked through in the first minute, shouting orders in a voice the wind kept stealing — and soldiers were dragging the breeches buoy wagon down the sand, and townspeople were coming out of their houses in whatever they slept in, Marta among them with a lantern held high in a fist that did not shake. Ines had her team and the rocket apparatus cart. Rolo appeared at Cyrus's elbow, wild-eyed, hair plastered flat.

"He's out there," the boy shouted. "Brandt. Rolo saw the manifest — he went aboard at the last port, he's—"

"I know."

"Is he—"

"Help with the lines," Cyrus said, and pushed him toward the wagon, and did not let herself think about whether she had just saved the man's life or merely postponed the question of it.

The rocket went up with a sound like tearing cloth and trailed its line out over the shoal, and the wind took it, and it fell short. The second fell into the sea. On the wreck the whistle still screamed, and now, thinner, underneath it, voices — carried off the water in the gaps between gusts, human and small and terrified, the sound that undid everything in a keeper that was merely professional.

Between rockets, Cyrus stood in the lee of the cart and looked at the wreck, and the lightning — the storms here always brought lightning late, grudgingly, after the wind had done its work — lit the scene for a full second, and she saw it all: the steamer broadside-on, wedged on the shoal with her back broken amidships, seas bursting over her in white sheets that must have been sweeping the deck clean of everything loose, everything living. Men clustered at the foremast. A lifeboat davit swung empty. And the light above her, her light, turning and turning, five flashes, eclipse, five flashes, faithfully showing the drowned man exactly where the rock was, forty minutes too late.

"There," someone shouted. "Line's fast! They've got the line!"

A reply had come back along the third rocket's tail — the crew had gotten the whip line aboard, hauled taut, made fast to the mast. The breeches buoy went out into the dark, a small puling shape swallowed instantly by rain, and came back heavy. A man spilled onto the sand, and soldiers caught him, and he was hauled up the beach coughing, alive, and the buoy went out again.

Cyrus hauled. They formed a chain at the inland block — soldiers, townsfolk, herself, hauling hand over hand while the sea fought the line and won small victories, fraying it, weighing it, and the buoy came back and went out and came back. One man. Two. A boy, perhaps sixteen, blue and rigid. Four. Six. Between hauls the lightning showed her the wreck losing pieces of itself, the funnel gone, then the stern section separating with a groan she felt in her teeth.

On the ninth trip the buoy brought back a heavy man in a civilian coat, and when they dragged him up the sand and turned him over in the lantern light, Cyrus looked down into the gray, vomiting, half-drowned face of Quartermaster Brandt.

He was clutching something to his chest with both arms, the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. For one mad instant she thought: *the pages, he's saved the pages,* and felt a hatred so pure and so sudden that she stepped back from it as from a fire. Then the lightning again, and she saw it was only his hat, crushed, worthless, and his hands were empty of everything else, and he was crying, great ugly sobs of a man returned against his will to the world, saying *thank you, thank you, thank God* to no one and everyone.

Ines, hauling beside her, spat into the sand. Marta did not spit. Marta looked at the man for a long, level moment, and then turned and hauled the line, because there were still sailors on the wreck, and that was the whole of Marta Vidal: the account settled later, the work now.

They brought eleven men off. Three did not come. When the last trip of the buoy came back empty and stayed empty, and the foremast went with a crash that even the wind seemed to pause for, Hargrove ordered the apparatus secured, and the beach emptied slowly, the rescued wrapped in blankets and marched up to the barracks, the townsfolk drifting home through the rain to houses that were not legally theirs anymore.

Cyrus stood last on the sand. The storm had not ended — it would run two more days, she knew, the back of it barely begun — but the immediate fury had passed inland, and the sea was working the wreck over with the patient malice of an animal with a carcass. By morning there would be planks on this beach, and barrels, and crates from the hold, and soldiers detailed to salvage, and somewhere among the floating debris perhaps the things Brandt had carried, though she doubted the sea would sort cargo so conveniently.

Her hands were torn. Her voice was gone. Her whole body rang like the tower's glass. And underneath the exhaustion, clear and cold as the water itself, sat the thought she had been refusing all night:

*I saved him.*

She had fired the flares. She had rung the bell. She had hauled the line that brought him out of the sea, hand over hand, her own shoulders burning, and she would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, without hesitating, and she did not know what that said about her — whether it was virtue or mere machinery, whether the light she kept could distinguish between a sailor and a vulture, or whether it burned the same for both because it had no choice, because that was what a light *was*.

Tomás would have known. Or Tomás wouldn't have troubled with the question at all. *You keep the light*, he'd have said. *What the light falls on is not yours to sort.*

But the man was alive. That was the fact the storm had left her. Brandt was alive, and drying out somewhere in the barracks, and tomorrow he would walk out into a town whose names he had sold by the page, and he owed his breathing to her hands, and the missing pages were still — wherever they were — unaccounted for.

She turned for the tower, and the light swept overhead, five flashes, eclipse, faithful as ever, and in its brief red-tinged glow she saw, at the tideline ahead of her, half-buried in the fresh wrack the sea had thrown up, an oilskin packet, swollen with water, rocking gently in the retreating foam.

She stood very still.

Then she knelt, and reached for it, and the sea pulled it a foot further up the sand as if offering — or withholding — something.
