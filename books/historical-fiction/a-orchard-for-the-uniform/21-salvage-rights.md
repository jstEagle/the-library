## 21. Salvage Rights

The storm passed in the night like a debt collector, and by morning it had taken what it came for and left the rest scattered on the shore.

Ines Ferrer found him because no one else would go. The tide had thrown the quartermaster up against the black rocks below the lighthouse path, tangled in a drift of kelp and broken crate-wood from the foundered supply ship, and when the word went round — *the man who bought the pages is alive, he's asking for water* — the town looked at its feet and went back to its work. Salvage crews were already moving along the strand, prying at crates that washed ashore, claiming back by hand what the sea had stolen for others. Nobody wanted the man himself.

Ines went because her husband would have gone. That was all she could say about it, then or ever after, though people asked her for years.

He was smaller than she expected. She had imagined the quartermaster Brandt as something large — the way you imagine a wolf, or a fire, or any force that takes from you — but the man half-buried in kelp was gray-faced and shivering, his uniform coat split at the shoulder seam, salt crusted white in the creases of his neck. His lips were blue. One boot was gone. He had the look of something the ocean had chewed and decided, on reflection, to spit out.

"He's alive," Ines called down to Rolo, who stood at a safe distance with his arms crossed, doing his best imitation of Marta Vidal's disapproval and achieving perhaps half of it. "Barely."

"Let the barely finish," Rolo said.

"Go get Mrs. Reyes," Ines said. "And a blanket. And don't tell anyone else until we've decided what to do with him, because if the town decides now, it'll decide wrong."

Rolo hesitated — she saw the war in him, the boy who polished soldiers' buttons in his head versus whatever he was becoming in the lamplight of the lighthouse these last weeks — and then he ran, which was answer enough.

Ines knelt in the wrack beside the man. The smell of the beach after a storm was one she'd known all her life: weed and brine and dead things, the whole coast exhaling. She put two fingers under Brandt's jaw and felt a pulse fluttering there, quick and thin as a bird in a cupped hand.

"You're the quartermaster," she said. It wasn't a question. Cyrus had described him well enough: the soft hands, the careful coat, the pages folded against his chest like a psalter. "You bought our ledger out from under us, page by page, like a man buying cut flowers."

His eyes opened. They were pale eyes, watery even before the sea got to them, and they focused on her face with the slow effort of a man surfacing from very deep water.

"You're the blacksmith's wife," he rasped.

"Widow." She let the word sit between them. It did its work. She watched it land, watched him understand whose mercy he was lying inside of, and she felt nothing — and that nothing frightened her more than hatred would have. Hatred was warm. Hatred kept a person going. This cold clarity was harder to carry. "My husband forged the hinges on the workshop doors your army nailed shut. He's been dead three years. You never met him, so I'll forgive you for not knowing what you took when you took this town. But I know, and so we're going to have an arrangement, Mr. Brandt."

* * *

They carried him up to the light station because there was nowhere else. The tavern was full of salvage men; the church was full of the sick from the fishing quarter, flooded out of their houses; and no household on the coast road would open its door to a soldier in a ruined uniform. Cyrus made the decision standing in her own doorway with her sleeves rolled and her hair still wet from running down the cliff path, and she made it the way she seemed to make all her decisions lately: quickly, and alone, and with the full knowledge that she would carry the consequences.

"Bring him in," she said. "The lamp room's warm and nobody looks twice at the lighthouse. And Ines—" she caught her arm as they maneuvered the stretcher through the door, "—you don't have to do any of this."

"I know," Ines said.

That was the truth of it, and the weight of it. Nobody had to do any of this. Mercy was not requisitioned. There was no notice posted on the church door ordering them to nurse this man, no penalty for leaving him to the rocks. Every kindness they extended to Brandt would be a free act, chosen, and Ines found she could not stop choosing it, though she examined the choice hourly the way she'd once examined a weld for flaws.

They laid him on the cot in the storeroom beneath the lamp, where Tomás had once slept off night watches, and Ines set to work with the brisk competence her mother had taught her and her husband had admired: stripped the soaked wool off him, wrapped him in blankets, forced weak broth and brandy between his cracked lips, rubbed life back into his hands and feet until they burned pink. Fever came with the evening, as fever does, and through the long hours of it he talked.

That was how Ines learned the shape of him. Not the vulture — the clerk. The small man behind the big theft.

"There were forty-one names on the workshop list," he muttered into the dark, while Ines changed the compress on his brow. "Forty-one. I counted them myself. I counted everything. That's the whole job, counting. Nobody thanks you for counting." His hand clawed at the blanket. "Hargrove doesn't count. Hargrove signs. There's a difference, and the difference is that when it goes wrong, it's always the counter who—"

"Hush," Ines said. "Drink this."

"You're the one whose husband died." His eyes opened, fever-bright, fixed on her with terrible lucidity. "Ferrer. Ironwork. I have his invoice in my files. I approved it. Eleven crowns for gate hinges, paid in full, and I remember thinking the work was underpriced, and I thought it again when I sold the duplicate pages, and I thought — I thought, someone will fix the price eventually, it isn't my department—"

Ines set down the basin with great care, because her hands wanted to throw it.

"Mr. Brandt," she said quietly, "if you say another word about my husband's invoices, I will walk out of this room and let the fever do what the sea didn't."

He hushed. But she noticed — and she hated herself for noticing — that his breathing eased after that, as if confession were the fever's fuel and silence its cure.

* * *

By the third day he could sit up, and by the fourth he could stand at the narrow window of the storeroom and look out at the harbor, where the wreck of the supply ship lay heeled over on the shoals like a beached whale, gulls already working her seams. The town below went about the business of taking itself back piece by piece. Ines watched him watch it.

"You hate us," he said.

"I don't think about you enough to hate you." A lie, and both of them knew it, and neither corrected it.

"The pages." He turned from the window. Up close, in daylight, he looked older than his uniform suggested — mid-forties perhaps, with the softness of a desk man going to fat and the hollows of a man who had not slept since the ship went down. "You want to know where they are. Your friend Reyes hasn't asked me directly, which means she's waiting, which means she's smarter than I am. But you — you're direct. So ask."

"I'm not asking you anything," Ines said. She was folding bandages, because her hands needed work the way other people's minds needed distraction. "You'll tell us when you decide to. Or you won't, and you'll drown a second time, slower, in whatever it is you feel instead of guilt."

"That's cruel."

"That's honest. You've had enough of the other thing. Everyone in your profession has. Papers saying what isn't true, invoices saying what wasn't paid, receipts for goods that never existed. Well." She tied off the bandage roll and set it in the basket. "Here, we deal in what's true. My husband used to say iron doesn't lie. You heat it, it moves; you quench it, it holds. People are the same, only slower."

Brandt sat down slowly on the cot's edge. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its cleverness entirely.

"They're in my coat," he said. "The oilskin packet. Inner pocket. It's hanging by the door — your Mrs. Reyes has probably already found them, knowing her. I checked this morning and they're still there, which means either she hasn't looked, or she's letting me keep them a little longer so I feel safe." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it, like wind through a broken sash. "She'd be right to. As long as I hold those pages, I'm worth something. The moment I give them up, I'm just a quartermaster who lost a supply ship and can't account for certain items. Do you understand what happens to counters like me?"

"No," Ines said. "Tell me."

"Courts-martial," he said. "Or worse — quiet ones. Men above me have been selling this coast out of their own requisition ledgers since the first week. I bought your town's history because it was cheap and portable and I thought — I told myself — it was rescue. Preservation. A man has to tell himself something." His hands were shaking, and he looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "Your husband's invoice. Eleven crowns. I could recite half the ledger from memory by now. Forty-one names. The Vidal orchard claim goes back to 1841. There's a family called Serra with a disputed boundary that was settled by arbitration in 1877 and I have the arbitral note folded inside page thirty—"

"Stop." Ines's voice came out strange to her own ears. "Why do you know all that?"

"Because I read them," he said simply. "Night after night, on the transport, in the requisition office. They were the most interesting papers I've ever held. Everything else I count is money. These were lives." He looked up at her, and there was something naked in his face that she recognized from funerals — the expression of a man who has finally understood the size of a thing too late. "I never meant to become a thief. I meant to become invisible. It's different."

Ines stood very still. She had come here every day prepared to withhold forgiveness, to make withholding itself her work, her discipline, her nightly practice the way other women prayed. And now the man was handing it to her unasked, unwrapped, and she found she still could not give it — not because he hadn't suffered, but because suffering wasn't the payment. The pages were the payment. The truth was the payment. Everything else was weather.

"Mrs. Reyes will come to you soon," she said. "When she comes, tell her what you just told me. All of it. About the officers above you, and the accounts, and the pages in your coat. Because there's a man named Hargrove who's starting to pull threads, and there's a demolition date on the workshop, and somewhere between those two facts, Mr. Brandt, you're going to have to decide what kind of paper you want to be."

"And what kind is that?"

Ines picked up her basket and paused at the door.

"The kind that holds," she said.

* * *

Word traveled, as it always did. By the fifth day the town knew that the quartermaster was convalescing at the light station, and Ines felt the change in the streets the way you feel a change in the wind before you can name it. Old Ferro spat when she passed the forge ruins. The fishwives stopped talking when she came near the market stalls, then resumed louder than before. Marta Vidal said nothing at all, which from Marta was a hurricane.

It was Rolo who confronted her, on the cliff path, with the directness of the young.

"Ines. Why are you nursing him? He's the reason we almost lost everything. If he'd drowned, we wouldn't be sitting here wondering if he'll testify or sell us out. We'd just have the pages."

"We might," Ines agreed. "And we'd have a dead man on our rocks and his blood in our harbor, and everyone in this town would know we let him die, and the ledger would start right there — a new book, first entry, *and we let him die.*" She looked out at the shoals where the ship lay breaking. "Marta says the ledger was made so that taking could never happen again without being written down. Fine. Then everything gets written down, Rolo. Even this. Especially this. The day we nursed the man who robbed us, because he was drowning and we were able. If we leave that out, we're making a forgery too — a prettier one, but a forgery."

Rolo considered this with the serious frown he'd inherited from a father he barely remembered.

"But you don't like him," he said. "I've seen your face when you come down from the light station."

"I don't have to like him," Ines said. "Liking is for festivals. This is work."

* * *

On the seventh day, Ines climbed the lighthouse stairs at dusk and found Cyrus standing at the storeroom door, listening. From within came the scratch of a pencil. Brandt, well enough now to be useful to himself, was writing — lists, dates, figures, the litany of his own profession poured out onto salvaged paper.

"He hasn't stopped since noon," Cyrus murmured. "He says if they're going to hang him, he'd rather be hanged for a complete accounting. Ines — some of these numbers. If they're true, they go up past Hargrove. Past him by a long way."

"Do you believe him?"

Cyrus was quiet a moment. Below them, through the spiral of the stairwell, they could hear the sea working the shoals, patient as a creditor.

"I believe his handwriting," she said at last. "Tomás taught me that much — you can forge words, but the hand betrays itself when it stops thinking. His hand isn't performing. It's terrified." She turned to Ines, and in the lamplight her face was drawn and resolute. "Tomorrow I'm going to offer him a bargain. The pages for his testimony, and testimony against the men above him. But I need to know something first, from you, because you've spent more hours with him than any of us."

"Ask."

"When you sat with him through the fever. When he talked about the forty-one names, and your husband's invoice, and the arbitral note inside page thirty." Cyrus's voice dropped. "Was he sorry? Truly? Or was he only afraid?"

Ines thought of the man's shaking hands, the naked look, the recitation of a town's history learned by heart like a penance he hadn't yet been assigned. She thought of her own cold, chosen mercy, and how it had never once felt like warmth, only like duty — iron heated, moved, quenched, holding.

"He's both," she said. "Sorry and afraid, in equal measure, and I don't think he knows the difference anymore. Maybe none of us do." She handed Cyrus the folded scrap of paper she'd carried up — the notes she'd taken during the fever nights, dates and names and sums, transcribed in her own careful hand. "But fear keeps a man alive long enough to be sorry properly. Start with the fear. The rest may follow."

Cyrus took the paper, and her fingers closed around it like a hand closing around a rope.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we bargain."
