## 22. The Bargain

Quartermaster Elias Brandt woke for the fourth morning in the bed of a woman who would not look at him.

The room above the cooperage was small and smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil and, beneath it all, the sea — that green mineral stink that had followed him out of the water and into his clothes and hair and seemed now to have taken up residence in his lungs. He lay still beneath a quilt mended so many times it had become more patch than cloth, and he listened to the town waking below him: a cart wheel complaining, a dog, the hammer-rhythm of someone salvaging planks from their own drowned harbor.

He had been a quartermaster for nineteen years. He knew what things cost.

This was costing more than he had planned for.

His coat hung on the back of the chair where Ines Ferreira had draped it, wrung out and dried too fast by a stove until the wool had gone stiff and strange-shaped, like something that had died. The pages were still in it. Folded into the inner lining where he had sewn them years ago against inspection, against theft, against every calamity except the one that had actually come for him — cold water and a shoal and the particular mercy of a lighthouse keeper who ran her lamp through hell for strangers.

*Strangers.* He almost laughed. They were not strangers to him. He had read their names a hundred times. He had held them in his hands in a rented room in the port city while a clerk with shaking fingers counted out banknotes, and Brandt had told himself then what quartermasters always tell themselves: *paper is paper, money is money, and the war will swallow everyone equally.*

The names were orchard families. Workshop families. Three generations of small claims written in a clerk's careful hand — the Vidal rows, the Ferreira forge share, a boy named Rolo whose grandfather had planted twelve trees on the north slope. Brandt had never met any of them until this week, and now he slept under their quilt and ate their bread and could not stop seeing the names printed on the inside of his eyelids.

He got up. His ribs ached where the spar had caught him. His hands, when he held them out, were not quite steady, and had not been since the ship went down, and he suspected they would not be steady again for a long time, if ever.

Ines was below. He could hear her at the forge, though she was not shoeing anything — she was just working iron, beating it the way other people beat pillows, and the whole street could probably hear what she was not saying to him.

Brandt dressed. He put on the coat. The pages rode against his heart like a stone sewn there, which was, he supposed, exactly what they were.

* * *

Hargrove came at midmorning, and he came alone, which was worse than coming with soldiers.

Brandt found him standing in the cooperage yard looking up at the gutted hulk of the supply ship on the shoals, visible from shore now that the storm had spent itself, its masts leaning like a drunk against nothing. Gulls had already claimed the rigging.

"Captain." Brandt kept his voice level. Nineteen years of requisition forms gave a man a level voice.

"Quartermaster." Hargrove did not turn. He was a tall man going gray at the temples, with the kind of face that looked tired even when it wasn't, and he had the reputation — rare and dangerous — of being honest. "You should be dead."

"So should most of the cargo."

"Most of the cargo floated." Now Hargrove turned, and his eyes were the color of the water over the shoals, gray-green and unreadable. "Interesting, that. Crates of sealed army stores went down and stayed down. Crates marked as personal effects washed ashore intact. And one man swam free of a foundering vessel carrying nothing at all — no kit, no papers, no pack." He paused. "Except you weren't carrying nothing, were you. You were carrying nothing *visible*."

Brandt felt the cold of the sea again, climbing his spine. "I don't know what you're implying, Captain."

"No," Hargrove agreed mildly. "You never do. That's been the trouble with your accounts from the beginning." He took a folded paper from inside his own coat — not the army coat today, something plainer, civilian, as if he had wanted to have this conversation out of uniform — and opened it without reading it, because a man does not need to read numbers he has memorized. "Three shipments shorted against manifest. Two lots of requisitioned goods sold before they reached the depot. Payments routed through a merchant house in the port city that I have reason to believe is owned by your brother-in-law." He folded the paper away again. "I've been pulling that thread for six weeks, Quartermaster. It unravels upward. Do you understand me? It doesn't unravel to you and stop. It goes up past me, past my superiors, into rooms I'll never be invited into. Which means when it breaks, it won't break on you alone."

"The sea broke plenty already," Brandt said.

"Yes." For a moment something moved behind Hargrove's exhaustion — something that might have been pity. "Eleven men. I signed the sailing order. I've written eleven letters this week, and I can tell you the ink weighs more each time." He looked back at the wreck. "I didn't come to arrest you. I came to tell you that the thread is nearly pulled through, and when it snaps taut, everything on it shakes loose. If there is anything you're holding — anything at all — now is the hour to decide what kind of man you'd rather be when it happens."

"And if I'm holding nothing?"

Hargrove looked at him for a long moment. "Then you're the only clean man in this requisition, Quartermaster, and I envy you." He put on his cap. "The light keeper tells me you're well enough to travel by week's end. I'd think about what you want to be traveling *with*."

He walked out through the yard gate and up the street toward the town hall, and Brandt stood in the cold sun watching him go, and understood with perfect clarity that he had been given a gift wrapped in a threat, and that both of them knew it, and that only one of them was going to sleep tonight.

* * *

Cyrus Reyes came at dusk.

He heard her boots on the cooperage stairs — a deliberate tread, no attempt at softness; she was a woman who had decided long ago that sneaking was for people with something to hide, and whatever else she was hiding, she would not hide her arrival. She filled the doorway with the last light behind her, a broad-shouldered woman with wind-burned cheeks and eyes the color of slate, and she looked at him the way one looks at weather that has already done its damage and is deciding whether to do more.

"You're eating our bread," she said.

"I'm paying for it."

"With what? Your life?" She came in without invitation, which was her right here, apparently, and stood over him where he sat by the stove. Up close she smelled of lamp oil and salt and, faintly, of blossoms — pressed, dried, old blossoms, a smell that had no business being on a living woman in winter. "Marta Vidal says I should let them take you to the mainland and let the army sort you. Ines says I should have let the sea finish. Rolo wants to know if you really bought pages with dead families' names on them." She crouched, forearms on knees, bringing her face level with his. "So before I decide which of them is right, you're going to tell me why a man risks drowning to keep a stolen ledger against his skin."

"It wasn't sentiment," Brandt said.

"No?"

"No." And then, because the lie stuck in his throat like a fishbone, he heard himself say: "It started as leverage. Pages out of a ledger nobody was supposed to know existed. Insurance. If anyone ever asked questions about the accounts, I had something worth trading." He rubbed his unsteady hands together. "That's what they were. Leverage. For three years."

"And now?"

"Now they're the only proof those people exist," Brandt said, "and I've been wearing them like a medal." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what the worst of it is, Keeper? I've read them so many times I know them by heart. The Vidal rows. Twelve trees, north slope, planted 1861. The Ferreira forge share, one-eighth, renewed by mark because old Ferreira couldn't write. There's a page of births and deaths in the back margins — the clerk recorded them even though nobody asked him to, babies and burials, forty years of them, in pencil, like he couldn't help himself." He looked at the fire. "I fell asleep on that page once, on a transport, and woke up with the margin lines printed backward on my cheek. I told myself it was an accident."

Cyrus said nothing for a long moment. The stove ticked. Below them, Ines had stopped hammering, which meant she was listening, which meant she had been listening the whole time.

"What do you want for them?" Cyrus asked at last. No heat in it. Just the question, laid flat on the table between them like a knife someone has decided not to pick up yet.

"There it is," Brandt said softly. "Yes. All right. Here is what I want, Keeper, and I'll say it once, plainly, because I'm tired and half-drowned and I haven't got the stomach for clever anymore." He straightened, ribs protesting. "Hargrove has my accounts. Not all of them, not proof enough yet, but enough that within a month — two, if I'm lucky — the thread comes tight. When it does, officers above him will burn me to save themselves, and they will do it loudly, publicly, and they will use whatever scandal is nearest to hand. A drowned supply ship. A missing ledger. A lighthouse keeper running contraband documents out of her lamp room." He saw her flinch, very slightly, and hated himself for cataloguing it. "Oh yes. I know about your lamp room. I sold the original pages, madam — I know what was in them, and I know what's missing, and I know a transcription needs all of it to stand. You need these pages more than you need my ruin."

"Go on."

"My price is silence." He held her gaze. "Not freedom. Not protection. Silence. When Hargrove asks you whether the quartermaster recovered from the wreck carried anything, you found nothing. When the inquiry comes — and it will come — you never saw papers. I return the pages, complete, tonight, and in exchange the town forgets I ever held them. Let the army hang me for the money if it must. But not for the ledger. Not for *this*." His voice did something humiliating at the end of it, and he stopped talking and stared into the stove until it passed.

Cyrus studied him the way she might study a chart of a hostile coast. "And if I refuse? If I walk out of here and go straight to Hargrove and tell him everything?"

"Then you lose the workshop sections forever, because the moment you leave, I burn them." He said it evenly, and was appalled to discover he meant it — not because he wanted to burn them, but because a cornered man's threats are the truest things about him. Then he made himself soften it, made himself say the rest, the part underneath: "And I lose the only chance I'll ever get to put one thing right. So we'd both be fools, Keeper. I've been the fool alone for three years. I'd rather share it."

Outside, the gulls were settling on the wreck for the night, and the tide was turning, and somewhere up the hill the great lamp was being trimmed and lit by a woman who had run it through the worst night of the coast's memory to pull him off the water. He thought about that. He thought about it more than was comfortable.

"Why did you run the light?" he asked suddenly. "For us. Eleven men dead, and you ran the light all night anyway. You owed us nothing."

Cyrus rose. For a moment she looked older than her years and younger than her grief, both at once.

"Because the lamp doesn't ask who's in the water," she said. "It burns, or it doesn't. Everything else is weather." She turned for the stairs, then stopped. "Bring the pages to the lighthouse after full dark. Come alone. Come through the orchard gate, not the road — the sergeant walks the road at nine. And Brandt—" she glanced back, and the lamplight from the stairwell cut her face in half, lit and dark, "—if there are pages missing when I count them, the bargain dies and Marta gets her wish. Understood?"

"Understood."

She went down the stairs. He heard her speak briefly to Ines below — low words he could not catch, though he caught the tone, which was iron agreeing to bend exactly one degree — and then the street door, and the wind, and quiet.

* * *

Full dark came down like a lid.

Brandt waited until the church bell struck nine and a while longer, until the patrol boots had scuffed past on the road below and faded toward the harbor. Then he took the coat from the chair, and his fingers shook — from the cold, he told himself, from the ribs, from any cause except the true one — as he picked the stitching out of the lining with a borrowed sail needle. The oilskin packets came free one by one, heavier than they had any right to be, three years of a dead town's identity folded against a thief's heartbeat.

At the door, Ines stood in the hall with a lantern, and she did not move aside.

"I know what you are," she said. Her voice was low and even, forge-quiet, which was worse than shouting. "My husband worked that forge twenty-two years. One-eighth share, renewed by mark, because his father couldn't write and neither could he. I've seen the page you're carrying. I've seen my own name sitting in some officer's strongbox like a coin."

"Ines—"

"Don't." The word dropped like a hammer. "Give them to her. Give them back. And then get on whatever boat takes you off this coast, and pray you never wash up on it again." She lifted the lantern higher, lighting his way down the steps, her face set in something that was not mercy and refused to pretend otherwise. "But the bread was ours and you ate it, and the quilt was my mother's and you slept warm under it. So I'll say this much, quartermaster, and it's all you'll get: the lamp ran for you. Whatever you do with what's left of your life, remember that somebody burned light for you when you'd earned darkness."

He went down the steps past her, into the cold.

The orchard gate was where she'd said, sagging on a broken hinge, the trees beyond it black skeletons with a few stubborn fruit still hanging, frosted and forgotten. He walked the rows in the dark, and somewhere among them — he could not have said which row, and would never be sure — he understood that he had made his choice, and that it was not courage. Courage would have been confessing to Hargrove six weeks ago, burning his career down in daylight. This was smaller than courage. This was a coward choosing, at last, the safer crime: giving back what was never his instead of keeping it, buying silence instead of truth, saving himself by a margin of one decent act.

It was still, he told himself, walking, an act. Let the accountants of heaven decide how to book it.

The lighthouse rose against the stars, white stone gone silver, and at its top the lamp was already burning — that great slow eye turning, sweeping the black water, sweeping the wreck on the shoals, sweeping the orchard, passing over him and returning and passing over again like a sentence being repeated until it is believed.

The door at the tower's base opened before he knocked. Cyrus stood there with a storm lantern in one hand and, behind her, at the foot of the spiral stair, a table cleared and waiting, and on the table a counting frame, and a candle stub, and Marta Vidal's folded arms.

"You brought her," Brandt said.

"Marta counts faster than I do." Cyrus stepped aside. "Come in, Quartermaster. Let's see what you've brought home."

He climbed the stairs into the smell of oil and hot brass and old paper, and he laid the oilskin packets on the table one by one — seven of them, then eight, then the thin ninth from his breast pocket that he had saved for last because it held the birth-and-death margins in the clerk's pencil hand — and Marta Vidal untied the first packet with fingers that trembled and did not care who saw them tremble, and unfolded forty years of her town onto a lighthouse table.

She read the first line aloud. Then she stopped reading aloud, because her voice would not carry it.

Brandt stood back by the wall, empty-handed for the first time in three years, lighter and colder than he had ever been, and watched them count.

He was halfway through watching when boots sounded on the rocks outside — several pairs, moving fast, and a voice he knew calling up the dark toward the tower door:

"Keeper Reyes! Open the door — a telegram's come for Captain Hargrove, and it's bad news for everyone."
