## 23. Pages Restored

Rolo felt the pages before he saw them.

Ines carried them up the lighthouse stair in a fish crate lined with sacking, and even through the wood and the oilcloth he could smell the sea on them — that deep, mineral rot of things the water had held and given back grudgingly, kelp and salt and something older underneath, like the inside of an old boat. He was fourteen and he had hauled crates of herring since he was nine; his hands knew cargo the way other boys' hands knew their mothers' faces. But this crate made his arms careful in a way no fish ever had. He climbed the ninety-seven steps behind Marta Vidal and did not once let the wood knock against the stone wall, though by the fortieth step his shoulders burned with the effort of gentleness.

Cyrus Reyes met them at the top, in the room below the lamp where the storm logs lived in their long oak drawers. She had cleared the big table — Rolo noticed she'd done it before they arrived, because she always knew what a thing needed before it needed it — and she had laid out white linen, the good kind, the kind you buried people in. She smoothed it flat with both hands as Ines set down the crate, and for a moment nobody moved at all, and Rolo could hear the lamp turning overhead, that slow mechanical breathing the whole town had learned to sleep beneath.

"Well," said Marta. "Open it."

Ines looked at Cyrus. Cyrus nodded once, the way she did everything, like a woman signing her own name.

The oilcloth came away in folds stiff as bark. Beneath it the pages lay stacked in their original order — Brandt's order, though no one said his name — and Rolo leaned over Ines's shoulder to look, and then wished he hadn't, because looking felt like something you should be permitted first. The paper was warped into shallow waves, buckled along one edge where the seawater had gotten in past the oilskin, and the ink had run in places into gray ghosts of themselves, letters bleeding outward until words became weather. But most of it held. Most of it was still itself: columns of names in the clerk's small upright hand, orchard rows numbered, workshop shares recorded in fractions, dates going back further than anyone in the tavern could remember.

"Vidal," Marta read aloud, and her voice cracked on her own name like ice on a puddle. She put her palm flat over the page, not touching the ink, hovering above it the way you hover a hand over a sleeping child to feel whether the breath is still coming. "There we are. There we are, old girl."

* * *

They worked all afternoon, and Rolo learned that restoring pages was mostly waiting.

Cyrus would not let them flatten the paper by force. Each leaf went between sheets of clean linen under weights scavenged from the lamp room — brass fittings, the glass paperweight Tomás Reyes had kept on his desk, two smooth stones from the beach that Ines chose herself and scrubbed raw. Between the pressings there was nothing to do but read aloud, softly, testing which passages the water had spared and which it had taken.

"The Ferreira claim," Ines read, running her finger below a line where the ink had gone soft. "*Orchard rows eleven through nineteen, eastern slope, with rights to the spring.* That's legible. That one's whole."

"Good." Cyrus marked it in her own notebook with a tick. "Next."

"*Shareholding, cooperage workshop —* here it goes." Ines frowned. The page was one of the bad ones, its lower third washed to fog. "*...one-eighth part to Joaquim...* something. *...and his heirs...*" Her finger stopped. The rest was a tide pool of gray.

"Joaquim Pereira," Marta said immediately, from across the table, not looking up from the page she was holding to the window light. "Who else? There was only one Joaquim who ever put money into barrels. His grandson runs mussels now up the coast and thinks himself too fine for us."

"You're certain?"

"Girl, I sat behind the clerk's shoulder while he wrote half of these entries. My eyes were better than my knees even then." Marta set the page down. "But certain isn't enough, is it. Not for a register. Not if Hargrove is going to hold it against his own lists."

"No," Cyrus agreed quietly. "It has to come off the paper or it doesn't count."

Everyone looked at the fogged passage. Everyone looked, after a moment, at Rolo.

He hadn't expected it. He had spent the winter being useful in the small ways boys are useful — carrying, fetching, standing watch on the gallery when soldiers walked the harbor road, memorizing the rotation of the patrol because his young eyes caught the lantern rhythm faster than anyone's. He had copied pages too, nights in this very room, his hand cramped around a pen nib, learning Tomás Reyes's shorthand symbol by symbol the way you learn a dead language from a dead teacher. But copying was obedience. This was different. This was them looking at him the way you look at a tool you've finally decided to trust with your own fingers.

"I can try," he said, and hated how thin it came out, so he said it again, firmer. "I can try."

"Bring the lamp closer," Cyrus said.

* * *

The trick, Rolo discovered, was not to look at the words.

Looking at the words gave you the fog — the soft gray bloom where the ink had surrendered, a stain shaped vaguely like writing, mocking you with the idea of meaning. If he stared at the ruined line directly, his eye slid off it, the way a nail slides off wet wood. But Tomás's shorthand lessons had taught him something without either of them ever saying it aloud: that handwriting had a skeleton. Every clerk developed habits. The clerk who had written the ledger pressed harder on his downstrokes than his upstrokes; he crossed his t's with a single impatient slash; he made his capital J with a flourish that hooked back under itself like a fishing barb. Water could wash away the ink. It couldn't wash away the grooves the pen nib had cut into the paper, because pressure wasn't ink. Pressure was memory, pressed into fiber.

Rolo tilted the page toward the lamp. Held it almost flat, so the light raked across it sideways instead of falling straight on. And there — faint as breath on cold glass — were the shadows of the grooves. Little valleys in the paper where a dead man's colleague had leaned hard on his downstrokes forty years ago.

"It's like reading braille with your eyes," he said, forgetting to be shy.

"What did I tell you," Marta murmured to Ines, with grim satisfaction. "The boy sees."

He worked. The lamp turned overhead; the afternoon died against the windows; nobody spoke except to bring him things he hadn't asked for — a cushion for his chair, bread and salt fish he ate without noticing, a second lamp when the first guttered. He read the ghost-grooves letter by letter, whispering as he went, building each word twice, once from shadow and once from sense, checking them against each other the way you check a knot by pulling both ends. *One-eighth part to Joaquim Pereira and his heirs, being the cooperage share purchased from the estate of—* and there the groove failed entirely, the paper worn through, and Rolo sat back with his eyes stinging.

"Estate of," he reported. "Then nothing. The hole eats the rest of the line."

"Read me the next line down," Cyrus said. "And the one after."

He read them. She took her notebook and wrote rapidly, and he watched her do the thing he was beginning to understand she did best, which was to think sideways — taking the surviving fragments from the lines above and below and squeezing the missing middle between them like a man fitting a plank into a gap by measuring both walls.

"Pereira bought his share in the same season the Ferreiras bought rows eleven through nineteen," she said. "Same hand, same ink batch, same year. And the entry above ends with a purchase price, and the entry below begins with witnesses. So somewhere in the hole there's a price, a date, and likely two witness names." She looked up. "Do any of the elders remember the Pereira purchase?"

"My father spoke of it," Marta said slowly. "Autumn purchase. Barrels had to be ready for the pressing, so it would have been late summer, before the harvest." She closed her eyes, and Rolo watched an old woman reach sixty years down into herself and come back up holding something. "Two witnesses. It would have been the two men who witnessed everything in those years, because they could write. Silas Renwick. And—" her eyes opened, sharp with triumph— "Tomás Reyes."

The room went quiet. Cyrus's pen had stopped moving.

"He witnessed ours too," Marta went on, gentler now. "Every page of our section. I used to tease him about it — that he signed more names in that book than he ever planted trees. He said someone had to keep the town honest, and it might as well be the man who could see it from the water." She looked at Cyrus with something naked in her old face. "You didn't know that?"

"No," Cyrus said. "No, I didn't know that." She looked down at the notebook, and Rolo saw her thumb press flat against the page the way Marta's palm had hovered over the ledger, wanting contact with something that was already gone. Then she blinked twice and became practical again, and Rolo understood that this was also a skill — putting the grief in a drawer so the work could breathe. "Late summer. A price. We'll cross-check against the requisition inventory lists — Hargrove's men itemized everything, including the cooperage ledgers they shipped out. If the price appears in both places, that's corroboration."

"That'll hold?" Rolo asked. "For the register?"

"It held for empires," Marta said dryly. "Two sources agreeing is how the whole world pretends to know anything."

* * *

They broke at dusk for the lighting, because the light did not wait for grief or ledgers. Rolo went up with Cyrus as he always did now — keeper's boy was not yet his title but the duties had quietly become his, the way a stray dog becomes yours by continuing to arrive. They trimmed the wick together, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and stood a moment in the gallery while the beam swung out over a darkening sea.

From up here the requisitioned town looked like a model of itself. Soldiers' cookfires in the square. The workshop's silhouette against the last gray light, still whole, still doomed — twenty-two days now, Cyrus had counted, or maybe it was twenty-one, the number shrank every morning whether anyone slept or not. Somewhere out in that dark was the supply road over which a fat quartermaster had carried stolen pages folded against his chest like a saint's bones, and somewhere beyond that was the war the whole coast pretended not to hear, a low continuous rumor under the wind.

"Do you think he knows what he carried?" Rolo asked. "Brandt. Do you think he even read them?"

Cyrus considered it honestly, which was the thing about her that had finally made Rolo stop being afraid of her sometime that winter. Most adults answered questions like that with the answer they wanted you to have.

"I think he counted them," she said at last. "I think he counted them the way you'd count coins. Which is worse, somehow." She checked the lens rotation with her fingertips, feeling the mechanism more than watching it. "Your hands are steady tonight?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Because there are four more fogged passages, and Marta's memory won't last forever, and neither will my patience with your modesty." But she smiled when she said it, small and sideways, and went down the stairs ahead of him, and Rolo stayed one extra minute in the cold just to feel what it was like to be someone whose steadiness mattered.

* * *

The second passage nearly beat him.

It was a boundary description — the worst kind, all compass bearings and landmarks named after trees that had been cut down before Rolo was born — and the water had taken it diagonally, so that each line lost its beginning and its end and only the middles survived. He fought it for two hours, tilting the page at angles, warming the paper gently near the lamp to see if heat raised the ghost-grooves any darker (it did, a little), filling his notebook with guesses and striking them through until it looked like a field of graves. Ines brought him tea and told him to stop before he ruined his eyes. Marta told him to keep going. Cyrus said nothing, which he had learned was her highest form of confidence.

What broke it open was not cleverness. It was walking.

Near midnight his head swam and Cyrus ordered him outside onto the gallery for ten minutes of cold air, no argument. He stood leaning on the rail with the wind pushing his hair back, staring down at the black shapes of the orchards below the cliff, and his eye followed the rows — and stopped. Because the rows didn't follow the slope the way you'd plant them if you were lazy. They curved. All of them curved, gently, in the same direction, following some older line that had nothing to do with drainage or sunlight.

He ran back down the stairs two at a time.

"The bearing in the fourth line," he said, breathless, jabbing the fogged page. "It says northwest by north, and everyone reads it as wrong because no boundary in this town runs that way. But look at the orchards from the gallery — the old rows bend. They follow the spring channel. The boundary doesn't go northwest across the slope, it follows the curve of the water underground. The clerk wrote it straight because paper is straight. The land never agreed."

Silence. Then Marta laughed — a short, startled bark, like a bird flushed from cover. "The Spring Line," she said. "That's what the old ones called it. The Spring Line dispute. My grandmother fought about it with a Corvo for twenty years and neither of them could say why the maps argued, only that they did." She gripped the table edge. "Boy. You've just settled a feud older than the army."

"We can't settle it," Cyrus said, but her eyes were bright and her pen was already moving. "We can only record what the land shows and what the paper says and let them agree with each other. Which is more than anyone's done since the book was written." She looked at Rolo across the lamplight, and whatever passed over her face, she let him see all of it. "Where did you learn to think like that?"

"Nowhere," he said, and then, because lying to this room had become impossible: "Here. Watching you."

Marta snorted. Ines hid her smile in her sewing. Cyrus shook her head once, but she wrote down his bearing correction exactly as he'd given it.

By three in the morning they had recovered four of the seven damaged passages entire, two more in part, and lost only one beyond prayer — a marginal note, probably, the ghost-grooves too faint even for Rolo's raked light, the paper itself gone soft as skin. Cyrus wrapped the restored leaves in fresh linen with the tenderness of a nurse, and Rolo watched her hands and thought about how many kinds of keeping there were: keeping a light, keeping a ledger, keeping watch, keeping faith. He was fourteen years old and until this winter he had thought history was a subject at school, a list of distant kings. Now he knew it was a smell — salt and old ink and lamp oil — and it lived in a lighthouse, and tonight he had put his hands inside it.

They were gathering coats, blowing out lamps, when boots sounded on the stair.

Not the patrol rhythm — Rolo knew the patrol rhythm the way he knew his own pulse now. These steps were slower, heavier, unarmored, climbing with the particular reluctance of a man who has been dreading the climb. Cyrus opened the door before the knock came, because of course she did.

Captain Ellery Hargrove filled the doorway, hat in his hands, sea spray silvering his shoulders. Behind him the night was getting ready to do something — Rolo could feel it in his teeth, a pressure like the one before the winter gales, the barometer falling somewhere far out over the water.

"Mrs. Reyes," Hargrove said. His voice was level, courteous, exhausted, exactly as always. "Forgive the hour." His eyes went past her — to the linen-wrapped bundle on the table, to the ink-stained notebook lying open beside it, to Rolo, frozen halfway into his coat with a pen still tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil.

"I'm afraid I've received a report," the captain said, "about lights burning in this station well past regulation hours. And I find myself obliged to ask what, precisely, you are restoring."

Nobody breathed. Outside, far off over the black water, thunder cleared its throat — the first of the great storms, arriving right on schedule, as if it had been summoned by the question.
