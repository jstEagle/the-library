## 24. The Workshop Still Stands

Marta Vidal had not walked the length of Cooper's Row since the soldiers came, and she found that her feet remembered it anyway — every cobble loose in its bed, every doorway's shadow falling where it had always fallen. The row smelled of salt and cold iron and, beneath it, faintly, of apples gone over in the grass of the abandoned orchard plots. Two weeks. That was what remained. Fourteen mornings like this one, grey and windless, the gulls arguing over something dead on the tideline.

She stopped at the corner by the old cooperage and let herself look at the workshop properly for the first time since the requisition.

It stood. That was the whole miracle of it, and she had to keep telling herself so or she would weep in the street where Sergeant Dray could see her from his post at the barricade. The workshop still stood: long and low, built of the same grey granite as the harbor wall, its slate roof patched a dozen times across three generations, its windows fogged with the breath of sixty years of presses and lathes and boiling glue. The army had strung wire across its doors and stenciled a number on the lintel — *INVENTORY HOLD 12* — as though the building were a crate of boots. But inside, Marta knew, behind that wire and that number, waited everything they needed: the press old Amador Vidal had bought secondhand from a newspaper man in the city, the brass seals with the workshop's mark cut into them, the stock of heavy rag paper, the ink stones, the drying racks. Everything that made a piece of paper into a truth.

Two weeks until the demolition crews came with their charges and their chalk marks, and the press would be scrap, and the seals would be melted down for their brass, and there would be nothing left in the world capable of saying *this record is true* except the mouths of four frightened people.

"You're staring," said Ines at her elbow, arriving out of the fog with a market basket on her arm and her blacksmith's widow's shawl pulled up over her hair. "Old women who stare get asked questions."

"I'm an old woman carrying fish," Marta said. "Nobody asks old women carrying fish anything." She had, in fact, two mackerel wrapped in cabbage leaves, bought that morning for exactly this purpose, so that any soldier who stopped her would smell herring and not suspicion. "Is he at the barricade?"

"Dray? Aye. And the young one with the chapped hands. They've taken to searching baskets on Tuesdays."

"Then it's fortunate I'm only walking past with my supper." Marta tucked the basket closer and resumed walking, slow, deliberate, the walk of a woman whose knees hurt and whose business was nobody's. Ines fell in beside her. They passed the barricade at the head of the row, where the sergeant looked up from his stove, nodded at Marta — he had learned her face, they all had, the fierce old woman who was too old and too respected to be worth troubling — and went back to warming his hands.

Only when they had turned down the lane toward the harbor did either of them breathe fully again.

"The boy counted the guard changes," Ines said quietly. "Rolo. There are four men on the workshop rotation during the day and two at night, and the night pair shelters in the cooperage doorway from midnight because the wind comes off the water straight into their teeth. From midnight to the relief at four, nobody looks at that door but the gulls."

"And the door itself?"

"Padlocked, but the hasp is held by screws. Rolo says he's watched the soldiers open it a hundred times and never once seen them check whether the screws turned. They use the key and think the lock is the whole of the world." Ines allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Blacksmith's daughter-in-law, me. Screws are my native language."

Marta said nothing for a dozen steps. She was looking at the workshop's roofline against the white sky, at the chimney that had smoked when her father smoked his pipe in the loft, at the window through which she had first seen the sea at six years old and understood, in some wordless child's way, that this place was hers and she was its. The ledger had been born in that building. Her grandfather had helped make it so — not the writing of it, but the meaning of it, the iron will underneath the ink. *An orchard for the uniform.* The soldiers of the old war had come through stripping the trees, and the town had answered not with muskets but with paper: every claim written, witnessed, sealed. Paper had beaten soldiers once already in this family's history.

It would have to do so once more.

"There's a thing we must settle tonight," she said at last, "and I want you to hear it from me before Cyrus dresses it up gentle. The copying is done. Every page, every name, Rolo's fair hand and Ines's steady one and the keeper's own — done, God rest Tomás Reyes for starting it. But a copied ledger is only paper. Anyone can copy anything. What makes ours more than a forgery is the seal and the press — the workshop's authentication, struck while the workshop still lives. Without that, Hargrove can burn our pages in his stove and call them kindling."

Ines glanced sideways. "You mean we go in."

"I mean we go in *broad daylight*," Marta said, "with the guards' help, if we can manage it, and I'll tell you why. Night work smells of crime. Lanterns moving in a sealed building — that's how you get shot, or worse, how you get caught and the papers seized and everything Tomás died hiding lost in an evidence sack. But daylight — daylight is dull. Daylight is a woman crating inventory under orders. Nobody shoots a woman crating inventory. They carry boxes for her."

"They'd have to believe you had orders."

"I'll show them orders." Marta patted the basket, above the fish. Beneath the cabbage leaves, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a single sheet: an inventory requisition in a clerk's hand, salvaged months ago from the town hall's litter, blank in all the places that mattered. Rolo had spent three evenings learning to imitate the clerk's cramped capitals. It was forgery, of course. It was also, Marta thought, the most honest document she had ever carried, because everything it claimed was true: the workshop's contents must be removed before demolition; the removal must be recorded and sealed; the record must be preserved. The army had simply not yet gotten around to writing down its own intentions. She was doing their clerical work for them. She was, she reflected, practically a patriot.

"Cyrus won't like it," Ines said.

"Cyrus doesn't have to like it. Cyrus has to keep the lamp lit, which keeps her chained to the tower and gives us both our alibi and our excuse. If anything goes wrong in that workshop, it happens to Marta Vidal alone, an old woman meddling with crates, and the keeper of the light knew nothing of it." Marta heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she had used at forty funerals and two wars' worth of bad news. "I'm eighty-one years old, Ines Almeida. They cannot hang me twice, and the first time would take so long my grandchildren's grandchildren would be old."

They came out onto the quay. The tide was halfway down, baring the green-slicked stones, and beyond the moles the requisition barges rode at anchor like patient animals. Marta looked at them without hatred. Hatred was a fire that burned the woodpile too fast; she had learned to bank hers down to coals and cook with it daily instead.

* * *

That evening the circle met in the lamp room, because the lamp room was the one room in the parish no soldier entered uninvited. The light station was army property now on paper, but the paper had never been signed properly — Hargrove's own oversight, or perhaps his mercy — and Sergeant Dray's men treated the spiral stair with the superstitious dread sailors feel for high places. So they climbed: Cyrus last, carrying the oilskin packets wrapped in her storm apron; then Ines; then Rolo, fourteen and all elbows, his eyes shining with the terrible joy of a boy who has finally been trusted with something heavier than firewood; and Marta herself, slower than the others, counting the steps because counting kept the fear at heel.

The lamp was not yet lit — half an hour to dusk — and in the grey glass light the great lens stood around them like a cathedral of glass and brass, throwing slow rainbow ghosts across the whitewash. Cyrus spread the completed transcription on the chart table and weighted its corners with lamp keys.

"It's finished," she said. No ceremony. Marta noticed that about the keeper: she saved her ceremonies for private moments, and conducted her public life like someone defusing a mine. "All four hundred and twelve names, all the boundary descriptions, all the shares and signatures. Water-stained where Brandt's coat failed him, but legible. Rolo recovered the Ferreira passage entirely."

"The hardest ink," Rolo said, trying not to sound proud and failing completely. "The clerk wrote that page with a broken nib. You can tell because the downstrokes—"

"Yes, love, you've told us," Ines said fondly. "Tell it again at the hearing."

"If there is a hearing," Cyrus said, "it will be because these pages convince him. And they won't convince him unless—" she touched the last page, the empty margin where the seal should strike "—unless this is real. Not copied. Not drawn. Struck from the actual seal, on the actual press, with the workshop's own ink, while the workshop still exists to vouch for it. After demolition day there is no authentication left in the world. Before it, there is a window."

"How wide a window?" Marta asked, though she knew.

"Six days of guard rotations," Rolo said immediately. "Maybe five. After that they start prepping the charges and the watch doubles."

Cyrus looked at Marta across the table. The keeper's face in the grey light was younger than her years and older than her grief, and Marta thought, not for the first time, that Tomás Reyes had chosen better than he ever knew, and been taken before he could tell her so.

"Marta has a plan," Cyrus said. "I've argued with it. I lost."

"You didn't lose," Marta said. "You conceded correctly, which is different and harder." She laid the forged requisition on the table between the weights. "Daylight. Tomorrow morning. I walk up to the barricade with this and a list of crates, and I inform Sergeant Dray that I've been engaged by Captain Hargrove's own quartermaster section to prepare Inventory Hold 12 for removal. Old women are invisible, girls. Invisible is armor. But invisible women pushing a locked door at midnight become visible very fast, whereas invisible women standing in plain sight holding army paperwork become furniture."

"And the printing?" Ines asked. "The press takes hours. The seals need heat for the wax impressions on the certification leaves. You can't do all that in one visit under a guard's eye."

"No," Marta agreed. "Not in one visit. In several. Crating takes days — everyone knows crating takes days. Each visit, I print a little. A stack of certification leaves here, a run of the seal impression there. Rolo ferries the finished leaves out in the bottom of his toolbox, under the spanners, because no sergeant alive searches a boy's spanners. By the time the crews arrive to demolish the building, every leaf will be out of it and the press will have printed nothing but the army's own crate labels." She smiled, and felt the coals of the old banked fire warm her chest. "We will rob them with their own forms, children. Their paperwork will carry our truth out of the fire. Let them burn the walls. We were never keeping the walls."

Silence, except the wind worrying the lantern glass overhead. Then Rolo, very quietly: "My father worked that press."

"Your father set type on that press the day the new ledger was begun," Marta said. "I held the lamp for him. He'd have liked this plan. He always said the best way to hide from soldiers was to be useful to them."

Cyrus began rolling the transcription closed with careful hands. "One condition," she said. "If Dray doubts the order — if he sends a runner to verify it — you drop the basket and walk away. Do you hear me, Marta? Walk away. We find another way."

"We won't find another way," Marta said. "There isn't one. But yes, keeper. I hear you." She did not say — because there was no need, and because saying it aloud would frighten the boy — that she had already decided what she would do if the runner came back with Hargrove's puzzled frown. An eighty-one-year-old woman caught with a forged order would spend her last years in a cell and the ledger would wait for braver weather. An eighty-one-year-old woman who tore the order up and ate the accusation whole, claiming senility, claiming spite, claiming anything at all — well. Cells had held Vidals before. The name always came out again, like seed after frost.

* * *

Morning came up milky, with a swell running from the southeast that promised weather within the week. Marta dressed in her good black — the dress of authority and mourning, indistinguishable at a distance — pinned her shawl with the silver brooch her mother had worn to the founding of the cooperative, and took up the basket with the fish (fresh ones today; the ruse required eating well) and the folded requisition on top.

Cooper's Row was quiet. The barricade stove smoked. Sergeant Dray stood beside it stamping his feet, and beside him the young one with the chapped hands, and beyond them both, fifty yards down the row, the padlocked door of Inventory Hold 12, its hasp screws waiting in the granite like patient punctuation.

"Sergeant." Marta did not stop walking until she was well inside speaking distance, because hesitation was for guilty people. "You'll want to read this before your tea goes cold."

Dray took the paper. His eyes moved along it — Marta watched him read, and read carefully, because he was not a stupid man, only a tired one — and she saw the exact moment the military mind did what military minds do best: surrendered to paperwork. An order was an order. It was not his to understand, merely to facilitate.

"Quartermaster section," he said. "Removal prep." He frowned. "Nobody told me."

"Nobody tells sergeants anything, Sergeant, or so my late husband always complained." Marta produced, from thirty years of managing a town, a smile of perfect bland sympathy. "I expect the captain's office assumed the quartermaster had told you, and the quartermaster assumed the captain had. That's how the army works, isn't it? Like the church — everything important happens in triplicate and nobody speaks to anyone."

The young soldier laughed before he could stop himself, and Dray shot him a look, and something in the morning loosened by a degree. Dray handed back the paper. "You'll need the door opened each day, then."

"And locked again after. And I'll thank you not to smoke near the paper stock — it's damp enough already." Marta was already moving, the basket swinging, an old woman with work to do. At the door, as the young soldier fitted the key with the reverence of a man granted a small sacrament, she paused and looked back down the row at Dray.

"Sergeant. When they knock this building down — will you stand and watch it?"

He considered. "Probably, ma'am. Somebody has to account for the rubble."

"Somebody does," Marta said. "Somebody always does. Mind you write the names down when you do it."

Inside, the workshop smelled as it had always smelled — linseed, glue, stone dust, and under it the deep mineral breath of the press. Dust lay on everything in a grey felt, undisturbed since the seizure. The great wheel stood dark. On the wall, someone had left a chalk drawing a child had made forty years ago, a lighthouse with a crooked beam, and no one had ever had the heart to wash it off.

Marta set down her basket, unwrapped the oilcloth from the type case Rolo had smuggled in the day before hidden under storm-log reams, and laid her hands on the press wheel, cold and familiar as a sleeping animal.

"Well, old friend," she said to the machine, to the room, to the dead who had worked here. "Let's make some truth while there's still time."

Outside, the young soldier turned the key in the lock behind her, sealing her in with the past — and Marta Vidal, alone among the ghosts with ink on her fingers and fourteen days left in the world, began to work.

Through the fogged window, far off on the headland, she could see the lighthouse standing white against the grey. And below it, small as an ant, a figure in army blue coming up the coast road at a pace that had nothing casual in it — a runner, moving fast, moving toward the barricade.

Marta's hands went still on the wheel.

Then, deliberately, she reached for the ink stone, and began.
