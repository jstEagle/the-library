## 25. Daylight Forgery

Marta Vidal had spent eighty-one years learning that the boldest thing a woman could do was walk through a door as though she owned what waited on the other side. She had done it at nineteen, when she walked into the orchard office and demanded her father's share be written down. She had done it at forty, when the tax assessor came and she stood in his way until he wrote the numbers correctly. And now, on a morning gray as unpolished pewter, she did it again: she put on her good shawl, took up a wicker basket with two loaves of bread in it — bread was important; no one questioned a woman carrying bread — and walked toward the workshop where the soldiers were already stacking crates in the yard.

The demolition order had gone up three days past, nailed to the church door beneath the requisition notice like a smaller, crueler scripture. *Structure to be cleared within six weeks. Presses, seals, type, and records to be inventoried and removed.* Marta had read it four times while the wind tried to tear it from the nails, and each time she read it she felt the same cold arithmetic settle in her chest: without the press, without the seals, the pages they were copying by lamplight would remain what they were — ink on paper, a story, a hope. With the press and the seals, they would become a document. A document could be argued with. A document could stand.

She had argued the plan herself, in the lighthouse kitchen, over Ines's objections and Rolo's wide-eyed terror and Cyrus's long silence.

"They will hang you," Ines had said. Not cruelly. Simply, the way one says *the tide comes in.*

"No," Marta had answered. "They will salute me. That is the beauty of it. Soldiers do not question an old woman with orders. They have never once questioned me. They look through me. I am furniture to them, Ines. Furniture that talks." She had let that sit in the room like smoke. "So I will talk."

Cyrus had been washing the same cup for the length of the argument. At last she had set it down and said only, "If you are caught, you never saw the packets. You never heard of them. You went to save the seals because they belonged to your father." A pause. "Which is even true."

That was the thing about Cyrus Reyes that Marta had come, slowly and against her own resistance, to admire: the woman had learned to tell the truth the way a fencer uses a blade — not to hide behind, but to fight with.

Now the workshop rose ahead of her, its stone walls the color of old honey where the morning light touched them, its great doors standing open for the first time in Marta's memory. Soldiers moved in and out with crates. A sergeant — Ferro, the one with the mustache he groomed more faithfully than his boots — directed two privates who were wrestling the second press onto a handcart, its iron body groaning against the cobbles.

Marta stopped at the threshold, adjusted her shawl, and let her face arrange itself into what Cyrus called her church face: stern, patient, faintly aggrieved, the face of a woman who has been kept waiting.

"Sergeant," she said, in the tone she had used on four generations of town clerks. "I was told yesterday I'd find Captain Hargrove's man here. Are you his?"

Sergeant Ferro looked at her the way men look at weather — something to be endured, not understood. "Who's asking?"

"Marta Vidal. The captain's clerk sent word last evening that I'm to crate the contents of the print room before your men handle them. Fragile items. Seals and matrices. If a seal cracks in transit, someone's paperwork becomes worthless, and I imagine someone's career follows it." She said this without hurrying, the way one recites scripture. "Where is the print room?"

For a moment there was nothing but the sound of the sea below the bluff and the scrape of the handcart. Then Ferro frowned — not suspicion, Marta noted with a thrill she kept carefully off her face, but the particular frown of a man who has too much to move and too few hands to move it. "Nobody told me anything about a print room."

"Of course they didn't." She sighed, and it was a masterpiece of a sigh; she had practiced it on her late husband for thirty years. "They never do. But the inventory list is in the front office, sergeant, and if my name isn't on it, then you may arrest me and explain to Captain Hargrove why the seals arrived at headquarters cracked in half. He strikes me as a man who counts things."

That landed. She watched it land — saw the little calculation pass behind Ferro's eyes, weighing the small trouble of an old woman against the large trouble of a damaged requisition. Every soldier she had ever met had been fighting exactly that war.

"Wait here," he said, and went inside.

* * *

While he was gone, Marta allowed herself, for the first time in days, to truly look at the workshop.

Her father had helped build it — not the walls, which were older than anyone living, but the roof beams, hauled up from the beach by teams of oxen when he was a young man with rope burns on his palms. Inside those walls the ledger had been printed, page by page, sixty years ago, after the season the soldiers came through and stripped the orchards bare. *An orchard for the uniform,* her grandmother had called it, spitting into the dust: the army taking fruit by the cartload and leaving nothing, not even a receipt. The ledger was born from that theft. Every name written in it was a wall against the next one.

And now the army had come back for the ledger itself, and meant to knock down the building that made it, and the world expected everyone to call this progress or necessity or war. Marta had outlived bishops and magistrates and two wars' worth of official language, and she had concluded that official language was simply theft wearing its Sunday coat.

Ferro returned with a corporal carrying a leather folio. "Show me your name," the corporal said, and there it was — because Cyrus had forged it there two nights ago, in a clerk's careful hand, third line of the print-room inventory: *Vidal, M. — packing of seals and matrices under supervision.* The ink was fresh enough that Marta had insisted on holding the page near the lamp and checking the bleed. Forgery, she thought, was just honesty with better timing.

"Under supervision," Marta repeated aloud, tapping the words with one knuckle. "Meaning your men don't touch the seals. They carry boxes. I pack."

The corporal glanced at Ferro. Ferro shrugged with his whole shoulders, a gesture that said *not my problem* in every military dialect on the coast. "Fine. But we're clearing this floor by Friday. Whatever you don't pack goes on the cart regardless."

"You'll have everything packed by Thursday," Marta said, "if your men stop gawking and start hauling," and walked past both of them into the print room as though she had walked into it every morning of her life.

In truth she hadn't set foot in it since her father died, forty years ago. It smelled the same: ink and oil and paper and the cold mineral breath of stone. The cases of type stood along the far wall like a congregation at prayer. On the workbench sat the seals — brass dies in their wooden case, the town's authentication mark worn smooth at the edges from decades of pressing. Her father's thumb had worn that edge. Her father's hand had pressed every original page.

Marta laid her palm flat on the bench and stood very still.

Then she rolled up her sleeves and began to work.

* * *

The plan had been built across four nights of whispered argument, and it was simple in the way all desperate plans are simple: the circle could copy the pages, but copies proved nothing. Only documents bearing the workshop's authentication seal — pressed with the actual die, on paper stock from the workshop's own stores, with the counter-mark of the town registry — would carry weight beyond these cliffs. So the seals had to be used. Here. Now. Under the soldiers' noses, in plain sight, because the safest place to commit a crime is inside your enemy's own errand.

Each night since the demolition notice, Cyrus had slipped Marta a handful of completed transcription pages, copied from Tomás Reyes's hidden duplicate, carried in the false bottom of the bread basket. Each night Marta had studied the layout of the guards' shifts, learned their faces and their habits — the tall one who chewed tobacco, the young one who sang under his breath, Ferro who feared his captain's ledgers more than God. And today, with the presses half-loaded and the soldiers' backs turned by the labor of moving iron, she would print.

Not forge, she corrected herself, feeding the first transcription page onto the press bed with hands that trembled only slightly. *Print.* The word mattered. Forging was making something false. This was making something true — truer than anything the army's own registers contained. The names were real. The claims were real. The signatures of clerks fifty years dead were real, because Rolo had copied them stroke by stroke from the hidden duplicate until his hand ached. All she added was the seal, and the seal was hers by inheritance, by blood, by the plain fact that her father had cut the die and no one alive had better right to it.

The press was the smaller of the two, still bolted to its bed in the corner, and the soldiers, in their efficiency, had left it for last — too heavy to rush, too awkward for two men. Good. Marta inked the plate with the roller, breathing the familiar bitterness of the ink, her father's motions returning to her hands like birds coming home to roost. Lay the page. Set the type-block with the seal matrix seated in its cradle. Pull.

The lever resisted, then gave. The screw descended. The impression bit into the paper with a sound like a quiet kiss.

When Marta lifted the finished sheet, the seal sat crisp in the lower corner, the counter-mark beside it, the whole page suddenly transformed — no longer a memory but a record, no longer grief but evidence. Sixty years of names, authenticated. She held it a moment longer than prudence allowed, feeling the paper's texture, warm where the press had worked it.

Then she folded it, placed it in the first crate, covered it with a layer of genuine scrap paper, and called out in a strong clear voice:

"You! The tall one. Bring me that box of packing straw, and mind you don't drop it — there's glassware in some of these."

* * *

The morning became a strange, almost holy absurdity.

Marta packed. The soldiers carried. Between loads, while Ferro supervised the loading of the big press outside, she pulled the lever again, and again — six impressions before the midday break, nine by mid-afternoon, each page fed in beneath the noise and motion of the clearance, each sealed page buried in a crate under honest rubbish. The soldiers grew fond of her, in the way soldiers grow fond of any civilian who feeds them; the loaves of bread vanished by noon, and the young one who sang began calling her *abuela* without irony, and she gave him the last heel of the second loaf and told him his mother would want him eating better.

"Do you have sons, señora?" he asked, resting on the doorstep, wiping his hands.

"One. Dead thirty years." She said it plainly; grief that old can be handled barehanded. "He'd have liked this shop. Everyone's grandfather built part of it. My father raised two roof beams with these hands—" she held them up, knotted as tree roots, "—and they'll pull the roof down next month and call it necessary."

The boy looked uncomfortable. "Orders," he offered.

"Orders." She nodded slowly, as though tasting the word. "My father used to say every theft in history was an order somebody followed. Then he carved his name on the beam so no one could say the roof built itself." She pointed upward, and the boy, helpless before an old woman's pointing finger, actually looked — looked up at the sooty beam where, high among the shadows, letters were cut deep into the wood: *J. VIDAL, 1854.* He stared at it a long moment.

"It's a good beam," he said at last, lamely.

"It's a good name," Marta said. "Mind the difference."

He didn't understand her. He wasn't meant to. But he carried the next crate more gently, and when Marta fed the eleventh page under the press and pulled the lever, he was outside arguing with Ferro about whose turn it was to push the cart, and the impression kissed the paper unheard, unwitnessed, true.

* * *

By the time the light went amber over the water, thirteen pages lay sealed in three crates stacked by the door, waiting for tomorrow's transport — which was the final beauty of the arrangement, the piece Cyrus had insisted on and Marta had doubted until she saw it working: the army itself would carry the authenticated ledger out of the town hall's shadow and into the quartermaster's warehouse, where Rolo knew a gap behind the flour sacks wide enough for a boy to slip through at night and swap the sealed originals for blanks. Let the army guard its own theft. Let the army's own crates become the ark.

Marta closed the wooden case of seal dies, wiped the press screw with oiled rag the way her father had taught her — *iron remembers kindness* — and stepped back to survey the room. Half-emptied shelves. Straw on the floor. The smell of ink fading under the smell of soldiers' boots. Tomorrow they would load the type cases, and the day after, perhaps, the seals themselves would go into an army wagon, and whatever happened after that, the names would be safe in triplicate: one copy in the lighthouse, one in the warehouse, one in Marta's own coffin-bound memory, which had never yet lost a name it cared to keep.

She was pulling her shawl straight when Sergeant Ferro appeared in the doorway with the corporal, and both wore expressions she had seen before on clerks and priests: the expression of men arriving with bad news they hoped was someone else's fault.

"Señora Vidal," Ferro said. "Captain Hargrove is on his way up from the landing. He wants to inspect the print room personally before the seals are transported. Tonight."

The words dropped into Marta's chest like stones into a well. She heard them fall and heard, beneath them, the silence at the bottom — the thought that arrived cold and complete: *he counts things.* Thirteen pages printed in a single day. Fresh ink. A crate list that would show paper drawn from stores at rates no honest packing required. A woman who claimed to be following orders that existed only in fresh handwriting.

Hargrove, who noticed discrepancies. Hargrove, who was courteous, exhausted, and utterly procedural, and who had looked at Cyrus Reyes across a lamp-oil delivery as though reading a page.

"Tonight," Marta repeated. Her voice came out steady. Eighty-one years of practice. "Well then. He'll find the room in better order than his own army leaves anything. Fetch me a lamp, sergeant — if the captain wants an inspection, he shall have one worthy of the name."

She turned back to the press as the soldiers hurried off, and alone in the gathering dusk, with the sealed pages sitting three feet away in their straw, Marta Vidal allowed herself one breath of pure, undiluted fear — and then put it away like the shawl she would need tomorrow, because fear, like everything else in this town, had always been something a woman carried, not something that carried her.

Down the hill, lanterns were climbing. He was coming.
