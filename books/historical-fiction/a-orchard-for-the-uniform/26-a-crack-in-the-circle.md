## 26. A Crack in the Circle

The wind came off the water that morning carrying the smell of the army's cookfires, and Simão Prado stood in his doorway and hated it the way he had hated everything for forty years.

He was sixty-one years old and he had lived his whole life in the shadow of an orchard that did not belong to him. Not legally — that was the poison of it, that it *did* belong to him in every way that mattered to a man's blood, and did not belong to him in any way a clerk or a captain would ever write down. His grandfather had planted the east rows of the Vidal orchard when the trees were whips no thicker than a thumb, had grafted them, had carried water up the hill in the drought year when Marta Vidal's grandmother lay abed and let other people save her land for her. And when the ledger was made — after the soldiers came the first time, before Simão was born — the Prado name had been left out of it. Left out. As though his grandfather's hands had never touched the soil. As though sweat were not also ink.

His father had gone to the town hall twice a year for thirty years, hat in hand, asking the clerks to look again at page fourteen. The clerks had looked. The clerks had found the Vidals' claim written fair and square, witnessed and sealed, and had found nothing beneath it but blank paper where a second name might have been.

"They wrote it down wrong on purpose," his father used to say, in the evenings, in a voice worn smooth as river stone from saying it so long. "Or they wrote it down right and God knows which is worse."

Simão had inherited the grievance the way one inherits a bad roof: constantly leaking, too expensive to fix, impossible to ignore.

* * *

He saw them come out of the lighthouse on the third evening of that week — the widow Reyes first, then old Marta Vidal leaning on her stick, then Ines the blacksmith's widow with her shawl pulled tight, and last the Corvo boy, Rolo, carrying a satchel pressed against his chest like a sleeping infant. They moved apart as they reached the cliff path, spacing themselves the way smugglers do, and Simão watched them from behind his fence with the particular patience of a man who has spent decades noticing what nobody wants noticed.

He knew what they carried. He had known for two weeks, ever since he'd seen lamplight burning in the lamp room at hours when no honest keeper needed it, ever since he'd heard Ines's low voice through the fog reading aloud strings of names — names he could recite in his sleep, because he had spent his childhood memorizing the ones that were *there* in order to torment himself with the one that wasn't.

They were copying the ledger. The hidden ledger, Tomás Reyes's copy, the thing the whole town whispered about now in the way people whisper about things they are afraid of and drawn to at once.

And they were going to restore it. Exactly as it was. Every name, every claim, every seal — including the absence of his own.

Simão stood at his fence until the dark took them, and something in him set like mortar.

* * *

He went to the tavern that night, which he almost never did, because the tavern was full of men who had names in the ledger and he had learned young that a man without a name drinks alone whether he sits at the bar or not. But tonight he wanted to hear them talk about it. He wanted them to say it out loud, joyfully, carelessly, so that his anger would have something specific to fasten to.

He got more than he wanted. They were toasting the lighthouse woman now. Cyrus Reyes, the outsider who had turned out to be the keeper of their history — you couldn't make it up, old Ferro said, slapping the table, God sends the strangest angels. Ines had taught half the room Tomás's shorthand marks over mugs of thin wine. Even the boy, Rolo, was a hero; somebody had bought him a lemonade and he sat glowing among the grown men like a candle in a cake.

"And when it's done," Ferro said, raising his mug, "when every page is sealed with the workshop press, we'll walk the whole book into the register ourselves, and let Hargrove choke on it politely."

Laughter. The good kind, the kind that comes from people who have been afraid together and are beginning to believe they might not die afraid.

Simão drank his wine at the end of the bar and said nothing, and felt the old absence in him grow teeth.

Because here was the thing none of them understood, none of them *could* understand, wrapped as they were in the warm blanket of being remembered: the ledger was not justice. It was only a record. It recorded who had been granted what by men who favored their own cousins and neighbors and fellow congregants, and the Prados had been neither cousin nor neighbor nor congregant to anyone who mattered. When the ledger was restored — sealed, authenticated, entered into the register, made holy — the absence of his name would be made holy too. Set in official ink. Sealed under wax. Forever.

They thought they were digging their town out of a grave. They were building the grave deeper, and laying him in it.

* * *

He walked home past the orchard. He always walked past the orchard; it was a wound he kept re-opening on purpose, the way some men press a bruise to prove they can still feel. The army had strung wire along the lower wall and posted a sentry who smoked and looked bored, and beyond the wire the east rows stood unpruned, going wild, the fruit swelling unpicked on branches his grandfather's hands had shaped.

Sixty-one years. He had watched those trees through four owners' neglect and three harvests stolen outright — once by tax men, once by a blight crew that took the sound fruit and left the rotten, and once, long ago, by soldiers who stripped the orchard bare and left nothing, which was why the ledger existed at all. Marta told that story sometimes, the story of the title, and everyone nodded at the moral of it: *write it down so it can never happen again.*

But writing it down had been done by the same hands that had always held the pen in this town. And the Prados had never held anything.

He stopped at the wall, at the gap where the stones had crumbled and he could see the east rows clearly, and he said quietly, to the trees, to his grandfather, "I'm the last one who even remembers your hands were here. And I'm the one they'll erase last of all."

The trees said nothing. Trees never do. That was the whole trouble with trees.

* * *

It took him three days to decide, and he decided the way most men decide — slowly, telling himself he hadn't yet, right up until the moment he had.

Not because he loved the army. He despised them; his hatred was old and comfortable and required no maintenance. But Hargrove's soldiers had taken everything from everyone equally, and there was a bitter arithmetic in that. For the first time in his life, the lawlessness cut across all the names in the ledger alike. If the requisition stood, then the Vidals lost their orchard and the Prados lost their chance at it in the same stroke, and there was a filthy kind of fairness in that — the fairness of the flood, which drowns the well-drowned and the dry-throated identically.

And if the ledger came back — the ledger *as it was* — then the old order came back with it, sealed and sanctified, and Simão Prado would spend whatever years remained to him watching other families harvest trees his grandfather planted.

Unless someone opened the book while it was still wet.

He told himself that was all he wanted. Not vengeance. Amendment. A line added beneath page fourteen: *Prado, Simão, son of Rafael, grandson of Duarte, planter of the east rows.* One line. Was that so much? Would it cost the circle anything, truly, to add one true sentence to their sacred record?

He knew the answer before he finished asking it. He had watched Cyrus Reyes argue in the tavern weeks ago — he'd heard it secondhand, retold a dozen times, each telling making her sound sterner — *truth over comfort.* She would sooner burn the pages than amend them, he suspected. Truth, she'd say, means the whole truth, including the truth that the ledger was unjust. And Marta Vidal would say the ledger must stand as it stands or it stands for nothing.

Two honest women, both certain, both wrong about him.

Well. Then he would be wrong back.

* * *

He went to see them on the fourth evening. Not the soldiers — not yet. He owed himself that much honesty at least: he would give them the chance to do right before he gave the army the chance to do harm.

The lighthouse stood against the early dusk with its lamp already lit, a struck match against a bruised sky. He climbed the path slowly, his knees complaining, rehearsing and abandoning speeches. At the door below the gallery stairs he knocked, and Cyrus Reyes opened it with the wariness he expected — she had been searched once already; she knew what knocks meant now.

"Senhor Prado." She knew his name. That surprised him more than it should have. "Come in out of the wind."

The ground floor of the light station smelled of oil and cold stone and, faintly, of fresh ink. He noticed the smell the way a dog notices blood. On the table under the window lay storm logs, stacked square, and atop them a log open to a page of weather entries — but the handwriting shifted partway down, one hand becoming another becoming a third, and the columns of wind-speed figures were just slightly too regular, too patient, to be weather.

She followed his eyes and closed the log without hurry. "You've come about the lamp oil allotment," she said. "I've already filed—"

"I've come about page fourteen," Simão said.

The silence afterward had texture. He felt her go still the way a hare goes still — not calm, but calculating distance and cover.

"Page fourteen," she repeated.

"The Vidal claims. East rows, west rows, workshop shares." He kept his voice level, an old man's reasonable voice, the voice he used with tax assessors. "You're copying it. All of it. Exactly."

"We're restoring a record that was stolen."

"You're restoring a record that was *written*," he said, and heard his own voice crack on the word, sixty years of crack packed into one syllable. "Written by the same five families who always wrote things in this town. My grandfather planted the east rows of that orchard with his own hands. Grafted every tree above the spring line. Carried water the summer old Vidal's wife took to her bed and didn't lift a finger to help. And when the book was made, his name wasn't in it. Not denied — worse than denied. *Absent.* As if he'd never existed. As if the trees grew themselves."

Cyrus Reyes looked at him for a long moment. He had expected anger, or fear, or the smooth deflection of a guilty person. Instead he saw something worse: understanding.

"You want us to add your family's claim," she said.

"I want you to write one true sentence."

"It isn't ours to write." She said it gently, which made it unbearable. "Senhor Prado — I know what you're saying is probably true. I believe you. But if we start amending — who decides? Me? Marta? You? The man whose claim was refused next decides his refusal was unfair, and he's right too, maybe, and then the next man, and the record becomes a argument instead of a witness. The ledger is what it is. What it is includes the wrong done to you. That wrong is *in* it — by its absence — and when it's restored, the absence gets restored too, and I will not pretend that doesn't cost you. But the alternative is a book no one can trust, and a book no one can trust protects no one. Not even you."

"Not even me." Simão laughed, and it came out ugly. "Madam, the book as it stands has failed to protect me for sixty-one years. You propose to seal it for eternity. And you tell me it costs me nothing."

"I didn't say nothing. I said I won't pretend otherwise."

"There it is." He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something rather than refused something. "Truth over comfort. That's your phrase, isn't it? I've heard it retold in the tavern like scripture. Well, madam, your truth is my erasure. Your inheritance is my disinheritance, bound in seals and wax and entered into the register by an army officer, no less — the state itself stamping its approval on the theft of my grandfather's labor. You aren't restoring justice. You're restoring the injustice, and calling it justice because it's *old*."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. "No," she said, quieter. "Maybe it is partly that. Maybe every restoration is partly that. But tearing it up helps no one, least of all you."

"No," Simão agreed. "Tearing it up helps no one. Which is why I'm not proposing to tear it up." He let the pause stretch until she heard what lived inside it. "I'm proposing you add one line. Or I speak to Captain Hargrove. He seems a reasonable man. Exhausted, courteous. He'd listen to an old man's grievance, I think. Especially one about a document being forged under his nose in the lighthouse he so generously lets you keep."

There it was. Said. He watched it land on her face and he hated himself even as he felt the terrible relief of a man who has finally set down something heavy regardless of where he sets it down.

"You'd hand them the pages," she said. "Marta. Ines. The boy. You'd hand *Rolo* to them — sixteen years old — to correct a line in a book."

"I'd hand them nothing," Simão said. "I'd hand them a question. *Add the line, and there's nothing to find.* That's all. One sentence, madam. Sixty years, and all I ask is one true sentence."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I'll know exactly how much your justice weighs." He put his hat back on, straightening the brim with both hands, an old ritual that steadied him. "I'll give you until Sunday. Three days. The same courtesy you'd give anyone whose name was worth writing down."

* * *

He walked home in the full dark, past the orchard wall, past the bored sentry's cigarette glow, and the wind had risen and smelled of rain coming from far out at sea. Behind him the lighthouse beam swung its slow white arm over the water, over the town, over the wild east rows, over him, impartially lighting everything and claiming nothing — the only truly fair thing left on this coast, he thought, and even it belonged to a woman whose husband had helped write him out of history.

At his gate he stopped and looked back once. The lamp room glowed steady above the cliff. Somewhere up there were the pages, and somewhere inside him was the knowledge of what he had just threatened, and the two facts sat in him side by side like strangers forced to share a bench, neither willing to leave.

Three days. He had given them three days, and himself three days, and he honestly did not know which man would arrive at Sunday — the one who wanted a single line of ink, or the one who wanted the whole rotten fair book drowned in the sea with everything else.

Down in the harbor, faint on the wind, he heard the army's bugle sound the evening call, and the first drops of rain began to strike the dust of the road.
