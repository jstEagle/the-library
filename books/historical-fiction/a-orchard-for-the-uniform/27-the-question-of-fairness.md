## 27. The Question of Fairness

The storm had passed without striking, leaving behind a sky scrubbed thin and a sea that lay flat as poured tin, and still the lighthouse smelled of it — salt in every seam, brine worked into the grain of the stair rail so that even on the fairest days the tower remembered what it was built against. Cyrus climbed down from the gallery that evening with her hands smelling of brass polish and lamp oil, and found them waiting for her at the kitchen table: Marta Vidal with her spine straight as a mast, Ines with her mending abandoned in her lap, and Rolo sprawled sideways across two chairs like something the tide had left.

They came three nights a week now, sometimes four. The transcription was nearly whole. Tomás's pages and Brandt's water-stained sheets lay married together on the table under the good lamp, weighted at the corners with the flat stones Cyrus had carried up from the beach years ago because they pleased her, gray and oval, each one holding its small cold weight of sea inside it. Two hundred and eleven names restored. Orchard boundaries drawn again in ink where they had lived only in memory since the soldiers rowed ashore. Workshop shares, mill rights, the burial ground behind the church with its forty-one plots assigned by family line.

It should have felt like victory. Instead, for six days, the table had been silent in a way that had nothing to do with peace.

Cyrus hung her oilskin on its peg and did not sit down at once. She stood at the window instead, looking out at the harbor, where the army's transport barges rode at anchor like patient animals, and let herself feel the shape of the thing she had to say. She had been turning it over all day while she trimmed wicks and scraped the lens, turning it the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath.

"Rolo," she said at last, "read us entry forty-four."

The boy sat up. "That's the Ferran claim."

"That's the Ferran claim."

He wet his thumb and turned the pages with the exaggerated care he'd learned from watching them all handle the old sheets — the boy had developed reverence late but completely, the way some boys learn to pray. "'Entry forty-four,'" he read. "'The south orchard, twelve rows between the stone wall and the creek bend, held in common by the families Vidal and Amaral, apportioned by lot each harvest. Entered by hand of Clerk Duarte, spring of 1841.'"

"And entry forty-five?"

Rolo read it, and his voice slowed as the meaning arrived. "'Entry forty-five. Petition of Joaquim Ferran, laborer, for a share of the south orchard, denied. Grounds: his family is not of the founding lines. Signed, Duarte.'" He looked up. "That's old Senhor Ferran's grandfather. That's—"

"That's Tomás Ferran's family," Ines said quietly. Her needle had stopped moving. "The man who came to the tavern last week. The one who says if the ledger comes back the way it was, his people are tenants on their own ground forever."

The lamp ticked as it burned. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the rocks with its endless small patience.

Marta Vidal folded her hands. Cyrus knew that gesture. It was what Marta did before she said something she considered self-evident, and it had preceded most of the hard sentences of the past months. "The ledger is the ledger," Marta said. "We restore what was written. We do not rewrite it. The moment we improve upon it, we make it opinion, and opinion can be argued with. Ink cannot."

"The ink denied him," Cyrus said.

"The ink *recorded* that he was denied." Marta's voice stayed level, but Cyrus saw her knuckles whiten where her hands clasped. "There is a difference, and the difference is everything. We are not writing history, Cyrus. We are proving it. If we alter one line — one — then Hargrove, or whoever sits behind Hargrove, needs only to find that line and the whole record dies. Every name. Yours. Mine. Ines's boys. All of it, poisoned by our mercy."

"I'm not proposing we alter it."

"You're proposing exactly that."

"I'm proposing we finish it." Cyrus finally sat down, at the far end of the table, where she could see all their faces in the lamplight. She had rehearsed this argument alone in the gallery for two nights, speaking it to the wind, and the wind had agreed with her, which was no recommendation at all. But here it had to be spoken to human beings, who were harder. "Tomás copied the ledger as it stood in 1841. Brandt stole pages that were already old when he bought them. Neither of those men wrote anything new. But the ledger itself stopped being written decades ago, and people kept being born, and dying, and marrying, and clearing new ground, and none of it went into any book because there was no book. We have spent five weeks restoring a record. Are we restoring a corpse, or a living thing?"

"A record doesn't live," Marta said. "A record *holds*. That is its whole virtue. You of all people should understand that — you keep a light that says only one thing, night after night, and it saves lives precisely because it never improvises."

Cyrus took the blow without flinching, because it was well aimed. "My light also burns different colors in fog," she said. "Red and white. Because the truth of where you are changes, and a light that couldn't tell you so would run you onto the shoals while swearing it was helping."

* * *

They argued until midnight. It went the way arguments go among people who love each other badly and well at once — circling back, sharpening, wearing grooves. Ines mostly listened, her needle moving and stopping, moving and stopping, and when she spoke at all it was to ask questions that neither side wanted asked. Rolo said nothing for a long time, which from Rolo was remarkable, and watched them with an expression Cyrus couldn't read until suddenly she could: he was watching them the way he had once watched the soldiers drill on the quay, with the hungry attention of a boy deciding what kind of person to become.

It was nearly one in the morning when Marta pushed back her chair and stood, and Cyrus understood that the argument had entered its last hour, the way weather enters its last hour before it either breaks or turns.

"My mother picked olives in that orchard," Marta said. "Not owned — *picked*. My grandmother before her. When the ledger gave my family our twelve rows in 1841, it was the first paper that ever said a Vidal belonged anywhere, and I will tell you what that paper cost. It cost every family that wasn't in it. I know that. I've known it since I was a girl and old Ferran used to stand at the wall during harvest and watch us pick fruit from trees his father planted and wasn't paid for planting." Her voice roughened. "I am not a fool, Cyrus Reyes. I know the ledger was unjust. I helped carry it out of the town hall myself, and my arms knew its weight, and my conscience knew it too. But listen to me. Listen." She leaned forward, both palms flat on the table, on top of the dead clerk's careful ink. "If we amend it — if we add the Ferrans, the Ocañas, anyone — we are doing in secret what was done to us in public: deciding, by our own authority, who belongs. We would be building a new injustice on top of the old one and calling it repair. And worse — worse — we would be handing our enemies the knife. One altered line and the whole restoration is counterfeit. Do you want the Ferrans' names in the book, or do you want the book to survive? Because you may not be able to have both, and if you must choose, you choose the book. The book holds everyone's future. The Ferrans' grievance holds only their grief."

"It holds four generations of it," Cyrus said. "And grief that old doesn't stay in one family. It gets out. It walks around. It walked into the tavern last week and threatened to inform on us, or have you forgotten?"

"I haven't forgotten. I was there."

"Then hear me." Cyrus rose too. She hadn't meant to stand; her legs decided on their own. "You say we'd be deciding who belongs by our own authority. Marta — the original ledger decided that too. By *someone's* authority. A clerk's pen in 1841. What makes that decision sacred and ours profane?"

"Time," Marta said instantly. "Time makes it law."

"Time made it *fact*," Cyrus said. "Law is another question entirely, and you know it, because you've spent your life telling anyone who'll listen that what the army does is lawful and wrong." She heard her own voice climb and pulled it down. "I loved Tomás. Not the ledger — Tomás. And Tomás believed in that book the way you do. He hid it in my walls at the risk of his neck. So believe me when I tell you what I found in his margins, because you haven't seen them all and I have. He questioned it. Right there in the storm logs, in his little shorthand, page after page, he questioned it. Entry forty-four, he wrote beside it: *who counted the lots?* Entry forty-five: *Ferran cleared the creek field himself, ask Duarte.* He noticed. He noticed everything, and he copied it anyway, faithfully, because he understood what you understand — that a record altered is a record destroyed." She paused, and the hardest part of it came up her throat like a stone swallowed in reverse. "But he didn't stop noticing. And if he had lived, I believe — I don't know, but I believe — he'd be standing where I'm standing now, saying the same thing. Restore it true, every denial and every injustice, exactly as it was. And then — *then* — append. Not erase. Append. A supplement, in our own hand, dated, signed, confessing openly: here is the old book, complete, unaltered, and here is what it failed to hold. Let Hargrove see both. Let whoever comes after see both. That isn't forgery. That's testimony."

* * *

Ines set down her mending at last, and the sound of it — wool and wood, small and final — quieted the room.

"May I say something," she said, though she was already saying it, "as someone whose name is in the book twice over. My father's line and my husband's." She smoothed the stocking across her knee without looking at it. "When the soldiers came, I lost my house and my share of the smithy, and I grieved, and I'm grieving still. But I'll tell you what frightens me more than losing it. It's getting it back and having it mean nothing. Having my sons inherit a piece of paper that restores them to land they'll hold while knowing the Ferran boy next door has none, and watching them learn to look at him the way the founders' children always looked at the rest of us — kindly, from above." She raised her eyes. "Marta, you said time makes it law. I say time makes it habit. And habit is harder to break than law."

"So we break the book instead?" Marta's voice cracked at last, and Cyrus heard underneath it not anger but exhaustion, and age, and the specific loneliness of a woman who has guarded something for sixty years and cannot bear to be told the guarding was imperfect. "You want to open it up on a table and start improving on the dead?"

"No," Cyrus said. "I want to tell the truth about it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." She picked up entry forty-five and laid it flat in the lamplight between them, the clerk's faded brown ink, the word *denied* sitting in the middle of the page like a nail driven through it. "This stays. Exactly as written, in facsimile, every ugly word. And beneath it, on a fresh leaf, we enter the petition again — the same words, the same grounds, the same family — and this time we write what the record shows: *renewed, 1913, by the hand of the restored ledger itself, which could not exist without the labor of the family it excluded.* Because that's the truth too, Marta. Isn't it? Who hauled the press parts when we printed the seals? Who stood watch? Whose grandson deciphered the water-stained pages half the night?" She looked at Rolo, who had gone very still. "The Ferrans have been building this book for weeks. They just don't know yet that it's theirs."

Silence. The lamp burned. Down in the dark the sea kept its accounts.

Marta looked at the page for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had dropped its armor and what was underneath was simply tired, and old, and honest. "Do you know what my mother said when I first showed her our name in that ledger? I was nine years old. She kissed the page. Kissed it, like a relic. And I have spent my whole life believing that kiss was clean." She straightened slowly, pressing her hand to the small of her back the way she did when the hour was late. "You may be right. God help us, you may be right, and I hate how much it costs to think so. But hear my conditions, and they are iron. Nothing erased. Nothing overwritten. The supplement bound separately, clearly marked, so that no one — not Hargrove, not a court, not a priest — can say the old text was touched. And the town votes. Not us four in a kitchen at one in the morning playing Providence. Everyone whose name is in the book, and everyone whose name isn't. They hear both sides, and they decide."

"They'll decide in fear," Ines murmured. "Half of them will vote whatever keeps the army happy."

"Then they'll vote wrongly," Marta said, "but they'll vote freely, which is more than the ledger ever gave them." She gathered her shawl, and at the door she stopped, her back to the room. "Your Tomás sounds like he was a better man than the book he saved."

"He was," Cyrus said. "That's why he saved it anyway."

* * *

After they'd gone — Marta first, unbending; Ines lingering to stack the pages in their oilskins with hands that moved like a ritual; Rolo last, pausing at the threshold with a question visibly forming and dying on his lips — Cyrus banked the lamp and climbed the stair alone. At the gallery rail the wind had come around to the southeast, soft but steady, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't started yet. Out past the shoals the horizon was a bruise.

She thought about Tomás's marginal note — *ask Duarte* — and about the clerk dying now in the room the army had given him, and whether a man could repent in ink after selling paper by the sheet. She thought about the neighbor, Tomás Ferran, standing at his orchard wall for fifty years, and whether forgiveness could be printed and bound and sealed, or whether it was the one document that had to be handwritten, slowly, generation after generation, and mostly refused.

She thought about Hargrove, whom she had not seen in nine days, and found that she was worrying about him as one worries about weather — professionally, distantly, and without any illusion of control. His requisition lists would show discrepancies now. Brandt's silence had been bought, but silences were like storms: they held only until they didn't. And if the captain came asking questions again, he would come to the lighthouse, because he was a thorough man, and thorough men always came eventually to the place where the light was kept.

Below her, in the tower's stone throat, two hundred and eleven restored names slept in oilskin. Tomorrow, perhaps, there would be more — appended, confessed, argued over at a kitchen table by lamplight, which was, Cyrus was beginning to suspect, how all real history got written: not by victors, but by tired people who couldn't sleep and wouldn't stop.

She went down to trim the wick for the night watch, and found on the kitchen table, where Rolo must have left it in passing, a scrap of paper in his careful schoolboy hand:

*Entry forty-six. Proposed. Petition of Rolo — surname blank, grounds pending — for a share of the light.*

She laughed out loud, alone, at one-thirty in the morning, the first laugh in longer than she could measure — and then, turning the scrap over, stopped laughing. On its reverse, in a different hand, in pencil, in letters she did not recognize, someone had written a single line:

*They meet Tuesdays and Fridays. The tall one keeps watch at the point.*

And beneath it, faintly, the impression of an army franking stamp.
