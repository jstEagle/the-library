## 28. Hargrove Suspects

The wind had turned in the night, swinging round from the northeast with a smell of iron and cold water, and Cyrus Reyes stood at the lamp room rail with her hands wrapped around a mug of chicory that had gone cold without her noticing.

Below, the harbor was already at work — soldiers loading crates onto the transport barge, the workshop's dismantled press crated under tarpaulin, the whole town being folded up like a letter no one would read. Six weeks, Hargrove had said. Then the telegram had come, and the six weeks became less than four, and the sound of hammers had not stopped since.

She should have felt afraid. She supposed she did, somewhere beneath the ribs, where fear lived now like a tenant who paid no rent and never left. But mostly she felt the strange, weightless calm of a woman who has passed the point of retreat. The pages were finished. Sealed. Hidden in three places only Marta knew all of. Whatever came now came to people who had already chosen.

The lighthouse door opened below her — two knocks, spaced evenly, the knock of a man who had learned that doors answered better to patience than force.

She set down the mug. She did not hurry down the stairs, and she noticed that she did not hurry, and understood that even this was a kind of answer.

* * *

Captain Ellery Hargrove waited in the entry hall with his cap in his hands, which was how he always waited — as though he were calling on a widow to express condolences rather than an officer conducting an inquiry. Perhaps, Cyrus thought, he could no longer tell the difference himself.

"Mrs. Reyes."

"Captain." She lit the small stove because her hands wanted something to do that was not trembling. "You'll take coffee? It's chicory. The army took the coffee."

"I'll take chicory," he said gravely, as if agreeing to terms.

She poured two cups. They sat at her own table, the one Tomás had built from driftwood and stubbornness, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The wind worried the glass overhead. Somewhere below, a crate dropped on stone and a soldier swore.

"You've been busy," Hargrove said at last.

"We all have."

"Yes." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. He did this when he was choosing words; she had watched him do it across the table in his quarters, when she brought oil, when they spoke of weather and wicks and nothing else. "Mrs. Reyes, I'm going to say something plainly, and then I'm going to ask you something, and you may answer or not as your conscience directs. I find I've lost my appetite for the other way of doing things."

Cyrus folded her hands. *Here it comes,* said the cold tenant beneath her ribs.

* * *

He had seen it first in the crates bound for the transport — the town's records, boxed for shipment to the district archive, where documents went to be forgotten with full honors. He had been walking the rows with Sergeant Doyle, initialing manifests, when he stopped at box fourteen and lifted out a folder that should have been forty years of salt-damp paper and wasn't.

The ink was fresh.

Not fresh the way a forgery is fresh — no blotted haste, no wrong-colored ink, none of the crude tells he'd spent two decades learning to smell. Fresh the way careful work is fresh: even strokes, proper iron-gall darkening at the edges of each letter, pages aged deliberately at their margins but not at their spines. Someone had written these documents recently and well. Someone who knew old inks, old hands, old forms.

And the seals. He had held one to the gray morning light for a long time, longer than Doyle thought comfortable. The workshop's seal. Pressed clean, pressed true — pressed recently, because the wax still carried a faint gloss that sixty years could not have left behind.

He had put the folder back exactly as he found it, initialed the manifest, and walked the rest of the rows without seeing a single page.

All afternoon the discrepancy had sat in him like a swallowed hook. Because here was the thing — the thing he could not file, could not report, could not stop turning over — the documents were *good*. Not good forgeries. Good *records*. He had spent three evenings since cross-checking against his own requisition lists, the ones he kept in duplicate because he trusted no quartermaster above the rank of lieutenant, and the names matched. The orchard holdings matched. The workshop shares matched, share by share, family by family, back three generations. Whoever had made these papers had not invented a history. They had *restored* one.

A criminal forges to gain. These papers would gain their makers nothing except what the original ledger had already granted them and the army had already taken away.

Which meant either he was looking at the most principled fraud of his career, or he was looking at the truth.

And above both those possibilities hung a third, the one that had kept him awake until the northeast wind rose: that someone had informed. That the moved-up demolition date and this fresh ink were threads of the same rope, and that somewhere above him — Brandt's accounts, those beautiful, impossible accounts, columns that balanced only if you didn't look too hard — someone was tying knots.

He drank his chicory. It was bitter and honest and he was grateful for it.

"The crated records," he said. "Box fourteen, and eleven others like it. The ink is recent. The seals are recent. The work is very fine."

Cyrus looked at him across the driftwood table, and he watched her decide — he could see it happen, the way he could watch weather decide over open water — whether to lie.

* * *

She did not lie. She also did not confess. She did something he had almost never encountered in twenty years of service and interrogations and boards of inquiry:

She told him the truth sideways.

"You've read them, then," she said. "The names."

"I've checked some of them."

"Then you know they're right."

"That isn't the question I asked, Mrs. Reyes."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

The wind moaned in the lantern housing above them, a low note like a wet finger on a glass rim. She refilled his cup without asking. He let her.

"Captain," she said, "do you know why I bring you your lamp oil myself instead of sending the boy?"

Hargrove blinked. "I assumed — efficiency."

"I bring it because the first week you were here, you stood in your requisitioned quarters holding a list of everything my town owned, and you apologized to me for the quality of the paper it was written on. You apologized. For paper." She set the pot down. "I decided then that you were either the best liar I'd ever met or the loneliest honest man in a dishonest uniform, and I've had two years of practice telling those apart, so I trust my judgment."

"And which am I?"

"I still don't know. That's why I bring the oil myself. To keep checking."

He almost smiled. It hurt his face to do it, she could see, the way a limb hurts when blood returns to it.

"Mrs. Reyes. If I confirm that those documents are forged — freshly made, falsely sealed — I am required to seize them, arrest those responsible, and forward the matter to the district tribunal. You know what the tribunal does with civilians who forge state-adjacent instruments during wartime administration."

"I've heard stories."

"They're accurate." He leaned forward, and now there was no cap in his hands, nothing for them to hold, and they lay flat on the table like a man setting down weapons. "So before I confirm anything, I am going to ask you once, woman to man, keeper to officer, and whatever you say next will determine what I do for the rest of my life. Were those documents in box fourteen written this year?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the sea worked at the shoals. Above, the great lens turned in its bath of mercury, patient as a planet.

Cyrus met his eyes.

"Captain Hargrove," she said, "you have read the names. You have checked them against your own lists, which you keep in duplicate because you don't trust your quartermaster — and you're right not to, but that's another conversation. You know those names are true. You know that ledger was taken from people whose grandmothers planted those orchards, and you know it's sitting in a hold on that barge going to an archive nobody will ever open. So the question isn't whether fresh ink is on old paper." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle. "The question is which of us is forging history — me, with my pen, or your government, with its absence of one."

He sat back slowly.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind of answer worth giving."

* * *

Later — much later, walking back down the cliff road in the failing light with the lamp flaring to life above him, right on schedule, because some things in this world still ran on duty alone — Ellery Hargrove admitted to himself what he had known since box fourteen.

He believed her.

Not because she hadn't confessed. Because she hadn't lied. He had watched a hundred mouths construct denials, and denial had architecture — too much detail, too little, eyes that went to the exits. Cyrus Reyes had done none of it. She had simply refused to hand him the noose and stood there while he decided whether to use his own.

He stopped halfway down the road and looked back. The lighthouse beam swept past him, over the black water, over the shoals, over the transport barge riding at anchor with the town's memory in its hold, and swept past again, faithful as breath.

If he reported the fresh ink, the tribunal would come. They would take Marta Vidal, who was seventy years old and had buried a husband and three sons in that soil. They would take the blacksmith's widow. They might take the boy. And the documents — the true ones, the restored ones — would burn in a courtyard somewhere as a warning, and the official record would show that the town of the orchards had never existed, and in thirty years no living person would be able to prove otherwise.

If he did not report it, he was guilty of dereliction in wartime administration. If it surfaced — and it would surface; Brandt's accounts were unraveling, and whoever had sent the telegram about the demolition date was watching him — he would lose everything. Rank, pension, name. A man of forty-six does not easily rebuild a name.

He stood there between the two futures the way a man stands between tides, letting both run over his boots.

Then he thought about the seals. About the care of them. About the weeks of work they represented — lamplit, secret, patient, done by people who had every reason to run and instead had stayed to write down their own names in their own hand because no one else would.

*They didn't forge anything,* he thought. *They signed it.*

He took out his notebook, opened to the day's entries, and beside the notation *Records inspected, boxes 9–22, condition satisfactory,* he wrote nothing further. He closed the book.

It was, he understood, the smallest possible crime. An omission. A blank space where a sentence belonged. But he had begun to suspect that history was not made of sentences. It was made of blanks — spaces where someone chose not to write the lie.

* * *

Cyrus climbed back to the lamp room after he left and found her chicory where she'd abandoned it, cold as harbor water, and drank it anyway.

He hadn't arrested her. She turned that over the way you turn a shell, listening for the sea inside it. He hadn't arrested her, and he hadn't pretended not to know, and he had drunk her chicory and let her insult his government to his face and gone down the cliff road with his cap in his hands like a mourner.

Either she had just watched a man choose her side. Or she had just watched a man decide to gather more evidence first.

From the gallery rail she could see the whole curve of the bay: the barge, the workshop's broken roofline, the barracks windows going yellow one by one. And along the shore road, small in the last light, a figure walking toward the town who was not a soldier — a neighbor's coat, a neighbor's uneven gait, old Corvo the stonemason, who had not spoken to her since the circle began and who was, she realized with a slow coldness spreading under her sternum, walking directly toward the barracks gate.

The lamp turned. The beam swept the water, and swept it again, and Cyrus stood very still in the wind, watching, as the gate opened to let him in.
